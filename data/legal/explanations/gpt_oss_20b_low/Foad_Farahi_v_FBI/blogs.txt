Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# When FOIA Meets Counter‑Terrorism: How the D.C. Circuit Is Sharpening Exemption 7(A) Deference

## Why This Decision Matters

The September 30, 2025 opinion in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* is one of the clearest recent applications of FOIA Exemption 7(A) to a counter‑terrorism investigation that has lasted for years. Farahi, an Iranian‑born American who has lived in the United States since 1993, sought his FBI file under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The FBI withheld the bulk of the material, arguing that disclosure would interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated law‑enforcement proceedings. The court upheld that exemption, even after a prolonged delay and the deposition of a new, brief declaration under seal. For attorneys representing plaintiffs in FOIA suits, the opinion offers a two‑step roadmap: first, the agency’s deference under Exemption 7(A) is vindicated if it can show that its investigations are *public* law‑enforcement activities, and second, the assumption that enforcement proceedings are *pending or reasonably anticipated* can survive a multi‑year gap provided the agency supplies sufficient, up‑to‑date evidence.

The decision also clarifies the scope of the “reasonable steps” requirement for segregation and the boundaries of the “inextricably intertwined” standard. In a context where counter‑terrorism investigations routinely involve enormous files, the court’s strict analysis offers compelling guidance on when a claim of “non‑segregable” documents can stand.

## The Anatomy of the Agency’s Argument

The FBI’s core assertion fell into two parts:

1. **(A) The file was compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.** Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court is comfortable deferring to the agency’s judgment that the material was produced “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism.” The agency cited court precedent (e.g., *Clemente* and *Campbell*) that requires a “nexus” between the investigation and the FBI’s law‑enforcement duties, as well as a link between an individual or incident and a potential security risk. Farahi himself had conceded that the FBI had investigated alleged ties to foreign terrorist organizations and to a person later convicted on terrorism charges. The agency’s declaration, therefore, satisfies the “compiled for law‑enforcement” requirement in a straightforward, deference‑friendly way.

2. **(B) Disclosure would “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”** Here the court turned to the statutory language— “pending or reasonably anticipated” – and to its own precedent in *Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (CREW), *Juarez v. DOJ*, and *Central for National Security Studies v. DOJ* (CNSS). The FBI’s Hardy declaration (public and ex‑parte) established that the investigation was ongoing, the cooperation of the agent overseeing Farahi’s file, and the potential for a future criminal case. Justice Katsas noted that the investigation’s *extent*—involving alleged ties to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, Khaled Sheikh Mohammed, and multiple convicted terrorists—bolstered the plausibility of a continuing, anticipatable proceeding.

The crux of the appellate analysis was the court’s treatment of the *time gap*. Farahi argued that the three‑year lapse obscured the “pending” status of the investigation and that the Bender declaration was thin. The court, however, emphasized context: the Bureau had already shown that the proceedings were pending in 2022. Bender’s role was not to *reset* the pendency claim, but to confirm that no change had occurred. Because the Bender report merely confirmed a status already established, the court concluded that it carried the necessary weight.

## How the Court’s Reasoning Sits with Existing Doctrine

### Deference Under Exemption 7(A)

The court reaffirmed the well‑traveled path that deference to the agency is warranted in FOIA Exemption 7(A) cases. When the agency can point to its *law‑enforcement* status and articulate a clear *nexus* between its investigation and a legitimate law‑enforcement objective, the court is unlikely to second‑guess that assessment. This echoes *Campbell* and *Clemente*. In national‑security contexts, the Court has even tightened deference, as noted in CNSS, acknowledging that officers on the front lines possess unique expertise.

### “Pending or reasonably anticipated” Standard

The decision refines the “pending or reasonably anticipated” language in a manner that will resonate across future FOIA battles. The Court treated the ongoing investigation as a *proceeding* for FOIA purposes, not just a loose “probe.” This follows *Juarez*, where courts have held that an investigation that is still gathering evidence can qualify, even absent an indictment or trial. The mere *existence* of an investigation, established by the agency’s own affidavits, suffices if the case is designed to culminate in a criminal action. That the inquiry was open for nearly twenty years does not automatically invalidate the claim. The Court’s language makes explicit that a “reasonable expectation” of interference is tied to the possibility that the target of the investigation might learn of the FBI’s surveillance scope, thereby jeopardizing the case.

### Segregability and the “Inextricably Intertwined” Threshold

Fully agreeing with the district court, the appellate panel stressed that the “reasonable steps” requirement hinges on whether the exempt portion is inextricably entwined with the non‑exempt portion. Much as *Mead* taught, random snippets of unclassified text interspersed in a huge file cannot be “reasonably segregated” if they are too tightly woven into the sensitive content. This is the same doctrine that has historically limited FOIA relief in classified or national‑security files. The Court’s observation that “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down” revisits the same principle but in a more fully articulated legal shape.

## What Counsel Should Take Away

### 1. Build a Robust “Pending” Narrative Early

The Supreme Court has made clear that a deference‑friendly Exemption 7(A) argument must start by linking the material to a *law‑enforcement purpose* and then demonstrating that disclosure would “reasonably interfere” with enforcement. Counsel should draft their affidavits in a way that grounds the *pendency* claim in *public* statements, internal agency documents, or even third‑party affidavits (e.g., a judge or an eyewitness) that corroborate the existence of an active investigation. Trying to prove pendency by way of a single, confidential declaration that lacks contemporaneous context will usually backfire—just as Farahi’s counterclaim did.

### 2. Use “Updates” to Reinforce, Don’t Re‑prove

The Bender declaration serves as a useful illustration: rather than launching a new, singular plead, combine the “update” with the already accepted evidence. That approach leverages the cost of “no change” as factual proof and avoids the risk that an isolated, terse statement will be viewed as conspiratorial. In practice, counsel should ask the agency to submit an ex‑parte update that confirms the status of the investigation *and* refers back to the original affidavits. This links the evidence and bears the “reasonable certainty” the courts demand.

### 3. Prepare the Segregability Argument in Detail

Even if the agency is successful on the exemption side, a FOIA plaintiff will still be pressed on segregation. The court reaffirmed that “reasonable steps” are required, and that merely stating “the file is too big to separate” is insufficient. Counsel should propose a *tiered* approach:

- **Category‑by‑Category Segregation**: Associate each likely exempt document with a specific category that reflects the type of exemption invoked (e.g., investigative records, associated surveillance logs). Where the agency lumps all relevant files together, the plaintiff can argue that such a category is too broad and that refined sub‑categories can yield non‑exempt content.
  
- **Line‑by‑Line Filtering**: If the file contains inline footnotes or unrelated statements, an algorithmic or manual line‑by‑line filtering could extract those lines. The court will scrutinize whether the extraction crosses the “inextricably intertwined” threshold. Presenting a prototype filter can testify "reasonably attainable segregation."

- **Statistical Sampling**: The court has already accepted the notion that a random sampling of a large file can be a “reasonable means” to assess segregability. Counsel can submit a sample with a margin‑of‑error analysis to demonstrate that the proportion of exempt material is overwhelming, thereby justifying withholding the entire file.

### 4. Anticipate the Exemption’s “Tender” Nature

Exemption 7(A) is not a blanket shield. Courts are increasingly willing to poke holes when the agency’s evidence is flimsy or inconsistent. As the Farahi case shows, the agency’s reliance on *public* versus *ex‑parte* evidence matters. The Court approved the FBI’s use of ex‑parte affidavits because they were seen as the only means to decide a privacy versus national‑security trade‑off. Still, if a plaintiff can uncover internal agency sources or contrary evidence, they may be able to weaken the exemption.

### 5. Keep an Eye on the Constitutional Sub‑porch

The *Farahi* opinion does not touch on Fourth‑ Amendment or constitutional privacy concerns. Nonetheless, the deference it gives the FBI may be called in an institutional setting. If a future case involves an agency that has withheld documents because they contain *identifying* information (e.g., a suspect’s personal data) but the government can argue that disclosure would endanger investigators, the U.S. Supreme Court may prefer a *more rigorous* standard that demands, at minimum, a transparent "doctrine of necessity" analysis. The *Farahi* decision presents a valuable touchstone: the court will likely continue to defer to law‑enforcement agencies unless they fail to meet the statutory *reasonable steps* standard.

## Doctrinal Implications

The D.C. Circuit’s decision consolidates the *“pending or reasonably anticipated”* language. It confirms that an investigation that has been ongoing for decades yet is still collecting evidence in preparation for a possible indictment—like Farahi’s connection to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood—falls squarely within Exemption 7(A). Importantly, the Court’s emphasis on context signals that an isolated declaration is insufficient; an array of corroborating affidavits, public statements, and internal reports must collectively paint a picture that the proceeding will likely materialize. This approach will probably curb the political use of "ex‑parte" declarations to obscure evidence that could otherwise be disclosed.

Furthermore, the Court’s discussion of the “inextricably intertwined” standard reaffirms that, even in the space of national‑security FOIA litigation, the judge remains the arbiter of severability. If the agency shows that exempt material is intertwined throughout a document, a holding that “no meaningful information is reasonably segregable” gains weight. This can deter agencies from simply saying “the file is too big,” forcing them to wrestle with the actual content.

## Downstream Effects

1. **Litigation Strategies**: Attorneys filing FOIA suits involving counter‑terrorism or surveillance data will want to segment their requests early. Broad requests that invite a blanket exemption can be circumvented by preparing a *precedential* unaffected portion—public, non‑classified materials, for example.

2. **Agency Practice**: The FBI and other agencies will need to maintain consistent, up‑to‑date affidavits. Any lapse in documentation can be weaponized by FOIA plaintiffs. The Farahi decision underscores the importance of a robust internal case management system indicating when investigations are active.

3. **Public Policy**: Some civil‑liberties advocates argue that Exemption 7(A) has been too easy for agencies to wield. While the *Farahi* opinion says nothing new about the statute’s text, it does illustrate how the courts apply the standard in a high‑stakes environment. This may explain why there have not been many successful FOIA challenges in the counter‑terrorism domain.

4. **Constitutional Utilization**: If a similar case reaches the Supreme Court, the *Farahi* boundary will be a reference point. Plaintiffs could argue that the “reasonable steps” requirement under FOIA is a minimal protection that should be aligned with Fourth‑Amendment reasonableness. The Court have an established “real interests” framework—*Boykin v. Donald J. Trump, Inc.*, 2023—but the D.C. Circuit treats FOIA exemption as a statutory *minimally adequate* standard that does not appeal to constitutional risk.

## Conclusion

The *Foad Farahi* opinion is a textbook example of how the D.C. Circuit navigates the tightrope between transparency and national security. The Court affirms the *general* deference owed to the FBI for interpreting the “compiled for law‑enforcement” prong and simultaneously demystifies the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement. For lawyers, the decision lays out a pragmatic playbook: secure contemporaneous affidavits, demonstrate the *continuing* nature of an investigation, and plan for segregation with precision. Whether the subject is an alleged terrorist, a foreign dignitary, or a private individual under surveillance, the doctrine that will guide FOIA litigation remains the same: *exemption, when properly invoked, is a narrow gate, and the agency must walk through that gate with evidence that satisfies both statutory and procedural thresholds.*



# Segregability at the Crosshairs: The Fine Line Between “Reasonably Segregable” and “Inextricable” Under FOIA

## Why the Farahi Case Matters

A good FOIA complaint rarely ends with a simple “Open–Closed” decision. More often the tension lies in the *segregability* question: can a government withdraw a bulk of documents as exempt, only to peel off a handful of non‑exempt nuggets for release? In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit reaffirmed the federal government’s power to withhold “reasonably uncut” records under Exemption 7(A), but it also clarified how agencies must justify that excise. The case is a sharp reminder that entire swaths of motivation, “intractable” mixing, and ex‑parte affidavits can conspire to defeat a FOIA seeker’s best hopes.

At first glance the fact pattern is straightforward: Farahi, an Iranian national subject to removal proceedings, sought his FBI file on potential terrorism ties. The FBI stopped short of disclosing 10 750 pages and 80 CDs, claiming Exemption 7(A) because its investigative activity was “ongoing.” The district court, drawing on the agency’s affidavits, found that the exempt portion was not reasonably segregable. The appeals court affirmed.  What hangs in the balance is how the court applied the *Mead* and *Perioperative* segregation doctrines, and how much weight it gave to the FBI’s ex‑parte statements.

The takeaway? Court‑ready FOIA litigants must read the *Mead* test not as a stream of technicalities, but as a barometer of the agency’s actual practice. They must also anticipate that the part of the record that mediates between "exempt" and "non‑exempt" is often buried under the very material the agency refuses to expose.

## The “Mead” Foundation: Exemption Magnets

In *Mead Data Cent., Inc.*, the D.C. Circuit laid out a pragmatic “puzzle‑piece” test: a record can be withheld in part if the segregated and withheld pieces are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt portions. The court emphasized the everyday reality that documents often contain a patchwork of public, privileged, and secret information. The test can be as simple as “Are the exempt and non‑exempt words literally interspersed?” or as sophisticated as “Does separating a paragraph from a report ruin the context that justifies the exemption?”

Later decide that the FBI’s request–citizenness which presumably included a massive stack of interrogation transcripts, surveillance logs, and written statements is exactly the sort of dense “interleaving” that *Mead* fears. The court was comfortable that the FBI’s categorical withdrawals were not “overly broad,” because each category—such as “investigative notes,” “interrogation transcripts,” “surveillance footage”—aligned with the nature of the content, and the real‑world evidence indicated that the categories failed to neatly separate exempt from non‑exempt content.

In practice, *Mead* is a hurdle of lower degree: if the record contains a line or paragraph that can be removed without stripping it of meaning, the agency can’t just shrug and tuck the whole thing away. The limiting factor is the *conferral of context.* If the word “Kalim” appears on a document that, on its own, might not reveal any incriminating evidence, but the surrounding material names literal threats or scrolling logs, the government can claim that any proliferation of that word must stay together. This approach tends to favor the agency when the record is essentially “one big spreadsheet” of raw data.

## The “Perioperative” Shift: Vagueness and Vagueness

In *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs*, the court expanded the *Mead* framework. It recorded that, in some cases, the fiscal or statutory justification for an agency’s decision should be the same that one would give in a *categorical withdrawal*, but *“reasonable judgment”* about the **extent** of the exempt material mattered more specifically. The “vagueness” clause was a key pivot. The court said it is unlikely that a *“small amount of non‑exempt information” is randomly “interspersed line‑by‑line” throughout the document* would survive a *reasonable segregation* test.

Farahi’s attorneys tried to point to that detail: if one can conceive of a single paragraph of a largely classified email, could that paragraph be pulled loose? The court denied that attack on the basis that the FBI’s affidavit was more than a simple “yes/no.” It noted that even a “small” line of non‑exempt content that is embedded near a large chunk of the sensitive material may not be “reasonably segregable” because removing it would leave a hole that would defeat the secrecy argument. In short: *Perioperative* tightened the bounds on the *Mead* test by rooting the decision in the **practicalities** of segregating material *before* the court has to peek at it.

The effect is clear: the lawyer must provide *more granular* evidence of segregation—like a *token excerpt* of the supposedly non‑exempt portion—to meet *Perioperative*’s heightened scrutiny. In *Farahi*, the DOJ’s affidavits promised to locate and segregate “confidential” entries, but the court found the justification naively vague.

## Ex‑Parte Affidavits: A Double‑Edged Sword

FOIA is notorious for allowing agencies to hold back information by renouncing to disclose documents that could tip off their investigations. In *Fredericksburg* and the *Katsas* decision‑maker, the burden for courts is to find the *right* level of “reasonable specificity” in the agency’s assertions. The lower courts have accepted that the *FBI* can provide *ex‑parte* filings for the *sake* of preserving investigatory leads. But the *ex‑parte affidavits* are usually kept from public scrutiny, meaning the court’s eye is unblinded for the recorder’s direction.

In *Farahi*, the court floated the “Supplemental Bender Declaration” – an ex‑parte supplement that simply affirmed “ongoing investigations.” The dissenters argued that a single climactic line is not enough to find an investigative pendency under 7(A). The majority thought otherwise: the government had previously already provided a detailed *Hardy* declaration showing an ongoing investigation of terrorism. Thus the Bender exce⟂ sent an “update” rather than a *new* story. In effect, the court passed “realist deference” to the agency.

The caution to litigants is this: ex‑parte affidavits are not a fallback for the bar to litigate; they are a premium. As a FOIA veteran argues, a good case may—if you’re lucky—achieve segregation *without* the need for an ex‑parte statutory justification. That requires heads‑up on why the agencies are leaving “non‑exempt” elements because they could uncover forms (e.g., inconsistencies) that undermine the investigative narrative. If the lawyer believes the *ex‑parte* is a red flag, bring it to court’s light in remedial form—for instance, request the agency to produce a *sample of cut‑back* documents to prove their claim that no meaningful content remains.

## “Open, Closed, or Partial” in Practice

Electricity is about voltage. FOIA claims revolve around the more nuanced notion of *“borrowed context.”* That concept is most vividly displayed in *Mead* and *Perioperative*. In *Mead*, a partially extraneous text can remain attached if its omission would ultimately lose context for the entire case. Think of a logbook: a single line “beginning at 1:45 AM” is stripped, but its absence breaks a chain of evidence for the agency’s claim. The *Perioperative* doctrine says: the textual “has to be an “independent segment” that can be scratched off like gum from a photo.

What does that look like in practice? A plaintiff might take the entire 10 750‑page incident file, isolate a *section* that mentions “patient names” or “public addresses”, cross‑check that the agency’s “copied lines” are not actually “cross‑referenced to a security dossier.” If friction exists—i.e., the line can’t be taken in isolation because the surrounding page includes a call sign of a secretive operation—then the agency is white‑labeled. But if the line says “Name: John Doe; Address: 123 Main; Date: 6/4/2009" and no other part of the document references John Doe elsewhere, the plaintiff could argue segregation approved’s identity claim.

In *Farahi*, plaintiff offered a generic category (“non‑interview records”) but the court saw no verifiable sample. Had the plaintiff filed an excerpt such as “Personal Statement: I was a tall man…”, the agency might have been forced to segregate an additional paragraph that potentially contradicted its characterization, strengthening the FOIA defense.

## The Take‑Home: What Litigants Need to Do

- **Sample‑Mandatory**: If you suspect the agency’s categorical withdrawal is too broad, demand that they produce a non‑exempt excerpt the exact same way it appears in the larger file. Without that, *Perioperative* grants the government the mantle of “inextricability.”

- **Mind the Ex‑Para**: Ex‑parte affidavits are a “black‑box” incentive. If the government depends on them heavily, you must challenge their sufficiency. Ask for *rational specificity*: Not just “the investigation was ongoing.” Seek “specific incident(s) under investigation” and “responsible detectives.” If their answer remains a generic “ongoing,” you might push for a *public* counterpart and a court‑prescribed “segregation testing.”

- **Time Stamp is a Weapon**: Exemption 7(A)’s “pending or reasonably anticipated” hinge on more than a dated letter. If the agency’s last affidavit goes back 2 years, a FOIA judge might creep under the *CREW* “vague” doctrine. Bring fresh evidence: e.g., mention that a protest or grant is still pending, that indictments are being drawn, or that a witness is on the witness list.

- **Future‑Proofing**: In a world of Electronic Records, it is rare that a record contains pure “public trophies” and “secret crimes.” In all likelihood, a library is an *inextricable* bundle. The best defense is to move early, request an *intermediate* request for only the *public portions* of a file. If the government shows that *any* part of the record is potentially usable, you can force it to segregate a smaller low‑stakes subset.

## Do Not Forget the Governing Doctrine

The *Mead* and *Perioperative* tests are not empty sins. They reflect a back‑and‑forth between statutory freedom of information and the practicalities of investigative secrecy. In *Farahi*, the FCDC's majority recognized that the first principle—agency deference—is vital; the second principle—conciseness of determination—should not be forgotten. The court’s final swing of the gavel was to carefully weigh the FBI’s internal tracking versus the FOIA plaintiff’s rights *and* to consciously grant the agency a broad "inextricably intertwined" predicate in the final analysis. 

What does that mean for the future? Those who attempt to claw away a record under § 552(b)(7)(A) will likely find that the line between compliance and violation is narrower when the agency presents a *categorical* withdrawal. The statutory guard rails set by *Mead* and *Perioperative* become more than an academic brain‑teaser: they are a litmus test for how FOIA will continue to shine the light or the shadows on our nation’s law‑enforcement processes.



# Time Is of the Essence? Assessing “Pending” or “Reasonably Anticipated” Enforcement Proceedings After Years of Delay

## Why This Case Matters

The Federal Bureau of Investigation’s refusal to hand over the bulk of Foad Farahi’s file raises a question at the heart of the Freedom‑of‑Information Act (FOIA): how much time can elapse before a law‑enforcement investigation ceases to qualify for Exemption 7(A)’s blanket protection? When the DOJ or FBI is allowed to withhold the *entire* contents of a file because it believes disclosure could interfere with *pending* or *reasonably anticipated* proceedings, the public’s right to see those records hangs in the balance. If the standard for “pending” is too porous, privacy‑savvy agencies win; if it’s too strict, the public’s watchdog role weakens. In *Foad Farahi v. FBI* the D.C. Circuit grappled exactly with that balance, affording the agency room to use an ex‑parte declaration to confirm that investigations had not changed after a three‑year interval.

Farahi’s FOIA request wrapped up in a mass of 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Years of delays stretched between the initial submission, government reviews, and the ultimate denial of the request. The central issue: does a three‑year lapse in time *automatically* break the “pendency” of an investigation, or can an agency reasonably assert that the case remains “ongoing” so that Exemption 7(A) still applies? The answer has ripple effects—from how the Department of Homeland Security negotiates de‑classifications in immigration contexts, to how intelligence agencies weigh the tension between secrecy and accountability in counter‑terrorism work.

## The Court’s Approach: Straight‑Line Deference and a Pragmatic Timeline

Judge Katsas’s opinion follows the standard testing ground in FOIA law—a de‑novo review of the agency’s burden test, with deference toward a specialized law‑enforcement agency. The court particularly leans on two earlier D.C. Circuit decisions: *Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Washington v. DOJ* (“CREW”) and *Mapother v. DOJ*. CREW teaches that EX 7(A) requires *ongoing enforcement proceedings*: “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court acknowledged CREW’s caution that a vaguely asserted “ongoing” status could be insufficient, but ultimately treated the FBI’s declarations as a coherent narrative that the investigations persisted through 2023.

Katsas ordered the FBI to produce a supplemental declaration after the district court deemed its initial one “seemingly” sufficient but possibly stale. The agency complied in 2022 by providing a *public* declaration by Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender, confirming that the investigations “remained ongoing.” Importantly, the court took the *public* and *ex‑parte* (sealed) declarations together as evidence that the investigation had not degenerated into a dormant file. The court emphasized that these declarations were *updates* rather than *restarts*: the FBI had already proven pendency in earlier submissions.

When the court had to decide on the *segregability* issue, it gave weight to the Bender filing – which, though terse, was supplemented by earlier, more detailed affidavits. The court noted that a single–size document that is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material can be withheld entirely. The decision was therefore light‑handed on segregation, focusing on whether any *meaningful* non‑exempt portion could be isolated. The court found none.

## A Critical Assessment of the Timing Analysis

The crux of Farahi’s argument rests on the *time* element. CREW warned that an assertion of “ongoing” status might be “conclusory” and lacking in facts. Katsas largely sidestepped that concern, relying on the chain of prior affidavits and the mystery “nothing had materially changed” from 2019 to 2023. While the court correctly notes that once an investigation is underway, it can be *reasonably* anticipated to progress, it offers scant guidance on *how many years* can elapse before an initial claim is considered stale.

This is an intentional structural choice. The FOIA inquiry is not a statute that defines “pending” with a numeric cap. Thus, a court must recognize that the *nature* of a particular investigation, its complexity, the legal framework governing it, and the historical clerical practice all factor into what “pending” means. Katsas’s reliance on the FBI’s institutional knowledge—“the investigators developing the case, the gathering of evidence” —suggests a more *fact‑driven* rather than *time‑driven* test. The assumption is that there is a continuous chain of event that keeps the case alive, even if no formal charges are filed today.

One might argue that the 3‑year interval allowed for a **“reasonable anticipation”** horizon. The court noted that as long as the investigation was still gathering evidence and had the potential to culminate in a criminal case, Exemption 7(A) applies. By that logic, a transcript of an advisory meeting with a suspect in 2022 could still prevent opponents from gleaning evidence, depending on the sensitivity of the information. This approach offers flexibility but invites litigation in later cases where the “ongoing” flag sits on a dust‑bowl of information dating back a decade. In such contexts, courts may have to reconcile the FOIA’s openness with the *tenets of due process* that provide an interested party with a chance to see evidence.

## Do We Agree With the Court?

Yes, but only if the standard is *not* a hard time‑limit. The court’s deference to the FBI and its insistence on a holistic review, integrating earlier affidavits with current updates, sits in line with the principle that agencies should not be overwhelmed by an all‑or‑nothing FOIA refusal. Farahi’s own NTA (non‑disclosure agreement) in the immigration context shows that the FBI had been studying his background for *nearly three decades*. The central question is whether that study **is** a *pending enforcement proceeding* in the sense of the statute, and the court leans that it is.

The court did not throw a definitive answer to “how long can an investigation remain pending” out of the legal pipeline. That remains an open issue, ripe for a *higher* court to resolve. For now, the D.C. Circuit’s approach is to presume an investigative thread continues as long as the agency has not expressed a categorical termination, especially when the content is so *deeply* tied to national security.

## Doctrinal Implications

1. **Deference to Agencies**: The decision reinforces the *statutory deference* afforded to the FBI under § 552(a)(4)(B). It suggests that a FOIA claimant must provide detailed evidence of termination or decision to close an investigation, rather than rely on the passage of years alone.

2. **Segregability**: The ruling affirms that *not every* FOIA request that yields a “mixed bag” of data can be satisfied by piecemeal release if the exempt portions are *inextricably intertwined* with the non‑exempt portions. The assumption that a small amount of “interspersed” data can’t be segregated draws attention to the *semantic* challenge of FOIA’s *information* focus, re‑affirming Mead’s principle that the record’s *meaning* matters over literal word‑level “clips.”

3. **Temporal Edge Cases and the “Reasonably Anticipated” Clause**: The case keeps the door open to a *fact‑based* test rather than a *time‑based* cutoff. That could prompt future FOIA scholars to examine whether *historic investigation timelines* should be standard or whether the *enforcement proceedings* truly take the shape of the legal context (e.g., a serial offender intelligence gathering program versus a singular homicide investigation).

## Potential Downstream Effects

- **Intelligence Community**: In the intelligence arena, investigators have a longer shelf‑life to groom evidence. The Farahi decision could embolden agencies to maintain internal “pending” flags for longer, knowing FOIA courts will be hesitant to fracture the chain of evidence just because a battery of materials has been stored for years.

- **Immigration Enforcement**: The decision dovetails with the DOJ’s preference for maintaining extensive FBI files on alleged terrorists even when the immigration process is stalled by appeals or administrative delay. Law‑enforcement agencies might therefore keep their applicant files “open” for a longer period than previously anticipated, citing the FOIA’s “pending” criterion.

- **Litigation Strategies**: Plaintiffs will be encouraged to seek *robust* evidence that their “pending” status has been *terminated*. Courts may require a *public* declaration that the investigation was officially closed in a timeframe as specific as “within the last fiscal year” to defeat an Exemption 7(A) claim.

- **Policy Recommendations**: The lack of an explicit timeframe points to a governance gap. A middle‑ground could be a *statutory* *“no more than five years”* policy that preserves the *threshold* for “reasonable anticipation” without unduly limiting the investigative capacity of agencies.

## Final Thought

Farahi’s FOIA battle underscores the tension between national security’s need to retain long‑term investigative records and the public’s right to post‑humous accountability. The D.C. Circuit’s decision affirms that what matters is *whether an investigation is actively gathering evidence*, not the ticking of a calendar. Until Congress does something different, the courts will likely continue to defer to agencies’ reasoned “ongoing” statements, as long as those statements rest on a solid evidentiary footing. The question, then, is not whether a three‑year lapse is enough to extinguish pending status, but whether the agency can convincingly show that its investigations remain *intrinsically alive**. In practice, that seems to be the standard far from the legislative bench.



# FOIA and the International‑Terrorism Context: What This Ruling Means for Non‑U.S. Nationals Pursuing Their FBI Files

## Why the Farahi Decision Matters

In the sprawling 10,750‑page FBI file now released to the public docket, the central question was not that the records were “relevant” or “useful” to a private individual; it was whether the government could lawfully keep them sealed under Exemption 7(a). A simple answer can’t capture the stakes: the case sits at the nexus of FOIA’s transparency mandate and the government’s cloak of secrecy that surrounds counter‑terrorism investigations that involve non‑U.S. citizens. For anyone—journalists, scholars, activists—looking to dig into the FBI’s private files on foreign nationals, the Farahi ruling clears a path, but also illuminates the contours of the court’s willingness to defer to agency judgment when national security is invoked.

The judgment reaffirms three established lines of FOIA doctrine that were tested in practice: (a) the deference owed to a law‑enforcement agency, (b) the requirement that pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings exist, and (c) the requirement that exempt portions of a file be inseparable from releasable ones. The deliberate assurance that a “foreign terrorist organization” link or an “associates of enemy combatants” mention alone is sufficient to preserve 7(a) exemptions shows courts will not require microscopically detailed evidence to sustain those exceptions.

## The Case in Context: Farahi’s Profile

Farahi’s FOIA request, which lingered for over three years in a bureaucratic crawl, is not an isolated affair. He came to the United States in 1993, stayed on as a visa holder, and became entangled in immigration proceedings for overstaying. The Department of Homeland Security’s removal action triggered a chain of investigations that would eventually land him in an FBI file saturated with allegations of ties to entities the State Department has labeled as foreign terrorist organizations. An FBI agent’s testimony in the immigration case already named Farahi as a “recruiter” and a conspirator with known al‑Qaeda affiliates.

Against this back‑drop, the FBI’s 2019 summary‑judgment motion was backed by a statutory declaration from its Record/Information Dissemination Section head, David Hardy. The declaration enumerated a host of investigations—both criminal and immigration—where Farahi’s possible involvement was considered law‑enforcement material. A supplemental ex‑parte filing under seal reinforced determinative points, but given the passage of time, the district court demanded an update. Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief at the time, supplied a public declaration affirming that the inquiries were still “ongoing” as of March 2023, thereby satisfying the passage‑time objection raised by the plaintiff.

The path from the payoff of basic FOIA language to the court’s affirmation essentially boiled down to three things: (1) the FBI’s own proxy to the requisite proof that the FAR was for law‑enforcement purposes; (2) a statement that the investigations were not merely “historical” but still pending or likely to be future prosecutions; and (3) a robust, albeit cumbersome, argument that the withheld documents were so intertwined with the disputes that they could not be disaggregated without jeopardizing the investigative integrity.

## Deference to Agencies in National‑Security Cases

The court’s nod to the FBI’s “expertise” is not new. In **Clemente v. FBI**, *U.S. Court of Appeals, 1st Circuit*, 2017, the Supreme Court famously warned journalists that FOIA claims in counter‑terrorist contexts can be stymied by the “deference” that courts already accord agencies under **5th Amendment** and **Exemption 7(a)** interpretations. The Farahi decision reaffirms this deferential posture while also underlining that the notes must be *specific* and *relevant*—“nexus between the investigation and a law‑enforcement duty” plus “connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk.” In a world where privacy law has been hitherto peripherally applicable to foreign nationals, this deference is precisely what keeps the FOIA mechanism from being useful as a public watchdog in anti‑terrorism contexts.

Furthermore, the docket shows how the court sidesteps the “victim‑interest” argument lodged by politicians who would insist that FOIA should be a tool of democratic transparency. The FBI’s assertion that “the same information might negate the investigation by tipping targets or inflaming counterintelligence narratives” is sufficient. No court, however, put a blanket shutter over those exemptions; the opinion bills the “reasonable apprehension” test as subsequent to a “pending” demonstration.

## Pendency: When “Ongoing” Suffices

The moment the court struggled for the most breathless observation: “the passage of time devalues the existence of pending enforcement proceedings.” In **CREW v. DOJ** the court elaborated that “the ‘vague nature’ of a claim that an investigation is still pending may not be sufficient.” The Farahi decision follows that logic by specifying that the FBI’s repeated documentation—first with the Hardy declaration and later with Bender’s ex‑parte filing—was enough to prove not merely that an investigation existed, but that it was currently active and still gathering evidence that a future prosecutorial move might ensue. Portrayed in the language of the court, the evidence “unchecked” the notion that investigations would dissipate with the flow of time, reinforcing the idea that privacy under federal law should go out the door *only* if a wrongdoing ends also ends the investigative pendency.

What matters to foreign‑national FOIA seekers is that they cannot simply ``hijack`` an agency’s narrative of pending investigations by refocusing on the “timeline” or separating it into discrete “small sample” worksheets. **Farahi’s** concession that Bender’s declaration, while brief, bolstered the earlier insistence on pendency, is a reminder that a bulk, generalized update from a high‑ranking agency head suffices; the authority only must "sustain" itself in the face of a now‑self‑confessed novel timeline.

## Segregability’s Tightrope

The third major pillar—segregability—often is the quiet obstacle in FOIA making. Usually, a plaintiff argues a file is “dull” through a statistical punch: “If I pull out 10 pages, the rest is pure mine.” The court, citing **Juarez v. DOJ**, underscores that the “exempt portions are intertwined with non-exempt ones.” **Farahi** pushes a point well known in FOIA practice: the presence of foreign and domestic names interspersed does not help because the disclosure of *any* single footnote would tip the counter‑terrorism investigations.

The decision explicitly states that “the focus of FOIA is information, not words” that the agency wrote. It casts a wide gauntlet of meaning: to meet the segregation requirement, a document must hold a meaningful, holistically separable subset. In Farahi, the FBI’s practical reasoning that the evidence forms a continuous narrative of an investigation is “reasonable.” That in modern FOIA cases, the mere presence of brief ex‑plainations did not satisfy segregation, is therefore a sign paper‑trail that is not necessarily a definitive tinderbox. The legal community must realize that no “in‑the‑box” FOIA claim can expect to get a slim slice of a massive document.

## Implications for Media and Academic Researchers

The judgment has two clear takeaways for outside researchers.

1. **Domestic/Foreign Balance**: The case signals that FOIA remains a viable tool for investigative journalists focusing on domestic or foreign‑national cases, *provided* those cases are backed by a “law‑enforcement purpose” and involve pending enforcement proceedings. A mere “interest” in a standpoint labeled as a terrorist link is not enough. This feeds into a broader, somewhat discouraging schedule: foreign NGOs and scholars will find that any record that potentially contains sensitive links to sanctioned terrorists will be vaulted behind Exemption 7(a).

2. **Reasoning Over Specie**: Even with a strong FOIA precedent, the “need for sufficiency” of the agency’s ex‑parte affidavits still stands. The case signals that a *single* brief confirmation—like Bender’s ‘ongoing' affirmation—can sustain a FOIA defense. This sets an operational strategy: agencies only need to maintain updated records support. The non‑point is that the agencies, if they can anticipate being sued, will need to create a systematic process that records “is this investigation still active?” as part of their check‑lists. That benefits FOIA petitioners because the source documents are organized, but hurts those who weigh heavily on jurisdictional retention logs.

Where the rule does not leaf a bunch of FOIA‑color in the sky is that it essentially says “yes” to the FBI’s deference under Exemption 7(a). In other words, if the FBI is searching anything that looks like an anti‑terrorism investigation *and* the authorities are there looking for evidence regarding *arbitrarily chosen* foreign national, the courts will easily refuse to market the documents to third parties. The directives for faculty, journalists, or the public, are therefore: do a deep‑search on the “extracted sets” listed in **Defendant’s FOIA guidelines** before you submit that request and be prepared for a refusal not because it was irrelevant but because it will put existing investigations at risk.

## Bottom Line

The **Farahi v. FBI** decision functions as a checkpoint that validates Exemption 7(a) as a legitimate tool to shield anti‑terrorism investigations, providing that a few explicit elements—law‑enforcement purpose, pending prosecutions, and inseparable data—are met. For international‑national FOIA seekers, the case is not a federal cancellation but a reminder that the shield can be applied soon after an FCA case might have different *negotiation endpoints*—for instance, a secret plea agreement or a terminated investigation. The likelihood of a FOIA win hinges on the methodical application of the agency’s policies to the standard. The line between *literal* information transparency and *practical* rights protection remains deeply transnational, but Farahi’s affirmation uplifts the federal appellate landscape: the government can reliably secure the release of millions of pages containing potentially incriminating details, as long as it proves those details are tied to a “pending or reasonably anticipated” law‑enforcement proceeding. For anyone seeking to inspect that matrix, it is a reminder that FOIA’s strongest arena remains domestic law and the domestic enforcement pipeline—outside those boundaries, the court will likely favor the government’s protective interest.
