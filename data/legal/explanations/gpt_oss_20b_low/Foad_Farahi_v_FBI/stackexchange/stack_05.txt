Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What are the requirements for category‑by‑category withholding under FOIA, and why did the court find Farahi’s challenge to the categories ‘unavailing’?
Question:
Farahi objected that the FBI used overbroad categories without explanation.  The court said he forfeited that argument because he offered no specific reasoning for overbreadth.  What standard does a plaintiff need to meet to succeed in a category‑by‑category withholding challenge?  Must the agency justify each category?  How does the court determine whether a category is wide enough to conceal exempt material?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under FOIA, a plaintiff must show that the agency’s category‑by‑category withholding is *not* warranted.  The plaintiff must (1) identify the specific categories the agency claims are exempt and (2) explain why each category is over‑broad, i.e., why the material covered by the category is not actually subject to the exemption that the agency cites.  If the plaintiff fails to *provide a reasoned, category‑specific argument*, the court will deem the challenge “unavailing” and dismiss it (see *Khine v. DHS*, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019); *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, 2025 U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit).

**How the court applies the standard**

1. **Deference to the agency’s determination of law‑enforcement purpose.**  
   Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court gives deference to its assertion that the file was compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” (cf. *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119; *Cambridge v. USA*, 164 F.3d 20, 32).  The agency need not explain why it appears that way; the deference pure‑law requirement is satisfied.

2. **Proving “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings.**  
   *CREW* (746 F.3d 1082, 1096) and *Juarez* (518 F.3d 54, 61) hold that the agency only needs to attest to the *existence* of a pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding.  The arguments that the declaration was *conclusory* are outweighed by the richer context supplied in earlier Hardy affidavits and by the FBI’s public statements about a continuing counterterrorism investigation.

3. **Category‐by‑category withholding** – *CREW* establishes the procedural bar: the agency must use categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category.  The plaintiff must point to those categories and explain *exactly* why they are over‑broad.  Farahi failed to do that.  He simply alleged that “the FBI used overbroad categories” without telling the court what the categories were or why the exemption did not apply to the material included in them.  The court therefore concluded that his fight over category breadth was “unavailing” and dismissed it (*Khine*, 987‑995).

4. **Segregability** – even if the exemption applies, the agency must show that the exempt portion is **not reasonably segregable** from the non‑exempt material.  *Juarez* says a court may rely on the agency’s declaration if it explains why exemption‑protected portions *cannot be separately extracted* without impairing the agency’s interests.  The FBI supplied a “detailed justification” in Bender’s ex‑parte declaration that the exempt material was intertwined with non‑exempt material.  Farahi’s brief objection that “some non‑exempt words might be released” was dismissed; the court held that a small amount of scattered non‑exempt data does not satisfy the “reasonable segregability” requirement.

**Bottom line**

A FOIA plaintiff who wants to defeat an agency’s category‑by‑category withholding must:

* Itemize the specific categories the agency has identified,
* Show why the exemption the agency cites does not apply to *each* category (i.e., explain the overbreadth),
* And, if the exemption is claimed, demonstrate that at least some portion of the record is *reasonably segregable*.

If the plaintiff does not identify the categories and explain their overbreadth, the court will consider the challenge “unavailing” and refuse to give it oral argument, as the court did for Farahi.
