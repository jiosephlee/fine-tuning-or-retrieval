Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How do FOIA courts distinguish between ‘law‑enforcement duties’ and ‘security‑related duties’ when applying Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The opinion references that the FBI’s determination was entitled to deference, especially for national‑security concerns.  Does a security‑related investigation automatically confer deference, or must the agency still demonstrate a clear nexus to a traditional law‑enforcement duty?  How do courts reconcile a broader privacy or national‑security interest with the statutory requirement of a ‘law‑enforcement purpose’?
Answer:
**Answer**

Exemption 7(A) protects “records … compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” that “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are *pending or reasonably anticipated* (U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A)‑(B)).  The court in *Farahi* applies the same two‑step test that courts have used for every Exemption 7(A) case:

1. **Law‑enforcement purpose.**  
2. **Interference with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings.**

The question is whether the “law‑enforcement purpose” can be satisfied by a *security‑related* investigation, even when that investigation is not part of a classic criminal prosecution.

---

### 1.  Law‑enforcement purpose: “law‑enforcement” ≠ “criminal prosecution”

The statutory language says only “law‑enforcement purposes.”  Courts have read that term broadly: it includes “the then‑ongoing or reasonably anticipated investigations of the violation of federal law” (see *Craigmiller v. U.S. Postal Service*, 839 F.3d 767, 773 (D.C. Cir. 2016)).  In *Farahi*, the FBI’s own affidavits were the key evidence.  The public Hardy declaration explained that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law‑enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations” (J.A. 42).  The ex‑parte Hardy declaration went further, detailing the nature and scope of that investigation.  The court was therefore satisfied that the FBI had met its burden of showing a “rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties” (cf. *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32; *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119).

The fact that the investigation was “security‑related” does not defeat the *law‑enforcement* requirement.  The Supreme Court’s ordinary definitions of “law‑enforcement” are broad enough to encompass counterterrorism investigations, and the court in *Farahi* specifically applies the same reasoning that has been applied in prior counterterrorism FOIA cases (e.g., *Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927).  Thus, a security‑related investigation automatically provides the statutory “law‑enforcement purpose” once the agency can tie it to an investigation of violations of federal law.

---

### 2.  Interference with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings

The second requirement is more restrictive.  The court held that ongoing investigations, even non‑criminal ones, count as “proceedings” so long as “the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case” (*Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 59).  The FBI’s affidavits showed that the investigation into Farahi was still active in March 2023 (Bender’s declaration, J.A. 131) and that the investigation had been articulated as “ongoing” in the Hardy declaration (J.A. 49).  The court therefore concluded that the required pendency was present.

Critically, the court noted that the Bender declaration was not being read *in isolation*: it was an update to the earlier Hardy affidavits.  In *Farahi*, the court emphasized that the agency had already established that “enforcement proceedings remained ongoing” when it first moved for summary judgment (J.A. 122).  The subsequent declaration was only meant to confirm that nothing had changed.  Because the agency had already satisfied the burden of pendency in the earlier affidavits, the Bender declaration—though terse—was adequate as a *supplement*.

The passage of time does not automatically defeat the pendency requirement, but it does require the agency to provide a contemporaneous update.  The court was satisfied that the FBI had done so, and there was no plausible argument that the pending proceedings had ceased to exist.

---

### 3.  Reconciliation with privacy and national‑security interests

The statutory scheme balances privacy and national‑security interests against the public’s right to know.  Exemption 7(A) is a narrow, *specific* relief that is triggered only when both of the above elements are met.  The court’s deference to the FBI (“Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference”) applies *only* after the agency has met the statutory burden.  The court did not rely on the mere fact that the investigation was security‑related to grant the exemption; it required evidence that the investigation was a *law‑enforcement purpose* (i.e., an investigation of violations of federal law) and that it was “ongoing” in the sense that a future criminal case could be built.

Hence, a security‑related investigation **does not automatically confer deference**.  The agency must first demonstrate that the investigation is a law‑enforcement investigation of a federal violation and that the investigation is pending or reasonably anticipated.  When those facts are present—as the court found in *Farahi*—the government may invoke Exemption 7(A) and receive deference from the court.

---

**Bottom line for students**

- *Law‑enforcement purpose* is satisfied by any investigation of violations of federal law, including counterterrorism or national‑security investigations.  
- *Interference* is limited to *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings; the agency must provide a contemporaneous indication that the proceedings still exist.  
- A security focus alone does **not** confer automatic deference; the statutory burden must be met first.  
- In *Farahi*, the FBI met both elements through its affidavits, so Exemption 7(A) applied and the court granted deference.  

This standard preserves the court’s role as a check on executive secrecy while recognizing that security investigations can be legitimate law‑enforcement activities.
