Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# FOIA and the International‑Terrorism Context: What This Ruling Means for Non‑U.S. Nationals Pursuing Their FBI Files

## Why the Farahi Decision Matters

In the sprawling 10,750‑page FBI file now released to the public docket, the central question was not that the records were “relevant” or “useful” to a private individual; it was whether the government could lawfully keep them sealed under Exemption 7(a). A simple answer can’t capture the stakes: the case sits at the nexus of FOIA’s transparency mandate and the government’s cloak of secrecy that surrounds counter‑terrorism investigations that involve non‑U.S. citizens. For anyone—journalists, scholars, activists—looking to dig into the FBI’s private files on foreign nationals, the Farahi ruling clears a path, but also illuminates the contours of the court’s willingness to defer to agency judgment when national security is invoked.

The judgment reaffirms three established lines of FOIA doctrine that were tested in practice: (a) the deference owed to a law‑enforcement agency, (b) the requirement that pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings exist, and (c) the requirement that exempt portions of a file be inseparable from releasable ones. The deliberate assurance that a “foreign terrorist organization” link or an “associates of enemy combatants” mention alone is sufficient to preserve 7(a) exemptions shows courts will not require microscopically detailed evidence to sustain those exceptions.

## The Case in Context: Farahi’s Profile

Farahi’s FOIA request, which lingered for over three years in a bureaucratic crawl, is not an isolated affair. He came to the United States in 1993, stayed on as a visa holder, and became entangled in immigration proceedings for overstaying. The Department of Homeland Security’s removal action triggered a chain of investigations that would eventually land him in an FBI file saturated with allegations of ties to entities the State Department has labeled as foreign terrorist organizations. An FBI agent’s testimony in the immigration case already named Farahi as a “recruiter” and a conspirator with known al‑Qaeda affiliates.

Against this back‑drop, the FBI’s 2019 summary‑judgment motion was backed by a statutory declaration from its Record/Information Dissemination Section head, David Hardy. The declaration enumerated a host of investigations—both criminal and immigration—where Farahi’s possible involvement was considered law‑enforcement material. A supplemental ex‑parte filing under seal reinforced determinative points, but given the passage of time, the district court demanded an update. Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief at the time, supplied a public declaration affirming that the inquiries were still “ongoing” as of March 2023, thereby satisfying the passage‑time objection raised by the plaintiff.

The path from the payoff of basic FOIA language to the court’s affirmation essentially boiled down to three things: (1) the FBI’s own proxy to the requisite proof that the FAR was for law‑enforcement purposes; (2) a statement that the investigations were not merely “historical” but still pending or likely to be future prosecutions; and (3) a robust, albeit cumbersome, argument that the withheld documents were so intertwined with the disputes that they could not be disaggregated without jeopardizing the investigative integrity.

## Deference to Agencies in National‑Security Cases

The court’s nod to the FBI’s “expertise” is not new. In **Clemente v. FBI**, *U.S. Court of Appeals, 1st Circuit*, 2017, the Supreme Court famously warned journalists that FOIA claims in counter‑terrorist contexts can be stymied by the “deference” that courts already accord agencies under **5th Amendment** and **Exemption 7(a)** interpretations. The Farahi decision reaffirms this deferential posture while also underlining that the notes must be *specific* and *relevant*—“nexus between the investigation and a law‑enforcement duty” plus “connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk.” In a world where privacy law has been hitherto peripherally applicable to foreign nationals, this deference is precisely what keeps the FOIA mechanism from being useful as a public watchdog in anti‑terrorism contexts.

Furthermore, the docket shows how the court sidesteps the “victim‑interest” argument lodged by politicians who would insist that FOIA should be a tool of democratic transparency. The FBI’s assertion that “the same information might negate the investigation by tipping targets or inflaming counterintelligence narratives” is sufficient. No court, however, put a blanket shutter over those exemptions; the opinion bills the “reasonable apprehension” test as subsequent to a “pending” demonstration.

## Pendency: When “Ongoing” Suffices

The moment the court struggled for the most breathless observation: “the passage of time devalues the existence of pending enforcement proceedings.” In **CREW v. DOJ** the court elaborated that “the ‘vague nature’ of a claim that an investigation is still pending may not be sufficient.” The Farahi decision follows that logic by specifying that the FBI’s repeated documentation—first with the Hardy declaration and later with Bender’s ex‑parte filing—was enough to prove not merely that an investigation existed, but that it was currently active and still gathering evidence that a future prosecutorial move might ensue. Portrayed in the language of the court, the evidence “unchecked” the notion that investigations would dissipate with the flow of time, reinforcing the idea that privacy under federal law should go out the door *only* if a wrongdoing ends also ends the investigative pendency.

What matters to foreign‑national FOIA seekers is that they cannot simply ``hijack`` an agency’s narrative of pending investigations by refocusing on the “timeline” or separating it into discrete “small sample” worksheets. **Farahi’s** concession that Bender’s declaration, while brief, bolstered the earlier insistence on pendency, is a reminder that a bulk, generalized update from a high‑ranking agency head suffices; the authority only must "sustain" itself in the face of a now‑self‑confessed novel timeline.

## Segregability’s Tightrope

The third major pillar—segregability—often is the quiet obstacle in FOIA making. Usually, a plaintiff argues a file is “dull” through a statistical punch: “If I pull out 10 pages, the rest is pure mine.” The court, citing **Juarez v. DOJ**, underscores that the “exempt portions are intertwined with non-exempt ones.” **Farahi** pushes a point well known in FOIA practice: the presence of foreign and domestic names interspersed does not help because the disclosure of *any* single footnote would tip the counter‑terrorism investigations.

The decision explicitly states that “the focus of FOIA is information, not words” that the agency wrote. It casts a wide gauntlet of meaning: to meet the segregation requirement, a document must hold a meaningful, holistically separable subset. In Farahi, the FBI’s practical reasoning that the evidence forms a continuous narrative of an investigation is “reasonable.” That in modern FOIA cases, the mere presence of brief ex‑plainations did not satisfy segregation, is therefore a sign paper‑trail that is not necessarily a definitive tinderbox. The legal community must realize that no “in‑the‑box” FOIA claim can expect to get a slim slice of a massive document.

## Implications for Media and Academic Researchers

The judgment has two clear takeaways for outside researchers.

1. **Domestic/Foreign Balance**: The case signals that FOIA remains a viable tool for investigative journalists focusing on domestic or foreign‑national cases, *provided* those cases are backed by a “law‑enforcement purpose” and involve pending enforcement proceedings. A mere “interest” in a standpoint labeled as a terrorist link is not enough. This feeds into a broader, somewhat discouraging schedule: foreign NGOs and scholars will find that any record that potentially contains sensitive links to sanctioned terrorists will be vaulted behind Exemption 7(a).

2. **Reasoning Over Specie**: Even with a strong FOIA precedent, the “need for sufficiency” of the agency’s ex‑parte affidavits still stands. The case signals that a *single* brief confirmation—like Bender’s ‘ongoing' affirmation—can sustain a FOIA defense. This sets an operational strategy: agencies only need to maintain updated records support. The non‑point is that the agencies, if they can anticipate being sued, will need to create a systematic process that records “is this investigation still active?” as part of their check‑lists. That benefits FOIA petitioners because the source documents are organized, but hurts those who weigh heavily on jurisdictional retention logs.

Where the rule does not leaf a bunch of FOIA‑color in the sky is that it essentially says “yes” to the FBI’s deference under Exemption 7(a). In other words, if the FBI is searching anything that looks like an anti‑terrorism investigation *and* the authorities are there looking for evidence regarding *arbitrarily chosen* foreign national, the courts will easily refuse to market the documents to third parties. The directives for faculty, journalists, or the public, are therefore: do a deep‑search on the “extracted sets” listed in **Defendant’s FOIA guidelines** before you submit that request and be prepared for a refusal not because it was irrelevant but because it will put existing investigations at risk.

## Bottom Line

The **Farahi v. FBI** decision functions as a checkpoint that validates Exemption 7(a) as a legitimate tool to shield anti‑terrorism investigations, providing that a few explicit elements—law‑enforcement purpose, pending prosecutions, and inseparable data—are met. For international‑national FOIA seekers, the case is not a federal cancellation but a reminder that the shield can be applied soon after an FCA case might have different *negotiation endpoints*—for instance, a secret plea agreement or a terminated investigation. The likelihood of a FOIA win hinges on the methodical application of the agency’s policies to the standard. The line between *literal* information transparency and *practical* rights protection remains deeply transnational, but Farahi’s affirmation uplifts the federal appellate landscape: the government can reliably secure the release of millions of pages containing potentially incriminating details, as long as it proves those details are tied to a “pending or reasonably anticipated” law‑enforcement proceeding. For anyone seeking to inspect that matrix, it is a reminder that FOIA’s strongest arena remains domestic law and the domestic enforcement pipeline—outside those boundaries, the court will likely favor the government’s protective interest.
