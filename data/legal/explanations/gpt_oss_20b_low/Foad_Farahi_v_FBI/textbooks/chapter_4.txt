Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 4: # IV. FBI’s Certifications, Segregability Analysis, and Court Agreement

## Content and Purpose of Hardy’s Public and Ex‑Parte Declarations

The FBI’s Section Chief of Records, Information, and Dissemination (RIDS), David Hardy, submitted two separate affidavits in support of the agency’s summary‑judgment motion. The first, a public declaration, set out the key facts the FBI posited that would satisfy FOIA Exemption 7(A): (1) that the extensive collection of materials—about ten thousand pages and eighty CDs—had been compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national‑security and international‑terrorism law”; and (2) that the material concerned “ongoing law‑enforcement investigations” involving “the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” By invoking the standard pre‑disclosure, Hardy aimed to establish the basic premises of the exemption, namely that the records were law‑enforcement files and that their release would pose a foreseeable risk to law‑enforcement proceedings.

Hardy’s accompanying ex‑parte declaration, filed under seal and accepted by the district court’s ex‑parte process, sought to provide the detail that could not be published without jeopardizing the investigation. This sealed affidavit furnished evidence of the FBI’s ongoing investigative work—such as the identification of suspects, the nature of their alleged offenses, and the strategic objectives of the investigation. While its contents cannot be reproduced in the record, the court relied on them to confirm the allegations made in the public declaration. The ex‑parte declaration also underscored that the FBI had complied with FOIA’s segregation duty to the extent that no material was “reasonably segregable” from the exempt portions, a point that would later be crucial to the court’s decision.

The juxtaposition of public and ex‑parte affidavits reflects the FOIA framework: the agency must avoid disclosing material that could tip off suspects or compromise an active investigation, yet the court must still be able to assess the agency’s claims. The public affidavit establishes the broad picture, while the sequestered affidavit supplies the data that substantiates the picture without revealing it to the requester.

## Bender’s Update and Its Role in the Record

In November 2022, when the district court denied the FBI’s initial summary‑judgment.

motion without prejudice, Judge J.A. ordered the agency to provide fresh evidence that the enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated and that the files remained non‑segregable. The FBI complied by submitting a new set of affidavits from Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender’s public declaration contained a succinct statement: “the relevant investigations remain ongoing as of March 2023.” Though brief, the statement repeated the same claim made in Hardy’s earlier affidavit and reiterated as a factual update. The ex‑parte Bender declaration elaborated on the reasons the FBI viewed the investigations as ongoing—mentioning ongoing surveillance, continuation of intelligence gathering, and the absence of a closure date for the cases at hand.

From a procedural standpoint, Bender’s affidavits did not introduce new facts but rather served as a confirmation that no substantive change had occurred in the FBI’s assessment over the intervening period. The court recognized that these documents were necessary to satisfy the FOIA “reasonable steps” requirement for segregation. Since the FBI had already demonstrated the interrupting nature of the investigations in Hardy’s affidavits, the Bender affidavits functioned as an update, not a new argument. The court therefore treated Bender’s statements as an extension of the original evidence, weighing them in light of the already‑established claim of pendency.

## Demonstration of Pending Investigations

The core of Exemption 7(A) hinges on two facts: that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated law‑enforcement proceedings. The FBI’s affidavits fulfilled both prongs. The public Hardy declaration had sufficiently described the nature of the investigations—national‑security and international‑terrorism matters linked to a foreign‑national suspect—and activated the jurisdiction of FOIA. The ex‑parte affidavits, by discussing ongoing surveillance and unfinalized criminal proceedings, gave step‑by‑step confirmation of the investigations’ continuity.

The court applied the “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard with the guidance from earlier cases such as *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. Department of Justice* and *Mapother v. Department of Justice*. The agency’s evidence that no end date had been set, that evidence was still being collected, and that investigative staff remained actively engaged, sufficed to satisfy the “reasonable anticipation” portion of the test. The court noted that ongoing investigations constitute “proceedings” for the purposes of Exemption 7(A) and that the FBI could demonstrate the existence of such proceedings by merely stating that the investigation exists.

In its analysis, the court admitted that the Bender affidavits were brief but contextualized them: the earlier affidavits had already established pendency; Bender’s statements were consistent with that established picture and confirmed that nothing had changed—thereby making their brevity understandable.

## Analysis of Non‑Segregability Under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)

Under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and the accompanying regulation, a government entity must identify the portions of a record that are exempt and the portions that are not, segregating the non‑exempt material so that it may be released. Exemptions are meaningful only if the exempt portion is “reasonably segregable” from the non‑exempt portion; if exempt parts are “intertwined” with non‑exempt parts, the entire record may be withheld.

The FBI’s policy, as described in the affidavits, adopted a category‑by‑category approach. Each category was defined broadly—such as all documentation relating to “Khaled Sheikh Mohamed,” all records concerning “international terrorist financing,” and so on—rather than by narrow, searchable taglines. The court determined that these categories were so wide that the FBI could not reliably separate exempt material from the rest. The records in question were large, heavily cross‑referenced, and bound by subject matter that could not be isolated with a simple delimiter without substantial editorial effort, well beyond what FOIA permitted.

The district court relied heavily on Bender’s ex‑parte declaration to conclude that the portions were “inextricably intertwined.” The declaration described the FBI’s experience with documents in the file: that each document was a composite of victim reports, intelligence summaries, suspect interviews, and cross‑references to other files. When these entwining elements pervade the file, the court found that the FBI could not demonstrate that any non‑exempt portion could be “separated” without compromising the privileged aspect of the investigation. The court therefore affirmed the agency’s determination that the entire collection was non‑segregable.

## Court’s Reliance on Agency Affidavits

The district court gave deference to the FBI’s assessments of both pendency and non‑segregability. Because the agency operates within the specialized domain of law enforcement—where the presumption of national‑security relevance is implicit—courts routinely grant a "fair measure of deference" to a government scientist's insider knowledge of its files. The court cited precedent that deference is especially warranted when FOIA claims involve national‑security investigative work. The government’s affidavits, by demonstrating the investigator’s knowledge, satisfied the burden that the exempt portions would "reasonably be expected to interfere."

Moreover, the court highlighted that the affidavits were the only viable means to resolve the dispute on segregability. The essence of the FOIA dispute was whether the exempt material could be separated from the non‑exempt. In the absence of the claims the FBI filed (which, if public, could undermine operational secrecy), the court had no other factual basis to confirm or refute the FBI’s statements. Consequently, it accepted the affidavits in determining that no meaningful, reasonably segregable data existed.

In sum, the court’s agreement rested on the interplay of FOIA’s pragmatic privacy safeguards, the statutory framework, and the DOJ‑controlled gambit: the agency’s affidavits contained the only information that could legally confirm whether the investigation was still pending or whether the records could be presented. The court found that the evidence at hand satisfied the statutory thresholds for Exemption 7(A), that the FBI complied with segregation obligations, and that the claims of interference were well founded. The court therefore affirmed the FOIA denial, respecting the functional balance between transparency and law‑enforcement effectiveness.
