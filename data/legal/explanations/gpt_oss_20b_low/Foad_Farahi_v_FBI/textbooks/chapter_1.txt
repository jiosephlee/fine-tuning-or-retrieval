Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # I. Background and Factual Context

In preparing to read and reason through a court opinion, it is essential first to ground oneself in the factual matrix that prompted the dispute. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the origin of the FOIA controversy lies in Farahi’s long‑standing residence in the United States and the attendant immigration proceedings that followed a visa overstay in 2002. The case will later shift to national‑security concerns, but the facts that prompted the FBI’s file creation and the subsequent FOIA request remain the backbone of the litigation.

## Foad Farahi’s Immigration Status and Removal Proceedings

Foad Farahi is an Iranian citizen who arrived in the United States in 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) placed Farahi in removal proceedings after his visa had expired. Over the years, he repeatedly sought relief from removal by filing applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. Each attempt met with a refusal, and the immigration docket evolved into a potentially messy web of petitions and denials. Importantly, the removal proceedings were the first formal context in which the FBI became involved. We are told that during this immigration litigation, DHS introduced FBI testimonies attesting that Farahi had ties to “high‑level terrorists,” a claim that would later become more tested in the appellate opinion.

## Alleged Ties to Terrorist Organizations and Individuals

The FBI’s destructive threat isn’t the immigration status itself, but the allegations that Farahi was connected to a host of designated terrorist individuals and organizations. According to the record, the FBI had collected material suggesting that Farahi was a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood and that he had contact with Khaled Sheikh Mohammed, a key Al Qaeda figure. The allegations also extended to links with other known terrorists, such as Jose Padilla, and involvement with charities that were later deemed to have financed terrorism, such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. These claims, as the opinion explains, formed the basis of the FBI’s investigative narrative and thereby the core of the records that would be withheld under FOIA Exemption 7(A).

## Previous FOIA Request and FBI’s Initial Response

Farahi submitted a FOIA request to gain access to his FBI file. The FBI identified an alleged 10,750 pages and 80 compact discs that potentially contained responsive information. In its initial response, the FBI complied partially: some portions of the file were released, but the bulk of the material remained hidden. Farahi sued after the agency missed the statutory deadline for a complete answer, and the subsequent litigation forced the FBI to review its holdings and provide statements from its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). These statements, which eventually became the central evidence for the agency’s possession of an Exemption 7(A) shield, demonstrated that the FBI had “compiled” the documents for law‑enforcement purposes and that their release could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were then pending or reasonably anticipated.” The eventual summary‑judgment motion, denied at first and then granted upon updated declarations, was the primary trigger for the appellate review. The facts, as we see them in the opinion, show why the DOJ’s and the court’s focus lies squarely on the content of those seized documents and the timing of the investigations that inspired them.
