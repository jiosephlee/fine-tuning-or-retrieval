Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # II. Procedural Posture and FOIA Regime

The court’s opinion traces a long procedural path that began with Foad Farahi’s Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request and ended with the district court’s grant of summary judgment, which was subsequently affirmed on appeal. This chapter dissects that trajectory and situates it within the statutory architecture of FOIA, emphasizing the roles of Exemption 7(A), statutory “exemptions” versus “exclusions,” and the procedural requirements for agency obligation to segregate and disclose non‑exempt portions of records.

## Statutory Structure of § 552(a) and § 552(b)

Section 552 of the United States Code codifies the FOIA. Subsection (a) establishes the **primary procedural framework** that governs how requests must be made, responded to, and contested. It requires agencies to notify a requester “in writing” of the agency’s determination (re‑opening, dismissing, or serving the record) and sets deadlines—13 days for a full, unqualified response and 30 days for a “partial” response or a request for a “public‑interest summary.” If an agency cannot comply within those time limits, it must provide a **remainder**—i.e., a list of records it intends to release after the deadline lapses—and a plausible reason for the delay.

Subsection (b) lists 18 statutory **exemptions** that can bar disclosure of otherwise responsive records. Exemptions are *fully automatic* bars once the agency meets its burden to show that the requested material meets a specific statutory criterion (e.g., the “national security” exemption or the “executive‑confidential” exemption). Exemptions differ from the 12 “exclusions” in § 552(c), which are governed by a system of “public‑interest summaries.” Exclusions are *faceted* disclosures; the agency can publish a summary of the sensitive portions while releasing the remainder, provided it meets its summary‑disclosure duty.

Both sections have operational intersections: a request must first be processed under § 552(a). If the agency finds that a request is eligible for a summary (or could compel partial compliance), it can invoke § 552(c); otherwise, it judges whether any of the 18 § 552(b) exemptions fully exclude the material. 

The section of focus in Farahi’s case is Exemption 7(A), which shields records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” *and* that “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The court repeatedly underscored that the *law‑enforcement* nexus arises automatically for any agency whose core functions involve preventing or investigating crime—such as the FBI. Consequently, the FBI’s determination that the “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” test is satisfied receives a degree of deference (see *Campbell* and *Clem*). The *interference* test, however, obliges the agency to demonstrate that the release of specific records would *interfere* with *pending or reasonably anticipated* proceedings—a standard the court scrutinized closely.

## FOIA Exemptions and the Duty to Disclose Non‑Exempt Portions

FOIA’s core principle is the balance between open government and legitimate confidentiality. Once an agency concludes that an exemption applies, the agency is required to identify which numeric sections or categories of the file are exempt and then to **segregate** and release any non‑exempt material—this is the “reasonable steps” obligation codified in § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). The statute permits the agency to issue a “public‑interest summary” (if the record is subject to § 552(c) exclusions) or to disclose the entire non‑exempt content directly. 

In practice—especially with large, hard‑copy/ digital files—the **segregability** analysis can become complex. The court noted that the FBI’s *Bender* supplement tried to persuade the court that non‑exempt information was *inextricably intertwined* with the exempt portion. Jurisprudence allows an agency to keep a file whole if “nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions” (*Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61). Crucial here is the agency’s burden to show, with reasonable specificity, *why* segregation is impossible. The district court found this burden met in the Bender declaration and in the earlier Hardy declarations; thus the FBI need not set out a fine‑grained matrix of individual pages or documents.

The court further pointed out that the “segregability” obligation is not a matter of “tipping the scales on a line‑by‑line basis.” Even if random excerpts could be redacted, that does not reduce the overall non‑exempt portion to a protected, semipublic record. The *Mead* precedent held that the focus remains on the *body of information*, not on piecemeal editing. Accordingly, the court rejected the argument that some isolated passages could be released.

## Summary Judgment in FOIA Action and De Novo Review

The plaintiff had brought a civil action under FOIA after the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding. The secretary of the agency moved for **summary judgment** in favor of the agency—a motion that is routine in FOIA litigation because the primary question often hinges on whether an exemption applies, a question that can be proven from the agency’s evidence alone. Under *Rep. Comm. for Freedom of the Press* v. FBI, *3 U. S. 4th* at 350, summary judgment in FOIA actions is reviewed **de novo** by the court. That is, the appellate panel examines the summary‑judgment motion fresh, without deference to the district court’s earlier findings.

The standard in de novo review is that the court must **confirm** that the agency satisfied its burden to establish an exemption. If the agency’s evidence is sufficient, the court gives *equal weight* to the evidence from the agency’s affidavits and the plaintiff’s counter‑evidence. The district court’s initial denial of summary judgment, after finding that only a “seemingly” met the burden, illustrates the effect of new time allowing the agency to update its basis. A **summary‑judgment motion on a revised claim** can be granted if the agency can again meet the burden—exactly what the FBI did by presenting updated declarations including Dr. Bender’s statements.

Because the appellate court de novo reviews the admission of evidence in summary‑judgment motions, it must examine whether the agency’s evidence, as recorded in the *Hardy* and *Bender* affidavits, is competent, detailed, and consistent in showing (1) the law‑enforcement nexus, and (2) that release would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. The court found that satisfactory evidence was present, even after scrutinizing the “cessation of time” issue, thus affirming the agency’s summary judgment.

## Agency Obligations Under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)

The clause § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) obliges an agency to “make reasonable efforts to segregate and release non‑exempt records.” The concept of “reasonable steps” does not mean a perfect segregation of a massive file but requires: (1) identification of the exempt portions, (2) an explanation of why those portions are exempt, and (3) a plan (or justification) for a partial release. For *serious* files, the agency may file an **ex parte affidavit** that explains why segregation is impossible. This ex parte evidence is accepted by the court so long as the plaintiff does not offer a contrary  higher‑quality, in‑person opposed testimony—an exclusion that could be remedied by an in‑camera hearing, but only in the extraordinary event the material is so restricted that disclosing it would jeopardize national security (as argued for *ex parte* declarations in *Perioperative Services*, *57 U. S. 4th* 1061).

The appellate court places emphasis on the *FBI’s* compliance with this requirement. The “Bender” declaration, comprised of a public and an ex parte subsection, was deemed sufficiently detailed. The court concluded that the FBI had *reasonably* marked out the portions of its 10,750‑page, 80‑CD file that fell under Exemption 7(A)—mostly interrogation transcripts, surveillance logs, and interagency memos—while arguing that the remaining pages were “inextricably intertwined” with exempt information. The appellate court also noted that the *FBI* had followed the procedural steps for responding to a FOIA request: it supplied remainders, extended the deadline, and eventually filed the required declarations. No procedural steps were omitted that would render the agency ineligible for § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) relief.

In sum, the procedural analysis establishes that the **district court’s denial of summary judgment was based on an insufficient evidentiary standard**, whereas the **subsequent renewal of the FBI’s motion, fortified by the *Bender* declaration, satisfied the depository’s burden** to show that the higher‑level Exemption 7(A) applies and that the agency was unable to segregate the exempt portion. The appellate court, reviewing the motion **de novo**, confirmed that the agency’s representations were convincing and bound by statutory precedent. Consequently, the Court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment and upheld the FBI’s withholding of the requested records.
