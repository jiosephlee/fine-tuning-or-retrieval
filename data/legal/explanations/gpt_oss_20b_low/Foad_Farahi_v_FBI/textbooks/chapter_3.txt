Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 3: # III. Exemption 7(A): Legal Standard and Deference

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) balances the public’s right to know against the government’s legitimate need to protect certain information. Exemption 7(A) embodies that balance for records that originate within a law‑enforcement context. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* the court clarified both the doctrinal requirements of Exemption 7(A) and the policy rationale for a preferred level of deference to law‑enforcement agencies. The following analysis unpacks these elements and situates them in the D.C. Circuit’s jurisprudence.

## Definition of “Compiled for Law‑Enforcement Purposes”

Section 552(b)(7)(A) protects records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” from FOIA disclosure when they “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” A record is *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* when it is created, gathered, or maintained to fulfill a law‑enforcement activity. The onus for establishing this element lies on the government. Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its determination that a file was “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” carries *fair measure of deference* under the *deference* doctrine (see *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017)).  

In practice, the government must show a *rational nexus* between the records and its law‑enforcement duty. The record must also *concern an individual or incident* that potentially represents a security risk or federal law violation. The *per‑agency* standard is satisfied when the documents are produced as part of an ongoing investigation. In *Farahi*, the FBI’s declarations—both public and ex‑parte—attested that the held files were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” thereby establishing the required nexus and satisfying the burden. The court emphasized that the FBI is “specialized in law enforcement”; therefore, its judgment that the files were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes is entitled to a “fair measure of deference.” (This deference mirrors the *strict scrutiny of reasonableness* applied in *Mapother* and *Crews*, but is narrower because the agency’s expertise is central to the record’s creation.)

## Requirement that Disclosure “Could Reasonably Be Expected to Interfere”

Once the records are shown to be law‑enforcement compiling, the government must still prove that the disclosure could *reasonably be expected* to interfere with a proceeding. The *“could reasonably be expected”* standard requires the government to articulate how release would harm the investigation. Typical harms include:

1. **Self‑incrimination for the target** (the investigator anticipates that disclosure could reveal investigative techniques).
2. **Obstruction of investigations** (targets might warn associates or destroy evidence).
3. **Exposure of confidential relationships** or sources.

In *Farahi*, the FBI argued that releasing the information could “tip off the targets about the scope of the investigation.” The court accepted that this is an established, *widely accepted* justification for Exemption 7(A) disclosure (e.g., in *CNC, v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 929 (D.C. Cir. 2003)). The *ex‑parte* declaration from Bender supplied the court with confirmation that the ongoing investigation would be jeopardized by premature release.

The *“could reasonably be expected”* threshold is satisfied on a *balance‑of‑probabilities* basis: the government need only present *reasonable* evidence that disclosure would interfere; it does not have to prove the interference would occur inevitably.

## Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings

A further textual requirement is that the anticipated interference arise **from proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated**. The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly stressed the “pending” component. *Mapother* defined a pending proceeding as “an existing enforcement proceeding that is live, i.e., the federal government is actively seeking a judgment.’’ *Crews* extended the definition to include proceedings that are “reasonable” to anticipate based on available evidence—e.g., “an ongoing investigation that may lead to a future criminal case.”

In *Farahi*, the FBI identified a nexus to “pending law‑enforcement proceedings” in its Hardy declarations and later confirmed continuity of those proceedings through the Bender declaration. The district court’s “seemingly” met the burden note underscores that the evidence was persuasive, though not ironclad. The court’s refusal to deny summary judgment without prejudice was premised on an acknowledgment that the *chronology* required updating; a standard rule that the *law* references *time* when evaluating “pending” versus “reasonable anticipation.”

The key takeaway is that *pending* and *reasonably anticipated* are distinct but both require evidence. The proceeding need not be completed; it merely must be *looming* with a realistic chance of materialization.

## Deference to Agencies in the FOIA Context

Deference is a pillar of FOIA adjudication. The relevant standards differ from *deference* in other contexts:

1. **Statutory Deference**: For FOIA, the highest level is *deference in the factual determination* that a record is exempt (the *“practical”* standard). The court presumes the agency’s words are truthful unless the plaintiff shows *contradictory* evidence. (cf. *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32; *Clemente*, 867 F.3d 111, 119.)

2. **Policy Deference**: When the agency asserts an institutional policy (e.g., that certain documents are gathered for national‑security investigations), the court examines whether the policy *includes an exception* for FOIA disclosure. If the policy is *unanimous and appears to be a law‑enforcement policy*, *Farahi* capitalized on that deference (“such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security”).

3. **Expertise Deference**: Exemption 7(A) is steeped in *law‑enforcement expertise*. The court therefore places a higher weight on the agency’s *professional judgement* (e.g., that an investigative file is intertwined with “national security” concerns). This tenet is precisely what the court exercised when “the FBI specialized in law enforcement,” whereby its decision on applicability “is entitled to a fair measure of deference.”

In sum, the policy deference rule, coupled with the *ex‑parte* feeding of the court on sensitive details, results in a solid shield for law‑enforcement records subject to Exemption 7(A).

## Prior Cases Shaping the Standard

1. **CREW (Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014))** – *CREW* laid out the fundamental test for Exclusion 7(A): Records must be compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, and the disclosure must reasonably interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The court distinguished between *“pending”* and *“reasonably anticipated”*, emphasizing that actual knowledge of ongoing enforcement proceedings is a critical element.

2. **Juarez (Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008))** – *Juarez* refined the *“reasonable anticipation”* doctrine, noting that a government can show a pending or *reasonably anticipated* proceeding merely by attesting to its existence (not requiring a formal indictment). *Juarez* also underlined the *inextricable intertwining* principle, permitting agencies to withhold entire records when the exempt part cannot be segregated from the non‑exempt part.

3. **CNSS (Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003))** – *CNSS* expanded the scope for national‑security cases, affirming that investigation of a national‑security threat can qualify it for Exemption 7(A) even if the investigation is not linked to a criminal case. The court made a *policy‑deferential* approach explicit, saying that when national‑security concerns are implicated “the executive’s position should be respected.”

4. **Mapother (Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993))** – *Mapother* established the earlier standard for “pending” proceedings, contributing to the baseline that *Crews* later refined.

5. **Clemente (Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017))** – *Clemente* reaffirmed that the FBI’s discretion to issue the “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” determination is deserving of *fair measure of deference*.

These cases collectively set a procedural and substantive framework that *Farahi* respects: proof of law‑enforcement compilation, proof of probable interference, evidence of pending or anticipated proceedings, and a heightened deference to agencies’ expertise and discrete policy.

---

**Take‑away for students:** Exemption 7(A) is a layered shield. When tackling an FOIA claim that involves potential law‑enforcement records, first confirm the agency’s *law‑enforcement nexus*. Then ask whether the request could *reasonably* interfere with *ongoing or but‑to‑occur* proceedings. Finally, expect the court to defer to the agency’s professional judgment—a deference that is far more pronounced in the national‑security context than in other FOIA controversies. Knowing how the D.C. Circuit’s jurisprudence—especially *CREW*, *Juarez*, and *CNSS*—has interpreted each element will allow you to scaffold the government’s burden and anticipate the court’s likely conclusions.
