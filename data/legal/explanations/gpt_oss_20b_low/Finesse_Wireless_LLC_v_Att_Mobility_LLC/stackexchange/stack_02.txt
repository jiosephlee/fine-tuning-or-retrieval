Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does the court distinguish between an interference generating signal that is 'generated' versus 'sampled', and why does that matter for claim limitation 1[a] of the ’134 patent?
Question:
The decision focuses on whether the RF ADC samples the modeled PIM path. I’m confused about the technical distinction between a signal that is generated by the hardware after sampling and one that is present in the passband being sampled. Why does the court say the modeled PIM path cannot be the 'interference generating signals' if it’s produced after sampling?
Answer:
**Answer**

The crux of the infringement issue is whether the RF ADC in the accused Nokia radios “samples a passband of received signals that includes both the signals of interest *and* the interference‑generating signals,” as required by claim‑limitation 1[a] of the ’134 patent.  The district court found that the RF ADC did indeed sample that passband, but the appellate court disapproved of the evidence on a *substantial‑evidence* basis.  The key technical point is the distinction between a signal that exists in the received RF spectrum *before* sampling and a signal that is **generated** only after sampling.

---

### 1.  What the court says about “generated” vs “sampled”

* The RF ADC is defined as a receiver that “samples the claimed ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference‑generating signals’ (both depicted in dashed red path) to create a bit stream” (J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10).  
* Dr. Wells identified the “interference‑generating signals” with a *modeled PIM path* that is *generated in the GROOT FPGA* (J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8).  
* The court notes that “the modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference‑generating signals’” and “the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA” (J.A. 21–22).  
* Because that modeled path is **generated after** the RF ADC samples the incoming RF field, the court concludes that the sampled passband can contain no “interference‑generating signals.”  Hence, if the modeled path is indeed the claimed interference‑generating signal, limitation 1[a] is not satisfied (see J.A. 28303).

The court essentially applies the rule that a sampling device can only “sample” a signal that is present in the input waveform at the instant of sampling.  A signal that is synthesized *after* the sampling operation cannot be considered one of the “signals being sampled.”  This is the same principle that underlies claim construction: a detector cannot claim that “signal X is sampled” if X is a post‑processing artifact.

---

### 2.  Why that matters for claim‑limitation 1[a]

Limitation 1[a] imposes a *physical* requirement on the accused system: the RF ADC must ingest a composite analog waveform that contains both the signal‑of‑interest and the source signal that will later generate the unwanted IMPs.  In the ’134 patent:

> “over‑sampling… to create a bit stream, *wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference‑generating signals*….”

If the interference‑generating signal is not present in the analog signal at sampling time, the claimed method’s first step (over‑sampling the *passband of received signals* that contains the source signals) is not met.  The rest of the claim (isolation, estimation, cancellation) would then be non‑functional in the context of the claim, because its input has not been provided in the manner required.

The appellate court treated the evidence supporting sampling of a modeled path as *inconclusive* because the expert testimony never clarified whether the modeled path appeared physically at the ADC input or was synthesized afterward.  Since the expert was unable to confirm that the sampled waveform actually contained the interference‑generating signal, the evidence did not satisfy the *substantial‑evidence* standard for infringement.  Consequently, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement (see the Summary, “We reverse the denial of JMOL … because the jury’s infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence”).

---

### 3.  Bottom line for a law student

* **Generated vs sampled**: “Generated” means the signal is created by a digital or analog processing stage *after* the ADC has already digitized the input. “Sampled” means a signal that physically exists in the analog RF band and is captured by the ADC.  
* **Why it matters**: Claim 1[a] explicitly requires the ADC to sample a passband that contains both signal types. If the interference‑generating signal is only produced post‑sampling, the first step of the claim fails, and the method claim cannot be infringed.  
* **Court’s conclusion**: Because the expert failed to prove that the modeled PIM path existed *before* sampling, the court found the evidence insufficient and granted JMOL of noninfringement. The distinction between generated and sampled signals is therefore critical to interpreting and enforcing the claim limitation.

This reasoning illustrates how a strictly literal reading of a claim’s first step can hinge on whether a signal is physically present at the ADC input—or merely synthesized afterwards.  As the court put it, if the modeled PIM path is not the “interference‑generating signal” *at the time of sampling*, limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied.
