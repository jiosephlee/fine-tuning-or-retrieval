Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What standard does the Fifth Circuit use when reviewing a district court’s denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) on infringement?
Question:
The opinion cites Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc. for de novo review. I’m trying to understand whether the court still checks whether the evidence is substantial, or if the de novo review replaces that requirement. How exactly does the court evaluate ‘substantial evidence’ in the context of JMOL versus a factual finding?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit’s review of a district court’s denial of *Judgment as a Matter of Law* (JMOL) for infringement is *de novo*.  The court applies the *de novo* standard set forth in *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016).  The de novo review does not eliminate the substantial‑evidence requirement; rather, it simply gives the appellate court the same evidentiary standard that a jury would face, but in a more “light‑favorable‑to‑the-verdict” view.

### 1.  De novo review

In *Wi‑LAN* the court held that when a district court denies a motion for JMOL, the appellate court must:
> “review the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict and determine whether the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” 811 F.3d at 461.

Thus the appellate court is not bound by the district court’s finding; it may look anew at all of the evidence in the record—including jury instructions, exhibits, expert testimony, and the parties’ arguments—to decide whether, under the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence supports the findings.

### 2.  Substantial‑evidence requirement

The de novo standard is applied only to the *same* standard that governs a jury’s factual findings: substantial evidence.  As the Ninth‑Circuit case *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022) explains, a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a “reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.”  The court in *Finesse Wireless* adopts this test explicitly:

> “We review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence.”  (par. 24)

In the instant case the appellate court first considered whether the evidence *supported* the jury’s infringement convictions.  The jury had found the ’134 and ’775 patents infringed, but the court found the evidence was insufficient: Dr. Wells’ testimony was contradictory, he did not clearly map the required signals, and the Nokia documents did not demonstrate the requisite multiplications.  Under the substantial‑evidence test, the court concluded that “no reasonable jury could have found” infringement and so reversed the denial of JMOL.

### 3.  What the court actually looks for

Under the de novo, substantial‑evidence framework, the court asks:

1. **Is there any evidence that could support the verdict?**  
   The court examines all evidence that the trial court deemed admissible and considers it in the light most favorable to the verdict.

2. **Does the evidence point “so strongly and overwhelmingly” to a particular outcome?**  
   The court must be convinced that *reasonable jurors* could not reach a contrary conclusion.  If the evidence is ambiguous or contradictory—as in the Dr. Wells testimony—the court will treat it as insufficient.

3. **Did the trial court err in its factual findings?**  
   The appellate court may consider claims of clear or evident error (e.g., if the trial court accepted testimony that logically contradicted other evidence).  The *Wi‑LAN* court allows this when the evidence shows “no reasonable jury could have found” the contrary conclusion.

In *Finesse*, the appellate court applied all three steps.  It found that Dr. Wells’ testimony was “confusing and unclear” and that the expert’s contradictory statements made the evidence “insufficient to satisfy the substantial‑evidence standard.”  Consequently, even though the appellate court was reviewing the action *de novo*, it concluded that the evidence did not support a reasonable jury’s infringement finding.

### 4.  Bottom line for a student

* The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL **de novo**, not unaided by the district court’s reasoning.  
* The appellate court still applies the **substantial‑evidence** standard that governs a jury’s factual findings.  
* The de novo test does **not** replace the substantial‑evidence requirement; it simply gives the appellate court the authority to apply that requirement afresh, in the most favorable light to the verdict.

So when you see a court reverse a denial of JMOL, read the opinion carefully to see whether the court found the evidence “pointed so strongly and overwhelmingly” in favor of one party and whether it applied the substantial‑evidence standard under that light.  In *Finesse*, that was exactly what happened: the evidence was deemed too weak to support an infringement verdict, and the denial of JMOL was reversed.

### How does the court distinguish between an interference generating signal that is 'generated' versus 'sampled', and why does that matter for claim limitation 1[a] of the ’134 patent?
Question:
The decision focuses on whether the RF ADC samples the modeled PIM path. I’m confused about the technical distinction between a signal that is generated by the hardware after sampling and one that is present in the passband being sampled. Why does the court say the modeled PIM path cannot be the 'interference generating signals' if it’s produced after sampling?
Answer:
**Answer**

The crux of the infringement issue is whether the RF ADC in the accused Nokia radios “samples a passband of received signals that includes both the signals of interest *and* the interference‑generating signals,” as required by claim‑limitation 1[a] of the ’134 patent.  The district court found that the RF ADC did indeed sample that passband, but the appellate court disapproved of the evidence on a *substantial‑evidence* basis.  The key technical point is the distinction between a signal that exists in the received RF spectrum *before* sampling and a signal that is **generated** only after sampling.

---

### 1.  What the court says about “generated” vs “sampled”

* The RF ADC is defined as a receiver that “samples the claimed ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference‑generating signals’ (both depicted in dashed red path) to create a bit stream” (J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10).  
* Dr. Wells identified the “interference‑generating signals” with a *modeled PIM path* that is *generated in the GROOT FPGA* (J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8).  
* The court notes that “the modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference‑generating signals’” and “the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA” (J.A. 21–22).  
* Because that modeled path is **generated after** the RF ADC samples the incoming RF field, the court concludes that the sampled passband can contain no “interference‑generating signals.”  Hence, if the modeled path is indeed the claimed interference‑generating signal, limitation 1[a] is not satisfied (see J.A. 28303).

The court essentially applies the rule that a sampling device can only “sample” a signal that is present in the input waveform at the instant of sampling.  A signal that is synthesized *after* the sampling operation cannot be considered one of the “signals being sampled.”  This is the same principle that underlies claim construction: a detector cannot claim that “signal X is sampled” if X is a post‑processing artifact.

---

### 2.  Why that matters for claim‑limitation 1[a]

Limitation 1[a] imposes a *physical* requirement on the accused system: the RF ADC must ingest a composite analog waveform that contains both the signal‑of‑interest and the source signal that will later generate the unwanted IMPs.  In the ’134 patent:

> “over‑sampling… to create a bit stream, *wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference‑generating signals*….”

If the interference‑generating signal is not present in the analog signal at sampling time, the claimed method’s first step (over‑sampling the *passband of received signals* that contains the source signals) is not met.  The rest of the claim (isolation, estimation, cancellation) would then be non‑functional in the context of the claim, because its input has not been provided in the manner required.

The appellate court treated the evidence supporting sampling of a modeled path as *inconclusive* because the expert testimony never clarified whether the modeled path appeared physically at the ADC input or was synthesized afterward.  Since the expert was unable to confirm that the sampled waveform actually contained the interference‑generating signal, the evidence did not satisfy the *substantial‑evidence* standard for infringement.  Consequently, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement (see the Summary, “We reverse the denial of JMOL … because the jury’s infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence”).

---

### 3.  Bottom line for a law student

* **Generated vs sampled**: “Generated” means the signal is created by a digital or analog processing stage *after* the ADC has already digitized the input. “Sampled” means a signal that physically exists in the analog RF band and is captured by the ADC.  
* **Why it matters**: Claim 1[a] explicitly requires the ADC to sample a passband that contains both signal types. If the interference‑generating signal is only produced post‑sampling, the first step of the claim fails, and the method claim cannot be infringed.  
* **Court’s conclusion**: Because the expert failed to prove that the modeled PIM path existed *before* sampling, the court found the evidence insufficient and granted JMOL of noninfringement. The distinction between generated and sampled signals is therefore critical to interpreting and enforcing the claim limitation.

This reasoning illustrates how a strictly literal reading of a claim’s first step can hinge on whether a signal is physically present at the ADC input—or merely synthesized afterwards.  As the court put it, if the modeled PIM path is not the “interference‑generating signal” *at the time of sampling*, limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied.

### What does it mean for an expert’s testimony to be 'self‑contradictory' under the standard in Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., and how was that applied to Dr. Wells’ testimony in this case?
Question:
The opinion says Dr. Wells’ testimony was confusing and contradictory, yet it references a single platform—his sources x1 and x2—without mapping them to the required signals. How does the court determine that a jury could not have relied on such testimony?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, the Federal Circuit held that an expert’s testimony is *self‑contradictory* when the expert’s **opinions or facts are mutually incompatible within the same demonstration of a fact‑pattern**—for example, when the expert says, “the device contacts the vessel in three dimensions,” but also says, “the device only contacts the vessel in two places.”  The court emphasized that a self‑contradictory opinion is a fatal defect that can render the evidence *insufficient to satisfy a substantial‑evidence standard*, because no reasonable jury could rely on a witness who cannot give a coherent, single answer to a critical question.

---

### 1. What the standard requires

1. **Consistency** – The expert’s testimony must not contain assertions that directly negate one another about the same issue or element of the claim.  

2. **Clarity for a Jury** – The testimony must be presented in a way that a juror could follow the expert’s reasoning to a concrete conclusion.  If the expert flips back and forth among different interpretations of the same signals or functions, a juror cannot identify a single, defensible basis for the expert’s conclusions.  

3. **Substantial‑evidence Gate** – Even if the expert’s testimony is somewhat contradictory, the District Court may still uphold a verdict if the overall evidence is still sufficient.  However, in *Johns Hopkins* the Court found that the contradictions were so pervasive that the jury’s verdict could not have rested on the expert’s opinion.  Thus the evidence failed the *substantial‑evidence* test, and JMOL was proper.

---

### 2. How the court applied this to Dr. Wells

| Aspect | Evidence of Contradiction | Why it mattered |
|--------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| **Mapping of signals to claim limits** | Dr. Wells first identified the “downlink‑TX reference” as the *signal of interest* and the *modeled PIM path* as the *interference‑generating signal* (J.A. 14918–21). Later, after cross‑examination, he said the *modeled PIM path* is generated **after** the RF‑ADC samples, and so it cannot be the interference‑generating signal. | His later statement negated his earlier testimony that the modeled PIM path was the very signal the claim required the radio to sample. A jury cannot be sure whether the radio met limitation 1[a] of sampling both types of signals. |
| **Use of x1 and x2** | When challenged that the modeled PIM path was post‑sampling, he pivoted: “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2” (J.A. 15010). He did **not** directly say “x1 is the signal of interest” or “x2 is the interference‑generating signal.”  He repeatedly said the red path contains the “downlink TX reference” **and** “modeled PIM path,” but never clarified that the *x* signals mapped to those categories. | The testimony fails to give the jury a single, workable mapping of the red‑path signals to the claim’s required signals. The jury could have leaned on the first mapping or the second, but the expert never locked in a model. |
| **Consistent description of the RF‑ADC** | Evidence showed that the RF‑ADC does not sample the *modeled PIM path* (it is generated downstream). Yet Dr. Wells persisted in calling that the *interference‑generating signal* (J.A. 15010‑11). | Again, contradictory statements about the same hardware element undermine confidence that the jury’s verdict was based on a coherent expert opinion. |
| **Mapping of multiplications (for the ’775 patent)** | Dr. Wells listed only three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document (x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, etc.) and then claimed those matched the seven claimed multiplications (J.A. 14966–70). He never gave a mapping from the three to the seven; he merely asserted “the three distinct multiplications correspond to all seven.” | A jury cannot ascertain how the three operations fulfill a seven‑operation requirement when the expert’s explanation is vague and inconsistent. |

Because the contradictions were not merely peripheral or inadvertent but **central** to whether the accused radios met the technical limitations of the patented methods, the court concluded that *no reasonable jury could have relied on Dr. Wells’ testimony* to find infringement.  The portion of the record that hinged on Dr. Wells’ testimony did not survive the *substantial‑evidence* test—exactly the threshold cited in *Johns Hopkins*.

---

### 3. Bottom line

*Self‑contradictory testimony* means an expert switches between mutually exclusive conclusions about the same fact‑pattern in a way that leaves a juror unable to discern a single, defensible basis for the claim.  In the Finesse case, Dr. Wells repeatedly changed his description of which signals were being sampled, which were interference‑generating, and how many distinct multiplications were performed.  The court treated those changes as a failure to provide a coherent, adjudicable explanation; consequently, the jury’s verdict could not rest on his testimony, and JMOL of non‑infringement was granted.

### Why did the court hold that Finesse did not successfully surrender claim scope over two signals when it amended the claims to recite ‘three signals’?
Question:
The opinion cites Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp. as a basis for not treating the amendment as a clear disavowal. I’m unsure how claim amendments are evaluated for scope surrender, especially when the amended claims specify a numeric limitation that seems to exclude the prior art’s two‑signal approach.
Answer:
**Answer**

The court’s refusal to treat the amendment to the ’775 claims as a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of scope is grounded in the *Cont’l Circuits* precedent on claim‑amendment scope and in the specific factual record of the Finesse case.

---

### 1.  The “three‐signal” amendment does **not** waive two‑signal variants

Under *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), an amendment that says “digitally multiplying **three signals** S1, S2 and S3” may **require three separately identifiable signals** but it **does not require that the three signals be unique**.  The Court said that an amendment limiting the number of signals “does not rise to the level of a clear and unambiguous disavowal of claim scope over products with fewer signals.”

In *Finesse*, the examiners rejected a claim that read “digitally multiplying **two or three signals**” as obvious over Filipovic, but the party overcame that rejection by explaining that Filipovic teaches only active IMP mitigation (see J.A. 985–941).  The amended claim then restricts the number of signals to **three** but still allows those three signals to be non‑unique.  The Court explicitly noted that “at most, Finesse’s amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals” (see J.A. 968–969).  Therefore, the amendment does **not** disavow the possibility that the accused radios employ only two unique signals.

---

### 2.  No “clear and unambiguous” waiver was presented

A waiver is judged on the **tone and clarity** of the amendment, not merely on the numbers it contains.  The Court reminded the parties in *Cont’l Circuits* that a numerical limitation is a *finding* on the evidence: “claim language that is more or less than a number can sometimes be ambiguous” (915 F.3d at 798).  In *Finesse*, the party’s argument that adding “three” was a “clear disavowal” was dismissed because the amendment did not expressly or unambiguously exclude the two‑signal case.  No evidence was presented that the amendment was intended to exclude actual implementations that use only two independent signals.

---

### 3.  The appellate court applied the *Cont’l Circuits* standard correctly

The court looked back at the prosecution history and found that Finesse improved its claims in two ways:

1.  It avoided Filipovic’s obviousness rejection for passive IMP mitigation (see J.A. 985).
2.  It added a limitation of “three signals” in seven multiplications.

These changes *expanded* the claim scope, not narrowed it.  The addition of a numeric limit that does **not** preclude non‑unique signals is consistent with *Cont’l Circuits*.  Hence the amendment does **not** negate infringement when the accused device uses only two distinct signals.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

Because the amended claims do **not** require three unique signals, and because the amendment is not a “clear and unambiguous” declaration that two‑signal devices are excluded, the court correctly found that Finesse did **not** surrender claim scope over the two‑signal approach.  The *Cont’l Circuits* doctrine was applied to the specific language of the amendment and the surrounding prosecution history, not to the numerical limitation alone.  Consequently, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL for infringement of the ’775 patent.

### What is the significance of the court’s analysis of the seven third‑order multiplications in the ’775 patent, and how does the lack of a mapping to the Nokia document’s operations affect the infringement verdict?
Question:
The opinion discusses how Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document that listed only three multiplications. I’m not clear on why showing that the accused device performs ‘any’ of the seven multiplications is insufficient for infringement when the court says the device must perform *all* of them. How does the claim wording enforce that requirement?
Answer:
**Answer**

The question hinges on the claim‑construction of the ’775 patent’s key limitation—*“generating the n‑th order IC​S by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3 and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer”* (claim 1, 16‑54‑17).  

The *“seven third‑order multiplications”* are **not** a per‑multiplication limitation; they are the *comprehensive* set of multiplications that the claim requires the method to execute. The claim itself says that **all** seven specified products must be formed. That is why the court treats the limitation as “the seven multiplications must take place” (see the paragraph beginning “The key claim limitation at issue on appeal is that seven third‑order multiplications of the three signals must take place to meet the claim limitation.”).

Because the claim enumerates every multiplication, a device that performs **any** subset of those products is insufficient to satisfy limit­ation 1[d]. The court therefore treats the claim as a “whole‑product” restriction that cannot be satisfied by merely showing the device performs one or two of the multiplications. The language of the claim ("**including** generating an n‑th order IC​S **by** digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3 and S2×S3×S3") leaves no room for interpretation that the device “generates at least three of the seven” or “generates any three of the seven.” The claim explicitly lists *all* seven, and the court characters it as a “comprehensive set.” See the court’s discussion that follows the claim: “the key claim limitation at issue is that seven third‑order multiplications … must take place… The jury found all asserted claims of the ’775 patent infringed” (J.A. 169). The court proves that the burden of showing **all seven** was not met by the evidence presented at trial.

---

### Why the lack of a mapping between the device’s operations and the seven claimed multiplications matters

At trial, Dr. Wells used a Nokia document that listed **three distinct multiplications**:

1. \(x_1\cdot |x_1|\cdot |x_1|\)  
2. \(x_1\cdot x_1\cdot x_2'\)  
3. \(x_1\cdot |x_2|\cdot |x_2|\)  

Those operations do not match the textbook multiplications enumerated in the claim. The court pointed out that the Nokia document “does not show the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications” and that “there is no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed” (J.A. 26486).  

Because the claim requires **all seven** particular products, the court required that the expert testify not only that the device performs the claimed multiplications but also *how the device’s actual calculations map onto each of the seven products*. In the opinion, the court notes that Dr. Wells “did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications” (J.A. 14966–70). This omission renders the expert’s testimony an *inadequate basis* for the jury’s inference that the device performed the full set of needed multiplications.  

In patent litigation, the jury’s burden is to find infringement **based on the construction of each claim limitation**. If the court constructs a limitation to mean “all seven multiplications must take place,” a single or partial performance of the claim’s essential function is insufficient. The expert therefore must produce *correspondence data* that demonstrates how the device’s actual algorithm implements each of the seven claim‑specified multiplications. The Nokia document, lacking that correspondence, did not satisfy the requirement that the device “generates the n‑th order IC​S by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering **all** of the seven products.” That mapping was required, because the claim uses absolute language: “including generating an n‑th order IC​S by … and **S2×S3×S3**,” not “by generating an n‑th order IC​S by generating at least one of ...”.  

---

### Bottom line

1. **Claim construction**: The ’775 claim expressly enumerates seven distinct third‑order multiplications that *must* all occur. The language of the claim and the court’s construction treat the requirement as a *comprehensive* set, not a “select several” or “any subset.”  

2. **Expert evidence**: The expert’s testimony was insufficient because it identified only three multiplications from a Nokia document that do not correspond to the seven claim‑required products, and it did not provide an explicit mapping of those operations to each of the seven required multiplications. The lack of such a mapping prevents the jury from concluding that the accused device satisfied the claim limitation.  

3. **Infringement verdict**: Because the evidence did not support that the accused device performed *all* seven multiplications, the jury’s infringement verdict cannot be sustained. The court therefore reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award.  

In short, the significance of the “seven third‑order multiplications” is that the claim requires *every* one of them. The expert’s failure to map the device’s real‑world operations onto those seven products makes the infringement finding unsupported.

### On what basis does the Fifth Circuit vacate a damages award after reversing a JMOL ruling, and what rules govern that process?
Question:
The opinion states that reversing the JMOL decision automatically necessitates vacating the damages award. I’d like to understand the procedural logic and any statutory or case law that obligates the appellate court to vacate damages when infringement is found non‑infringing.
Answer:
**Answer:**

The Fifth Circuit vacates a damages award whenever it finds, on appeal, that the parties are *not* in the same “statutory position” the award is meant to compensate. In a patent case that position is **infringement**. Once the court determines that no infringement determined by the district court, the whole basis for the award collapses. The Fifth Circuit treats that as a *de novo* review of the district court’s denial of JMOL, and, upon finding that denial wrong, automatically nullifies any award that was predicated on a now‑invalid infringement verdict.

---

### 1.   How the appellate court reaches that conclusion

1. **Reversal of the district court’s denial of JMOL**  
   - The court applies the Fifth Circuit’s standard for reviewing a denial of JMOL, which it describes as a *de novo* review of the evidence.  *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d at 461.  
   - The court concludes that the evidence is “insufficient to support” the jury’s infringement determinations on both patents, and therefore reverses the denial of JMOL.  

2. **Vacating the damages award**  
   - In the same paragraph that describes the reversal, the opinion says:  
     > “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.”  
   - This statement is not a new rule; it is the court’s expression that an award tied to an invalid infringement claim cannot survive. The court takes the standard that a damages award is only valid when the underlying infringement exists.  

3. **No separate merits review of damages**  
   - The Fifth Circuit holds that the district court’s denial of a new‑trial or JMOL on damages is “an abuse of discretion” and is reviewed for **abuse of discretion**, not for merits.  *Wi‑LAN, Inc.*, 811 F.3d at 461.  
   - Because the appellate court has already determined that infringement did **not** occur, the relief sought by the plaintiff (damages) is no longer tenable; the award was premised on a claim that the court now finds false. The reservation of a novelty‑based remand or a separate damages‑review is unnecessary.

---

### 2.   Statutory and case‑law basis

| Source | Law | Relevance |
|--------|-----|-----------|
| 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) | Appeals of cases with evidence of infringement | Gives the appellate court jurisdiction to review the district court’s findings, including JMOL and, by extension, damages that hinge on infringement. |
| *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016) | De‑novo review of denials of JMOL | The court explicitly states it can “reverse” a denial when the evidence does not support an infringement finding. |
| *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022) | Review of a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence | The court’s authority to find infringement “not supported by substantial evidence” leads to vacating the award. |
| *Wi‑LAN, Inc.*, 811 F.3d at 461 | Review of denial of a new‑trial or JMOL on damages | Damage‑relief motions are reviewed for abuse of discretion; but if the appellate court has already reversed the infringement finding, the award is indeterminate. |

---

### 3.   Practical take‑away for litigants

1. **Vacating damages follows from vacating infringement** – The court never “misses” a step; it treats the vacated infringement and the vacated award as a package.  
2. **Damages are deer‑antagonistic to the infringement finding** – If the appellate court finds no infringement, any award that was purportedly for infringing conduct cannot stand.  
3. **No need for a separate “damages‑only” review** – Because the appellate court’s reversal of JMOL already defeats the premise of the award, it does not consider damages on their own merits.  

---

### 4.   Bottom line

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL, thus determining that the district court’s infringement verdicts were unsupported by evidence. Because the damages award was predicated on those findings, the appellate court automatically **vacated the award**. The basis for that action is the Fifth Circuit’s own precedent on de novo review of JMOl (see *Wi‑LAN*, 811 F.3d at 461) combined with the statutory authority under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) to review such findings. The court did not rely on any separate statutory provision to vacate damages; it simply followed the doctrine that an award cannot be maintained when the underlying statutory violation no longer exists.
