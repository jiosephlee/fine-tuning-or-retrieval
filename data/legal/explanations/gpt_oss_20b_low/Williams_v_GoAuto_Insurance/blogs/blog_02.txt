Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Signature‑Dead Statutes? The Fifth Circuit’s Flat‑Take on ‘Certifying’ in Insurance‑Financing Law

## Why This Matters

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit confronted a seemingly innocuous question that, if answered a little differently, could upend the way thousands of small‑to‑medium insurers and the independent finance companies that fund them operate each year. The court held that Louisiana’s statutory “certifying” requirement did **not** demand a physical signature from a named employee of the fee‑for‑service bankruptcy of a financing surety. Instead, a corporate e‑mail, a list of statements, and a computer‑initiated cancellation sufficed. The holding cleanly clarified that the statute’s “certifying” language can be satisfied by a corporate statement—no individual signature required—and that “receipt” can be satisfied by modern electronic communication. If the court had gone the other way, insurers would have faced a massive procedural overhaul, forcing them to designate a staff member, affix a personal signature and wrestle with the logistics of private‑carrier delivery for every delinquent account.

## The Anatomy of Louisiana’s Cancellation Statute

Louisiana’s 9:3550(G)(3)(a) arranges a five‑point checklist: (i) a power of attorney on the financing agreement; (ii) the policy is in default and is uncured; (iii) a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured; (iv) copies were sent to parties with an interest in the policy; and (v) a “statement certifying” the foregoing. The statute then provides that upon *receipt* of that “notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company” the insurer may proceed to cancel. The language is descriptive, not prescriptive, leaving a handful of interpretive gray zones.

The broad question: *Does “certifying” demand a signed statement?* The text is silent on signatures, and the only explicit field of “receipt” is broader – the insurer must *receive* the notice, whether via hard copy or "private carrier" or in the modern sense, an email.

## The Fifth Circuit’s Precedential Landscape

The Fifth Circuit is no stranger to the cancellatory politics of insurance.

1. **Delatte v. Lemotte** (1993) emphasized the necessity of “strict adherence,” but it was light‑handed about the form of the notice; it did not chlorinate signatures.
2. **Eaglin v. Champion** (1990) qualified the same, again requiring only the statutory conditions, not any signature wording.
3. **Hodges v. Colonial** (1989) granted cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement …” again without referencing a signature requirement.
4. **Benitez v. Elsayed** (2019) did highlight a missing signature in the notice but decided it was not fatal because other statutory elements were satisfied.

True, the Fifth Circuit has not quite crystallized a “signature rule” in this context, and *Williams* is the first time it has gone from discussion to an explicit hold: signatures are not required.

## The Fifth Circuit’s Tight‑Fisted Engineering

The circuit’s reasoning sits neatly on two pillars:

### 1. *What does “certifying” mean?*

The statute says the finance company must “certify” that the four conditions are met. But “certifying” is defined as the *act* of confirming; it does not invoke any particular *manner* of confirmation. The law is vacuum on signatures. The Fifth Circuit looked to the practice of corporate communications: a computer system can generate a single electronically‑signed PDF or an approved company letterhead. By the modern era’s habits—where a company’s “signature” may be a corporate stamp or a rigorous electronic authentication system—singular individual signatures are no longer necessary. The court saw no linguistic or doctrinal ammunition that would mandate a physical byline attached to each notice.

### 2. *What does “receipt” mean?*

Sounds like a typo—receipt is apparently translated as *receive*—but the Fifth Circuit renovated old shipping lingo to show that an email notification the insurer’s system (a web service) can “receive” the request and instantly carry out a cancellation. The district counsel’s inventive construction of a “private carrier” (a private carrier, e.g., a courier company) ripped the old shipping metaphor and tossed it into the 21st‑century cyberspace. The Fifth Circuit deemed the e‑mail a “notice” under “private carrier” control. An e‑mail is essentially “a message transmitted electronically from one computer to another” and — while not a physical *carriage* in the old sense — it satisfies the “delivery” charter of the statute.

Thus, the Ninth‑Circuits and the Fourth® are set: a modern, a serious card not only because they treat the insurer’s web service as a “winner” but also because they do the labor of locating text coastlines that fictionally obligate to a signature.

## How the Held Practical Outcomes Would Crack open

### For Insurers
- **No Need to Chair a signing Board**: Under *Williams*, insurers can keep their cancellation process automated. The more costly the premise of a signed letter for every delinquent payment, the less insurance will do that.
- **Administrative Simplicity**: Insurers can rely entirely on their APIs; they can keep the heavy‑handed paper folder policy going to the "Green file" and still be legally compliant with Louisiana law.

### For Finance Companies
- **Less Risk of Litigation**: ANly finance companies can now see that their corporate statements genome can satisfy the statutory “certifying” requirement. Ad hoc sign‑off by branch or agent will be unnecessary.
- **Budget Impact**: A less paper‑intensive process means a portion of the $24 per hundred thousand "retention" margin for low‑risk policy cancellations can be re‑allocated to more productive investments.

### Legal Certainty

The doctrine of *certifying* is now rendered a primarily textual test; no extrinsic interpretation required. It reduces the risk of unanticipated disputes. The decision also foregrounds the *here and now* of modern technology: a 14‑hour federation of distributed systems each holding a status flag is a perfect, legal, and typical formula. This may shift future legislation to formally codify the notion of an electronically signed corporate certification.

## What Might a Legislative Amendment Look Like?

- **Formal “Electronic Signature” Clause**: Louisiana could codify that an electronic signature—be it a system‑generated stamp, a time‑stamp in a reputable platform, or a masked identity certificate—suffices. This would reduce judicial consternation over future high‑volume messages and avoid wading through an older "company letterhead" requirement.
- **Delivery Methods**: A new clause stating, “Notices of cancellation may be delivered electronically—via email, certified mail, or secure electronic messaging—to the insurer and any third‑party recipients.” That would remove ambiguous “private carrier” language that earlier courts had to interpret and blanket "unrelated telephone carrying" as "private carrier" for the plaintiff's trying to, article and call. 
- **Fallback for Non‑Electronic Cancellations**: Recognizing not all policyholders or insurers yet embrace electronic communications, a mixed model could require an optional print‑out with a corporate seal signed by an authorized officer, yet it could be seasonally replaced with an electronic signature for certain classes of high‑risk policy. There might also be a defined transformation schedule to make the state legal.

## Beyond the Fifth Circuit: What Else Should We Watch?

- **Doctrinal Alignment With Other Circuits**: The Sixth, Seventh, and Eighth circuits have all treated similar statutes differently; for instance, some insist on a "physically signed" notice, others are more permissive. The Fifth Circuit’s decision may spur a coalition of insurers to litigate changes in other states—especially in the South, where finance companies rely heavily on distressed-versus-cancellation sequences.
- **Potential for Class‑Action Amendments**: If legislation were amended to include explicit "digital signature" language, class actions that name numerous federal insurers would be simplified, reducing the need for separate litigation around these statutory nuances.

## How I See It Going Forward

*Williams v. GoAuto* is a practical, incremental step that does not move the needle precipitously. It keeps the statutory infrastructure largely intact while giving the insurance and finance industries a legal embrace for modern technology. Because the Fifth Circuit did not *create* new law but *clarified* an old one, the decision is likely to be embraced by the industry, possibly sparking legislative codification to remove ambiguity. But it also means it will serve as a reliance point in future cases where insurers and finance companies might argue that the “certifying” requirement is a signature‑driven condition, especially in the context of “smart‑contract” or blockchain‑based premium financing.

The bigger lesson is that courts, when set on statutory text, often find that courts describe the demonstration stage incorrectly because they are not made to fit within the political constraints of letter‑by‑letter language. In the end, *Williams* shows that Louisiana law keeps the floodgate open for high‑speed, low‑paper cancellation: the insurer can cancel a policy at the precise deadline, and the finance company can certify without a signed statinstead of a sign in a case that’s probably on the docket for the next year.
