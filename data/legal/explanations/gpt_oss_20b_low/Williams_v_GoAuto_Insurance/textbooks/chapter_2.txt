Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 2: # II. Louisiana Statutory Framework for Cancelling Financed Policies

This chapter explains the Louisiana statutory scheme that governs the cancellation of insurance policies when the premiums are financed.  The statute at issue is La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3), which codifies the steps a premium‑finance company must take, the evidence it must provide, and the methods it may use to notify the insurer of a default.  Understanding these provisions is essential because the court’s holding hinged on whether GoAuto Insurance’s cancellation procedures “strictly adhered” to the statute.  We therefore examine the statutory language in the context of the facts presented in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*.

## Definition and Scope of an Insurance‑Premium Finance Company

While the statute does not supply an exhaustive definition of “insurance‑premium finance company,” it presumes the existence of a corporate entity that (1) pays a carrier the full amount of an insured’s premiums on a credit basis, and (2) receives from the insured monthly instalment payments that effectively reimburse the financer.  The entity must also have, in writing, a **power of attorney** (POA) that authorizes it to act on behalf of the insured with respect to the carrier.  The POA is dispositive: it is the statutory instrument that permits the finance company to direct the insurer to cancel a policy in the event of a default.

The court’s relief was narrowly tailored to the *cancellation* process; the rest of the financing relationship (payment collection, interest, etc.) is outside the scope of § 9:3550(G)(3).  The only individuals subject to the statute are the carrier and the finance company.

## Statutory Requirements for Notice of Cancellation

Section § 9:3550(G)(3) imposes a step‑by‑step procedure for a finance company that wants to request cancellation of an insured’s policy:

1. **Sending the Notice** – The finance company must transmit a notice of cancellation to the insurer.  The statute gives three permissible methods: (a) depositing the notice in the mail or with a private carrier; or (b) delivering it via electronic mail.  The notice itself is not static; it must be accompanied by a **statement** that certifies certain facts (see the next subsection).

2. **Content Requirements** – The statement that accompanies the notice must confirm:
   - **(i)** The finance agreement contains a valid POA as supplied in § 9:3550(G)(1).
   - **(ii)** The agreement is in default, and the default has not been cured tim­ely.
   - **(iii)** Upon default, the insurer was notified by the finance company of an impending cancellation (i.e., the insurer received a “Ten‑Day Notice of Cancellation” to the insured).  The statement must contain the date on which the notice was sent to the insured.
   - **(iv)** Copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to every person who, under the financing agreement, had an interest in any loss that might occur under the policy (e.g., governmental agencies, lienholders, or other third parties).  The statement must specify the names and addresses of those parties.

These requirements are cumulative: a finance company must satisfy every one of them before the insurer may consider canceling the policy.

## Certification Elements (POA, Default, Notice to Insured)

The statutory language emphasizes that the **certification** is a key part of the notice.  The court in *Williams* interpreted “certifying” as a declaratory statement that the financing company matters have been verified, rather than a requirement that the statement be signed by a named employee of the finance company.  The statute itself uses the present‑tense verb “certifies” without any adjective such as “signed” or “approved.”  Accordingly, the mere presence of the words “APAC certifies that …” sufficed in the opinion, because the statute did not posit that a signature was necessary.  

The three seventh‑paragraph elements can be summed as:

- **POA Validity** – A contractual or statutory power of attorney that authorizes the finance company to act on behalf of the insured with respect to the insurance carrier.
- **Default Status** – A situation where the insured has failed to cure a payment deficiency within the statutory timeframe (e.g., a ten‑day cure period).
- **Notice to Insured** – The insurer’s receipt of a separate “Ten‑Day Notice of Cancellation” addressed to the insured, confirming that the insured has been informed of the pending cancellation and covered the exact date of that communication.

If any of these elements is missing or is not certified, the notice fails to satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a).  The court reiterated that Louisiana courts have repeatedly accepted a “strict adherence” to Congress‑mandated procedures: an incomplete certificate yields an ineffective cancellation.

## Transmission Methods: Mail, Private Carrier, Electronic

Section § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) distinguishes permissible delivery methods:

- **Mail** – The notice of cancellation may be “deposited in the mail,” which typically means a postal or courier service that provides delivery confirmation and a date of arrival.
- **Private Carrier** – Defined as a commercial entity that transports items directly between parties.  While the statute does not offer a narrow definition, the standard legal meaning is a company that delivers a physical object or document.  In *Williams*, the district court treated an APAC employee who physically delivered the notice as acting as a private carrier, analyzing the concept under the Supreme Court’s interpretation in *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* (which treated a railroad as a private carrier under certain conditions).
- **Electronic Mail** – The statute explicitly allows e‑mail as a valid transmission method.  This modern method aligns with the prevailing corporate practice at the time of filing the case (early 2020s).  The court accepted that a properly transmitted e‑mail, subject to the certification requirements, satisfies the statutory mandate.

The last paragraph of the statutes (b)(ii) makes it clear that the insurer can cancel “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.”  Hence the precise method of delivery is less important than the fact that the insurer *received* it in a manner consistent with the statutory scheme.

## Clarification of “Receipt” and “Private Carrier” Under the Statute

The phrase **receipt** is crucial.  While the Louisiana Supreme Court has not yet definitively defined “receipt” in the context of insurance‑premium finance, the term’s ordinary meaning applies.  Black’s Law Dictionary defines *receipt* as “the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession.”  The statute requires the insurer to “consider that cancellation has been requested” **upon receipt of the notice**.  The court in *Williams* reasoned that the insurer’s automated web service that “listens” for an APAC cancellation request counts as a form of receipt: the system receives the message programmatically and then executes the cancellation.  The subsequent hand‑stamp of the printed cancellation documents by an APAC employee—after the policy was already cancelled—was indeed after receipt, not before, and therefore did not invalidate the cancellation.

Regarding **private carrier**, the statute’s language is intentionally broad.  Private carriers are any entities that transport objects physically between two parties, as opposed to public carriers (e.g., national postal services).  If an APAC employee physically gives the original notice to a GoAuto employee, that employee’s act constitutes the acting private carrier.  Automatic electronic transmission does not involve a carrier in the traditional sense, but the statute explicitly lists electronic mail as an accepted substitution.  Thus the “private carrier” exception becomes unnecessary when e‑mail is used; the currency of the statute allows both modalities.  The district court’s use of a private‑carrier analogy was unnecessary, but it did not undermine the later ruling that e‑mail fulfillment satisfied § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).

---

**Key Takeaway for Students:**  
Louisiana’s § 9:3550(G)(3) is a procedural checklist.  A finance company must send a notice that *certifies* POA, default, insured notice, and other interested parties.  The insurer may cancel only after *receiving* that notice, regardless of whether the delivery method is mail, private carrier, or e‑mail.  Whether the certification includes a signature or not is irrelevant because the statute does not require one.  In *Williams*, the court found GoAuto’s email‑based cancellation met the statutory criteria because the insurer received the notice electronically and the certification satisfied all statutory elements.
