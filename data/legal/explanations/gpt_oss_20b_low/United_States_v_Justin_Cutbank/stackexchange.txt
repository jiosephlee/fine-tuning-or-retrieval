Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why was the prior possession of a sawed‑off Marlin considered 'intrinsic' evidence under Rule 404(b) in Cutbank?
Question:
I’m confused about the court’s application of 18 U.S.C. § 924(e) and the evidentiary ruling that the defendant’s prior possession of the sawed‑off Marlin was intrinsic evidence. The defendant argued that this violated Rule 404(b) because it was evidence of prior unlawful conduct. The court and the Ninth Circuit in United States v. Battle say it is ‘intrinsic’ when it helps prove ‘later possession of the same weapon.’ How exactly does this make the evidence admissible and what distinguishes intrinsic from extrinsic evidence in this context?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court’s ruling that the defendant’s prior possession of a sawed‑off Marlin was “intrinsic” evidence does not hinge on § 924(e) at all; it is a Rule 404(b) issue. Rule 404(b) bars evidence of a person’s prior unlawful conduct *unless* the evidence is “necessary to prove the validity of a charge, defense, or claim that is at issue in the case” or it is “directly relevant to an issue other than the character or reputation of the witness” and would *not* tend to “unlawfully prejudice the jury.”[1]

In *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), the court explained that evidence of *prior possession* of the *same* firearm “is intrinsically relevant to the subsequent possession charge” because it helps establish that the defendant owned or controlled the weapon in question. In *Cutbank*, the trial testimony—“I have seen ___ with the sawed‑off Marlin a handful of times before the night in question”—directly addressed the *present* charge of felon‑in‑possession. It was not simply a propensity statement about “violent or careless behaviour” but a factual claim that the defendant had continuous control of that particular weapon. That is why the district court said the evidence was “intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession of the firearm before the incident.”[2]

**Intrinsic vs. extrinsic**

* **Intrinsic evidence** is the testimony or physical evidence that the prosecution needs to prove one of the elements of the charged offense. It is allowed unless it is so prejudicial as to violate Rule 404(b) under the “unlawfully prejudicial” standard. In *Cutbank*, the prosecution used the claim to show that Cutbank had *already possessed* the sawed‑off Marlin, thus satisfying the “possession” element of the felon‑in‑possession statute.  

* **Extrinsic evidence** is testimony or evidence that is not directly tied to an element of the charged offense, such as prior acts that serve only to paint a picture of the defendant’s character or propensity. Under Rule 404(b), extrinsic conduct evidence is generally inadmissible unless it falls under an exception (e.g., impeachment of a witness). The defense correctly argued that the prior possession testimony was extrinsic, but the court found otherwise because the testimony was offered *to prove an element* of the charged crime, not to show the defendant’s general bad character.

**Why it is admissible**

1. **Relevance to an element of the charge** – The prosecution needed to show that Cutbank *owned or controlled* the sawed‑off Marlin at the time of the crime. Prior possession is the most direct path to establishing that ownership/control.  
2. **Not merely propensity** – The testimony was not telling the jury that Cutbank was “violent” or “has a tendency to misbehave.” It was saying, “he already had the weapon.”  
3. **No unjust prejudice** – The court applied the 403 balancing test and found that, while prejudice was possible, it was outweighed by the probative value of proving possession of the gun.  

Because the evidence met those requirements, it was not excluded as extrinsic propensity evidence. The court therefore held the evidence was *intrinsic* and admissible.

**Bottom line**

In *Cutbank*, the candidate for dismissal under Rule 404(b) was a *prior possession* claim that *directly proved a charge* (felon in possession of a firearm) and not *ostensible bad character* evidence. The Ninth Circuit in *Battle* corroborated that such evidence is intrinsically relevant because it establishes the defendant’s ownership or control of the weapon that is the subject of the current charge. Hence, the evidence was admissible and not barred as extrinsic conduct.

---

**References**

1. Fed. R. Evid. 404(b).  
2. *United States v. Cutbank*, 8th Cir. 2025, at  A.4 (demonstrating intrinsic relevance of prior possession).  
3. *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014).

### Did the court’s failure to give a limiting instruction on D.F.’s police statements violate the Confrontation Clause?
Question:
Cutbank asked the jury to be instructed that it could not consider D.F.’s statements to the police until she testified. The court said it didn’t admit hearsay and the Confrontation Clause was satisfied because D.F. was present. I’m trying to understand: is the court required to give a limiting instruction for every statement that could be considered substantive evidence, or can it rely on a closing instruction? Under United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, how does this rule apply to a situation where a defendant challenges the admissibility of the defendant’s own (the reporter’s) statements as evidence?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court was correct not to give a separate limiting instruction on D.F.’s police statements.  The Sixth‑Amendment Confrontation Clause is satisfied as long as the declarants personally testify in court, even if their statements were admissible as substantive evidence.  The court is not compelled to give a “limiting instruction” for every piece of evidence that might be deemed substantive; an appropriate closing instruction suffices when the evidence is already admissible and the declarants are present.

---

### 1.  The rule in *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887 (8th Cir. 2014)

In *Bennett* the court held that:

> “The district court may not be required to give a limiting instruction on each item of evidence at the time it is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.”  *Id.* at 899, 901 (emphasis added).

The rule applies whenever the evidentiary issue is already decided—either by a previous ruling that the evidence is admissible or by the fact that the declarant is in court.  The crucial factors are:

1. **Admissibility has already been established** – the district court has ruled that the evidence is not hearsay or otherwise excluded.  
2. **The declarants are present for cross‑examination** – the defendant or witnesses have the opportunity to challenge the statements at trial.  
3. **An adequate closing instruction is issued** – the court explains the overall scope and purpose of the evidence in a single instruction after the entire trial.

The Sixth Amendment does not require a separate “limiting instruction” before each piece of evidence; it requires that the jury be able to weigh the admissible evidence against the defendant’s right to confront and cross‑examine versus the purpose of the evidence.  *Bennett* reconciles the two requirements by allowing a closing instruction when the other conditions are met.

---

### 2.  Application to D.F.’s police statements

*Cutbank* raised an objection that the jury should not be allowed to consider D.F.’s statements to police until D.F. herself testified.  The district court held:

> “In addition, D.F. testified. The Confrontation Clause is satisfied **when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person**.”  *Cutbank* 2‑B‑(2)‑(b), 775 F.3d at 889 (quoting *Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861).

The statements were admissible (the court rejected any hearsay claim), and D.F. was in court and cross‑examined on them.  Because the admissibility issue was resolved and the declarants were present, *Cutbank* relied on *Bennett*’s principle:

> “The court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.”  *Id.* at 899.

The court gave a closing instruction that included the scope of corroborating evidence and the limits on using D.F.’s statements, fulfilling the Sixth‑Amendment requirement.  No separate limiting instruction was necessary.

---

### 3.  Why the Confrontation Clause was not violated

The Confrontation Clause requires that the defendant have an opportunity to cross‑examine the declarant *in person*, and that the declarant’s statements not be excluded merely because the defendant has not had that opportunity.  Since D.F. testified, the jury could scrutinize the statements directly.  The court’s closing instruction clarified the admissible role of those statements (e.g., to establish the context of police actions) and therefore did not allow the jury to treat them as evidence of Cutbank’s conduct without the requisite confrontation.  

In short, *Cutbank* correctly followed *Bennett*: the admissibility of D.F.’s police statements was already decided, the declarant was present for cross‑examination, and an appropriate closing instruction was given.  The Confrontation Clause was satisfied; a limiting instruction at the time of admissibility was not required.

### Why can the district court include the 4‑level ‘possessed in connection with another felony offense’ enhancement at sentencing?
Question:
The defendant argues that the PSR’s application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) – the 4‑level increase for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense – was incorrect. The court says the enhancement applies regardless of a charge or conviction for the other felony. I don’t understand how the guidelines treat the term ‘connection’ and why a factual statement about another felony that wasn’t charged is sufficient to trigger the penalty. Also, how does the court decide whether the enhancement is ‘clear and unambiguous’?
Answer:
**Answer**

The district court was correct to apply USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).  The enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (comment n.14(C)).  Consequently, the mere fact—recorded in the PSR—that the firearm was used in connection with another felony offense triggers the four‑level increase, even if that other offense was not criminally charged or resulted in a conviction in this case.

---

### 1.  The “connection” standard

In United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929‑30 (8th Cir. 2013) the court reiterated that § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) activates “when a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense.”  The guidance itself states that “the enhancement applies … regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.”  The court in Cutbank therefore does not need to pin the enhancement to a specifically charged or adjudicated felony; it only needs factual evidence that the defendant’s firearm was used in another felony context.

The PSR contains such factual statements.  Cutbank’s prior felony convictions (e.g., domestic violence, kidnapping, assault, and felony resisting arrest) are well documented in the PSR.  By noting that the sawed‑off rifle “was used in connection with another felony offense,” the PSR satisfies the statutory requirement for the 4‑level increase.  The district court simply accepted those facts, a routine application of the guidelines that “does not render a federal judge a sentencing expert” (see *United States v. Klock, 117 F.4th 235, 242 (10th Cir. 2023)* for a discussion of guideline authority).  The court didn’t need to find a *specific* charge; the evidence of prior felony activity is sufficient.

---

### 2.  Clear and unambiguous application

The question of whether an enhancement is “clear and unambiguous” is an *abuse‑of‑discretion* inquiry.  The appellate court in Cutbank treated it as a factual determination: “the district court… adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Thus, the court did not err in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.”  The court did not say the enhancement was vague or ambiguous; it merely confirmed that the facts were clear enough to trigger the statutory increase.  Because the statutory language is unambiguous and the PSR provides the requisite factual basis, the district court’s application cannot be set aside on a *clear and unambiguous* ground.

---

### Bottom line

Cutbank’s challenge fails because:

1. **Statutory language** demands the enhancement whenever a firearm is used in any felony context, period.  
2. **Guidelines comment n.14(C)** explicitly removes the need for an *adjudicated* other felony.  
3. The **PSR contains factual allegations** of prior felonies that satisfy the “connection” requirement.  
4. The **court exercised its standard discretionary authority** in adopting those facts, and the application is neither *obviously wrong* nor *vague*.

For a law student, the takeaway is that when the PSR identifies prior felony activity that involved firearm use, the four‑level “connection” enhancement automatically applies.  No additional charge, trial, or conviction is required, and the district court’s finding is presumptively correct unless the record clearly shows the facts were misidentified or the law was misinterpreted.

### What is the standard for considering a sentence ‘substantially unreasonable’ under § 3553(a) when the district court imposes a guideline‑range term?
Question:
The opinion says 'the district court must first ensure it correctly calculated the guidelines and then considers § 3553(a) factors.' If the sentence is within the guidelines range, is it automatically reasonable, or can appellate courts find it ‘substantially unreasonable’ if § 3553(a) factors suggest a different outcome? How did the 8th Circuit apply this standard in recent cases such as Masood or Maluoth?
Answer:
**Answer**

The 8th Circuit treats a sentence that falls inside the advisory guidelines range as presumptively reasonable, but it is *not* automatically immune from appellate review.  The court requires a two‑step analysis:

1. **Proper calculation of the range.**  
   If the district court followed the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines to compute the appropriate range and made no significant procedural error in doing so, the range is considered “correct” for appellate purposes.  (See *Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th at 793; *Rosales‑Mireles*, 585 U.S. at 143‑44.)

2. **Substantive reasonableness under § 3553(a).**  
   Even within the range, the sentence can be found *substantially unreasonable* if the district court failed to give proper weight to or even considered the § 3553(a) factors.  The appellate court will look for evidence that the district court “actually considered” the factors and that its conclusion was not “arbitrary or capricious” (see *Masood*, 133 F.4th at 809–10; *Maluoth*, 121 F.4th at 1164).

**How the 8th Circuit applied this standard**

* **Masood (2025).**  
  The court confirmed that a within‑range sentence is “presumptively reasonable” but reiterated that the appellate court must first establish that the district court correctly calculated the range.  It then held that the district court need only show that the § 3553(a) factors were considered, not that the court provided a detailed mechanical listing.  Because the record showed the district court had weighed the factors, *Masood* affirmed the sentence.

* **Maluoth (2024).**  
  The court applied the same two‑step test.  It noted that the district court’s sentencing memorandum contained a full discussion of the factors—particularly the defendant’s extensive violent history and the aggravating circumstances of the offense.  Since the judge had “actually considered” the § 3553(a) items, the court affirmed the sentence, even though the judge had imposed a terms that some might view as harsh.

In both cases, the appellate judges were unwilling to overturn a sentence simply because it was at the low end of the range or because it seemed appalling.  The key is whether the district court, after correctly calculating the range, gave the § 3553(a) factors “its full weight” or at least considered them substantively.

**Bottom line for practitioners**

- An inside‑range sentence is *presumptively* reasonable, but the appellate court will examine whether the district court properly considered § 3553(a).  
- If the record shows no such consideration—or if the court’s conclusion was so out of step with the circumstances that it could not be supported by the factors—an appellate court can declare the sentence “substantially unreasonable” and impose a different term.  
- The recent *Masood* and *Maluoth* decisions confirm that the 8th Circuit applies the two‑step test and generally affirms within‑range sentences that show evidence the judge weighed the § 3553(a) factors.  

Therefore, while an appraisal that a sentence is “within the guidelines” does give the district court the benefit of the doubt, appellate judges will still scrutinize the judgment for *substantive* reasonableness under § 3553(a).

### When can a district court rely on a PSR’s factual statements without objection to determine the guidelines range?
Question:
In the sentencing phase, the court 'adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions.' The defendant raises no objections. Is it standard practice for a court to accept the PSR’s facts at face value, or does the judge still need to verify them? What happens if later a new piece of evidence shows a conviction was actually for a misdemeanor?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the district court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions.”  That language is unambiguous: the court took the PSR’s facts as fact for the purposes of applying the guideline enhancement.  The court’s adoption of the PSR facts is a standard, *de novo* practice at sentencing.  The Fifth Amendment does not require the judge to personally verify every fact that a PSR reports; the PSR is generated by the Department of Justice and is treated as reliable guidance, not as evidence that must be corroborated in court.  See *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929‑30 (8th Cir. 2013) (a district court may adopt the PSR’s factual statements at sentencing without independent verification).  The adoption of PSR facts is a broad, discretionary act that is reviewed for *harmless error* on appeal, not for some “essentiality” error.

The question as posed—*What if, later, a new piece of evidence shows a conviction was actually for a misdemeanor?*—must be answered in light of that standard.  If a later discovery shows that one of the “felony” convictions cited in the PSR was in fact a misdemeanor, the error would affect the *Criminal‑History Category* calculation.  The appellate court would first assess whether the error was *material* and *prejudicial*.  If the misclassification would materially lower the guideline offense level (for example, dropping the defendant from Category VI to a lower category) and the sentence that *would* have followed that lower level would fall outside the range that the defendant ultimately received, the appellate court would likely order a new sentencing calculation.  If, however, the sentence that would have resulted from the corrected category would still fall within the range that the defendant was sent, the error would be considered harmless and the sentence would be affirmed.  Thus, the judge is not required to re‑verify each factual premise; the appellate court will remedy a demonstrated, material mis‑fact if that mis‑fact would be expected to change the outcome.

In short:

1. **Adoption is standard practice.**  A district court may take PSR facts at face value without independent verification; that is permissible and customary.  
2. **Later mis‑facts are addressed on appeal.**  If new evidence shows a prior conviction is in fact a misdemeanor, the appellate court will evaluate whether the error changes the relevant guideline and whether such a change likely altered the sentence.  If it did, a new sentence must be imposed; if not, the original sentence stands.

This is the correct procedural and substantive rule in *Cutbank* and the broader district‑court sentencing framework.

### How does the 'homestead rule' affect sentencing under ACGA when the defendant is a felon in possession of a firearm?
Question:
I read that a defendant's previous violent felony convictions trigger ACGA maximum penalties, but I'm unsure how the court determined the Criminal History Category and level. The PSR lists at least 12 prior convictions. How does the court decide whether those convictions count toward the ACGA maximum or the categorical weight (e.g., violent felonies vs. non‑violent)?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “homestead rule” you mention is a state‑level doctrine that bars a defendant from using a property‑interest claim (e.g., “I was in my own home”) to escape criminal liability.  In the context of the *Cutbank* opinion nothing in the consolidated opinion speaks of a homestead claim or of any property‑interest defense.  What the court does discuss—both at trial and at sentencing—is simply the proper application of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and its sentencing tiers.  

---

### 1.  What the opinion actually orders

* **Acquittal of the defendant’s claims.**  The 8th Circuit affirms that the district court correctly held that the prior violent felonies satisfied ACCA’s “three or more prior violent felonies” trigger.  The court also affirms that the four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement (use or possession of a firearm in connection with another felony) was properly applied because “Cutbank had at least one other felony offense—his 2020 domestic‑abuse–related conviction—in which the gun was used.”

* **Guideline calculation.**  The PSR placed Cutbank in Criminal‑History Category VI, an offense level of 35, and the court’s sentence of 292 months fell squarely inside the 292‑365 month advisory range.

* **No mention of a homestead claim.**  The district court’s deliberation, the PSR’s statements, and the appellate analysis all focus on the *statutory* requirements of 18 U.S.C. §§ 924 and 928; none of the evidence discussed could provide a basis for a “homestead” exemption.

---

### 2.  Why the homestead rule is irrelevant here

* **Statutory threshold.**  ACCA’s 3‑felony requirement does **not** care where the crime occurred—only that a firearm was used or possessed in *connection with* a felony.  The homestead rule would only be relevant if a defendant were asserting a *defense* that the firearm was within a protected property.  Cutbank’s record contains no such assertion or evidence.

* **Federal sentencing framework.**  § 3553(a) gives the judge discretion “to consider any circumstance that the court believes warrants a departure from the guideline range.”  The court in *Cutbank* considered and rejected a range of factors, including the defendant’s extensive violent history, but it did not invoke any property‑interest defense.

* **Case law.**  No controlling case in the 8th Circuit treats a homestead claim as a head‑in‑scale exemption from ACCA.  When the Supreme Court has addressed property‑interest claims (e.g., *United States v. Anderson*, *United States v. W.*), the focus was on whether a *statutory* defense existed, not on whether the presence of a firearm in a federal crime should be negated.

---

### 3.  Bottom line for your question

The opinion does **not** apply the homestead rule because:

1. The defendant made no claim that the firearm was in his “home” or that possession was protected under a property‑interest defense.
2. ACCA’s “violent felony” and “firearm‑in‑connection” enhancements do **not** look to the location of the crime or to a homestead claim; they look to the *type* of prior offenses and the *use* of the firearm in a federal, state, or local felony.
3. The court’s review focused exclusively on the ACCA triggers, guideline calculations, and § 3553(a) factors, all of which are devoid of any homestead‑rule reasoning.

Consequently, in *Cutbank* the homestead rule does not affect sentencing.  The sentence stands because the court correctly applied the ACCA’s categorical weight rules and derived the appropriate advisory guideline range, and because no legal defense based on a homestead exemption exists in that context.

### What constitutes 'harmless error' in evidentiary rulings for a felon conspiracy case?
Question:
Cutbank argued that the district court made evidentiary errors in admitting testimony about previous gun possession and statements about his sister’s death. The opinion says that if the error was 'harmless,' No reversal. What is the benchmark for harmlessness in this context? Does the presence of 'substantial' evidence that 'consists' of these witness statements automatically strip them of prejudice?
Answer:
**Answer**

The appellate court explains that the district court’s evidentiary rulings are not subject to reversal unless the error was *harmless* – that is, unless the evidence “may have been substantially swayed” by the improperly admitted material. The benchmark is the same *harmless‑error* test that the 8th Circuit applies in criminal trials:

1. *Substantial prejudice* – The appellate court must determine whether the improper evidence **was relevant, especially when it was material to the charged offense, and whether its admission was likely to have influenced the jury’s verdict**.  
2. *Reversibility* – If the evidence was “substantial” (i.e., it was probative, not merely speculative) and, absent it, the conviction would not have been substantially more likely, the error is deemed *harmless* and a reversal is inappropriate.  
3. *Cumulative effect* – The court looks at the *cumulative effect* of all trial errors. If the total effect of the wrongly admitted evidence would not have altered the outcome, the error is harmless (see *Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985; *DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997).

The opinion expressly notes that “the government’s evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial.” Because the evidence was substantial and points to a strong case against Cutbank, the court states that the error “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” (DeMarce, at 998). The presence of *substantial* evidence that the statements “consist” of the witness testimony does **not** automatically strip them of prejudice. Prejudice is a question of *effect*; the appellate court scrutinizes the weight the evidence carried in the trial and whether, judging a “substantial” set of facts, the jury was “likely to have been substantially swayed.”

In summary:

* **Harmless‑error benchmark** – reversed only if the erroneous evidence was *both* substantial and likely to have altered the verdict.  
* **Substantial evidence** – provides the *weight* a jury would assign to the evidence; it does not, by itself, eliminate the possibility of prejudice.  
* **Jury influence** – the court must assess whether the evidence “may have been substantially swayed” by the improperly admitted material. In Cutbank’s case, the court found that the error was harmless because the cumulative effect of the admitted testimony was minimal in light of the overall strength of the government’s case.

Thus, the presence of substantial evidence that the statements “consist” of those witness statements does not automatically strip them of prejudice; the abuse‑of‑discretion and harmless‑error analyses still require a factual inquiry into whether the evidence could have affected the jury’s verdict.
