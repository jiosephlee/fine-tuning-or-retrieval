Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What constitutes 'harmless error' in evidentiary rulings for a felon conspiracy case?
Question:
Cutbank argued that the district court made evidentiary errors in admitting testimony about previous gun possession and statements about his sister’s death. The opinion says that if the error was 'harmless,' No reversal. What is the benchmark for harmlessness in this context? Does the presence of 'substantial' evidence that 'consists' of these witness statements automatically strip them of prejudice?
Answer:
**Answer**

The appellate court explains that the district court’s evidentiary rulings are not subject to reversal unless the error was *harmless* – that is, unless the evidence “may have been substantially swayed” by the improperly admitted material. The benchmark is the same *harmless‑error* test that the 8th Circuit applies in criminal trials:

1. *Substantial prejudice* – The appellate court must determine whether the improper evidence **was relevant, especially when it was material to the charged offense, and whether its admission was likely to have influenced the jury’s verdict**.  
2. *Reversibility* – If the evidence was “substantial” (i.e., it was probative, not merely speculative) and, absent it, the conviction would not have been substantially more likely, the error is deemed *harmless* and a reversal is inappropriate.  
3. *Cumulative effect* – The court looks at the *cumulative effect* of all trial errors. If the total effect of the wrongly admitted evidence would not have altered the outcome, the error is harmless (see *Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985; *DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997).

The opinion expressly notes that “the government’s evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial.” Because the evidence was substantial and points to a strong case against Cutbank, the court states that the error “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” (DeMarce, at 998). The presence of *substantial* evidence that the statements “consist” of the witness testimony does **not** automatically strip them of prejudice. Prejudice is a question of *effect*; the appellate court scrutinizes the weight the evidence carried in the trial and whether, judging a “substantial” set of facts, the jury was “likely to have been substantially swayed.”

In summary:

* **Harmless‑error benchmark** – reversed only if the erroneous evidence was *both* substantial and likely to have altered the verdict.  
* **Substantial evidence** – provides the *weight* a jury would assign to the evidence; it does not, by itself, eliminate the possibility of prejudice.  
* **Jury influence** – the court must assess whether the evidence “may have been substantially swayed” by the improperly admitted material. In Cutbank’s case, the court found that the error was harmless because the cumulative effect of the admitted testimony was minimal in light of the overall strength of the government’s case.

Thus, the presence of substantial evidence that the statements “consist” of those witness statements does not automatically strip them of prejudice; the abuse‑of‑discretion and harmless‑error analyses still require a factual inquiry into whether the evidence could have affected the jury’s verdict.
