Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Did the court’s failure to give a limiting instruction on D.F.’s police statements violate the Confrontation Clause?
Question:
Cutbank asked the jury to be instructed that it could not consider D.F.’s statements to the police until she testified. The court said it didn’t admit hearsay and the Confrontation Clause was satisfied because D.F. was present. I’m trying to understand: is the court required to give a limiting instruction for every statement that could be considered substantive evidence, or can it rely on a closing instruction? Under United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, how does this rule apply to a situation where a defendant challenges the admissibility of the defendant’s own (the reporter’s) statements as evidence?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court was correct not to give a separate limiting instruction on D.F.’s police statements.  The Sixth‑Amendment Confrontation Clause is satisfied as long as the declarants personally testify in court, even if their statements were admissible as substantive evidence.  The court is not compelled to give a “limiting instruction” for every piece of evidence that might be deemed substantive; an appropriate closing instruction suffices when the evidence is already admissible and the declarants are present.

---

### 1.  The rule in *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887 (8th Cir. 2014)

In *Bennett* the court held that:

> “The district court may not be required to give a limiting instruction on each item of evidence at the time it is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.”  *Id.* at 899, 901 (emphasis added).

The rule applies whenever the evidentiary issue is already decided—either by a previous ruling that the evidence is admissible or by the fact that the declarant is in court.  The crucial factors are:

1. **Admissibility has already been established** – the district court has ruled that the evidence is not hearsay or otherwise excluded.  
2. **The declarants are present for cross‑examination** – the defendant or witnesses have the opportunity to challenge the statements at trial.  
3. **An adequate closing instruction is issued** – the court explains the overall scope and purpose of the evidence in a single instruction after the entire trial.

The Sixth Amendment does not require a separate “limiting instruction” before each piece of evidence; it requires that the jury be able to weigh the admissible evidence against the defendant’s right to confront and cross‑examine versus the purpose of the evidence.  *Bennett* reconciles the two requirements by allowing a closing instruction when the other conditions are met.

---

### 2.  Application to D.F.’s police statements

*Cutbank* raised an objection that the jury should not be allowed to consider D.F.’s statements to police until D.F. herself testified.  The district court held:

> “In addition, D.F. testified. The Confrontation Clause is satisfied **when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person**.”  *Cutbank* 2‑B‑(2)‑(b), 775 F.3d at 889 (quoting *Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861).

The statements were admissible (the court rejected any hearsay claim), and D.F. was in court and cross‑examined on them.  Because the admissibility issue was resolved and the declarants were present, *Cutbank* relied on *Bennett*’s principle:

> “The court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.”  *Id.* at 899.

The court gave a closing instruction that included the scope of corroborating evidence and the limits on using D.F.’s statements, fulfilling the Sixth‑Amendment requirement.  No separate limiting instruction was necessary.

---

### 3.  Why the Confrontation Clause was not violated

The Confrontation Clause requires that the defendant have an opportunity to cross‑examine the declarant *in person*, and that the declarant’s statements not be excluded merely because the defendant has not had that opportunity.  Since D.F. testified, the jury could scrutinize the statements directly.  The court’s closing instruction clarified the admissible role of those statements (e.g., to establish the context of police actions) and therefore did not allow the jury to treat them as evidence of Cutbank’s conduct without the requisite confrontation.  

In short, *Cutbank* correctly followed *Bennett*: the admissibility of D.F.’s police statements was already decided, the declarant was present for cross‑examination, and an appropriate closing instruction was given.  The Confrontation Clause was satisfied; a limiting instruction at the time of admissibility was not required.
