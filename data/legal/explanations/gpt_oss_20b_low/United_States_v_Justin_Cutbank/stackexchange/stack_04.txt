Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What is the standard for considering a sentence ‘substantially unreasonable’ under § 3553(a) when the district court imposes a guideline‑range term?
Question:
The opinion says 'the district court must first ensure it correctly calculated the guidelines and then considers § 3553(a) factors.' If the sentence is within the guidelines range, is it automatically reasonable, or can appellate courts find it ‘substantially unreasonable’ if § 3553(a) factors suggest a different outcome? How did the 8th Circuit apply this standard in recent cases such as Masood or Maluoth?
Answer:
**Answer**

The 8th Circuit treats a sentence that falls inside the advisory guidelines range as presumptively reasonable, but it is *not* automatically immune from appellate review.  The court requires a two‑step analysis:

1. **Proper calculation of the range.**  
   If the district court followed the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines to compute the appropriate range and made no significant procedural error in doing so, the range is considered “correct” for appellate purposes.  (See *Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th at 793; *Rosales‑Mireles*, 585 U.S. at 143‑44.)

2. **Substantive reasonableness under § 3553(a).**  
   Even within the range, the sentence can be found *substantially unreasonable* if the district court failed to give proper weight to or even considered the § 3553(a) factors.  The appellate court will look for evidence that the district court “actually considered” the factors and that its conclusion was not “arbitrary or capricious” (see *Masood*, 133 F.4th at 809–10; *Maluoth*, 121 F.4th at 1164).

**How the 8th Circuit applied this standard**

* **Masood (2025).**  
  The court confirmed that a within‑range sentence is “presumptively reasonable” but reiterated that the appellate court must first establish that the district court correctly calculated the range.  It then held that the district court need only show that the § 3553(a) factors were considered, not that the court provided a detailed mechanical listing.  Because the record showed the district court had weighed the factors, *Masood* affirmed the sentence.

* **Maluoth (2024).**  
  The court applied the same two‑step test.  It noted that the district court’s sentencing memorandum contained a full discussion of the factors—particularly the defendant’s extensive violent history and the aggravating circumstances of the offense.  Since the judge had “actually considered” the § 3553(a) items, the court affirmed the sentence, even though the judge had imposed a terms that some might view as harsh.

In both cases, the appellate judges were unwilling to overturn a sentence simply because it was at the low end of the range or because it seemed appalling.  The key is whether the district court, after correctly calculating the range, gave the § 3553(a) factors “its full weight” or at least considered them substantively.

**Bottom line for practitioners**

- An inside‑range sentence is *presumptively* reasonable, but the appellate court will examine whether the district court properly considered § 3553(a).  
- If the record shows no such consideration—or if the court’s conclusion was so out of step with the circumstances that it could not be supported by the factors—an appellate court can declare the sentence “substantially unreasonable” and impose a different term.  
- The recent *Masood* and *Maluoth* decisions confirm that the 8th Circuit applies the two‑step test and generally affirms within‑range sentences that show evidence the judge weighed the § 3553(a) factors.  

Therefore, while an appraisal that a sentence is “within the guidelines” does give the district court the benefit of the doubt, appellate judges will still scrutinize the judgment for *substantive* reasonableness under § 3553(a).
