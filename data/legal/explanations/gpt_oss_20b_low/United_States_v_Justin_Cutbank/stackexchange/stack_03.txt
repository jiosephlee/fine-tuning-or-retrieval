Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why can the district court include the 4‑level ‘possessed in connection with another felony offense’ enhancement at sentencing?
Question:
The defendant argues that the PSR’s application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) – the 4‑level increase for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense – was incorrect. The court says the enhancement applies regardless of a charge or conviction for the other felony. I don’t understand how the guidelines treat the term ‘connection’ and why a factual statement about another felony that wasn’t charged is sufficient to trigger the penalty. Also, how does the court decide whether the enhancement is ‘clear and unambiguous’?
Answer:
**Answer**

The district court was correct to apply USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).  The enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (comment n.14(C)).  Consequently, the mere fact—recorded in the PSR—that the firearm was used in connection with another felony offense triggers the four‑level increase, even if that other offense was not criminally charged or resulted in a conviction in this case.

---

### 1.  The “connection” standard

In United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929‑30 (8th Cir. 2013) the court reiterated that § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) activates “when a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense.”  The guidance itself states that “the enhancement applies … regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.”  The court in Cutbank therefore does not need to pin the enhancement to a specifically charged or adjudicated felony; it only needs factual evidence that the defendant’s firearm was used in another felony context.

The PSR contains such factual statements.  Cutbank’s prior felony convictions (e.g., domestic violence, kidnapping, assault, and felony resisting arrest) are well documented in the PSR.  By noting that the sawed‑off rifle “was used in connection with another felony offense,” the PSR satisfies the statutory requirement for the 4‑level increase.  The district court simply accepted those facts, a routine application of the guidelines that “does not render a federal judge a sentencing expert” (see *United States v. Klock, 117 F.4th 235, 242 (10th Cir. 2023)* for a discussion of guideline authority).  The court didn’t need to find a *specific* charge; the evidence of prior felony activity is sufficient.

---

### 2.  Clear and unambiguous application

The question of whether an enhancement is “clear and unambiguous” is an *abuse‑of‑discretion* inquiry.  The appellate court in Cutbank treated it as a factual determination: “the district court… adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Thus, the court did not err in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.”  The court did not say the enhancement was vague or ambiguous; it merely confirmed that the facts were clear enough to trigger the statutory increase.  Because the statutory language is unambiguous and the PSR provides the requisite factual basis, the district court’s application cannot be set aside on a *clear and unambiguous* ground.

---

### Bottom line

Cutbank’s challenge fails because:

1. **Statutory language** demands the enhancement whenever a firearm is used in any felony context, period.  
2. **Guidelines comment n.14(C)** explicitly removes the need for an *adjudicated* other felony.  
3. The **PSR contains factual allegations** of prior felonies that satisfy the “connection” requirement.  
4. The **court exercised its standard discretionary authority** in adopting those facts, and the application is neither *obviously wrong* nor *vague*.

For a law student, the takeaway is that when the PSR identifies prior felony activity that involved firearm use, the four‑level “connection” enhancement automatically applies.  No additional charge, trial, or conviction is required, and the district court’s finding is presumptively correct unless the record clearly shows the facts were misidentified or the law was misinterpreted.
