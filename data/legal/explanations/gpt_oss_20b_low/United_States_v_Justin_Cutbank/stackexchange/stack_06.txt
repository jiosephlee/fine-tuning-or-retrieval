Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the 'homestead rule' affect sentencing under ACGA when the defendant is a felon in possession of a firearm?
Question:
I read that a defendant's previous violent felony convictions trigger ACGA maximum penalties, but I'm unsure how the court determined the Criminal History Category and level. The PSR lists at least 12 prior convictions. How does the court decide whether those convictions count toward the ACGA maximum or the categorical weight (e.g., violent felonies vs. non‑violent)?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “homestead rule” you mention is a state‑level doctrine that bars a defendant from using a property‑interest claim (e.g., “I was in my own home”) to escape criminal liability.  In the context of the *Cutbank* opinion nothing in the consolidated opinion speaks of a homestead claim or of any property‑interest defense.  What the court does discuss—both at trial and at sentencing—is simply the proper application of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and its sentencing tiers.  

---

### 1.  What the opinion actually orders

* **Acquittal of the defendant’s claims.**  The 8th Circuit affirms that the district court correctly held that the prior violent felonies satisfied ACCA’s “three or more prior violent felonies” trigger.  The court also affirms that the four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement (use or possession of a firearm in connection with another felony) was properly applied because “Cutbank had at least one other felony offense—his 2020 domestic‑abuse–related conviction—in which the gun was used.”

* **Guideline calculation.**  The PSR placed Cutbank in Criminal‑History Category VI, an offense level of 35, and the court’s sentence of 292 months fell squarely inside the 292‑365 month advisory range.

* **No mention of a homestead claim.**  The district court’s deliberation, the PSR’s statements, and the appellate analysis all focus on the *statutory* requirements of 18 U.S.C. §§ 924 and 928; none of the evidence discussed could provide a basis for a “homestead” exemption.

---

### 2.  Why the homestead rule is irrelevant here

* **Statutory threshold.**  ACCA’s 3‑felony requirement does **not** care where the crime occurred—only that a firearm was used or possessed in *connection with* a felony.  The homestead rule would only be relevant if a defendant were asserting a *defense* that the firearm was within a protected property.  Cutbank’s record contains no such assertion or evidence.

* **Federal sentencing framework.**  § 3553(a) gives the judge discretion “to consider any circumstance that the court believes warrants a departure from the guideline range.”  The court in *Cutbank* considered and rejected a range of factors, including the defendant’s extensive violent history, but it did not invoke any property‑interest defense.

* **Case law.**  No controlling case in the 8th Circuit treats a homestead claim as a head‑in‑scale exemption from ACCA.  When the Supreme Court has addressed property‑interest claims (e.g., *United States v. Anderson*, *United States v. W.*), the focus was on whether a *statutory* defense existed, not on whether the presence of a firearm in a federal crime should be negated.

---

### 3.  Bottom line for your question

The opinion does **not** apply the homestead rule because:

1. The defendant made no claim that the firearm was in his “home” or that possession was protected under a property‑interest defense.
2. ACCA’s “violent felony” and “firearm‑in‑connection” enhancements do **not** look to the location of the crime or to a homestead claim; they look to the *type* of prior offenses and the *use* of the firearm in a federal, state, or local felony.
3. The court’s review focused exclusively on the ACCA triggers, guideline calculations, and § 3553(a) factors, all of which are devoid of any homestead‑rule reasoning.

Consequently, in *Cutbank* the homestead rule does not affect sentencing.  The sentence stands because the court correctly applied the ACCA’s categorical weight rules and derived the appropriate advisory guideline range, and because no legal defense based on a homestead exemption exists in that context.
