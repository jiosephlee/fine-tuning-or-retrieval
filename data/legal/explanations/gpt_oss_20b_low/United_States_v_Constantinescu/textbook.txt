Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# I. Case Background and Procedural History

## Profiles of the defendants and their social‑media presence

United States v. Constantinescu involves eight individuals—Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight—who collectively cultivate large followings across multiple platforms such as YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, and Twitter. The defendants present themselves as seasoned stock traders, regularly livestreaming trades, offering “investment tips,” and claiming proprietary “inside” information. Their online personas create a perceived authority that attracts thousands of followers who view their posts as credible investment guidance.

These self‑proclaimed expertise and the voluminous reach of their channels are central to the indictment. The government alleges that the defendants leveraged their platforms not to inform but to manipulate, using their influence to shape market sentiment toward specific securities. The prosecution’s narrative frames the defendants’ social‑media activity as the vehicle through which the alleged scheme was executed.

## Summary of the charge allegations (conspiracy and securities‑fraud counts)

The indictment charges the defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and multiple counts of securities fraud under §§ 1348 and 2, totaling 27 counts. The alleged scheme, described in ¶s 13–14, follows a classic “pump‑and‑dump” pattern:

1. The defendants purchase a security.
2. They “pump” the price by posting false or misleading statements about the security’s prospects, thereby encouraging other investors—particularly their followers—to buy the same security.
3. Once the price rises, they “dump” by secretly selling the security for a profit.

The indictment quantifies the scheme’s financial impact, alleging that the defendants collectively profited an estimated $114 million. Daniel Knight pleaded guilty, while the others pursued dismissal motions.

## District court dismissal reasoning (failure to state an offense)

The U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Texas dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it “failed to state an offense.” The court’s analysis hinged on a two‑part premise:

1. For a securities‑fraud indictment to be sufficient, it must allege that the defendants *sought to deprive victims of a traditional property interest*—here, the “potentially valuable economic information” vital to making discretionary investment decisions.
2. The indictment, the court argued, merely alleged that the defendants intended to deny followers such information, *not* that they intended to deprive them of money or property.

Thus, the court held that without a claim that the defendants intended to cause economic loss or fraudulently induce the purchase of securities, the indictment failed to meet the statutory requirement of alleging a “scheme to defraud any person” and an accompanying intent to defraud.

## Appeal and court’s mandate to review sufficiency de novo

The defendants appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. The appellate court’s mandate, grounded in 60 F.4th 982 (Rafoi) and 665 F.3d 640 (Fontenot), is to review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo. The Fifth Circuit must treat the allegations in the indictment as true and determine whether the indictment states a charge that satisfies each element of the offense. This review is limited to the indicting document itself; it does not involve reconsideration of factual findings from the trial court beyond the indictment’s claims.

The Fifth Circuit examined the indictment’s allegations against the statutory framework of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory of fraud established in recent Supreme Court precedent. In doing so, it concluded that the indictment does, in fact, allege a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, thereby reviving the criminal case against the defendants.

# II. Sufficiency of Indictments: Legal Standards and Prior Fifth Circuit Guidance

The Fifth Circuit’s approach to the sufficiency of an indictment is to review the indictment “de novo,” treating the facts alleged as true and determining whether the indictment properly states an offense. Section II of *United States v. Constantinescu* anchors this framework in the authority of *United States v. Rafoi*, *United States v. Fontenot*, *United States v. Gaytan*, and *United States v. Guzman-Ocampo*. These precedents explain the material elements that must be present in a pleading to satisfy the constitutional guarantee of indictment and to protect the defendant’s double‑jeopardy interests. The court also interprets how those principles apply to securities‑fraud indictments, which often draw upon analogies to mail and wire fraud. The following sections unpack each component of the circuit’s guidance.

## De novo Review Requirement Per United States v. Rafoi

In *United States v. Rafoi* (60 F.4th 982, 993), the Fifth Circuit reaffirmed the de novo standard for appellate review of indictments. An indictment is considered at first instance as factually alleged; the court must therefore assume the alleged facts to be true and, based on those facts, decide whether an offense has actually been stated. This interpretation aligns with the Seventh Amendment’s assurance that only a grand jury may formally charge a defendant, and it frames the appellate court’s role as a “fact‑checking” exercise rather than a “re‑judging” one. *Rafoi* emphasizes that under this standard, an indictment may be allowed to proceed even if it turns out that the factual allegations were erroneous, provided that the allegations as written satisfy the requirements of sufficiency.

## Four Desiderata for a Sufficient Indictment

The Fifth Circuit has distilled three core requirements into a broader, nearly universal framework that the indictment must satisfy. These criteria ensure that the state’s demands on the defendant are both clear and constitutionally acceptable.

1. **Enumeration of Each Prima‑Facie Element**  
   The indictment must state each material element of the offense that the prosecution seeks to establish. This means that every element required for conviction under the statutory scheme must be present in the factual allegations. A failure to include one element renders the indictment insufficient because the defendant cannot be put on notice of the specific conduct that, if proven, would result in a conviction. The duty here is purely factual: the indictment must only assert the elements; it need not prove them.

2. **Fair Notice to the Defendant**  
   The assault of the indictment must contain enough detail that a defendant can understand what conduct is criminal and prepare an adequate defense. This requirement protects the defendant’s right to due process, as the Fifth Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that a vague indictment violates the defendant’s constitutional rights and must be dismissed or corrected. The council’s briefing notes or the indictment’s language should be unambiguous enough to inform the defendant of their alleged wrongdoing.

3. **Double‑Jeopardy Protection**  
   The indictment must prevent the defendant from being prosecuted multiple times for the same conduct. This protection requires that a subsequent prosecution cannot re‑charge the defendant based on the same facts charged in the indictment, unless the subsequent charge is a different offense or introduces new evidence. The Fifth Circuit has taken care to delineate that the indictment’s language must not be broad to the extent that it could be construed as covering more conduct than is actually alleged. The requirement is layered in a way that confirms that the indictment does not unnecessarily bar legitimate subsequent prosecutions that involve distinct conduct.

4. **Effectiveness in Preventing Double Jeopardy** *(added in later opinions)*  
   The circulating standard often lists “enumeration, notice, and double‑jeopardy protection” as three discrete elements. A fourth element, “effectiveness in preventing double jeopardy,” is implicit: the indictment must not allow the state to later pack additional conduct into the initial charge that would create a second offense. While not always expressly named, this element is inherent in the “double‑jeopardy protection” clause.

These criteria coincide with the precedential recommendations in *United States v. Gaytan* (74 F.3d 545), which expounded on the constitutional limits of an indictment, and *United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo* (236 F.3d 233). The Fifth Circuit mandates that indictments become the launching pad for formal criminal procedure; if they fail to meet these standards, the defendant cannot be prosecuted on those grounds without violating due process.

## Application to Securities Fraud – Praςmatism of Mail and Wire‑Fraud Analogies

The Sixth and Seventh Circuits have offered a richer body of jurisprudence regarding the application of these principles to securities‑fraud indictments. The Fifth Circuit often latches onto the mail- and wire‑fraud analogies as a conceptual scaffolding for securities fraud reasoning. In *United States v. Greenlaw* (84 F.4th 325), the court explained that § 1348 of the Securities Fraud Act “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and that courts have been willing to apply those concepts. The reach of “material misrepresentation” and “intent to defraud” in securities fraud is thereby anchored to those statutes, making the “façade” of mail and wire fraud essential in evaluating the sufficiency of a securities‑fraud indictment. The Fifth Circuit therefore assesses whether the indictment includes the “scheme to defraud” and the requisite intent to defraud, just as it does for mail and wire fraud. This alignment helps maintain a coherent statutory interpretation across related fraud statutes.

The requirement to enumerate the material elements translates to securities fraud in the same way it does for other crimes: the indictment must convey that the defendant, in concert with co‑conspirators, fabricated a false premise that deceived investors for the defendant’s benefit. Articulating an alleged false statement or misleading representation, and that the defendant “doubly” intended to deceive, meets the “pragmatic” standard. The fourth element—“double‑jeopardy protection”—ensures that the indictment specifically delineates which securities were allegedly involved so that the defendant is not obscured by a wide net that might encompass unrelated securities or unrelated speculative acts.

## Case Law: United States v. Fontenot, Gaytan, Guzman‑Ocampo

### United States v. Fontenot (665 F.3d 640)

In *Fontenot*, the Fifth Circuit reiterated that an indictment must state each material element, underscoring that the prosecution cannot rely on mere discretionary allegations (“the circumstantial existence of a crime”) to prove a criminal act. The court stressed that the sufficiency of an indictment can fail if any element is absent or if the alleged conduct lacks factual support to satisfy the statutory definition. The precedent has been cited in numerous subsequent cases, including *Constantinescu*, to affirm the de novo approach and the element enumeration requirement.

### United States v. Gaytan (74 F.3d 545)

The *Gaytan* decision clarified that the use of ambiguous or overly broad indictments defers to the defendant’s lack of notice. The court provided a checklist: enumeration, notice, and double‑jeopardy protection. *Gaytan* also illustrated that it is a judicial error for an indictment to allege conduct that the indictment’s language does not specifically charge the defendant with. This has direct relevance to securities‑fraud cases, as the precise language of the indictment determines whether the alleged scheme and intent can be treated as “material” under § 1348.

### United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo (236 F.3d 233)

In *Guzman‑Ocampo*, the Fifth Circuit held that a “sufficient indictment” must provide “material facts that place the defendant in a position to raise an affirmative defense." In other words, the indictment must provide enough details to allow the defendant to challenge the content of the indictment meaningfully. The court explained that denying a defendant that knowledge of the alleged wrongdoing can be considered an unfair “surprise,” thereby violating due process. An indictment that merely states that the defendant “engaged in wrongdoing” but fails to detail the specific fraudulent scheme or misrepresentation fails under this principle. The same logic applies to securities fraud indictments: the alleged scheme to defraud, the misrepresentations made, and the perceived economic interests of the victims must be set forth.

All three cases, through minor variations, converge on the same three pillars: factual enumeration, clear notice, and double‑jeopardy protection, with a fourth unanimous principle—“effectiveness in preventing double jeopardy.” The Fifth Circuit’s treatment of securities‑fraud indictments in *Constantinescu* reaffirms this framework, illustrating how the court applies the precedent to a concrete case involving alleged “pump and dump” schemes.

---

In sum, the Fifth Circuit’s sufficiency standard for indictments requires a litigant to satisfy a compact of element enumeration, clear notice, and double‑jeopardy protection; a securities‑fraud indictment must mirror these requisites by alleging a scheme to defraud, an intent to deceive, and the deprivation of a protected property interest (money or property). The court’s reliance on mail and wire fraud analogies, alongside the controlling decisions highlighted above, provides a robust scaffold for appellate courts to assess whether an indictment offers adequate legal and factual grounding for the state’s criminal charges.

# III. The Statutory Framework for Securities Fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1348 & § 1349)

The Fifth Circuit’s review in *United States v. Constantinescu* begins by dissecting the statutory text that underpins all federal securities‑fraud prosecutions. § 1348 defines the crime of securities fraud, while § 1349 provides the conspiracy element. Although the Houston opinion focuses on *Constantinescu*, the reasoning applies to any case in which the indictment alleges an alleged “pump‑and‑dump” scheme or similar deceptive conduct. The Court’s interpretive method echoes the way the statutes were drafted: by borrowing the well‑tested doctrinal framework of the mail and wire‑fraud statutes.

### Textual foundation

18 U.S.C. § 1348 (as amended) reads:

> “Any person who, by means of a scheme to defraud… in connection with… any security, shall be punished…”

The statute alternately employs the phrase “obtains, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, any money or property… in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.” The essential language is the verb *defraud* and the qualifying “scheme.” The statutory structure does not elaborate on what constitutes a *scheme* or what *intent* is required; instead it relies on a body of judicial interpretation.

18 U.S.C. § 1349 makes it a crime to conspire to commit the offenses enumerated in § 1348. The conspiracy element is, therefore, the same as for the underlying corruption scheme: it requires an agreement and an overt act that furthers the conspiratorial intent.

### The mail‑and‑wire‑fraud heritage

Because Congress drafted § 1348 in an era when mail and wire fraud were already established constitutional crimes, the statutes reflect the same definitional elements that courts have applied to those crimes for decades. The Fifth Circuit is consistent with prior holdings that treat *defraud* as “a deception used to obtain money or property” and that the “scheme” component is similar to that in *United States v. Greenlaw* (84 F.4th 325). The Court cites mail‑fraud cases (such as *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d 692) to show that the underlying fraud elements are not novel but inherited from earlier statutes. In particular, the Court notes that the mail‑fraud doctrine of “depriving the victim of a protected property interest” has a literal counterpart in the securities‑fraud statute, a point that will later justify the Court’s rejection of one of the defendants’ dismissals.

### Two essential elements

The jurists recognized that the statute is implicit in its affirmative language. Analyzing the text, the Court explains that a securities‑fraud prosecution requires:

1. **A scheme to defraud** – a plan that involves material misrepresentation with the purpose of deceiving a victim. The Supreme Court has summed up this element in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023): “To be convicted of fraud, the defendant must have engaged in deception, and the object of that deception must be the victim’s money or property.” The “scheme” element has also been discussed in *United States v. Greenlaw*, where the Court said the offense allegedly involves “a scheme to defraud… in connection with any security.”

2. **Intent to defraud** – the defendant must intend both to deceive and to obtain money or property. The Court refers to *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025) for the rationale that intent is satisfied simply by designing the deception to secure a benefit. Even if the victim does not suffer a pecuniary loss, the skull‑shaped requirement – the defendant must want to obtain the victim’s money or property – is enough. *Kousisis* clarifies that fraud can be concluded even when the victim does not end up worse off, as long as the defendant’s scheme was aimed at capturing the victim’s money or property.

*The Constantinescu* opinion brings these elements back into play when it critiques the defendants’ own assertions that the indictment merely promised them “valuable economic information” rather than a protected property interest. By noting that *Ciminelli* rejected the “right‑to‑control” theory but that the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory remains, the Fourth Circuit distinguishes between two ways a victim can be deprived:

* In the “right‑to‑control” theory, the victim’s property interest is the knowledge that would allow them to decide how to use their assets, which *Ciminelli* rejected.
* In the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory, the victim’s property interest is the actual money or securities they part with, which is protected under § 1348 and validated by *Kousisis*.

### Application in *Constantinescu*

The indictment alleges that the defendants “purchased a security, pumped the price of that security by posting false and misleading information… and dumped the security for profit.” These acts satisfy the *“scheme to defraud”* requirement because they involve deliberate deception (misrepresentation of trading positions and future price movements) that induces followers to buy the security. The Court, following *Greenlaw* and *Evans*, requires the article to describe a *material* misrepresentation, which is indeed suggested by the indictment when it says the social‑media posts were “false and misleading.”

The *intent to defraud* is inferred from the purpose to “obtain” securities or money. The indictment’s language explicitly states that the defendants wanted to “profit” from the scheme. *Kousisis* teaches that the focus is on the defendant’s objective to acquire the victim's money or property, not on whether the victim suffers loss. Thus, the indictment’s claim that the defendants “pumped and dumped to gain a profit” satisfies this intent requirement.

### Broader context and relevance

By tying the statutes’ interpretation to mail‑ and wire‑fraud doctrine, the Fifth Circuit highlights how the securities‑fraud law remains tightly linked to fundamental fraud principles. This alignment ensures that prosecutions remain grounded in well‑tested procedural and substantive standards. For law students, the takeaway is that the statutory language of § 1348 and § 1349 is sparse, but the courts have consistently defined its core elements through analogies to earlier fraud statutes. When reading an indictment, courts will look for a *scheme*—material deception that aims to procure money or property—and an *intent*—the desire to obtain that money or property, regardless of whether the victim ultimately loses money. Thus, the statutes demand more than mere prophecy or speculation; they demand a calculated plot that deceives investors into handing over their funds.

# IV. Application of Fraud Theories: Right‑to‑Control vs. Fraudulent‑Inducement

## Linking the indictment’s allegations of inducement to a protected property interest

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* centers on whether the indictment adequately alleges the elements of securities fraud under the two competing fraud theories identified by the Supreme Court: the right‑to‑control theory and the fraudulent‑inducement theory. For a case to be convicted of fraud, the threat must be to a *protected property interest*—traditionally, money or tangible property, or an interest in such property. The right‑to‑control theory focuses on the victim’s right to direct the disposition of their assets; the fraudulent‑inducement theory focuses on the victim’s actual transfer of money or property to the defendant through deception.

### 1. The right‑to‑control theory in *Ciminelli*

In *Ciminelli v. United States*, the Supreme Court rejected a prosecution that sought to prove that the defendant had *deprived* a construction company of its *potential* right to control a state‑funded contract. The government relied on a “right‑to‑control” rationale: depriving a victim of a *right‑to‑control* a tangible asset (the contract) could constitute fraud. The Court held that such a right is not a *traditional property interest* protected under the federal fraud statutes. Accordingly, the right‑to‑control theory could not support a fraud conviction. The Court made clear that a victim’s *information* or *potential* for control does not satisfy the requirement that the defendant obtain *money or property* from the victim. In *Ciminelli*, the indictment relied exclusively on the victim’s potential to decide how to direct its contract, not on the actual transfer of money or property.

### 2. The fraudulent‑inducement theory in *Kousisis*

The Supreme Court later clarified the fraudulent‑inducement theory in *Kousisis v. United States*. Here, the defendant lied to obtain government contracts and then used a subcontractor to perform the required disadvantaged‑business requirement. The fraud was grounded in the defendant’s *material misrepresentation* that induced the state to award contracts—*contracts that required the transfer of money* (the contract price) to the defendant’s company. The Court emphasized that fraud under this theory does not require the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss; the critical element is that the defendant *obtained the victim’s money or property* through deception.

*Kousisis* thus upheld a theory of fraud that rests on the *actual* transfer of money or property, not merely on an abstract or potential deprivation of control. The defendant’s intent to profit—to obtain the money or property—served as the motivating factor, even if the victim’s economic position after the fraud was unchanged or improved.

### 3. How the indictment in *Constantinescu* aligns with the fraudulent‑inducement theory

The indictment alleges that the defendants, each with massive social‑media followings, “purchased a security, pumped the price... by posting false and misleading information about the security… so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” and subsequently “dumped” the security for profit. The essence of the allegation is that followers were *deceived into buying securities*—an obligation that requires them to *transfer money* (or, alternatively, to part with their market‑value securities if they hold them). The indictment explicitly claims that the defendants “were induced to purchase the security” through misleading posts, and that they profited from the resulting price inflation.

Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the defendants’ *material misrepresentations* (the false claims about the security’s performance and potential) were *used to induce followers to part with money* to buy the security. The alleged act satisfies the two critical components identified by the Supreme Court:

1. **A material misrepresentation intended to deceive** – the prompt that followers believe the security is undervalued or guaranteed to rise, when in fact the defendants lack credible evidence and are simply inflating the price.
2. **Obtaining the victim’s money or property** – the followers’ purchases represent the transfer of money into the defendants’ accounts or the transfer of securities that amalgamate into the money they later sell at a higher price.

The indictment therefore alleges a *protected property interest*: the followers’ money (or the securities they bought). By focusing on the *receipt of money or property* by the defendants, the indictment falls squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement framework and does not rely on a right‑to‑control rationale. The Supreme Court’s rejection of a pure right‑to‑control argument in *Ciminelli* does not apply because the indictment does not claim that followers were deprived of their *right to control* the securities; it claims they were *deprived of their money or property* through deception.

### 4. The necessity of a protected property interest

A fundamental requirement for a fraud conviction is that the victim’s property interest be *protected* by the statute. Under *Ciminelli*, a non‑traditional property interest—such as a right to control—is insufficient. In contrast, *Kousisis* demonstrates that the Supreme Court is willing to accept a broader range of property interests, provided that the transaction involves an actual transfer of money or property. The *Constantinescu* indictment’s focus on followers' purchases achieves this requirement because it speaks to the *transfer* of money into the defendants’ possession—exactly the protected property interest required by the fraud statutes. The indictment’s claim that the defendants “pumped” the price and “dumped” the security further reinforces that the defendants’ objective was to *obtain* money from followers through deceit.

### 5. Conclusion

By aligning its allegations with the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the *Constantinescu* indictment properly alleges a scheme to defraud: it contends that the defendants communicated false information to induce followers to part with money (or securities) that the defendants then profited from. The indictment does not rely on the right‑to‑control theory that was rejected in *Ciminelli*; instead, it anchors its argument in the actual transfer of a protected property interest—money or securities—to the defendants. Consequently, the indictment satisfies the statutory requirement for a fraud claim and warrants a finding of sufficiency, as the Fifth Circuit ultimately held.

# V. Alleged Intent to Defraud: Profit Motive vs. Economic Loss

## Conclusion that intent to defraud is adequately alleged  

In the Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the district court had dismissed the indictment for failure to allege a “scheme to defraud” or an “intent to defraud.” The defendants relied on a traditional reading of the federal fraud statutes, arguing that the indictment only asserted a desire to magnify their followers’ potential economic information, not to deprive them of money or property. The appellate court rejected that line of reasoning, holding that the indictment sufficiently alleged both the scheme and the intent, not on the basis of an actual loss to the investor, but on the prosecution’s demonstration that the defendants used material misrepresentations to obtain money from their followers.

### The fraud statutes and the intent to defraud element  

Section 1348 of the federal securities fraud statute, like the mail and wire fraud statutes, requires an element of “intent to defraud.” The crime is completed when a defendant “engaged in deception” with the aims of deceiving and, traditionally, of obtaining money or property from a victim. The Supreme Court’s recent rulings in *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023) and *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) clarified that the statutory intent includes any fraudulent indecent to obtain the victim’s money or property, irrespective of whether the victim incurs a net pecuniary loss.

* In *Ciminelli*, the Court rejected a “right‑to‑control” theory that would allow a fraud conviction solely on the basis of depriving a victim of valuable economic information, because such information is not a protected property interest. The Court reiterated that a fraud conviction must involve the deprivation of money or property, or the use of misrepresentation to obtain such money or property.  
* In *Kousisis*, the Court held that the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory approved by Congress does not require that the victim actually end up impoverished. The defendant can be convicted of fraud even when the defendant receives an item of equal value in return, provided that he used a material misstatement to induce the victim to part with money or property.

These pronouncements directly bear on the plaintiffs’ contention that the indictment’s emphasis on profit rather than loss precludes a finding of intent to defraud, precisely the panic the defendants were hoping to instigate. The court’s decision clarifies that the emphasis on profit is a precise element of the intent, because the defendant is intending to obtain money or property through deception, not to inflict a loss.

### How the indictment satisfies the intent element  

The indictment’s allegations speak directly to the required elements of intent:

1. **Intent to defraud** is alleged by stating that the defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” This statement contains the intent to mislead; the language is purposefully prospective, indicating a deliberate plan to deceive.

2. **The target of the deception is the follower’s money or property.** The indictment reiterates that the defendants “purchased a security, pumped the price, and then dumped the security after secretally selling in order to realize a profit.” The scheme to “pump” relies upon inducing other investors to *buy* a security that the defendants do not own, thereby obtaining other investors’ money in exchange for a securities position that they do not possess. That is a direct investment in money or property: the follower’s capital.

3. **The defendants’ object was to obtain money.”** The indictment spells out that the defendants “profited $114 million from their scheme.” The intent element is satisfied because the defendants’ illustrative pursuit of monetary gain is an objective common to all federal fraud statutes.

Because the indictment includes these specific allegations, the appellate court concludes that “the indictment sufficiently alleges an intent to defraud.” The presence of the word “profit” does not place the indictment in conflict with § 1348. Rather, it is evidence that the defendants had the right motive: to obtain money or property and not to merely deprive their followers of information.

### Why the “economic loss” argument dissolves under Kousisis  

The defendants advanced the argument that the indictment fails to allege an intent to defraud because the victims are not harmed net‑wise by their purchase of the over‑valued security: they receive market‑value securities, so there is no economic loss. That argument hinges on a mistaken reading of the Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* decision. The Court explicitly held that the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory “does not require the victim to suffer an economic loss.” The intent element focuses on *obtaining money or property* through fraudulent means, not on the victim’s outcome. In *Kousisis*, the Court stated that a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether the defendant seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Consequently, the indictment’s description of the scheme satisfies the intent standard because the defendants attempted to obtain the follower’s money from a misrepresentation, even if the follower later realizes a net gain or simply receives equal value in securities.

The appellate court applied *Kousisis* to *Constantinescu* in the following way:

* The indictment alleges a material misstatement by the defendants—“false and misleading information about the security.”  
* The indictment alleges that the misstatement was sent to a specific class of people—followers who would act upon the misstatement.  
* The indictment alleges that the misstatement was intended to persuade the followers to part with money or property—buying the security.  

Therefore, the indictment satisfies the *obtain* element of *Kousisis* and the intent to deceive. The fact that the followers may or may not have lost money, or may have gained equity in the company, does not undermine this conclusion.

### Practical consequence for the prosecution  

The district court’s failure to reverse the indictment was based on a re‑examination of the statutory elements in light of the Supreme Court’s doctrinal clarifications. By focusing on the *intent* rather than the *consequence*, the court found that the indictment satisfied both required prima facie elements. Consequently, the indictment was properly reversible, and the Fifth Circuit remanded for trial proceedings consistent with this opinion. The underlying takeaway for law students is that an indictment that proves the defendant’s plan to obtain money or property through false statements can meet the *intent to defraud* requirement even in the absence of demonstrable economic loss to the victim.

--- 

###  Key takeaways

* The federal fraud statutes demand proof that the defendant *intended to obtain money or property* through deception.  
* The Supreme Court has made clear that the victim’s economic loss is not a prerequisite for the *intent* element.  
* The *Constantinescu* indictment directly alleges misleading conduct aimed at inducing followers to part with money, thereby establishing the intent to defraud.

# VI. Holding, Outcome, and Practical Implications  

The Fifth Circuit, in a brief but definitive opinion, held that the superseding indictment against the eight defendants was sufficient under the federal indictment standards, and that the indictment properly asserted the elements of securities fraud under the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory. Consequently, the district court’s dismissal was reversed, and the case was remanded for further proceedings consistent with the appellate opinion. The reversal and the court’s reasoning carry several practical implications for both prosecutors and defense counsel, especially when dealing with high‑profile social‑media influencers who use their platforms to influence securities markets.  

## Holding  

The court held, unanimously, that:  

1. **The indictment stated a securities‑fraud offense.**  
   The indictment enumerated each prima facie element required by 18 U.S.C. § 1348: a scheme to defraud, deception via material misrepresentations, intent to defraud, and an objective to obtain money or property.  
2. **The fraudulent‑inducement theory applies.**  
   By alleging that the defendants posted false information that induced followers to buy securities, the indictment satisfied the fraudulent‑inducement standard that “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.”  
3. **The right‑to‑control theory is inapplicable.**  
   The defendants’ conduct did not merely deprive followers of potentially valuable economic information; it obliged followers to part with money or property, thereby exercising a protected interest.  
4. **The indictment adequately alleges intent to defraud.**  
   Allegations that the defendants sought to “pump” the price of securities for their own gain constitute intent to obtain money or property through deception, even in the absence of demonstrable harm to the victims.  

The court’s reverse decision therefore reinstated the indictment and remanded the case for further prosecution, allowing the parties to proceed with trial preparations or settlement negotiations.  

## Remand Logistics  

On remand, the district court must:  

- **Reaffirm jurisdiction.**  
  The court must confirm that it retains original jurisdiction over the indictment and that the defendants remain in its venue.  
- **Proceed with discovery.**  
  The defendants may now file discovery requests, and the government is compelled to disclose evidence in line with Rule 12(b)(6) and 2‑A(d)(2) of the Federal Rules of Evidence.  
- **Consider plea negotiations.**  
  Defense counsel often seek preliminary agreements; the government can rely on the facts that the indictment now meets statutory standards.  
- **Plan for trial.**  
  The court will set a trial date, assess the sufficiency of evidence, and ensure that witnesses, including social‑media platform representatives, are available.  

The appellate opinion emphasizes that the reversal is purely procedural; it does not alter the underlying facts or the potential sanctions against the defendants.  

## Practical Implications for Prosecution  

1. **Easier Indictment Drafting for Social‑Media Cases**  
   Prosecutors can confidently craft indictments that target the “fraudulent‑inducement” element, emphasizing that defendants induced followers to purchase securities in exchange for money or property.  
2. **Focus on Material Misstatements**  
   The court reiterated that the core is a material misrepresentation. Detailed evidence of posts, screenshots, or testimonies can satisfy this element.  
3. **No Need to Demonstrate Economic Harm**  
   The Fifth Circuit affirmed that the juror or judge need not prove that followers suffered a net pecuniary loss; the objective to obtain money suffices. This lowers the evidentiary burden on prosecutors.  
4. **Potential for Stronger Sentencing**  
   A valid indictment strengthens the government’s position in sentencing, especially if the defendant flees or wishes to negotiate.  

## Practical Implications for Defense Counsel  

1. **Challenging the Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory**  
   Defendants may attempt to argue that the alleged misstatements do not reach the threshold of “materiality” or that failure to prove harm still undermines the charge. However, the court’s reliance on Kousisis suggests that the absence of harm does not negate liability.  
2. **Negotiating Plea Agreements**  
   Knowing that the indictment is solid, defense lawyers might negotiate plea deals that mitigate exposure, arguing that the evidence lacks specificity on the alleged intent.  
3. **Pursuing Procedural Motions**  
   Defendants may file motions to suppress evidence that was obtained during investigative steps predicated on the assault of the indictment’s sufficiency.  
4. **Addressing Defamation Claims**  
   Influencer defendants might invoke First Amendment defenses, asserting that their posts were protected speech rather than fraudulent statements. Yet the court’s standpoint indicates that truthful speech is excluded from the analysis; only false statements are actionable.  

## Broader Impact on Securities‑Fraud Prosecutions  

- **Standardized Theory Adoption**  
  The court’s clear endorsement of the fraudulent‑inducement theory provides a template for subsequent cases involving social media or other mass‑communication platforms.  
- **Signal to Influencers**  
  Influencers operating in the securities domain must recognize that a mere misstatement can trigger federal fraud liability, even when the intended audience is not harmed financially.  
- **Policy Considerations**  
  While the decision does not directly address regulatory policy, it indirectly reinforces the principle that fraud statutes protect property interests, not intangible economic knowledge.  
- **Cross‑Circuit Consistency**  
  Other circuits may follow the Fifth Circuit in applying similar standards, especially as the federal judiciary grapples with the evolving role of digital platforms in securities markets.  

## Key Take‑Aways for Law Students  

1. **Indictment Sufficiency is Determinate, Not Speculative**  
   The court’s de novo review forces the indictment to be bloodless; each element must be explicitly alleged, even when facts are inferred.  
2. **Fraudulent‑Inducement vs. Right‑to‑Control**  
   Understanding the two theories is essential; the former protects tangible property, the latter does not. In cases where a defendant causes a victim to part with money, the fraudulent‑inducement standard applies.  
3. **Intent Is Not “Harm”**  
   Intent to defraud is measured by the desire to obtain money or property, regardless of the victim’s net loss.  
4. **Social Media Is Not a Safe Harbor**  
   Influencers cannot rely on the anonymity or the “social‑media” context to escape liability; the content’s truthfulness is still subject to federal fraud statutes.  

This chapter underscores how the court’s holding clarifies the procedural pathway and substantive doctrine surrounding securities fraud prosecutions, especially when the crooks are content creators with large online followings. The Fifth Circuit’s decision fortifies the application of the fraudulent‑inducement theory and sets a precedent that will likely shape future litigation in this rapidly evolving field.
