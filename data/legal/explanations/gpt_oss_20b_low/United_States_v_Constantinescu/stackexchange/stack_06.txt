Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### On what basis did the Fifth Circuit reverse the district court’s dismissal of the indictment?
Question:
The district court dismissed the indictment for failing to allege a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed on de novo sufficiency review. What standard did the court apply to decide that the indictment was sufficient, and which factual allegations did it find compelling enough to support a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit applied a *de novo* sufficiency review under the standard set out in *United States v. Rafoi* (60 F. 4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023)).  In that review the court “takes the allegations of the indictment as true and determines whether an offense has been stated.”  The court then looks to whether the indictment **(1)** enumerates each prima‑facie element of the charged offense, **(2)** fairly informs the defendant of the charges, and **(3)** provides the defendant with the double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.  See *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F. 3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996).

In *Constantinescu* the government alleged securities‑fraud conspiracy and multiple counts of securities fraud under § 1348.  The statute requires a **scheme to defraud** *and* an **intent to defraud** (*Id.* at 339).  The court therefore examined the indictment’s factual allegations to see whether these two elements were sufficiently pleaded.

---

### 1.  Scheme to Defraud

The indictment alleged that each defendant:

* purchased a security;  
* “pumped” the price by posting *false and misleading* information on social media so that “other investors were induced to purchase the security” (indictment ¶ 13–14);  
* secretly “dumped” the security for a profit, resulting in a $114 million gain (indictment ¶ 13).

These facts establish a **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive others for the purpose of obtaining money or property from the victims – the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory.  The court rejected the defendants’ claim that the indictment only alleged depriving followers of “valuable economic information” because that theory, derived from the *right‑to‑control* doctrine, was rejected by the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli v. United States* (598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)).  The indictment instead alleges that followers were *induced* to part with **money** (to purchase securities), a protected property interest.  The court concluded that the indictment “did not run afoul of *Ciminelli* because it alleged defendants deprived their followers of a protected property interest by fraudulently inducing them to buy securities” (*Id.*, A).

### 2.  Intent to Defraud

The defendants argued that the indictment’s claim of intent to profit did not satisfy the *intent to defraud* element.  The court rejected that argument, citing *Kousisis v. United States* (145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025)).  The Supreme Court held that the fraud statutes do **not** require the victim to suffer an economic loss; it is sufficient that the defendant “schemes to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property’” regardless of whether the victim is left economically worse off.  The indictment expressly stated that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” (indictment ¶ 13), thereby showing an intent to deprive victims of their money through deception.  The court noted that the defendants’ objective was “to obtain money,” and that the “pump and dump” scheme was clearly aimed at “getting a profit” (see *Id.* at 1397).  Consequently the indictment sufficiently alleged an intent to defraud.

---

### Bottom line

Because the indictment, when viewed in the light most favorable to the government, **enumerated the material misrepresentation, the intent to obtain money or property, and the alleged financial result** (the $114 million gain), the Fifth Circuit held that it **sufficiently alleged a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud**.  Applying the *de novo* sufficiency standard, the court reversed the district court’s dismissal.
