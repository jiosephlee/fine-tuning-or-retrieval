Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Which elements must an indictment state for securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §1348?
Question:
The opinion refers to the need to allege a scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and that the victim’s money or property was obtained. How do judges determine whether an indictment "enumerates each prima facie element" in the context of §1348, and what factual allegations would be considered sufficient to satisfy that requirement?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 the offense is “defrauding any person in connection with any security … by means of false or fraudulent pretenses … to obtain any money or property … in connection with the purchase or sale of a security.”  
Because the statute is a “petty” fraud statute, its elements follow the same doctrinal pattern that courts have applied to mail and wire fraud:  

| Element | What must be alleged | Source in *Constantinescu* |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| **Scheme to defraud** | A set‑up a course of conduct that deliberately misleads the victim; it must be *material* and *intended* to deceive. | ¶ 13–14: “purchase a security, ‘pump’ the price by posting false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially inflate its price.” |
| **Intent to defraud** | The defendant must have an *intent to deceive* and an *intent to obtain money or property* by means of that deception. | ¶ 13: “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” The indictment therefore alleges the two required intents: to deceive and to obtain money. |
| **Victim’s money or property acquired** | The victim must part with money or property, and that money/property is the *object* of the fraud. | ¶ 13–14: The scheme “allowed defendants to profit … $114 million.” This shows that the victim’s money was actually acquired. |

---

### How judges determine “enumeration of each prima‑facie element”

1. **Treat the indictment as true.** A judge first accepts every allegation in the indictment as fact, a principle underscored in *United States v. Rafoi* (5th Cir. 2023).

2. **Apply the standard from *United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo*** and its progeny: the indictment must *enumerate* each “material” element. The court does **not** look for the *exact statutory language* but at the factual detail required to satisfy the elements.  
   * *Sex* the indictment present a *scheme* (a set of conduct) that would, if factual, commit fraud.  
   * *Sex* the indictment show *intent* (the state of mind) necessary for fraud.  
   * *Sex* the indictment identify the *victim’s loss* (money or property taken).

3. **Sufficient specificity.** A single “intent to profit” is not enough; the indictment must connect that intent to *deceiving the victim* to obtain money. As the Supreme Court clarified in *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*, fraud requires *“deception”* that *obtains* money or property, not merely that the offender wants to hurt the victim. The indictment in *Constantinescu* makes that connection explicit: the false statements *are* what induce followers to buy, and the purchase is what gives defendants the money.

4. **No “mirror‑image” requirement for loss.** The court, following *Kousisis*, may allow an indictment to claim fraud even if the victim’s net economic loss is ambiguous, as long as the victim actually gave up money or property.

---

### Factual allegations that satisfy the requirement

The indictment must state facts that, when taken together, satisfy every element:

- **Scheme to defraud:**  
  *“Defendants would purchase a security, ‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on social media so that other investors were **induced** to purchase the security and artificially increase its price.”*  
  (¶ 13–14)

- **Intent to defraud (deceive + obtain money/property):**  
  *“Defendants… post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were **induced** to purchase the security.”*  
  (¶ 13)

- **Victim’s money/property acquired:**  
  *“Defendants profited $114 million from their scheme.”*  
  (¶ 13–14)

These allegations are more than surface‑level; they specify *who* was acting (the defendants), *what* they did (purchased, pumped, dumped), *how* they misled (false posts), *who* was affected (followers who bought), and *what* was taken (money, reflected in the $114 million profit). The indictment therefore “enumerates each prima‑facie element” of § 1348.

---

### Bottom line for the student

When reviewing an indictment for § 1348, judges look to see whether the allegations pull together a **complete picture** that satisfies the three building blocks—scheme, intent, and victim’s loss—using the *factual detail* required, **not** the exact statutory phrasing. The *Constantinescu* indictment does so; it explicitly states that the defendants *misrepresented* securities to *induce* purchases, that they *intended* to obtain money through deception, and that the victims *did* part with money. That is why the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal.
