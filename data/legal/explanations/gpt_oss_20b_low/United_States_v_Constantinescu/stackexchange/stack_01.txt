Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What constitutes a 'protected property interest' under federal fraud statutes?
Question:
In United States v. Constantinescu the court rejects the district court’s dismissal by citing that the indictment alleged a scheme to defraud followers of *money* or *property*. The defendants argue the plaintiffs only deprived them of valuable economic information, not a traditional property interest. How does the Supreme Court’s view in Ciminelli and Kousisis clarify what is considered a protected property interest, and why does the court conclude the indictment does not fall under the right‑to‑control theory?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit is faced with a district‑court dismissal that rests on the claim that the indictment failed to allege that the defendants stole a *“protected property interest”*. The court turns to the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), and *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), to decide whether the alleged fraudulent scheme falls within the ambit of the federal fraud statutes.

---

### 1.  What is a “protected property interest”?

Both *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* recognize that the five fraud statutes (mail, wire, securities, fraud‑in‐the‑money, and fraud‑in‑the‑contract) protect *traditional* property interests—money, narrowly defined property, or the right to control property. *Ciminelli* cast a clear line: a *right to valuable economic information* is **not** a protected property interest. The Court explained that the “right‑to‑control theory”—which seeks to convict when a defendant “deprives a victim of the right to control his assets” by withholding information—does not satisfy this requirement because the victim’s economic distinctiveness is not a property right under the statutes.

---

### 2.  Applying the *Ciminelli* distinction to Constantinescu

The defendants in *Constantinescu* argue that the indictment only says they *“intended to deprive followers of potentially valuable economic information”*. That language, by itself, would mirror the *Ciminelli* scenario and would be insufficient: depriving a victim of information alone is not a protected interest, so the indictment would be defective.

The Court rejects that interpretation because the indictment does **not** rely on a rights‑to‑control argument. The indictment alleges that the defendants, through false statements on social media, *induced followers to buy securities*. That is a *fraudulent‑inducement* scheme, the Court notes, which is *explicitly* distinct from the right‑to‑control theory:

> “Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1388.

Because the defendants actually sought to *obtain money or property*—the followers’ cash used to purchase the securities—the statute’s protected property interest is satisfied. The indictment therefore states a scheme to defraud that involves a protected property interest.

---

### 3.  Why the indictment survives the “intent to defraud” objection

The defendants also claim that the indictment fails to allege intent to defraud, arguing that the defendants “intended to profit, not to hurt the victims.” *Kousisis* clarified that the fraud statutes do **not** require that the victim suffer an economic loss or that the defendant’s motive be to “hurt” the victim. What matters is that the defendant *“schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property.”* The Court cites *Kousisis*:

> “A defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392.

*Constantinescu* therefore contains the necessary intent element because it alleges that the defendants posted materially false information to induce purchases, and that the scheme was aimed at extracting the followers’ money to enable the defendants to “pump” the stock and later “dump” for profit. The fact that the followers end up holding the securities (even if at market value) does **not** defeat the fraud claim, per *Kousisis* and *United States v. Baker*, 923 F.3d 390.

---

### 4.  Conclusion

The Supreme Court cases establish that:

* **Protected property interest**: Only *money, narrowly defined property, or the right to control property* is protected. A right to valuable economic information is not.  
* **Right‑to‑control theory**: Offenses cannot be based solely on depriving a victim of the right to control their assets by withholding information.  
* **Fraudulent‑inducement theory**: Offenses can be based on deception that induces a victim to part with money or property, even if the victim ultimately does not suffer a net loss.

The *Constantinescu* indictment, by alleging that the defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities and thereby caused those followers to part with money, satisfies both the protected‑interest requirement and the intent‑to‑defraud requirement under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Therefore, the court concludes that the indictment *is* within scope of the federal fraud statutes and reverses the dismissal.
