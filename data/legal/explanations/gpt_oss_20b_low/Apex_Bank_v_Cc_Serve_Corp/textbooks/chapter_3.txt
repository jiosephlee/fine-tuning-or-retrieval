Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 3: # 3. Analysis of the Second DuPont Factor – Similarity of Goods and Services  

In a trademark opposition, the Board’s central inquiry is whether the prospective applicant’s services or goods are “likely to cause confusion” with the prior user’s. The second DuPont factor—**the similarity of the goods or services**—provides a factual basis for that inquiry. The appellate court in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* scrutinized the Board’s finding that Apex’s intended “banking and financing services” were highly similar to CC Serve’s “credit‑card services.” The court ultimately affirmed the Board’s conclusion, finding that the evidence supported the Board’s legal reasoning and that no error occurred in the factual determination.

## Definition of “credit‑card services” per the Trademark Identification Manual  

The Trademark ID Manual (TID Manual) is the USPTO’s go‑to source for parsing the specific meanings of service marks. When a term appears in the manual’s “Deleted Entries” list—as “credit‑card services” does following CC Serve’s registration—the Board must *first* determine the service’s precise scope. The manual’s full entry for “credit‑card services” reads:  

> *Issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit‑card debt, processing credit‑card payments and transactions, credit‑card authorization, credit‑card monitoring and alerts, managing credit‑card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.*

The Board applied this definition wholesale. By narrowing the market to this set of activities, the Board effectively bound the comparison to the *exact* service that CC Serve provides. The TID Manual’s guidance is binding on the USPTO examining and opposing officers, so the Board’s reliance on the manual was procedurally sound.

## Legal Logic Linking Credit‑Card Services to Banking/Financing Services  

The question is whether **banking and financing services**—the category Apex seeks to register—encompass or overlap with “credit‑card services.” The Board used two strands of legal reasoning:

1. **Dictionary Definitions**  
   The board consulted standard dictionaries (e.g., Merriam‑Webster, Oxford English) to interpret the common meaning of *banking*, *bank*, and *finance*. These sources define banking broadly as “the business of protecting, sending, and managing funds” and finance as “the management of large sums of money, especially by the issuing of securities.” The board noted that these definitions include “extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards.” The court agreed that such linguistic analysis is permissible and defensible under DuPont’s “case‑by‑case” approach: the Board was allowed to consider whether the services in question are *legally identical, in part,* because the dictionary frames the services as overlapping.

2. **Industry Practice and Service Structure**  
   The board stressed that credit‑card services are a subset of broader banking and financing activities: banks grant credit and issue cards, both fitting under the umbrella of “banking and financing services.” The appellate court favored this logic, noting that the board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar, so it was reasonable to extend that similarity to encompass the broader category.

## Board’s Synthesis of Dictionary Definitions and Third‑Party Registrations  

Beyond dictionary support, the Board examined *third‑party registrations* that fit the same or similar service categories. In particular, the board reviewed other marks registered for **credit‑card services** as well as those for **banking and financing services**. The Board’s approach:

- Identified marks that use the word “Aspire” or a derivative in connection with credit‑card services (e.g., “Aspire‑Finance”).
- Noted that these marks were *termed* under the same services listed in the “credit‑card services” definition from the TID Manual.
- Determined that the presence of these marks demonstrated that the market for credit‑card services is one in which multiple operators seek distinct marks for essentially identical offerings.

The court acknowledged that the Board carried out a *reasonable exercise* of fact‑finding, considering both the textual definition from the TID Manual and actual third‑party uses. Although Apex argued that CC Serve actually partners *with* banks rather than directly offering credit‑card services, the board’s analysis did not require an exclusive provision of credit‑card services; instead, it considered the overall *functional* overlap: managing credit‑card programs, handling payments, and offering related financial services—all of which fall under “banking and financing services.”

## Court’s Evaluation of the Evidence Supporting Similarity  

To determine whether the Board’s conclusion was supported by substantial evidence, the Supreme Court applied the standard set in *M2 Software* and the subsequent doctrine: “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.” The court reviewed the evidence on the second factor and found:

1. **Taxonomy and Scope** – The TID Manual provided a clear, authoritative definition that bound the comparison to a specific, well‑understood set of services.
2. **Dictionary Corroboration** – The dictionaries filled the semantics gap between “credit‑card services” and “banking and financing services,” showing a normative overlap in how industry and consumers understand these terms.
3. **Third‑Party Usage** – The presence of other “Aspire” marks in the credit‑card space signaled a *crowded* field, confirming that the services are traded among numerous competitors, and thereby reinforcing the similarity argument.
4. **Logical Consistency** – The Board’s synthesis was internally consistent; it applied a single definition for the services, tied that definition to broader banking/financing activities, and then compared the two parties within that unified framework.

Because the appellate court found that the Board’s factual determinations were sufficiently supported, it held “substantial evidence” existed to support the conclusion that the services are highly similar. The court recognized that *confusion* depends not on *direct competition* but on *relatedness* of offerings and *consumer perception* of common source. The court rejected Apex’s assertion that CC Serve’s involvement *with* banks rather than *direct* issuance of credit cards mitigated similarity, noting that the legal analysis did not require exclusive provision of credit‑card services to determine similarity.

## Rationale for Affording the Board the Second‑Factor Finding  

Having upheld the Board’s factual findings, the court affirms the Board’s legal conclusion on the second DuPont factor because:

- The Board’s analysis followed the *legal framework* established by DuPont and *Citing* sources, applying the test “whether the services are related in some manner or share circumstances that could give rise to mistaken belief.”
- The court found no *misapplication* of the TID Manual, dictionary definitions, or industry practice. The Board’s reliance on these resources was appropriate and granted the court deference under the *de novo* review of legal conclusions but *substantial‑evidence* review of factual determinations.
- The second factor is a factual question; the court is confined to the evidence the Board found and the reasonable interpretations thereof. The board’s conclusion that the services are “legally identical, in part,” is consistent with *Olde Tyme Foods* and other precedents emphasizing *similarity* rather than exact duplication.

The appellate court therefore concluded that the Board’s finding supporting likelihood of confusion under the second DuPont factor is properly affirmed. The court noted that the second factor weighs heavily in the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis but, combined with other factors, ultimately led the Board to a conclusion that Apex’s Marks should be refused registration, a finding that the court also affirms for this particular factor.
