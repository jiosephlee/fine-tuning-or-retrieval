Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What constitutes ‘similar’ services under the second DuPont factor when the parties’ services are not identical but are in the same financial services field?
Question:
In this opinion the Board found the services of CC Serve (credit‑card services) and Apex (banking and financing services) to be ‘legally identical, in part’ and thus highly similar under DuPont factor 2. I’m having trouble understanding how the Court reconciles the fact that CC Serve doesn’t actually issue banks’ credit cards itself but instead partners with banks to manage the programs. Does the delegation of service to a bank change the analysis, or is the mere participation in the credit‑card offering enough to treat the services as the same? How does the Board justify the conclusion that the services ‘may emanate from a single source’?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Because the Board determined that the *legal* function of both parties’ services is the same—i.e., both are “credit‑card services” in the sense that they extend credit, issue cards, process payments, and manage accounts—the fact that CC Serve does not issue cards directly but merely partners with a bank does not alter the analysis. The “participation in the same credit‑card offering” is enough for DuPont #2, because the factor asks whether the *services are related* and whether the “circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” In this case, the “source” is the brand name **ASPIRE**; both parties’ services are sold under that name (or a variation of it), and both are marketed to the same class of consumers—people who want to use a credit‑card product. That creates the “single‑source” nexus the Board found. The delegation of card issuance to a third‑party bank does **not** break that nexus; it is part of the contractual and operational framework that makes both parties’ offerings indistinguishable to the average consumer.

---

### 1.  What DuPont #2 requires

Judge Hughes cites *Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012), which holds that the factor is satisfied “when the respective products are related in some manner and/or the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” The comparison is **not** limited to exact duplication of product lines; it is a matter of *relationship* and *market context*.

### 2.  How the Board applied this to the facts

- **Service definitions**  
  The Board explained that “credit‑card services” “encompasses issuing credit cards, counseling, processing payments, authorizing, monitoring, managing accounts, and providing access to credit scores” (see J.A. 25). It noted that the dictionary definitions of “banking” and “finance” likewise include the issuance of credit cards. The Board therefore concluded that the services are “legally identical, in part” (J.A. 27‑28).

- **Marketing “source”**  
  The Board considered the “single‑source” question by looking at the public’s perception. Both parties used the word **ASPIRE** in connection with “credit‑card services” (CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark and Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK mark). The Board saw that both brands are marketed to the same consumer segment—people looking at credit‑card offers—so confusing the source was a real possibility. The Board’s language “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark” (J.A. 33‑34) reflects this reasoning.

### 3.  Does partnership with a bank change the analysis?

The Board expressly rejected Apex’s argument that the partnership “does not provide banking services itself.” Judge Hughes notes, “the Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services, and substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” The analysis focuses on the *end result* for the consumer (a branded credit‑card product) rather than the *off‑the‑table* contractual arrangements that produce it. The consumer cannot see or begin to see through the underwriting partnership, so the “delegation” is irrelevant to consumer perception; the relevant fact is that both services appear to come from the same brand.

### 4.  How “may emanate from a single source” is justified

This phrase is a shorthand for the “single‑source” question in DuPont #2. Because the Board found the legal *functions* of the services to be the same, it treated the services as emanating from the same point of origin: the **ASPIRE** mark. The ordinary consumer, when presented with an “ASPIRE” card, has no reason to think that two different companies are behind the brand; the emphasis is on the *perceived* source of the service, not on the contractual split between CC Serve and its partner banks.

---

### Bottom line for a law student

1. **DuPont #2 is a *relationship* test, not a *duplication* test.**  
2. The mere fact that CC Serve outsources card issuance to a bank does **not** sever the link between the consumer and the **ASPIRE** brand.  
3. The Board’s conclusion that the services “may emanate from a single source” is grounded in the consumer‑centric definition of “source” used in the *Coach Servs.* case and the legal equivalence the Board found between “credit‑card services” and “banking/financing services.”  

Thus the delegation of service to a bank is immaterial; what matters is that both parties’ offerings are indistinguishable brands to the relevant public.

### Why did the court vacate the Board’s finding on DuPont factor 6 while affirming the finding on factor 2?
Question:
The opinion states that DuPont factor 6 requires consideration of similar marks for similar goods, but the Board limited the analysis to credit‑card services only, excluding other banking‑related services. Since the Court previously found the services to be 'highly similar' under factor 2, why does the Court now view the similarity requirement of factor 6 as ‘too narrow’? Aren’t the two factors supposed to be independent? What is the legal basis for the Court’s decision to require a broader universe of similar marks under factor 6?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Court’s holding hinges on the relationship between the *second* and *sixth* DuPont factors and on the ordinary meaning of the word *similar* in the context of factor 6.

---

### 1.  The two factors are not “independent” in the way you first imagined

The six DuPont factors are “questions of fact” that the Board may address in any order or combination that is “relevant and of record” (see *M2 Software, Inc.*).  The Court repeatedly stresses that *similarity* under factor 6 is the **same** similarity that the Board considered under factor 2.  In *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc.* and *Juice Generation*, the Court held that *similar goods* need not be identical, only “similar in a way that would be understood by the relevant public.”  (See the citations in the opinion.)  Thus, when the Board *already* found that the parties’ services are “highly similar” under factor 2, it could not then restrict its view of similarity to a narrower set of goods only because of a “different legal standard” for factor 6.

---

### 2.  The Court requires the board to use the *same* universe of goods

The Board’s error was a *definition‑error*: it limited the “relevant public” to “consumers of credit‑card services” and ignored other banking‑related services even though those services had been found “highly similar” under factor 2.  The Court rejected that limiting reasoning, writing:

> “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.”  (See *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, § III‑B)

The Court therefore vacated factor 6 because the factual scope—what is “similar”—was nar­rowed incorrectly.  The proper scope is the entire set of goods and services that the Board has already established as *similar* under factor 2.

---

### 3.  Broadening the universe of “similar” marks

Because factor 6 asks for *“the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”* (DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361), the Court held that the Board must look at all third‑party marks that use the word “Aspire” on goods and services that the Court has already found *similar* to the parties’ services.  The Board’s exclusion of 42 other third‑party marks that used “Aspire” in banking and financing services was therefore “too narrow” and not supported by the law.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

* **Factor 2 was affirmed** because the Board correctly found the parties’ services “highly similar,” a finding supported by substantial evidence.

* **Factor 6 was vacated** because the Board’s *definition* of “similar goods” was narrower than the Court’s own finding under factor 2.  The Court held that the same factual determination of similarity must apply across all DuPont factors, so the Board could not adopt a different, stricter standard for factor 6.

* **Factor 1 was remanded** as it is directly affected by the outcome of factor 6; a change in the perceived weakness or strength of the marks (whom factor 6 examines) will alter the overall commercial impression that factor 1 analyzes.

In short, the Court rejected the Board’s narrowing strategy and required it to apply the *same* universe of “similar” marks that the Board had already identified in its factor 2 analysis.  The factors are “independent” only insofar as they ask different questions, but they cannot use **different sets of facts** about similarity.

### How does the Court apply the concept of commercial strength to a mark that has not been shown to be either ‘strong’ or ‘weak’?
Question:
The Board concluded that CC Serve’s "ASPIRE" mark was ‘entitled to the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks’ because it had not proved commercial strength, yet Apex had not shown weakness. The Court says this is legally flawed. Why is the lack of evidence for either strength or weakness insufficient for a decision on scope? What standard does the Court use when assessing the commercial strength of a mark?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Court rejected the Board’s conclusion that CC Serve’s “ASPIRE” mark was entitled to the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks” solely because it had not proven *commercial strength* and Apex had not proven *weakness*.  The Court held that such a finding is “legally flawed” because it rests on an erroneous understanding of how the *commercial‑strength* element is applied in the DuPont framework.

---

### 1.  What the DuPont factors actually require

The Lanham Act requires a *likelihood‑of‑confusion* analysis, and the TABA routinely applies the thirteen DuPont factors as a set of **facts on which findings of law are based**.  (See *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1381.)  Each factor is a *question of fact*; the Board’s conclusions are examined for **substantial evidence**.

The sixth factor, “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services,” does not simply ask whether the defendant has shown that its mark is weak.  Rather, the factor requires the Board to **examine the field of third‑party marks that are used on goods or services that are *similar* to the parties’ offerings**.  A crowded field suggests that consumers can distinguish among the marks, thereby indicating a lack of commercial strength, while a sparse field may suggest the opposite.

See *Palm Bay Imps.*, 396 F.3d at 1373; *Juice Generation*, 794 F.3d at 1338–39.  The factor is *not* a “check‑list” test that the mark must be demonstrably weak; it is a **fact‑finding device** that informs the overarching legal question of confusion.

---

### 2.  Why the Board’s “absence of evidence for strength or weakness” cannot stand

In *Apex Bank*, the Board noted that CC Serve had “not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that CC Serve’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection.”  From that statement the Board inferred that the mark is entitled to the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.”  

The Court calls this logic flawed for two reasons:

1. **The DuPont framework does not allow a *default* conclusion** that a mark is strong when the parties have not proven weakness.  The sixth factor is *insufficient* on its own to fix the scope; it must be weighed against the other factors.  The Court emphasized that once the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (second factor), it should employ the **same breadth of comparison** when assessing the field of third‑party marks (sixth factor).  By limiting its analysis to the nine “Aspire‑formative” marks for credit‑card services, the Board failed to consider the entire relevant universe of similar marks, many of which applied to banking and financing services—the very services Apex proposes to offer.  This incomplete field led the Board to misstate the mark’s commercial strength.

2. **Commercial strength is an *inferred* element, not a *proved* one**.  The Court repeatedly stresses that the Board must move beyond “absence of evidence” and actually *assess* the *strength* of the mark in view of the evidence presented.  Failure to do so means the Board’s finding is unsupported.  As the Court put it, “a find that *the mark is not weak* does not, by itself, establish that it is *strong’’.  Consequently, the Board cannot punt the issue of strength to the next appellate court (or to the claimant) by merely pointing out that no weakness was shown.

Because the Court saw that the Board’s reasoning under factor six was defective, it vacated the findings on factors six and one (the latter of which hinges on the “overall commercial impression” that includes the mark’s commercial strength or weakness).  The remand instructs the Board to recompute factor six with the proper field of third‑party marks, and to re‑evaluate factor one in light of that recomputation.

---

### 3.  The standard the Court applies when assessing commercial strength

The Court does not adopt a *new* doctrine; it applies the **conventional “substantial evidence” standard** to the DuPont analysis.  However, the Court clarifies that *commercial strength* is **not an isolated test**; it is evaluated in concert with the other DuPont factors.  The Court’s language in *Apex Bank* mirrors the earlier “ordinary or normal scope” standard in cases involving inherently distinctive marks (cf. *J. A. 52*).  But crucially, the Court emphasizes that the “normal scope” can only be granted after an exhaustive factual inquiry that includes:

* Whether the mark has a *larger* presence in commerce (e.g., volume of sales, orgeographic reach) – the Board must look beyond the mere presence or absence of proof of strength.
* Whether the surrounding market is *crowded* or *sparse* (the number and nature of similar marks on similar services) – again, a full survey, not a partial one.

If the evidence shows that the mark is *neither* significantly strong *nor* weak in the relevant marketplace, the appropriate remedy is a *narrower* scope or, at minimum, a *comparative* analysis of the mark’s commercial strength versus the surrounding field.  The Court makes clear that this is the *default* path under the DuPont framework; there is no statutory “point‑of‑neutrality” that automatically poises an inherently distinctive mark to the “normal scope.”  (See *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1382.)

---

### Bottom line for students

1. **Commercial strength is a factual determination**—the Board must actually evaluate the mark’s market presence, not simply note a lack of evidence for weakness.
2. **The sixth DuPont factor requires examining a full, relevant field of similar marks**; restricting the field (as the Board did) invalidates the strength analysis.
3. **The Court remands to properly consider the breadth of the field and re‑evaluate the mark’s commercial impression**.  Until that is done, the “normal scope” conclusion is premature.

In summary, the lack of evidence for *either* strength or weakness does *not* grant a default “normal scope” a la inherently distinctive marks.  The Board must conduct a **comprehensive, comparative fact‑finding** under the DuPont framework; the Court’s remand reflects that requirement.

### What is the role of ‘substantial evidence’ when the Board’s findings of fact on DuPont factors are contentious?
Question:
The opinion cites M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc. to explain that findings of fact are reviewed for substantial evidence, while conclusions of law are reviewed de novo. In practice, how does this standard apply when the Board’s factual determination of, say, the similarity of services is challenged? Should we look for a 'reasonable mind' that could accept the evidence, or is there a different threshold when the findings directly influence the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit’s standard is that a finding of fact by the TTB is *not* presumed correct; it is reviewed only for **substantial evidence**. In *M2 Software v. M2 Commc’ns*, the court explained that substantial‑evidence review boils down to a question of whether a “reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate to support the conclusion.” (450 F.3d at 1382; see also *Consol. Edison*, 305 U.S. 197, 229).

Applied to a trademark opposition, the Board first makes a *legal* determination (e.g., “confusion is likely”) and then attaches *underlying factual findings* that support that determination. 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d) itself treats the likelihood‑of‑confusion question “as a question of law based on findings of relevant underlying facts” (see *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1381). The DuPont factors supply those facts: each factor is a factual question (e.g., similarity of services, similarity of marks, commercial strength, etc.) that the Board must answer; only those questions that actually turn on how the Board’s conclusion was reached are appeal‑worthy.

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve*, the Board’s factual finding that Apex’s “banking and financing services” were *the same* (or “partly identical”) as CC Serve’s credit‑card services was the factual foundation of its decision that the second DuPont factor weighed in favor of confusion. The court affirmed that finding, holding that the evidence the Board relied on (dictionary definitions, third‑party registrations, the nature of the services) was “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept.” (see *Apex*, 5A at 27–28).

When Apex challenged the Board’s “similar‑services” finding, the appellate court applied the same standard: the federal appellate court simply asks whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence the Board used. If the evidence – here, the definitions and the analysis of the market – was sufficient, the panel affirms.  The court finds no need to apply a higher threshold or to “look for a reasonable mind that could accept the evidence” in a special way; the *substantial‑evidence* test is the standard, not the *de novo* test.

The situation changes only when the factual determinations are disputed *and* the dispute is central to the legal conclusion. The court’s remand of the sixth and first DuPont factors illustrates this: after finding that the services were “highly similar,” the panel realized that the Board had narrowed the universe of comparable marks too narrowly. Because that factual finding could alter a *legal* assessment of commercial strength and overall commercial impression, the court vacated the findings on those factors and remanded them for reconsideration. The standard did not shift to something more stringent; the rulings still hinge on whether the evidence supports the factual conclusion (i.e., the “number and nature of similar marks” that could be considered legal or not).

**Bottom line for a law student**

1. **Findings of fact** (e.g., similarity of services) are reviewed for *substantial evidence*—the evidence must be such that a reasonable person could accept it as supporting the conclusion.  
2. **Legal conclusions** (e.g., likelihood of confusion) are reviewed de novo.  
3. If a factual finding is essential to a legal conclusion, the court will scrutinize that factual finding; if the evidence is lacking or the facts are misapplied, the finding is vacated and the Board must reconsider.  
4. The *threshold* does not change depending on whether the fact under review is, say, “similarity of services” or “similarity of marks.” The appellate court continually asks whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence the Board cited. The court in *Apex* therefore affirmed the second DuPont factor’s finding—because the evidence was sufficient—and vacated the sixth and first factors—because the factual basis for those factors was incomplete or improperly applied.

So, when the Board’s factual determination of the similarity of services is challenged, the remand or affirmation hinges on whether the Board’s evidence meets the *substantial evidence* bar, not on any higher standard.

### Why does the Court consider the ‘overall commercial impression’ of the marks under DuPont factor 1 to be affected by a reconsideration of factor 6?
Question:
The opinion states that a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness from factor 6 may alter the ‘overall commercial impression,’ prompting the Court to vacate factor 1. What is the logical connection between the commercial strength of a mark and the overall commercial impression? Is there a precedent that directly links a change in factor 6 analysis to a change in factor 1 conclusions?
Answer:
### Short answer

Because the § 6 analysis is the *source of the mark’s commercial strength* – the very fact that the mark is either “strong” or “weak” is derived from the overall number and nature of similar marks in the relevant line of trade.  That commercial strength is the single backdrop against which the Board judges *overall commercial impression* in factor 1.  If a new look at factor 6 shows the mark to be weaker (or stronger) than the Board originally thought, the factor 1 “overall commercial impression” will change accordingly, because the first factor is defined in terms of that impression.  The Court therefore remanded for a fresh factor‑1 review that takes the revised factor‑6 finding into account.

---

## 1.  The doctrinal link

1. **Factor 1** – *The similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.*  
   The factor is expressly tied to “commercial impression.”  In *In re I.A.M. Symbolic, LLC* the Court explained that “commercial impression (the impression given to the relevant consuming public of the source of the goods or services) is the fulcrum of the first factor’s analysis.”  (Fed. Cir. 2017)

2. **Factor 6** – *The number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.*  
   This factor is the *indirect* gauge of a mark’s inherent distinctiveness and thus its commercial strength.  The more similar marks that are in use, the weaker the mark’s commercial strength is deemed to be, and vice versa.  
   The Court has repeatedly held that a mark’s “commercial strength” is derived from the competitive field (see *Juice Generation*, *Jack Wolfskin*, *Palm Bay Imps.*).  

3. **The logic** – Because the “overall commercial impression” of a mark is *presented to the consumer* in the context where that consumer must decide whether a given mark signals the source of the goods or services, the consumer’s perception is conditioned on how many other marks are in the same field and how easily they can be distinguished from each other.  If factor 6 is altered, the consumer’s mental model of the field changes, which in turn changes the “overall commercial impression” in factor 1.

---

## 2.  How the Court saw the connection in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve*, 2025‑2143

* **Step 1 – The Court found factually that the parties’ services are “highly similar” (factor 2).**  
  The Board’s fact‑finding that the services are “highly similar” establishes the *relevant public* and the *degree of similarity* that underlie both factor 2 and factor 6.  

* **Step 2 – The Court vacated factor 6** because the Board had erroneously restricted the relevant public to “consumers of credit‑card services.”  The Court therefore remanded for a full‑scope view that includes all similar marks for banking and financing services.  

* **Step 3 – The Court coupled that vacancy to factor 1**:  
  > “Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must likewise vacate the Board’s analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.” (J.A. 62–63)

  In other words, the Court is saying: *If the Board’s view of the mark’s commercial strength changes under a new factor‑6 analysis, then the consumer’s overall impression of the mark—and thus the factor‑1 finding—must be reconsidered.*

---

## 3.  Precedential support

The Court’s reasoning is not a novel invention; it follows the line of cases that treat factor 6 as a *substance question* that informs the consumer’s perception in factor 1.

| Case | Holding that factor 6 determines commercial strength, which informs factor 1 |
|------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Olde Tyme Foods v. Roundy’s** (1992) | The Court explained that a crowded field of similar marks narrows the scope of protection because the marks are “weak” – a statement about commercial strength that feeds into the factor‑1 analysis. |
| **Jack Wolfskin v. New Millennium Sports** (2015) | The Court distinguished “considerable number” of third‑party marks as evidence of weakness; the ensuing conclusion about commercial strength influenced the “overall impression” test. |
| **Juice Generation v. GS Enterprises** (2015) | The Court held that the presence of many similar marks rendered the plaintiff’s mark weak, which the Board then used to assess overall impression. |
| **In re I.A.M. Symbolic, LLC** (2017) | The Court reiterated that factor 1 depends on commercial impression; it noted that commercial impression rests in part on the mark’s strength as a “source signal.” |

These decisions collectively show that a Board’s or Court’s conclusion about the *commercial strength* of a mark—rooted in factor 6—is the backdrop for the factor‑1 “overall commercial impression” test.  A change in factor 6 therefore has a *direct* impact on factor 1.

---

## 4.  Bottom line for the student

1. **Factor 6** tells you how *strong or weak* the mark is in the marketplace by counting rival marks.  
2. **Factor 1** asks whether, given that strength, a typical consumer will *immediately* associate the mark with the source of the services.  
3. If factor 6 turns out that the mark is *weaker* than the Board originally concluded, the “overall commercial impression” (factor 1) will likely be lessening in favor of confusion.  
4. Therefore the Court properly remanded for a new factor‑1 analysis that incorporates the revised factor‑6 finding.

The logical connection is thus the *hierarchy of analysis*: factor 6 supplies the commercial‑strength premise; factor 1 applies that premise to judge the overall commercial impression.  A shift at the foundation (factor 6) necessitates a re‑evaluation of the conclusion that relies on it (factor 1).

### Under what circumstances does the Board not need to consider all thirteen DuPont factors?
Question:
The opinion cites that the Board 'need not consider every DuPont factor, only those that are relevant and of record.' In this case the Board considered factors 1, 2, and 6, but omitted several others. What criteria determine when a factor is deemed 'relevant and of record'? How does a party demonstrate that a particular factor must be considered?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Court makes it clear that the Board’s review is fact‑driven, not a “full‑check” of every DuPont factor.  
In *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc.*, 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) the court held that each of the thirteen DuPont factors “presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” The same idea is reiterated in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*: “The Board need not consider every DuPont factor, only those that are **relevant and of record**” (J.A. 63–64).

---

### 1.  What makes a factor *relevant*?

A factor is relevant if its underlying question can affect the ‘likelihood‑of‑confusion’ determination in the case at hand.  
* *M2 Software* makes clear that relevance is a legal question: the Board must address the factor only if the facts at issue in the case give rise to that factor’s question.  
* In the Apex case, the Board found that the second factor “assesses the similarity of the parties’ goods and/or services” (J.A. 24–28). Because the parties’ services were in the same sphere—credit‑card versus banking/financing services—the court deemed this factor relevant and tested it.  
* Conversely, the Board acted on the sixth factor because the parties “uncovered a *crowded* field of similar marks on similar goods” (J.A. 40–47). The factor was relevant because the existence of other “Aspire” marks in the same industry could influence the strength of the mark and the scope of protection.  

The court is silent on any further formal test; the standard is plainly that the factor’s underlying fact must potentially alter the ultimate finding of likelihood of confusion.

---

### 2.  What makes a factor *of record*?

A factor is “of record” when the Board has either **(a)** evidence and findings concerning the question, or **(b)** a factual basis that allows it to make a determination.  
* The record must contain facts that either directly address the factor’s question or are sufficiently close that the Board can infer the answer.  
* If the Board has no evidence concerning a factor, or the evidence is wholly irrelevant, the factor is *not* of record.  

In *Apex*, the Board never considered factors 3–5, 7–13. The opinion states the Board’s substantive analysis was limited to factors 1, 2, and 6, because those were the only factors for which the Board had evidence that could influence the likelihood‑of‑confusion question. The opinion explicitly **reminds** the Board that it may “**need not consider every DuPont factor**” when many are “not relevant or of record.”

---

### 3.  How may a party compel a factor’s consideration?

A party can move the Board to consider a factor by:

1. **Presenting new evidence** that directly addresses the factor’s question.  
   * Example: Apex submitted exhibits of third‑party “Aspire” marks for the sixth factor (J.A. 40–46). These exhibits made the factor *relevant* and *of record* by demonstrating that the same word is used by other actors in similar services.  

2. **Arguing that the factor is materially connected** to the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis.  
   * Since the Sixth factor depends on the “number and nature of similar marks on similar goods,” and the court had already found the goods to be highly similar (second factor), Apex correctly argued that the sixth factor must be considered to determine the “normal scope of protection” of CC Serve’s mark.  

3. **Citing appellate precedent** that proves the Board’s failure to address a factor constitutes an error.  
   * The opinion cites *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc.* and *Juice Generation* to clarify that a factor need not be identical in goods, only “similar.” The Board’s failure to consider non‑credit‑card “Aspire” marks was an error under that precedent, thereby warranting the vacatur.  

Thus, a party “demonstrates that a particular factor must be considered” by (a) showing evidence that the factor’s questions are relevant to the case and (b) showing that the record contains material facts sufficient for the Board to make a finding on that factor.

---

**Bottom line:**  
The Board must weigh *only those factors that are both (1) relevant to the likelihood‐of‑confusion analysis and (2) supported by material, admissible evidence on record.* A party can compel consideration by raising new evidence and connecting it to the core question of confusion, as Apex did for the sixth factor. If the Board fails to do so, appellate review will vacate that factor’s finding, as the court did here.
