Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 1: # 1. Case Background and Procedural History

The Ninth Circuit’s “Pacito v. Trump” order examines the background and procedural history that brought the case to the appellate court. The case arose from a series of executive actions taken by President Donald J. Trump in January 2025 and the corresponding district‑court rulings that imposed stay provisions on those actions. Five key procedural layers shape the litigation: the President’s directives, the district court’s preliminary injunctions, the government’s consecutive requests for stays, the Ninth Circuit’s partial granting and later vacatur of the stay, and the current status of the appeal.

## Executive Order No. 14163 and its statutory basis under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

On January 20, 2025, the President promulgated Executive Order 14163, entitled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” The Order directs that entry of all refugees under the USRAP be suspended pending further investigation. The statutory authority cited by the President is 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which permits the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens “when he finds that the entry of any aliens … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The Order’s “findings” and the subsequent suspension are thus grounded in the President’s statutory powers under this immigration statute.

## Executive Order No. 14169 and the suspension of USRAP funding

A parallel administrative move occurred on January 24, 2025, when the State Department suspended all funding dedicated to the USRAP program pursuant to Executive Order 14169, “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” While this Order was ostensibly directed at foreign aid, its practical effect was the de facto shutdown of domestic resettlement operations for refugees, including the funding necessary to support recruitment, processing, and resettlement in the United States.

## Plaintiffs’ identity and legal standing (admitted refugees and partner NGOs)

The litigation is brought by three categories of plaintiffs:

1. Refugees who had already been admitted to the United States before the orders took effect;
2. Refugees who had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country at the time of the orders; and
3. Three non‑governmental organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide overseas processing for refugee applicants and domestic resettlement services for admitted refugees.

These plaintiffs claim that Executive Order 14163 violated the Refugee Act of 1980, which codifies the statutory framework for refugee admissions and treatments, and that Executive Order 14169, by defunding the program, violated the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) due to the lack of a formal rulemaking process.

## District court preliminary injunctions (February 25, 28, March 24 orders)

On February 25, 2025, the Western District of Washington, pursuant to an oral brief, issued a preliminary injunction that barred enforcement of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order 14163. The injunction effectively kept refugees from being re‑admitted under the suspended program. Following the Order’s termination of cooperative agreements on February 26, the district court clarified the scope of the injunction on February 28, indicating that the Order also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the court issued a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated.

The district court orders directly challenged the President’s actions and the administrative closure of the refugee program, prompting the government to seek remedies in the appellate court.

## Government’s stay requests and the Ninth Circuit’s partial grant of the March 25 stay

The government requested a stay of the February 28 injunction, arguing that the Order was unnecessary and that the President’s sovereign discretion under § 1182(f) should not be questioned. On March 25, the Ninth Circuit’s motions panel granted the stay **in part**, invoking the Supreme Court’s deference in *Trump v. Hawaii* with regard to executive immigration authority. The panel carved out a limited exception: the stay did not apply to refugees who had already been conditionally approved for admission by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services prior to January 20, 2025. The court, therefore, stayed the injunction for those refugees but upheld it for others.

## Subsequent administrative stay (July 18) and the Ninth Circuit’s vacatur of that stay

On July 14, 2025, the district court issued an order that established a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee its implementation. In response, the government filed an emergency motion on July 17, seeking clarification or amendment of the stay. The court responded on July 18, 2025, issuing an **administrative stay** of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order—essentially a pause on the procedural framework pending a judicial review.

When oral argument was scheduled for September 3, 2025, the Ninth Circuit granted the government’s request and **vacated** its own administrative stay issued July 18, 2025. The panel also vacated its March 25 stay (as amended by the April 21 and May 9 orders) and declared that the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions were to be **stayed in their entirety**, except as specifically noted.

## Current procedural posture of the Ninth Circuit appeal

The Ninth Circuit’s stay order is an *interim* measure pending a full opinion on the merits. It does not decide the substantive challenges—whether the executive orders violate the Refugee Act or the APA—but suspends the enforcement of the preliminary injunctions that the district court had issued in response to those challenges. The appellate court is now awaiting the submission of the panel’s detailed opinion, which the parties anticipate will elaborate on the legal standards applied, the statutory analyses of executive authority and statutory mandates, and the final disposition of the case.

This procedural history underscores the ongoing conflict between the executive branch’s asserted immigration authority, Congress’s statutory provisions concerning refugee reception and placement, and the district court’s attempt to balance those competing interests for the benefit of affected refugees and their supporting NGOs.
