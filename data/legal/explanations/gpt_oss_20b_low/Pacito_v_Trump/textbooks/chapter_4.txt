Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # 4. The Statutory Duty to Provide Reception and Placement Services under § 1522  

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit was asked to decide whether the plaintiffs could compel the federal government—through a preliminary injunction—to continue furnishing the “reception and placement” services that Congress has legislatively authorized for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States when the President suspended the Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP).  The court ultimately held that § 1522 does **not** create a judicially enforceable duty to provide those services.  The following discussion explains why the statute’s structure and wording, combined with the APA’s review standards, support that conclusion.

## Textual Analysis of § 1522’s Title and Language

Section 1522 is titled **“Authorization for Programs for Domestic Resettlement of and Assistance to Refugees.”**  The use of the word *authorization* is significant.  In statutory interpretation, an *authorization* grants the executive branch permission to act; it does not compel action.  Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed., 2024) defines *authorization* as “the act of authorizing; the permission or consent given to do something.”  Hence, the statutory language signals that Congress is *allowing* the government to create and administer resettlement programs *if* it chooses to do so, rather than *requiring* it.

The bulk of § 1522 consists of subordinate clauses that detail how the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development, through the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR), may administer various grant, contract, and cooperative‑agreement programs.  Each clause is prefaced with the wording *“the Director is authorized…”* or *“the Secretary is authorized…,”* again reinforcing the permissive nature of the statute.

## Role of § 1522(a)(1)(A) and Its Conditional “To the Extent of Available Appropriations” Clause

Section 1522(a)(1)(A) contains the *most* traditionally “mandatory‑looking” provisions.  It requires the Director to “make available sufficient resources” for a set of services—employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and gender‑equitable training—*to the extent of available appropriations*.  The phrase *“to the extent of available appropriations”* is a *condition* that limits the statutory obligation to the resources Congress has actually appropriated.  Because the statute itself does not require any funds to be made available beyond what Congress appropriates, it cannot be interpreted as a blanket obligation that will persist even if the Treasury disburses no money.

Moreover, the “shall” language in § 1522(a)(1)(A) applies only *if* the Director chooses to use the discretionary funding provisions of §§ 1522(b)–(e).  The clause is effectively a *conditional mandate*:  it states what the Director must do if the Director decides to progress with the resettlement programs.  The statutory purpose is not to guarantee a program but to provide a framework for possible programs.

## Distinguishing Discretionary Funding Provisions (§ 1522(b)–(e)) from Mandatory Requirements

The proclamations in §§ 1522(b)–(e) are the statutory mechanisms by which the government actually *delivers* funding to resettlement agencies.  These sections authorize the ORR to:

* Enter contracts and cooperative agreements with nonprofits (§ 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii));
* Approve project grants for refugee services (§ 1522(c));
* Provide cash assistance and medical support to refugees § 1522(e);
* Undertake special duties such as overseeing unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)).

Each provision begins: “the Director *is authorized* to grant contracts or to award …”  The text again signals discretion.  Courts have consistently held that the “authorizing” language does not impose a *duty* but rather *permits* administrative action.  The mere existence of statutory authorizing language does not obligate the executive to act.

The *mandatory* thrust of § 1522(a) is thus carved out by the *conditional* nature of the funding provisions.  When Congress either does not appropriate the necessary funds or the executive decides not to pursue the programs, the statutory duty effectively lapses.

## Interpretation of the APA in Assessing Compliance with § 1522 Obligations

If the plaintiffs had alleged that the government breached a *statutory duty*, the APA would apply through the *abstract review* standard (5 U.S.C. § 5555).  Under this standard, the court must assess whether the agency “rationally concluded that the action was the proper one to take” (5 U.S.C. § 5525(a)(2)).  A *rational* conclusion includes a legitimate *policy judgment* about whether the agency should or could provide a program.

In *Ira*, 892 F.2d 146 (D.C. Cir. 1989), the court held that the APA does not compel the executive to enact a program if the statutory language is discretionary.  ESA Office for the Administration of the ACLU in *Pacito* parallels that analysis: the court noted that the APA does not require the agency to act in a particular way when the statute itself does not contain a *mandatory* provision.  Therefore, the APA provides no remedy for plaintiffs seeking to compel the government to act under § 1522, because the statute does not assert a *binding* obligation.

## Factual Evidence of the Government’s Ability to Provide Services

In the record, the Department of State terminated all cooperative agreements that had provided reception and placement services to refugees in February 2025.  The government indicated to the court on March 10 that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency.”  Since the presentation of that evidence, the government has shown no substantive progress toward reestablishing the services.  The Ninth Circuit, recognizing the *closure* of the cooperative‑agreement apparatus and the absence of a new agency, concluded that the executive branch cannot presently provide the services that § 1522 would hypothetically requisition.

Because the executive has already discontinued the authorized programs and has not demonstrated a clear path to restoring them, the courts find that the *process* for delivering the authorized services is effectively *inoperable*.  Under those facts, any alleged  *duty* would be illusory: the statutory responsibility was contingent on the existence of operational programs, which the government has not maintained.

## Arguments for and Against a Judicially Enforceable Duty

### Arguments for an Enforceable Duty  

1. **Congressional Intent**: Proponents argue that Congress, by passing the Refugee Act and the comprehensive § 1522, intended to ensure refugees receive humane, comprehensive services as part of their resettlement.  
2. **Executive Responsibility**: The defendants might claim the statutory language, especially the “shall” provisions, imposes an implicit duty to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been accepted into the United States.  
3. **Policy Stability**: Persistently suspending services could undermine the Refugee Act’s purpose and violate the *Rehabilitation Act*’s non‑discrimination mandate.

### Arguments against an Enforceable Duty  

1. **Statutory Mood**: The statutory title and the ubiquitous use of *authorized* suggest a *permission* framework rather than a *demand*.  
2. **Conditionality**: Section 1522(a)(1)(A) conditions any obligation on “available appropriations,” meaning the duty can only arise if Congress appropriates the needed funds.  
3. **Executive Discretion**: The APA permits the executive to exercise discretion over whether and how to implement a program, thereby eliminating a *mandate* that could be enforced judicially.  
4. **Practice of Precedent**: Courts have consistently refused to impose a *statutory duty* where the statute provides discretionary authority.  In *Ira* and *PACAR* (People), the courts emphasized that the statutory text is the vital determinant of enforceability, not the policy ends sought.  
5. **Political Question Doctrine**: The suspension and termination of refugee services, especially in light of the President’s explicit executive order, are considered a political question.  The political question doctrine applies when an issue is “deeply divided, or when it is inadequately specified by law,” which the 9th Circuit found subsisting in this case.

## Conclusion

The Ninth Circuit’s holding in *Pacito* emphasized that § 1522 contains no judicially enforceable duty to provide reception and placement services.  The statutory language authorizes, but does not require, the executive to fund and deliver those services.  The *conditional* nature of § 1522(a) and the discretionary nature of §§ 1522(b)–(e) collectively prevent any “mandated” obligation that a court could compel.  Consequently, plaintiffs seeking to force the government, through a preliminary injunction, to provide those services were unable to satisfy the APA’s rational‑conclusion standard or find a statutory duty imposing such an obligation.

The case underscores a general principle: **where a statute uses permissive words such as “authorizes” and conditions obligation on available appropriations, it does not create a court‑enforceable duty.**  The executive’s discretion, coupled with the need for congressional appropriations, limits judicial intervention in federal programs.  When the government terminates cooperative agreements and fails to replace them, the *enforced* standard does not compel the executive to act in the absence of a statutory mandate.
