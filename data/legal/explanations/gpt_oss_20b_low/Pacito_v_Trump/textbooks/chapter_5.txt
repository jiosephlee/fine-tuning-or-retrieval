Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 5: # 5. Ninth Circuit’s Decision on the Stay and Its Implications

The Ninth Circuit’s order issued September 12, 2025, represents the Court’s interim control over the litigation in *Pacito v. Trump*. While a full merits opinion remains pending, the stay order itself crystallizes the Court’s assessment of the stay‑analysis, the relative merits of the plaintiffs’ challenges, and the Court’s imperative to preserve the statutory framework for refugee reception and placement services. In this chapter we unpack the Court’s holding, explain the legal underpinnings of each element of the order, and examine its practical effects on the parties and on the broader regulatory landscape.

## Application of the Four‑Factor Stay Analysis

The Court’s decision relies on the established four‑factor framework from *Nken v. Holder*. The panel explicitly enumerated the factors and progressed through them in order:

1. **Strong showing that the stay applicant is likely to succeed on the merits** – The Court noted that the government “is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions.” The Court summarized that the statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f)–(a) gives the President an almost unbounded discretion to suspend the entry of any class of aliens if the President “finds” the entry detrimental. The Court reiterated the deference given to the President’s “finding” in *Trump v. Hawaii*, emphasizing that the statutory language is “clear” and fully empowers the President to suspend the USRAP. As such, the government has a “strong showing” of likely success.

2. **Irreparable injury to the stay applicant if the stay is denied** – The Court evaluated that the defendants (the government, the State Department, and its cooperative agreements) would suffer irreparable harm if the stay were denied, principally because the district court’s injunctions directly barred the administration from enforcing the executive order. The Court judged that this injury exceeded any potential injury to plaintiffs, stating that the government’s ability to “implement the President’s policy decision” was “on an irreparable level.”

3. **Potential injurious impact on the opposing parties** – Here the Court balanced the harms to plaintiffs (refugees and immigrant‑service organizations) against the government’s interest. The Court found that a stay would “substantially injure” the plaintiffs’ policy remedies by maintaining the suspension of refugee admissions and funding, but the Court deemed the government’s interest in preserving its discretionary agenda to outweigh that injury. The reasoning references *Hawaii* again, noting the deference to the President in immigration matters.

4. **Public interest** – The Court merged the second and third factors, concluding that the public interest “lies with the United States” because the executive order’s sovereign purpose and the wide discretion granted to the President align with national security assessments. The Court explicitly referenced *Hawaii*’s emphasis on the “broad discretion” conferred on the President.

The Court made it clear that the first two factors dominate the analysis, and because the government satisfied those, it was essentially a “no‑show” to win the stay, regardless of potential harm to the plaintiffs or the public.

## Conclusion that the Government Is Likely to Prevail on the Executive‑Order Challenge

The Court’s categorical statement that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to *Executive Order* 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions hinges on an unqualified reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The order argues that the President’s pronouncement of “detriment to the interests of the United States” constitutes a complete justification for a suspension, as the statute permits any duration the President deems “necessary.” While the Court does not agree that the President’s determination was scientifically or administratively justified (that would be a merits question), the Court holds that the President *has* the authority to issue the order, and any review of the reasonableness or practical impact of that suspension is a question of policy, not of legality.

Because the Court recognizes the longstanding deference afforded to executive immigration actions, it refrains from probing the “substance” of the President’s “finding.” This perspective faithfully follows *Trump v. Hawaii*, where the Court emphasized that the President’s “finding” alone, absent a constitutional violation, suffices to invalidate judicial scrutiny. Accordingly, the Court concluded that the government would almost certainly win the merits challenge, rendering the stay “essentially warranted.”

## Finding That the Government May Not Prevail on the APA Challenge to § 1522 Services

While the Court granted the stay on the executive‑order question, it withheld the stay concerning the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenge to the interpretation of § 1522. The Court’s opinion accentuates two themes:

1. **Statutory Designation of Authority vs. Obligation** – The Court differentiated the “authorization” language found in § 1522(b)–(e) from the “mandatory” phrasing in § 1522(a). In particular, the Court highlighted that the statutory purpose of § 1522 is to *authorise* programs for refugees provided that appropriations exist, not to compel the government to spend. The clause “to the extent of available appropriations” is consequently a *financial* limitation, not a *policy* mandate.

2. **Presidential Discretion in Funding** – The Court noted that whether or not the government chooses to fund refugee programs rests upon discretionary budgeting decisions that reflect priorities and resource constraints. Consequently, a failure to fund a particular cooperative agreement – whether voluntarily or due to funding constraints – does not automatically render the government in violation of the APA or of § 1522. Merely delayed termination or stability of existing cooperative agreements does not, in the Court’s view, constitute unlawful retrogression.

Because the plaintiffs’ challenge rests on the argument that the government *must* provide reception and placement services pursuant to § 1522, the Court sees this as a *mandate* issue rather than an *administrative process* issue, and thus does not grant a stay that would force the government to remit existing services. The Court released the government's liability to a standard of *quiet compliance* with the APA until and unless a merits ruling addresses whether the statutory provisions constitute a *mandate* for the government to retain or replace its cooperative agreements.

## Direction to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements Necessary for Reception and Placement Services

Although the Court indicated that the APA challenge may survive the stay, it also recognized that the administration has a statutory duty, under § 1522, to provide reception and placement services to refugees who had been admitted before the suspension went into effect. The Court therefore directed the government to “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” These services must mirror the “usual and customary services” afforded under former agreements, ensuring continuity for refugees already in the country.

The directive is precise: it concerns only *reactive* services to already‑admitted refugees, not the *forward* resettlement of new refugees who were admitted after the suspension. Consequently, the government is prevented from terminating cooperative agreements or abandoning service obligations for those refugees solely because the executive order has suspended admissions to the country for new entrants.

In practice, the State Department is required to negotiate with the three cooperative‑agreement holders that were formerly deemed unavailable. The governmental order is explicit that the cooperative agreements must be “necessary for reception and placement services described in § 1522.” The court emphasizes that failure to comply would likely expose the department to later liability for violating statutory preconditions of § 1522 and the APA.

## Effect of the Stay on District‑Court Injunctions and on Plaintiffs’ Policy Remedies

The stay order retains all *pre‑existing* district‑court injunctions in their entirety, except for the portion of the February 28 and March 24 orders that the Court notes as “partially void” under the APA challenge. Thus, the preliminary injunction that barred enforcement of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of *Executive Order* 14163 remains in force, continuing to suspend refugee admissions. However, the stay also *vacates* the district court’s “administrative stay” issued July 18, 2025, and the earlier partial stay issued March 25, 2025. This means that the district court’s injunctions are no longer subject to the temporary holding of the administrative stay; they remain operative but may now be subject to a different preparatory framework under the appellate panel’s guidance.

For the plaintiffs, the stay thus preserves the *policy remedy* of the district court’s injunction: the suspension of refugee admissions continues to stand. At the same time, the Court’s direction to restore cooperative agreements somewhat mitigates the plaintiffs’ claim that the executive order and subsequent funding cuts unlawfully deprived refugees of mandatory services. The plaintiffs can no longer argue that the administration is simply refusing to deliver services; rather, they must contend that the government *chooses* not to secure and maintain a proper cooperative framework, thereby potentially violating § 1522 and the APA.

In effect, the stay keeps the “law as it stands” for the present moment: the executive order remains operative; the reopened cooperative agreements remain pending; and the district court’s injunction remains in place but is now potentially subject to a further challenge when the merits case finally resolves the APA question.

## Broader Implications for Executive Power over Refugee Admissions and Statutory Service Mandates

The Ninth Circuit’s decision illuminates the delicate boundary between presidential discretion and congressional statutory mandates. On one hand, the Court reaffirms that the President enjoys “vast discretion” to suspend the admission of refugees within narrow statutory boundaries—consistent with *Trump v. Hawaii* and subsequent memoranda. The stay confirms that a broad, purely policy‑based justification, such as an executive finding of “detriment,” is sufficient for a stay, unless the case raises deeper constitutional questions.

On the other hand, the decision underscores that congressional language invoking a *duty* for reception and placement services cannot be easily dismissed as mere authorization. Although the Court chooses not to grant an early stay on the APA claim, the Court’s direction to restore cooperative agreements looks forward to the premise that § 1522 contains a statutory requirement—at least a “relational” requirement to provide pre‑admission or post‑admission services. The court’s signposts suggest that any future merits ruling will have to grapple with whether § 1522’s statutory verbs (e.g., “shall”) impose enforceable obligations or merely grant flexibility.

In practice, the order places pressure on the Department of State and the Office of Refugee Resettlement to demonstrate that they have adequate funding and a viable administrative framework to deliver the mandated services. Failure to do so could expose the administration to additional litigation. Moreover, the stay’s nuance—allowing a partial stay on the APA challenge but an unequivocal stay on the executive‑order challenge—creates a jurisprudential platform for future sources to argue about reservation of power in immigration.

For the Ninth Circuit, the *Pacito* order illustrates a classic appellate strategy: adopt a pragmatic interim order that preserves the status quo while signaling a substantive policy analysis to be delivered later. Plaintiffs will benefit from a longer period to organize and gather evidence regarding the actual impact of the suspension on refugees and organizations, while the administration gains a window to stabilize its cooperative agreements—an effort that could ultimately shape how the final merits decision will address the interplay of executive prerogative and statutory guidance.
