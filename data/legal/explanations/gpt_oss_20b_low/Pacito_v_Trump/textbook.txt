Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# 1. Case Background and Procedural History

The Ninth Circuit’s “Pacito v. Trump” order examines the background and procedural history that brought the case to the appellate court. The case arose from a series of executive actions taken by President Donald J. Trump in January 2025 and the corresponding district‑court rulings that imposed stay provisions on those actions. Five key procedural layers shape the litigation: the President’s directives, the district court’s preliminary injunctions, the government’s consecutive requests for stays, the Ninth Circuit’s partial granting and later vacatur of the stay, and the current status of the appeal.

## Executive Order No. 14163 and its statutory basis under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

On January 20, 2025, the President promulgated Executive Order 14163, entitled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” The Order directs that entry of all refugees under the USRAP be suspended pending further investigation. The statutory authority cited by the President is 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which permits the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens “when he finds that the entry of any aliens … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The Order’s “findings” and the subsequent suspension are thus grounded in the President’s statutory powers under this immigration statute.

## Executive Order No. 14169 and the suspension of USRAP funding

A parallel administrative move occurred on January 24, 2025, when the State Department suspended all funding dedicated to the USRAP program pursuant to Executive Order 14169, “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” While this Order was ostensibly directed at foreign aid, its practical effect was the de facto shutdown of domestic resettlement operations for refugees, including the funding necessary to support recruitment, processing, and resettlement in the United States.

## Plaintiffs’ identity and legal standing (admitted refugees and partner NGOs)

The litigation is brought by three categories of plaintiffs:

1. Refugees who had already been admitted to the United States before the orders took effect;
2. Refugees who had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country at the time of the orders; and
3. Three non‑governmental organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide overseas processing for refugee applicants and domestic resettlement services for admitted refugees.

These plaintiffs claim that Executive Order 14163 violated the Refugee Act of 1980, which codifies the statutory framework for refugee admissions and treatments, and that Executive Order 14169, by defunding the program, violated the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) due to the lack of a formal rulemaking process.

## District court preliminary injunctions (February 25, 28, March 24 orders)

On February 25, 2025, the Western District of Washington, pursuant to an oral brief, issued a preliminary injunction that barred enforcement of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order 14163. The injunction effectively kept refugees from being re‑admitted under the suspended program. Following the Order’s termination of cooperative agreements on February 26, the district court clarified the scope of the injunction on February 28, indicating that the Order also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the court issued a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated.

The district court orders directly challenged the President’s actions and the administrative closure of the refugee program, prompting the government to seek remedies in the appellate court.

## Government’s stay requests and the Ninth Circuit’s partial grant of the March 25 stay

The government requested a stay of the February 28 injunction, arguing that the Order was unnecessary and that the President’s sovereign discretion under § 1182(f) should not be questioned. On March 25, the Ninth Circuit’s motions panel granted the stay **in part**, invoking the Supreme Court’s deference in *Trump v. Hawaii* with regard to executive immigration authority. The panel carved out a limited exception: the stay did not apply to refugees who had already been conditionally approved for admission by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services prior to January 20, 2025. The court, therefore, stayed the injunction for those refugees but upheld it for others.

## Subsequent administrative stay (July 18) and the Ninth Circuit’s vacatur of that stay

On July 14, 2025, the district court issued an order that established a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee its implementation. In response, the government filed an emergency motion on July 17, seeking clarification or amendment of the stay. The court responded on July 18, 2025, issuing an **administrative stay** of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order—essentially a pause on the procedural framework pending a judicial review.

When oral argument was scheduled for September 3, 2025, the Ninth Circuit granted the government’s request and **vacated** its own administrative stay issued July 18, 2025. The panel also vacated its March 25 stay (as amended by the April 21 and May 9 orders) and declared that the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions were to be **stayed in their entirety**, except as specifically noted.

## Current procedural posture of the Ninth Circuit appeal

The Ninth Circuit’s stay order is an *interim* measure pending a full opinion on the merits. It does not decide the substantive challenges—whether the executive orders violate the Refugee Act or the APA—but suspends the enforcement of the preliminary injunctions that the district court had issued in response to those challenges. The appellate court is now awaiting the submission of the panel’s detailed opinion, which the parties anticipate will elaborate on the legal standards applied, the statutory analyses of executive authority and statutory mandates, and the final disposition of the case.

This procedural history underscores the ongoing conflict between the executive branch’s asserted immigration authority, Congress’s statutory provisions concerning refugee reception and placement, and the district court’s attempt to balance those competing interests for the benefit of affected refugees and their supporting NGOs.

# 2. Legal Framework: Refugee Admission, the Refugee Act, and the APA

In *Pacito v. Trump* the parties confront a clash of statutory regimes that shape the permissible exercise of executive power over refugee admissions, the statutory duties that accompany those admissions, and the mechanisms by which courts may review agency actions that affect refugees. This chapter unpacks that framework, concentrating on the statutes that are directly cited in the opinion and the procedural doctrines that govern appellate and judicial‑review analysis.  

## The Refugee Act of 1980 and Its Continuation‑of‑Services Mandate

The Refugee Act of 1980 adopted the United Nations Convention and Codified it into the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Act established the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) as a permanent, statutory program for the admission of refugees. A key, often under‑emphasized feature of the Act is its “continuation‑of‑services” mandate: once a refugee is admitted, the United States retains an affirmative duty to provide certain reception, placement, and assistance services. In *Negusie v. Holder*, the Supreme Court described the Refugee Act as a “general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons” (555 U.S. at 520). Section 1522 of the INA, titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees,” reflects that continuity.  

Unlike sections of the INA that grant discretionary authority to the President (e.g., 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)), § 1522 is more prescriptive. It obligates certain agencies—primarily the Office of Refugee Resettlement (the Office of an Assistant Secretary of Health and Human Services for Program Development)—to provide employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and healthcare to newly admitted refugees, insofar as appropriations allow. Failure to provide those services in a timely manner can constitute a statutory defect in the administration of the refugee program. In *Pacito*, plaintiffs argue that the executive order’s suspension of refugee admissions and the subsequent defunding of cooperative agreements infringe that continuation‑of‑services duty.

## Statutory Authority for Suspending Refugee Admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

Section 1182(f) is the statutory vehicle for presidential suspension of refugee admissions. It authorizes the President, upon a finding that “the entry of any aliens or any class of aliens … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to issue a proclamation suspending entry of those aliens for a period deemed necessary. The statute is broad: it grants the President unchecked discretion to determine the class of aliens and the duration of suspension. The *Pacito* opinion cites *Trump v. Hawaii* to underscore the deference courts give to the President when exercising that power. The government claims it has satisfied the first prong of the *1182(f)* authorizing finding, which the court affirms. The issue is whether that power can be offset by statutory duties imposed by the Refugee Act. The court’s opinion separates those concerns, holding that the *1182(f)* authority is not invalidated by the Refugee Act; but the Refugee Act may still impose compliance obligations.

## Key APA Provisions (5 U.S.C. § 551, § 551a, § 552) Governing Judicial Review of Agency Action

The Administrative Procedure Act (APA) provides the federal judiciary with broad supervisory power over agency actions. In *Pacito*, the plaintiffs’ claim that the executive order and the defunding of cooperative agreements violated the APA rests on two principal theories:

1. **Lack of a Notice-and‑Comment Process** – Under § 551, an agency action must be supported by a written statement of facts, reasons, and a record of the proceedings on record. If the executive order or related funding decisions were taken without the requisite procedural steps, that could trigger a § 552(1) judicial review.

2. **Failure to Meet Substantive Public‑Interest Requirements** – § 551a mandates that when an agency imposes a restriction on entry or grants a benefit, it must conduct a rigorous, data‑driven review, assess alternatives, and describe the impact. For the refugees, the court examines whether the government complied with the statutory requirement to ensure that “the refugee program... prompts the necessary improvements to immigration laws and the processing of refugees.” (See *Negusie*, 555 U.S. at 520–21.) The *Pacito* panel concludes that the government may be failing to meet these requirements because it has discontinued funded assistance with no reasonable plan to replace it.

The APA also governs the procedural posture of the case: the district court’s preliminary injunctions and the appellate court’s stay orders are evaluated under § 552(4) (the delegation of the authority to the judicial branch to grant relief pending final agency action). The APA’s “rule of law” provides the framework for the judicial determination of whether an agency’s action is “arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to law.”

## Preliminary Injunction Standards and the Four‑Factor Test for Stays (Nken v. Holder)

When a district court grants a preliminary injunction, it weighs the prospective merits of the case and the public interest. In *Nken v. Holder*, the Supreme Court codified a four‑factor test for granting a stay pending appeal:

1. **Likelihood of Success on the Merits** – The court first asks whether the party seeking the stay is likely to prevail. In *Pacito*, the panel recognises a high likelihood of success for the government on the *1182(f)* claim, but not on the APA claim, because the statutory duty to provide services is not clear‑cut.

2. **Irreparable Harm** – The second factor concerns whether the party would suffer irreparable injury if the injunction (or preliminary order) remains in effect. The plaintiffs claim such harm because they could be denied resettlement assistance; the court quantifies that injury in its leave‑to‑file discretionary calculation.

3. **Balance of Hardship** – The third factor pits the injury to the applicant against the injury to the third party—in this case, the public interest in preventing large‑scale refugee arrivals. The court notes that the public is likely harmed by a government that fails to provide the statutory services needed for resettlement.

4. **Public Interest** – The fourth factor weighs the public’s interests in national security, economic impact, and humanitarian considerations.

The panel in *Pacito* applies this test to decide which parts of the district court’s injunctions to keep in place. By vacating some orders (the prior stays) and issuing a stay pending appeal, the court tries to strike a balance between the different interests. For *Pacito*, the court leans heavily toward the public interest in enabling the President’s asserted “detriment” finding.

## Differentiating Merits Review of Policy versus Statutory Compliance

The opinion repeatedly distinguishes between a review of *policy* (the presidential claim that refugees would harm national interests) and *statutory compliance* (the Refugee Act’s required services). The court holds that deciding the policy origin of the executive order is faculty reserved for the President, and that is a question the courts evict, citing *Trump v. Hawaii*. The merits review of whether the President’s finding was legitimate is “not the question” in a *1182(f)* context. Conversely, reviewing whether the executive’s administrative actions violate or comply with statutory mandates such as § 1522 is a separate, valid question. The court therefore holds that the merits review can be conducted on the APA side but not on the *1182(f)* side.

In practical terms, the *Pacito* order proceeds on what the appellate court can remedy immediately. It partially enjoins the executive’s enforcement of the suspension to protect refugees’ statutory rights until the merits of the *1182(f)* claim are decided in a hearing in Washington that will occur after the Supreme Court’s hearing in October. The court also directs the government to reinstated cooperative agreements so that refugees receive the statutory reception and placement services, thereby addressing the statutory compliance question while allowing the policy dispute to continue.

Understanding how these regimes interact is essential for reading and anticipating the ultimate resolution of the case.

# 3. Analysis of the President’s Authority to Suspend Refugee Admissions

## Interpretation of “detrimental to the interests of the United States” in § 1182(f)

The statutory threshold for a presidential suspension of alien entry is a single, procedural finding: the President must determine that “the entry of any aliens or any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Unlike many executive‑sanctioned restrictions, § 1182(f) does not define the term “detrimental” nor prescribes a methodology for arriving at the finding. The Ninth Circuit in *Pacito v. Trump* therefore held that the executive order is sufficient on this ground if the President’s articulation in his proclamation or order “conveys that the entry would be detrimental,” regardless of whether the Congress or a court has elaborated on that finding. The Court relied on that inference from *Trump v. Hawaii* where the Supreme Court emphasized the plain meaning of the language and noted that the President “unquestionably fulfilled the requirement” when he decided to suspend refugee admissions. The Ninth Circuit underscored that a showing of detrimental interest is a beginning step; it does not require the President to anticipate or quantify the manner in which the interest would be harmed. Thus, the objective weight of a President’s written finding—here, the executive order’s language—suffices to meet the statutory premise.

The Court’s analysis is purely textual. It refrained from searching for extrinsic evidence such as classified intelligence reports or economic studies beyond the executive’s own statement. The standard is deference; the district court was permitted only to accept the administrative finding as presumptively legitimate, not to conduct a “searching inquiry” into the President’s merit. That is the operative deference in *Hawaii*: the court cannot query the policy rationale except to verify that the finding of detrimental interest itself is present. The Ninth Circuit applied that standard to the executive order, concluding that the citation of “detrimental interests” satisfied the statutory requirement, thereby giving the President a broad, uncontested basis for suspension.

## Deference doctrine as applied in *Trump v. Hawaii*

*Hawaii* has become the touchstone for discussing deference in immigration matters. The Court held that the President, when exercising the discretionary power under § 1182(f), sits “at the top of the hierarchy” of immigration authorities. The Ninth Circuit echoed that language, noting that the statutory allocation of power “exudes deference to the President and vests him with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality] Act.”

Deference entails two primary effects. First, the courts treat the President’s findings as presumptively valid, prompting judicial intervention only if the executive action plainly violates a clear, unambiguous statutory or constitutional limitation. Second, when the statutory language confers discretion, courts do not substitute their own policy judgments for those of the executive. In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit applied this deference to argue that the President’s finding that refugee admissions would be detrimental met the statutory standard. The Court reasoned that *Hawaii* makes no sense at all to question that finding absent evidence that the President was violating law or acting in bad faith. Accordingly, the executive order remains within the “wide discretion” the Statute grants the President, and any judicial review of the finding is limited to a formal determination of statutory compliance.

The Court’s use of *Hawaii* is precise: it quotes the Supreme Court’s language on the “praiseful” nature of the deprivation clause, and it emphasizes that the discretionary power in § 1182(f) should not be converted into a rule that “the executive can do whatever it likes.” The Ninth Circuit therefore emphasized that a refusal to enforce the executive order would be a violation of the President’s statutory authority. The deference doctrine in this case confines the Court’s scrutiny to an “in rem” review of the executive action (i.e., compliance with the literal text of § 1182(f)) and precludes a substantive examination of the President’s policy rationale.

## Scope of the President’s power to impose entry restrictions

The statutory text of § 1182(f) allows the President to:

1. suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens “as immigrants or non‑immigrants”; and
2. “impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions” he may deem appropriate.

The Ninth Circuit noted that these powers are “broad.” The President is not required to specify the mechanism of the restriction; he may suspend the entire program or impose a selective quota, a visa limitation, or any circumstance that effectively bars entry. The Court stressed that the statutory language imposes no cap on the duration or extent of the suspension; the President “may, for such period as he shall deem necessary.” Consequently, the executive order’s blanket suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) falls squarely within that authority. The specific legal issue—whether the President can deny entry to refugees under a program that still satisfies congressional mandating statutes—was resolved by establishing that the statutory permission is independent of any congressional constraints on the *method* of entry, so long as the President’s declaration satisfies the single pleading requirement.

The Ninth Circuit also rejected the notion that Congress could limit the President’s discretion by passing statutes that modify the Refugee Act of 1980. The Court noted that the Refugee Act, while setting ongoing obligations in the context of “priority entry” and “permanent residence,” does not restrict the President’s *temporary* suspension authority. Therefore, insofar as the Refugee Act imposes ongoing commitments, the President still retains the power to suspend refugee admissions in response to a “detrimental” finding. The Court’s conclusion hinges on a “plain interpretation” of § 1182(f), placing the President in a position to unilaterally block refugee entry pending any further statutory findings or congressional action.

## Congressional intent and statutory limits regarding refugee admissions

Congressestate that the Refugee Act of 1980, codified in various sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, creates a *program* of asylum and refugee admissions that continues as long as “the relevant statutory provisions are satisfied.” It also imposes an ongoing duty to provide resettlement, health, and financial assistance, but it does not intrude on the President’s duty to determine whether the admission of refugees should continue at all. The Ninth Circuit therefore viewed the congressional intent as deliberately delegating discretion to the executive for matters of national security and foreign policy.

The Court paid particular attention to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which specifies Service provisions for refugees who have already entered, and to the fact that the President, by ­suspending the program entirely, effectively neutralizes that statutory program. Because § 1182(f) does not reference limitation clauses embedded in the Refugee Act, the Court held that the President’s suspension did not conflict with the Refugee Act’s ongoing obligation or with the “hostile” reinterpretation of *Hawaii*. In addition, the Court considered that Congress, through appropriations and the legislative history surrounding the Refugee Act, has not legislated any limitation to the President’s discretion over entry restrictions. The Ninth Circuit thus concluded that the broad statutory language reflects congressional willingness to allow the President to suspend refugee admissions entirely when it deems national interest compromised.

## Likelihood of success on the merits for a challenge to the suspension

The Ninth Circuit, as the internal briefing and oral argument progressed, signaled that the district court’s preliminary injunctions will likely be overturned on merits. The primary reason? The Court recognises a “strong showing” that the President will “likely prevail” on a merits challenge to the executive order. The Court explicitly said that “the government is likely to prevail” vis‑à‑vis the executive order’s compliance with § 1182(f).

The Court also noted that the President’s assertion that the suspension “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” satisfies the statute, and that the Court would not question that finding’s validity. The fact that the President’s order is fully consonant with the statutory framework, and that deference is required, tips the scales decisively. The Court’s remaining factors in the stay analysis—irreparable injury, potential injury to third parties, and public interest—were outweighed in part because the government’s service obligations under § 1522 were either unfilled or unmet, generating some injury to refugees but not to the broader public. The Court’s brief order nevertheless directed the U.S. to hire new resettlement agencies “to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.” That order reflects the Court’s desire to protect refugees' statutory rights while acknowledging that the suspension is a legitimate exercise of the President’s power.

In sum, the Ninth Circuit forecasted an outcome in favor of the executive: the suspension is “unlikely to be overturned” because (1) the statutory framework is satisfied; (2) deference precludes a substantive review of the President’s policy reasoning; and (3) congressional statutes do not limit the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions. The possibility that the merits challenge will succeed remains minimal, to the point that even a partial stay is unlikely to remain in place.

# 4. The Statutory Duty to Provide Reception and Placement Services under § 1522  

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit was asked to decide whether the plaintiffs could compel the federal government—through a preliminary injunction—to continue furnishing the “reception and placement” services that Congress has legislatively authorized for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States when the President suspended the Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP).  The court ultimately held that § 1522 does **not** create a judicially enforceable duty to provide those services.  The following discussion explains why the statute’s structure and wording, combined with the APA’s review standards, support that conclusion.

## Textual Analysis of § 1522’s Title and Language

Section 1522 is titled **“Authorization for Programs for Domestic Resettlement of and Assistance to Refugees.”**  The use of the word *authorization* is significant.  In statutory interpretation, an *authorization* grants the executive branch permission to act; it does not compel action.  Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed., 2024) defines *authorization* as “the act of authorizing; the permission or consent given to do something.”  Hence, the statutory language signals that Congress is *allowing* the government to create and administer resettlement programs *if* it chooses to do so, rather than *requiring* it.

The bulk of § 1522 consists of subordinate clauses that detail how the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development, through the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR), may administer various grant, contract, and cooperative‑agreement programs.  Each clause is prefaced with the wording *“the Director is authorized…”* or *“the Secretary is authorized…,”* again reinforcing the permissive nature of the statute.

## Role of § 1522(a)(1)(A) and Its Conditional “To the Extent of Available Appropriations” Clause

Section 1522(a)(1)(A) contains the *most* traditionally “mandatory‑looking” provisions.  It requires the Director to “make available sufficient resources” for a set of services—employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and gender‑equitable training—*to the extent of available appropriations*.  The phrase *“to the extent of available appropriations”* is a *condition* that limits the statutory obligation to the resources Congress has actually appropriated.  Because the statute itself does not require any funds to be made available beyond what Congress appropriates, it cannot be interpreted as a blanket obligation that will persist even if the Treasury disburses no money.

Moreover, the “shall” language in § 1522(a)(1)(A) applies only *if* the Director chooses to use the discretionary funding provisions of §§ 1522(b)–(e).  The clause is effectively a *conditional mandate*:  it states what the Director must do if the Director decides to progress with the resettlement programs.  The statutory purpose is not to guarantee a program but to provide a framework for possible programs.

## Distinguishing Discretionary Funding Provisions (§ 1522(b)–(e)) from Mandatory Requirements

The proclamations in §§ 1522(b)–(e) are the statutory mechanisms by which the government actually *delivers* funding to resettlement agencies.  These sections authorize the ORR to:

* Enter contracts and cooperative agreements with nonprofits (§ 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii));
* Approve project grants for refugee services (§ 1522(c));
* Provide cash assistance and medical support to refugees § 1522(e);
* Undertake special duties such as overseeing unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)).

Each provision begins: “the Director *is authorized* to grant contracts or to award …”  The text again signals discretion.  Courts have consistently held that the “authorizing” language does not impose a *duty* but rather *permits* administrative action.  The mere existence of statutory authorizing language does not obligate the executive to act.

The *mandatory* thrust of § 1522(a) is thus carved out by the *conditional* nature of the funding provisions.  When Congress either does not appropriate the necessary funds or the executive decides not to pursue the programs, the statutory duty effectively lapses.

## Interpretation of the APA in Assessing Compliance with § 1522 Obligations

If the plaintiffs had alleged that the government breached a *statutory duty*, the APA would apply through the *abstract review* standard (5 U.S.C. § 5555).  Under this standard, the court must assess whether the agency “rationally concluded that the action was the proper one to take” (5 U.S.C. § 5525(a)(2)).  A *rational* conclusion includes a legitimate *policy judgment* about whether the agency should or could provide a program.

In *Ira*, 892 F.2d 146 (D.C. Cir. 1989), the court held that the APA does not compel the executive to enact a program if the statutory language is discretionary.  ESA Office for the Administration of the ACLU in *Pacito* parallels that analysis: the court noted that the APA does not require the agency to act in a particular way when the statute itself does not contain a *mandatory* provision.  Therefore, the APA provides no remedy for plaintiffs seeking to compel the government to act under § 1522, because the statute does not assert a *binding* obligation.

## Factual Evidence of the Government’s Ability to Provide Services

In the record, the Department of State terminated all cooperative agreements that had provided reception and placement services to refugees in February 2025.  The government indicated to the court on March 10 that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency.”  Since the presentation of that evidence, the government has shown no substantive progress toward reestablishing the services.  The Ninth Circuit, recognizing the *closure* of the cooperative‑agreement apparatus and the absence of a new agency, concluded that the executive branch cannot presently provide the services that § 1522 would hypothetically requisition.

Because the executive has already discontinued the authorized programs and has not demonstrated a clear path to restoring them, the courts find that the *process* for delivering the authorized services is effectively *inoperable*.  Under those facts, any alleged  *duty* would be illusory: the statutory responsibility was contingent on the existence of operational programs, which the government has not maintained.

## Arguments for and Against a Judicially Enforceable Duty

### Arguments for an Enforceable Duty  

1. **Congressional Intent**: Proponents argue that Congress, by passing the Refugee Act and the comprehensive § 1522, intended to ensure refugees receive humane, comprehensive services as part of their resettlement.  
2. **Executive Responsibility**: The defendants might claim the statutory language, especially the “shall” provisions, imposes an implicit duty to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been accepted into the United States.  
3. **Policy Stability**: Persistently suspending services could undermine the Refugee Act’s purpose and violate the *Rehabilitation Act*’s non‑discrimination mandate.

### Arguments against an Enforceable Duty  

1. **Statutory Mood**: The statutory title and the ubiquitous use of *authorized* suggest a *permission* framework rather than a *demand*.  
2. **Conditionality**: Section 1522(a)(1)(A) conditions any obligation on “available appropriations,” meaning the duty can only arise if Congress appropriates the needed funds.  
3. **Executive Discretion**: The APA permits the executive to exercise discretion over whether and how to implement a program, thereby eliminating a *mandate* that could be enforced judicially.  
4. **Practice of Precedent**: Courts have consistently refused to impose a *statutory duty* where the statute provides discretionary authority.  In *Ira* and *PACAR* (People), the courts emphasized that the statutory text is the vital determinant of enforceability, not the policy ends sought.  
5. **Political Question Doctrine**: The suspension and termination of refugee services, especially in light of the President’s explicit executive order, are considered a political question.  The political question doctrine applies when an issue is “deeply divided, or when it is inadequately specified by law,” which the 9th Circuit found subsisting in this case.

## Conclusion

The Ninth Circuit’s holding in *Pacito* emphasized that § 1522 contains no judicially enforceable duty to provide reception and placement services.  The statutory language authorizes, but does not require, the executive to fund and deliver those services.  The *conditional* nature of § 1522(a) and the discretionary nature of §§ 1522(b)–(e) collectively prevent any “mandated” obligation that a court could compel.  Consequently, plaintiffs seeking to force the government, through a preliminary injunction, to provide those services were unable to satisfy the APA’s rational‑conclusion standard or find a statutory duty imposing such an obligation.

The case underscores a general principle: **where a statute uses permissive words such as “authorizes” and conditions obligation on available appropriations, it does not create a court‑enforceable duty.**  The executive’s discretion, coupled with the need for congressional appropriations, limits judicial intervention in federal programs.  When the government terminates cooperative agreements and fails to replace them, the *enforced* standard does not compel the executive to act in the absence of a statutory mandate.

# 5. Ninth Circuit’s Decision on the Stay and Its Implications

The Ninth Circuit’s order issued September 12, 2025, represents the Court’s interim control over the litigation in *Pacito v. Trump*. While a full merits opinion remains pending, the stay order itself crystallizes the Court’s assessment of the stay‑analysis, the relative merits of the plaintiffs’ challenges, and the Court’s imperative to preserve the statutory framework for refugee reception and placement services. In this chapter we unpack the Court’s holding, explain the legal underpinnings of each element of the order, and examine its practical effects on the parties and on the broader regulatory landscape.

## Application of the Four‑Factor Stay Analysis

The Court’s decision relies on the established four‑factor framework from *Nken v. Holder*. The panel explicitly enumerated the factors and progressed through them in order:

1. **Strong showing that the stay applicant is likely to succeed on the merits** – The Court noted that the government “is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions.” The Court summarized that the statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f)–(a) gives the President an almost unbounded discretion to suspend the entry of any class of aliens if the President “finds” the entry detrimental. The Court reiterated the deference given to the President’s “finding” in *Trump v. Hawaii*, emphasizing that the statutory language is “clear” and fully empowers the President to suspend the USRAP. As such, the government has a “strong showing” of likely success.

2. **Irreparable injury to the stay applicant if the stay is denied** – The Court evaluated that the defendants (the government, the State Department, and its cooperative agreements) would suffer irreparable harm if the stay were denied, principally because the district court’s injunctions directly barred the administration from enforcing the executive order. The Court judged that this injury exceeded any potential injury to plaintiffs, stating that the government’s ability to “implement the President’s policy decision” was “on an irreparable level.”

3. **Potential injurious impact on the opposing parties** – Here the Court balanced the harms to plaintiffs (refugees and immigrant‑service organizations) against the government’s interest. The Court found that a stay would “substantially injure” the plaintiffs’ policy remedies by maintaining the suspension of refugee admissions and funding, but the Court deemed the government’s interest in preserving its discretionary agenda to outweigh that injury. The reasoning references *Hawaii* again, noting the deference to the President in immigration matters.

4. **Public interest** – The Court merged the second and third factors, concluding that the public interest “lies with the United States” because the executive order’s sovereign purpose and the wide discretion granted to the President align with national security assessments. The Court explicitly referenced *Hawaii*’s emphasis on the “broad discretion” conferred on the President.

The Court made it clear that the first two factors dominate the analysis, and because the government satisfied those, it was essentially a “no‑show” to win the stay, regardless of potential harm to the plaintiffs or the public.

## Conclusion that the Government Is Likely to Prevail on the Executive‑Order Challenge

The Court’s categorical statement that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to *Executive Order* 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions hinges on an unqualified reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The order argues that the President’s pronouncement of “detriment to the interests of the United States” constitutes a complete justification for a suspension, as the statute permits any duration the President deems “necessary.” While the Court does not agree that the President’s determination was scientifically or administratively justified (that would be a merits question), the Court holds that the President *has* the authority to issue the order, and any review of the reasonableness or practical impact of that suspension is a question of policy, not of legality.

Because the Court recognizes the longstanding deference afforded to executive immigration actions, it refrains from probing the “substance” of the President’s “finding.” This perspective faithfully follows *Trump v. Hawaii*, where the Court emphasized that the President’s “finding” alone, absent a constitutional violation, suffices to invalidate judicial scrutiny. Accordingly, the Court concluded that the government would almost certainly win the merits challenge, rendering the stay “essentially warranted.”

## Finding That the Government May Not Prevail on the APA Challenge to § 1522 Services

While the Court granted the stay on the executive‑order question, it withheld the stay concerning the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenge to the interpretation of § 1522. The Court’s opinion accentuates two themes:

1. **Statutory Designation of Authority vs. Obligation** – The Court differentiated the “authorization” language found in § 1522(b)–(e) from the “mandatory” phrasing in § 1522(a). In particular, the Court highlighted that the statutory purpose of § 1522 is to *authorise* programs for refugees provided that appropriations exist, not to compel the government to spend. The clause “to the extent of available appropriations” is consequently a *financial* limitation, not a *policy* mandate.

2. **Presidential Discretion in Funding** – The Court noted that whether or not the government chooses to fund refugee programs rests upon discretionary budgeting decisions that reflect priorities and resource constraints. Consequently, a failure to fund a particular cooperative agreement – whether voluntarily or due to funding constraints – does not automatically render the government in violation of the APA or of § 1522. Merely delayed termination or stability of existing cooperative agreements does not, in the Court’s view, constitute unlawful retrogression.

Because the plaintiffs’ challenge rests on the argument that the government *must* provide reception and placement services pursuant to § 1522, the Court sees this as a *mandate* issue rather than an *administrative process* issue, and thus does not grant a stay that would force the government to remit existing services. The Court released the government's liability to a standard of *quiet compliance* with the APA until and unless a merits ruling addresses whether the statutory provisions constitute a *mandate* for the government to retain or replace its cooperative agreements.

## Direction to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements Necessary for Reception and Placement Services

Although the Court indicated that the APA challenge may survive the stay, it also recognized that the administration has a statutory duty, under § 1522, to provide reception and placement services to refugees who had been admitted before the suspension went into effect. The Court therefore directed the government to “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” These services must mirror the “usual and customary services” afforded under former agreements, ensuring continuity for refugees already in the country.

The directive is precise: it concerns only *reactive* services to already‑admitted refugees, not the *forward* resettlement of new refugees who were admitted after the suspension. Consequently, the government is prevented from terminating cooperative agreements or abandoning service obligations for those refugees solely because the executive order has suspended admissions to the country for new entrants.

In practice, the State Department is required to negotiate with the three cooperative‑agreement holders that were formerly deemed unavailable. The governmental order is explicit that the cooperative agreements must be “necessary for reception and placement services described in § 1522.” The court emphasizes that failure to comply would likely expose the department to later liability for violating statutory preconditions of § 1522 and the APA.

## Effect of the Stay on District‑Court Injunctions and on Plaintiffs’ Policy Remedies

The stay order retains all *pre‑existing* district‑court injunctions in their entirety, except for the portion of the February 28 and March 24 orders that the Court notes as “partially void” under the APA challenge. Thus, the preliminary injunction that barred enforcement of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of *Executive Order* 14163 remains in force, continuing to suspend refugee admissions. However, the stay also *vacates* the district court’s “administrative stay” issued July 18, 2025, and the earlier partial stay issued March 25, 2025. This means that the district court’s injunctions are no longer subject to the temporary holding of the administrative stay; they remain operative but may now be subject to a different preparatory framework under the appellate panel’s guidance.

For the plaintiffs, the stay thus preserves the *policy remedy* of the district court’s injunction: the suspension of refugee admissions continues to stand. At the same time, the Court’s direction to restore cooperative agreements somewhat mitigates the plaintiffs’ claim that the executive order and subsequent funding cuts unlawfully deprived refugees of mandatory services. The plaintiffs can no longer argue that the administration is simply refusing to deliver services; rather, they must contend that the government *chooses* not to secure and maintain a proper cooperative framework, thereby potentially violating § 1522 and the APA.

In effect, the stay keeps the “law as it stands” for the present moment: the executive order remains operative; the reopened cooperative agreements remain pending; and the district court’s injunction remains in place but is now potentially subject to a further challenge when the merits case finally resolves the APA question.

## Broader Implications for Executive Power over Refugee Admissions and Statutory Service Mandates

The Ninth Circuit’s decision illuminates the delicate boundary between presidential discretion and congressional statutory mandates. On one hand, the Court reaffirms that the President enjoys “vast discretion” to suspend the admission of refugees within narrow statutory boundaries—consistent with *Trump v. Hawaii* and subsequent memoranda. The stay confirms that a broad, purely policy‑based justification, such as an executive finding of “detriment,” is sufficient for a stay, unless the case raises deeper constitutional questions.

On the other hand, the decision underscores that congressional language invoking a *duty* for reception and placement services cannot be easily dismissed as mere authorization. Although the Court chooses not to grant an early stay on the APA claim, the Court’s direction to restore cooperative agreements looks forward to the premise that § 1522 contains a statutory requirement—at least a “relational” requirement to provide pre‑admission or post‑admission services. The court’s signposts suggest that any future merits ruling will have to grapple with whether § 1522’s statutory verbs (e.g., “shall”) impose enforceable obligations or merely grant flexibility.

In practice, the order places pressure on the Department of State and the Office of Refugee Resettlement to demonstrate that they have adequate funding and a viable administrative framework to deliver the mandated services. Failure to do so could expose the administration to additional litigation. Moreover, the stay’s nuance—allowing a partial stay on the APA challenge but an unequivocal stay on the executive‑order challenge—creates a jurisprudential platform for future sources to argue about reservation of power in immigration.

For the Ninth Circuit, the *Pacito* order illustrates a classic appellate strategy: adopt a pragmatic interim order that preserves the status quo while signaling a substantive policy analysis to be delivered later. Plaintiffs will benefit from a longer period to organize and gather evidence regarding the actual impact of the suspension on refugees and organizations, while the administration gains a window to stabilize its cooperative agreements—an effort that could ultimately shape how the final merits decision will address the interplay of executive prerogative and statutory guidance.

# 6. Judge Lee’s Concurring‑in‑Part and Dissenting‑in‑Part

## Chapter Description  

In the Ninth Circuit’s stay order in *Pacito v. Trump*, Judge Lee joined the panel in part and dissented in part. His concurrence affirms the court’s deference to the President’s immigration authority, yet he diverges on whether the federal government is legally required to provide reception and placement services to refugees already in the United States. This chapter unpacks Judge Lee’s reasoning, focusing on how he interprets § 1522 of the Immigration and Nationality Act and what that means for the court’s role in enforcing statutory mandates.

---

## Judge Lee’s concurrence on executive authority to suspend admissions  

Judge Lee’s concurrence begins by agreeing that the President possesses broad discretion under § 1182(f) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). He cites *Trump v. Hawaii* to emphasize that the statutory language “exudes deference” and “vests” the President with sufficient power to suspend refugee admissions.  

In his view, the President’s determination that “entry into the United States of refugees under the U.S.R.A.P. would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” satisfies the statutory requirement that “the President find…that the entry…would be detrimental.” Lee observes that the executive order’s suspension of refugee admissions is therefore “clear, and does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.” Consequently, he agrees with the majority that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the challenge to Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions.  

Because of this deference, Lee endorses the court’s decision to stay the district court’s injunctions regarding the suspension of refugee entry. In this portion of the opinion, he sees no reason to sustain an injunction that would hinder the President’s policy decision.

---

## Judge Lee’s dissent regarding the mandatory nature of § 1522 service obligations  

While agreeing with the majority on the suspension issue, Judge Lee diverges concerning the statutory duty to provide services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority argues that § 1522 imposes “mandatory” requirements for reception and placement services, therefore warranting an injunction to compel the State Department to reinstate funding. 

Lee contests that interpretation. He conducts a textual analysis of § 1522, concluding that the statutory language is “largely discretionary” and that it “authorizes” rather than “mandates” the programs. He repeatedly draws attention to the section’s title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the semantic distinction between “authorization” and “mandate.”  

He bases his dissent on the argument that Congress, by titling the provision and using the word “authorize,” has merely granted permission to the executive for these programs, not imposed a binding obligation. Therefore, a court lacks authority to compel the government to provide services that Congress has not explicitly required.

---

## Interpretation of § 1522 as an authorization rather than a requirement  

### Title and language  

Lee stresses that the heading of § 1522 is critical: it frames the section as an authorizing instrument. He points to the passage “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” as revealing the intended purpose of § 1522—to allow the executive branch to design and fund programs as it deems appropriate.  

### The use of “shall” versus “authorize”  

Even where the text contains the statutory keyword “shall,” Lee notes that the phrase is typically coupled with qualifiers that cushion the mandatory force. For example, § 1522(a)(1)(A) states: “to the extent of available appropriations.” This limiting phrase indicates that any obligation is subject to Congress’s appropriation decisions. Similarly, § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) discusses the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies—forms that inherently rely on agency discretion rather than a statutory mandate.

---

## Discussion of how statutory structure (a § b–e) signals discretionary spending  

Lee examines the internal organization of § 1522:

1. **Section (a) – Conditions and consideration.**  
   This part lists “shall” obligations—employment training, English language training, cash assistance—but it is in the context of “In providing assistance under this section [i.e., the discretionary funding provisions under § 1522(b)–(e)].” Thus, the requirements are conditional on the government choosing to spend those funds.

2. **Sections (b)–(e) – Discretionary funding provisions.**  
   These subsections grant the Director or Secretary the authority to allocate funding under various program items: employment training, cash assistance, medical assistance, and services for unaccompanied minors. The recurring phrase “the Director is authorized” underscores that these are initiatives the executive can elect to create or discontinue as it deems fit, again subject to appropriations.

Lee’s reading is that the statutory structure demonstrates a deliberate separation between conditions (the “shall” provisions) and the discretionary authority to provide fundable programs. The language thus signals to the courts that Congress intended the executive to have latitude in how it implements refugee services, rather than imposing a hard-and-fast obligation.

---

## Implications for future enforcement actions and judicial deference to agency discretion  

Judge Lee’s dissent has several broader implications for how courts approach statutory mandates that relate to agency discretion:

1. **Limits on judicial intervention.**  
   Courts should refrain from ordering the executive to perform programs that Congress has merely authorized. This protects agency autonomy and respects the separation of powers.

2. **Deference doctrine in immigration.**  
   The dissent reinforces the standard set in *Trump v. Hawaii*: that courts traditionally grant broad deference to the executive in immigration matters, except where statutory language is unambiguously mandatory.

3. **Potential future litigation.**  
   If subsequent challenges arise over the availability or scope of refugee services—particularly when there is a dispute over whether funding was actually provided—judicial relief will likely focus on ensuring that the executive has reached compliance with a statutory *obligation* rather than a *permission*. If the court determines, as Judge Lee argues, that Congress has not imposed an obligation, the government may be able to shift service provision to partners or even suspend it pending appropriations.

4. **Policy discretion versus statutory duty.**  
   The Leslie dissent underscores the practical reality that agencies must balance policy goals with legal constraints. In practice, this means that courts must carefully parse statutory language to discern whether an agency is bound to perform a specific function or whether it has latitude to decide how, or even whether, to perform that function.

---

Through this concurring‑in‑part and dissenting‑in‑part contribution, Judge Lee clarifies that while the President may lawfully suspend refugee admissions, the federal government is not strictly compelled by the INA to allocate or deliver reception and placement services to refugees who have already entered the United States. This distinction is essential for legal practitioners when assessing the scope of judicial relief available in immigration‑related cases involving statutory funding provisions.
