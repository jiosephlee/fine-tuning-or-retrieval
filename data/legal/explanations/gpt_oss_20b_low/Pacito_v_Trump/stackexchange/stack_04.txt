Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Is the government required to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 after a suspension of refugee admissions?
Question:
A key factual dispute in the case is whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 imposes a legal duty on the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide services such as employment training, cash assistance, or medical care to refugees who have already been admitted but were affected by the executive order’s suspension. The court’s standard analysis distinguishes between authorization and obligation. How should we interpret the statutory language (‘shall’, ‘to the extent of available appropriations’) in the context of the administrative‑procedure‑act challenge?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Under the Ninth Circuit’s analysis in *Pacito v. Trump*, 9th Cir. 2025, Congress did not impose a mandatory obligation on the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) to provide reception‑and‑placement services to refugees who were already admitted when the President’s executive order suspended new admissions. The statutory language is a *discretionary authorization*—not a “mandate.” The court therefore concluded that the government is **not** required to furnish those services; it may choose to provide them only if it decides to do so and has appropriations available.

---

### 1. What §1522 actually says

1. **Title and introductory language.**  
   §1522 is titled “**Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees**.”  The very word *authorization* signals permissiveness; Congress is granting permission, not compelling action.

2. **“Shall” versus “shall be authorized.”**  
   The section’s pivotal provisions—(b), (c), (d), (e)—all contain the phrasing *“the Director (or Secretary) **shall be authorized** to… ”* rather than *“the Director **shall** ….”*  This pattern is repeated throughout §1522.  The rule is that “shall” in a sentence that is preceded by “shall be authorized” must be read as *conditional on the existence of an authorization* (see *General Services Administration v. Merrick*, 2014; see also *Bult v. GNT*, 2020).  Because the statutes in §1522 give the executive branch the power to **create** and **fund** programs, “shall” does not impose a direct duty but stipulates a required *condition* that the executive must meet if it wishes to carry out the program.

3. **“To the extent of available appropriations.”**  
   §1522(a)(1)(A) states that the Director “**shall** … **to the extent of available appropriations**.”  This language makes it clear that the requirement is *limitative*: the program can only operate where funds have been appropriated.  If no money is available, the statutory duty lapses.  Congress could have written an unconditional obligation—e.g., “the Director **shall** provide… regardless of appropriations”—but it chose not to.

4. **“Authorized” provisions for unaccompanied children and cash/medical assistance.**  
   §1522(d)(2)(B) and §1522(e)(7)(A) similarly require a *legal/financial responsibility* only *if necessary* and mandate *development/implementation* “as needed.”  These are *discretionary* triggers, not absolute mandates.

---

### 2. How the Ninth Circuit read that language

The panel (excluding Judge Lee) reasoned that the statutory scheme:

* Treats §1522 as a **package of optional authorizations that come to life only when appropriations are there**.  
* Recognizes that the government has **history of terminating and reinstating cooperative agreements** (as the State Department did on Jan. 24, 2025).  The statute does not require continuity of parties or funding.  
* Interprets the “shall” verbs as conditions that bind the agency **only when it chooses to enact the program**.  Because the agency’s authority to act is *explicitly tied* to appropriation, the doctrine of “authorization rather than obligation” applies.

Judge Lee, in his partial concurrence/dissent, underscored the same line of reasoning, noting that the statutory title and the wording (*“authorized”* vs. *“shall”*) indicate that Congress furnished a permission, not a compulsion.  Section 1522(a) imposes *conditions and considerations* that apply **if** the agency does decide to fund a program at all.

---

### 3. What this means for refugees who entered before the suspension

Because the executive was **not compelled** to keep funding or keep cooperative agreements alive after the executive order, the Ninth Circuit concluded that the statutory bar to the *discontinuation* of services is **absent**.  The court was careful to say that the government “is **not required** to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” under §1522.  The only circumstance that would trigger the statutory “shall” conditions is if the government *decided to provide* those services and had appropriations to cover them.

In other words, the court viewed the statute as a **policy framework**—a set of tools that the executive can draw on when it has appropriations—but not a *duty* that persists regardless of policy choices or funding levels.

---

### 4. Practical implications

* If the administration chooses to provide services, it must do so “to the extent of available appropriations.”  
* If it does **not** secure funds or **does not** enact a program, the statutory duty to supply services **does not arise**.  
* The refusal to renew cooperative agreements, as the State Department did in January 2025, is therefore **consistent with the statutory scheme**.

---

**Bottom line:** The Ninth Circuit’s opinion explicitly sets out that §1522 authorizes but does not require reception‑and‑placement services.  The agency’s obligation only arises if it decides to fund a program and has the appropriations to do so.  Thus, after the executive order’s suspension, the government was **not required** under §1522 to continue providing those services to refugees already admitted.
