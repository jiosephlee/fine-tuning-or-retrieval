Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Are Employers Really Prohibited from Charging Penalties?  
Minnesota’s Wage Law and the Limits on Employee‑Requested Penalties  

---

## Why This Case Matters  

Bruce Cohen’s appeal to the Eighth Circuit is not about a single fine‑granted wage sheet—it is a litmus test for the shape of wage‑law enforcement in Minnesota and, by extension, in other states that rely on state‑level penalty mechanisms. The court’s holding that only the Commissioner may seek “average daily wage penalties” under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) and that federal statutory penalties under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) are likewise off‑limits to injured employees streamlines the doctrine of enforcement, but also raises questions about plaintiff standing in wage‑law cases nationally. If courts harden the boundary between **employee‑claimed** penalties and **government‑imposed** penalties, litigants may be forced to take a back‑seat role when a company’s policy ends but its past non‑compliance still carries a punitive potential.

---

## The Core Holding: Where Penalties Reside

### 1. The MPWA: Penalties Under the Commissioner’s Finger

The key statutory passage at issue is § 181.101(a) of the MPWA, which requires the Commissioner to **serve a demand** for unpaid wages and, if the employee fails to pay within ten days, to **charge and collect** a penalty. The analysis hinges on the plain‑meaning reading that the statute itself “provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages … as well as commissions.” The crux—and, as the 8th Circuit underscored—is that § 181.101 expressly limits the penalty‑imposing power to the Commissioner and makes no allowance for an employee to unilaterally apply for a penalty. In *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, the Minnesota Supreme Court held that where the statutory language is unambiguous, the legislative intent is presumed clear. The 8th Circuit therefore reaffirmed that the *only* recourse to average daily wage penalties is through the administrative apparatus, not through a civil court action filed by the aggrieved worker.

### 2. The MFLSA: A Federal Parallel with a Different Cut

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, § 686(d) allows for “statutory penalties” that are payable to the federal Commissioner. The 8th Circuit reminded us that “the statutory penalties imposed—unlike overtime wages—are payable to the Commissioner, not to the employee.” This carved‑out distinction is critical because it chips away at a plaintiff’s claim for punitive relief. The court hinted at *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*—a case that carved a path for employee‑initiated penalties on the federal level—but dismissed the similarity, noting the absence of any statutory authority in *Tier Two* wage‑law provisions to support a private penalty claim. The Eighth Circuit also vacated the district court’s summary‑judgment award on the MFLSA partiblity claim due to a lack of standing: had the court not been convinced that the plaintiff had a stake in the alleged penalty, it could not litigate *de facto* punitive claims.

### 3. The MWTA: No Penalties at All

Minnesota’s Wage Theft Act sets out *neither* a statutory avenue for employees to obtain penalties nor a mechanism for the state to reward employees with those penalties. The court, following precedent that a lack of statutory authority equates to an absolute blackout on punitive relief, outright dismissed Cohen’s MWTA claim.

---

## The Court’s Analytical Journey

1. **The first layer**: a literal, textual analysis of § 181.101(a). The court correctly identified the government’s exclusive role in the penalty mechanism. By not resorting to a purposive reading, the circuit kept the decision tightly in the confines of statutory interpretation.

2. **The second layer**: a jurisdictional mootness and standing analysis that is, in our view, more novel than surprising. The court dearly applied *Friends of the Earth*’s standing framework to a state‑level wage‑law scenario, yet concluded that “the requisite personal interest” had dissipated if the employer’s violation is no longer active. This raises the specter of “retroactive nullity” in wage‑law cases, where a plaintiff’s fully satisfied claim can effectively “euthanize” its own punitive claim.

3. **The third layer**: the adoption of a “no‑penalties” stance for the MWTA shows the court’s aversion to expanding punitive relief beyond what the legislature expressly passed. This may signal a stricter future for wage‑law plaintiffs who wish to pursue state‑level enforcement mechanisms.

---

## Implications for Wage‑Law Litigation

### 1. The Role of the Administrative Framework

The decision clarifies that the Commissioner—an often under‑utilized vehicle—may still be the sole avenue to secure punitive wages. Plaintiffs must therefore pivot from a courtroom strategy to an administrative one or collaborate with the Commissioner’s office in a paper‑based demand process. This creates an administrative hurdle that could be circumvented by a strategic partnership with the staff, but also demarcates a clear boundary of power.

### 2. The Myth of “Employee‑Sued” Penalties is Dethroned

Where as lawyers previously entertained the possibility that an employee could bring a civil action for penalties, the Eighth Circuit shows that the constitutional basis – *standing* – was untappable under the MPWA and that federal statutes do not provide for such penalties either. The *Friends of the Earth* case, which has been frequently cited in wage‑law advocacy, is effectively inapplicable because of the sheer difference in statutory language concerning the pivot of punitive payment.

### 3. Mootness and “Future‑Caution”

Even if a plaintiff believes that punitive penalties still loom, the court signals that the continued existence of the employer’s policy is a prerequisite for standing. The rule of “if the policy is eliminated, the claim is moot” might be treated similarly in the future. Plaintiffs must now argue that the employer’s violations are ongoing, which adds a qualitative dimension to wage‑law litigation—a shift from “was this unlawful? yes” to “will this remain unlawful? yes.”

---  

## Strategies for Plaintiffs: A Playbook

1. **File an Administrative Demand**  
   *Action*: Draft a demand letter to the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry (DLI) that follows § 181.101(a) procedures. The DLI’s enforcement actions will include punitive penalties payable directly to the employee.

2. **Seek Companion Enforcement (MFLSA)**  
   *Action*: File a federal complaint under 29 U.S.C. § 686(a) and request a “statutory penalty” that will be awarded to the federal Commissioner. However, be prepared to clarify that the penalty is not *directly* payable to the employee, but the action could still enhance the overall damages package.

3. **Consider Injunctions Early**  
   The court remarked that the plaintiff’s request for a “rescission” order was deemed “new” when amended. While this wing might be open in a new filing or a supplemental claim, it underscores the importance of a firm pre‑trial plan for injunctive claims, or even the possibility to bring them in a joint motion with the administrative demand.

4. **Maintain the Policy in Blue**  
   The plaintiff may need to document the employer’s current policy to secure standing. This could mean working to borrow evidence that the policy remains active or seeking proof that the employer still operates under the same overtime scheme.

5. **Explore Litigation With the Department of Labor**  
   In some states, the *right* to collect a penalty exists only as a *state action*. In Minnesota, the solution is *administrative*; in other jurisdictions, worker-centered enforcement might live in the state’s attorney general office or a dedicated enforcement division.

---

## A Broader Lens: How Minnesota’s Example Reflects a National Trend

Minnesota’s drawing of the line between employee‑fueled and government‑fueled penalties is not an isolated glitch. Morestates are pivoting on the principle: *government officials control the fiscal and punitive levers*, and *private plaintiffs should compel enforcement rather than impose it*. If this principle spreads across the federal courts—particularly in a post‑*Friends of the Earth* climate—wage‑law practitioners might begin to rely more on administrative remedies than on private federal claims. The Eighth Circuit’s handling dovetails with a broader shift in *clarity of enforcement pathways* that may reduce litigation costs but also reduce the amount of courts’ discretionary power to hand out punitive relief.

At the same time, the courts remain careful not to exclude cases entirely. The court’s refusal to dismiss the MFLSA claim on its face—only on exceptional standing issues—exemplifies a willingness to let the litigation proceed if the plaintiff can demonstrate an ongoing issue. This “mootness wipe‑out” approach is a double‑edged sword: on one hand it protects the courts from adjudicating claims where there is no tangible threat; on the other, it forces plaintiffs to keep the violation alive to secure relief.

---

## Conclusion

Bruce Cohen’s appeal is a useful case study in the *intersection of state policy, federal statute, and standing law*. The 8th Circuit reaffirmed that police officers of wage‑law penalties are not a general ticking, employee‑triggered stop‑gap; they are a government‑owned mechanism. The remedy for employees seeking punitive damages must go through that mechanism—be it the Minnesota Commissioner or the federal Commissioner. Employers can no longer be “tricked” into paying a single employee’s request for a penalty without proceeding through the official process. For plaintiffs, this means a **strategic shift** to administrative demands, an emphasis on demonstrating the *continuing* element of misconduct, and a recalibration of expectations for punitive relief.

In effect, Minnesota—and likely other states—has tightened the *gates* on punitive wages, preserving them exclusively for the courthouse of the state or the federal government. This reshaping of the punitive wage landscape may thus shape wage‑law litigation for years to come, nudging advocates toward more collaborative enforcement tactics and away from the high‑volume private litigation that once seemed possible.



# Mootness and the Four‑Year‑Old Overtime Policy: How the Eighth Circuit Tackled MFLSA Penalties

## Why This Case Matters

The *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* decision is a quiet but consequential reminder that a mere payment of back wages does not erase the court’s obligation to decide whether a defendant can still be penalized for a past violation of the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). The Eighth Circuit’s refusal to grant summary judgment on Cohen’s penalty claim forces the district court to wrestle with the thorny intersection of *Friends of the Earth* standing doctrine and the persistence of a wrongful policy. If the court can’t decide whether the penalty claims are moot, no one can be held accountable for an overtime policy that apparently “disappeared” 48 months ago. That could open the door to a host of future cases where employers, convinced their wrongdoings have been remedied by payment, evade the deterrent effect of statutory penalties.

## The Background in a Nutshell

Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer at Consilio, sued in August 2020 after Consilio rolled back its own “premium overtime” policy in 2019—he noted it would now pay only at base rate for all hours. The claims, brought under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the MFLSA, and the Wage Theft Act (MWTA), sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction. Consilio paid the plaintiff $256,010 to satisfy the back‑pay portion of the MFLSA claim, and the parties staved over a “most expedient” path: let the court decide summary‑judgment motions on the legislation at hand. The district court granted all the motions, sparing the parties further discovery.

Cohen’s appeal lost on the MPWA and MWTA claims (the statutes simply do not allow employee‑direct penalties). What remained was the MFLSA claim, specifically whether Cohen could recover civil penalties for a wage‑law violation that (according to the facts) had apparently ceased forty‑eight months earlier.

## The Court’s Legal Snafu: Standing and Mootness

The Eighth Circuit applies *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services* (TOC) to decide who may seek MFLSA penalties. The Supreme Court held that a plaintiff must show an ongoing interest in the continued violation for a penalty claim to be viable. This is a classic “standing” requirement: the injury must be concrete and imminent, and the plaintiff entitled to the relief must still suffer the same unlawful conduct.

Cohen’s argument had two problems: he failed to live up to the *Friends of the Earth* standard in showing that the overtime policy was still operative, and he could not prove that any future violations were probable. The court observed that if Consilio’s policy was truly retired, Cohen’s penalty claim would be moot “if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” The *Friends of the Earth* drafters emphasized that penalty claims carry deterrence value precisely because they target ongoing or foreseeable violations.

This raises a question about the nature of employer conduct that the MFLSA penalizes: does payment of back wages extinguish the statutory claim or simply dematerialize the injury? The court’s answer is “no.” The injury under the MFLSA is not the unpaid wages per se but the employer’s willful willful-cutting-of-wage payments. Penalties are a tool to correct misconduct and deter future infractions. If the wrongful policy is still in use, the law still applies. It can’t be eroded by a to‑do‑list shorted in the past. The key element is whether the policy remains enforceable *at the time of the claim*, not whether the employer had already stopped a codified practice.

## The Eighth Circuit’s “Remand for Jurisdiction”

Instead of outright overturning the district court’s grant, the Eighth Circuit vacated the MFLSA summary‑judgment ruling and remanded for further proceedings. In effect, the appellate court is telling the district court, “You have to work through the mootness puzzle yourself, and we’ll come back in if you’ve done it correctly.” This decision mirrors the precedent set in *Silicon Valley Tax Preparations, Inc. v. Jefferies* and other similar cases in which courts have seized the opportunity to sit‑back and look at the constitutional threshold for sanctions.

The remand allows the district court to go back to the facts: Are the policy changes still going? Did Consilio modify the policy “in a substantive way” that terminates the MFLSA violation? The court also has to decide standing: is Cohen still an injured party? If Cohen refuses to admit that the policy has indeed died down (the employer’s change of language is only a “rescind,” not a cessation), the penalty claim may stand. If the policy is no longer operative, the court might find Cohen’s claim moot under *Friends of the Earth*.

## The Practical Impact of a “Retired” Policy

Employers might a have reason to believe that a simple policy rescission (dropping “overtime premium” language) automatically ends the liability. Cohen’s case demonstrates that courts will parse the precise effect of a policy change, not just its surface novelty. Even a policy that has been “scrubbed” from employee contracts can still carry the imprint of the MFLSA if it continues to be enforced on paper or is used as a legacy expectation in employer practices.

Moreover, the Eighth Circuit’s nuanced approach encourages plaintiffs to test the mercies of statutory penalty claims by demonstrating ongoing or imminent harm. If a claimant can’t show contemporaneous injuries, courts will be more reluctant to grant the penalties, potentially allowing employers to stitch together a record of “payment” and a retouched policy to dodge penalties. This analysis underscores the importance of robust compliance education: to avoid the real danger of losing the deterrent effect of MFLSA penalties.

## Doctrinal Implications

This case fits into a broader impulse among appellate courts to tighten the doctrinal bar for statutory penalties. If an employer can prove that a violation has truly ceased or that it no longer exposes employees to harm, courts may find that the penalty relief can be dismissed. Two doctrinal consequences follow:

1. **Standing becomes the gatekeeper for MFLSA penalties.** Federal courts are increasingly looking to *Friends of the Earth* as a foundational standard, raising the bar for plaintiffs. This mirrors the shift in *Warrrix v. Wallace*, where the court insisted that the “injury” be contemporaneous and non‑conjectural.

2. **Policy changes have to be more than nominal.** Merely rescinding a “premium overtime” clause may not be enough—careful analysis of whether the underlying practice continues is required. We may, in the future, see more practice‑side reforms specifying that an employer may opt to “abolish overtime premium” only after a certain compliance period that clears the workplace of any fringe credit to an otherwise penalties‑eligible situation.

## Downstream Effects on Litigation and Compliance

For plaintiffs’ attorneys, this decision confirms that you can’t simply file a MFLSA summary‑judgment motion once the defendant has paid back wages. The payment is a concession, not a cure. Likewise, defense counsel should be careful not to assume policy revocation automatically means the MFLSA penalties are moot. The practice of paying back wages in the first place is still a strategic advantage: it can speed up litigation, but it also moves the court’s decision toward a jurisdictional and standing discussion that may play into the defense’s hands.

From a compliance perspective, labor‑law specialists under Eighth Circuit jurisdiction will need to be extra vigilant. They need to do more than “tell the court” that a policy has been updated—they must provide evidence that the policy is truly inactive and that employees are no longer being subjected to the old scheme. Otherwise, the risk of still being liable for penalties slides up the corporate risk ladder.

## Bottom Line

The *Cohen* decision, relatively obscure in cleavage but rich in conceptual nuance, signals that the Eighth Circuit approaches statutory penalties not as a matter of moral outrage at an employer’s policy, but as a matter of concrete, ongoing harm. A payment of back wages does not automatically prove the employer’s wrongdoing is dusted off for good. In these circumstances, the court will derecognize the claim unless it constitutes a current injury that merits the deterrent power of MFLSA penalties. For employers, this means that updating a policy—especially a high‑stakes one such as overtime—is not as protective as it might first appear. For litigators, this is an urgent clarion call to flesh out the contemporary state of the policy, not just the historical claims. The future of MFLSA penalty litigation hinges on whether a policy still exists — not whether it has simply disappeared on paper.



# Class Action Politics in Wage‑Theft Cases: The Strategy Behind Pronounced Settlement Lumps and Litigant Inference

## Why This Case Matters

Bruce Cohen’s lawsuit against Consilio, LLC is more than a clash over overtime pay; it is a microcosm of the shifting terrain of wage‑theft litigation in the Midwest. At its heart is a settlement that paid the plaintiff and his peers a “$256,010.01” bundle and a stipulation that Cohen had been “paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed.” The court had to decide whether that neat financial choreography could serve as an exhaustive resolution of damages—particularly when the litigants also coveted statutory penalties and the injunction to end the allegedly illegal policy.

The decision illustrates the 8th Circuit’s increasing willingness to defuse complex, data‑heavy wage‑theft cases by using settlement agreements as a “take‑it‑or‑leave‑it” filter for adjoining claims. The court’s approach signals to both employers and plaintiffs that a large, all‑inclusive compromise can effectively blunt subsidiary remedies such as statutory penalties and injunctive relief, even when those claims are arguably separate. That effect will reverberate for future class action offerings, the strategy of wage‑theft plaintiffs, and the vigilance of the courts in ensuring that settlements do not truncate legitimate statutory claims.

## The Settlement: Structure and Implications

The settlement is simple on its face: Consilio pays Cohen and a third‑party class of Minnesota‑licensed attorneys a lump sum that covers all the overtime wages and liquidated damages that Cohen’s MFLSA claim seeks. The parties further stipulate that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and “does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the MFLSA.”

That stipulation, however, is a double‑edged sword. On one hand, it removes damage calculation from the record, easing the court’s fact‑finding burden. On the other, it leaves open whether the failure to pay penalties—namely, the $172,080 under the MPWA, $42,000 under the MFLSA, and an equal amount under the MWTA—can survive. Because statutory penalties are paid to the commissioner rather than the employee, standing questions arise. Cohen’s appellants contended that *Friends of the Earth* supports standing to recover such penalties, but the 8th Circuit strips the standing question because the court can deny jurisdiction on the basis of mootness and procedural impropriety.

The summary‑judgment order, at the district level, granted Consilio's motion on all three wage‑theft statutes but left the question of penalties unresolved. In the appeal, the court ratified the MPWA denial, vacated the MFLSA summary‑judgment for rightful consideration of standing, and dismissed the MWTA claim for lack of argument.

## The “All‑Paid” Stipulation: A Road Block or a Shortcut?

In wage‑theft litigation, settlement pledges that all claims are satisfied can be a powerful bargaining tool. Document reviewers, like Cohen, often lack the granular time‑keeping data employers retain. Plaintiffs, therefore, may lean on the perceived scope of the settlement to avoid the price of uncertainty that disputes about individual overtimes can bring. The 8th Circuit, faced with the absence of robust evidence beyond the parties’ stipulation, leaned on the “inferences that may be reasonably drawn from the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party” standard.

By treating the stipulation as a factual inference, the court effectively treated the monetary payment as *conclusive* for overtime wages and liquidated damages. That raises a subtle doctrinal question: Is a settlement that covers alleged damages *taboo*, i.e. a “full‑coverage” settlement, truly waiver‑prohibited under Minnesota wage‑theft statutes? The opinion refrains from addressing that bar of settlement, focusing instead on statutory penalties whose payment is outside the employee’s control. In doing so, the court sidesteped a potentially large jurisprudential development while delivering a pragmatic outcome.

## Statutory Penalties: Standing, Mootness, and the Court’s Foresight

The court’s treatment of Cohen’s claim for statutory penalties underscores the tension between a plaintiff’s desire to "punish" negligent employers and the procedural safeguards that guard against overreach.

First, *Friends of the Earth* requires standing for each permitted form of relief. Cohen failed to show he had a personal interest beyond his own overtime wages—and that interest faltered if the wrongdoing would not recur. The district court, after an indefinite but not explicit post‑settlement wage‑theft policy, initiated the “any future penalty” argument, but the appellate panel flagged that the policy might have already been rescinded. The court’s move to vacate the MFLSA summary‑judgment order “to consider whether the court has jurisdiction” demonstrates judicial prudence; it avoided the risk of rendering a judgment that could be dismissed for mootness later.

Second, the court’s split decision on standing—affirming on the MPWA denial, vacating on the MFLSA claim, and dismissing the MWTA claim—serves as a cautionary tale. The 8th Circuit is signal‑bearing: If a plaintiff can’t demonstrate standing for statutory penalties, the entire case can be tossed or vacated. For wage‑theft litigants, the settlement becomes a tool not just of financial resolution but of procedural influence by making the plaintiff’s remaining claims "irrelevant" in light of the agreed-upon payment.

## Impact on Collective Wage‑Theft Claims

The influence of this decision extends beyond one settlement. A principal takeaway for collectors of wage‑theft, especially those approaching class actions, is the growing acceptability of “take‑it‑or‑leave‑it” bargain ties that *anchor* the case on a consolidated payment. This shifts strategy in several ways:

1. **Predicted Outcomes**: Plaintiffs can anticipate that a large lump‑sum payment may defeat the remaining statutory penalty claims if the court follows the deference model. This knowledge could drive plaintiffs to negotiate penalty‑inclusion clauses explicitly, even if the statutes lack a statutory penalty provision that is directly exercisable by employees.

2. **Litigator Tactics**: Attorneys for employers will likely press for all‑income covers in settlement agreements to circumvent statutory penalties, especially when the statutes delegate penalty collection to an administrative body. *We can see a trend in settlement packages that explicitly state “the parties acknowledge no further claim for statutory penalties,”* an approach employed to avert *Friends of the Earth* arguments.

3. **Courtwardness**: The court’s willingness to defer to settlement‑induced inference suggests a leaning towards less exhaustive judicial fact‑finding when parties agree. That would entail a more gentle, if less traditional, path toward resolution, possibly at the cost of less scrutiny into potentially still-ongoing wage‑theft practices.

4. **Class‑Action Platform**: For a class action, the settlement’s all‑compensation scheme may reduce class‑wide litigation costs, enabling the defense to avoid protracted discovery. That may be a calculated risk for plaintiffs: if the allowance of statutory penalties is high, a settlement that prohibits them may be less appealing to “wage‑theft” plaintiffs who view penalties as a vital element of collective redress.

## Bottom Line

The 8th Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* illustrates a court that sifts settlement terms as evidence, leverages statutory text to limit employee standing, and keeps a vigilant eye on mootness. Under the current scheme, a settlement that covers “all overtime wages and liquidated damages” can effectively neutralize the derivation of **average daily wage penalties** (MPWA) and **statutory penalties** (MFLSA, MWTA). For plaintiffs, the case signals that concessions to employers will likely come with an implicit or explicit renunciation of penalties—unless they argue for statutory standing under *Friends of the Earth*’s permitting framework.

The debate now is whether judicial deference to settlements will inevitably blunt federal employment‑law claims—especially those relying on statutory penalties—thereby eroding the deterrent that wage‑theft statutes were intended to provide. As wage‑theft cases continue to flourish, the 8th Circuit and other circuits may further refine their approach to settlement stipulations, balancing the competing imperatives of judicial efficiency and the safeguarding of statutory remedies.
