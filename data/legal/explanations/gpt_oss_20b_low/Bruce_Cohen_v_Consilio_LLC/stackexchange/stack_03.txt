Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### How should a court interpret the phrase “provide notice” in the MWTA when an employee claims the employer failed to notify of an overtime policy change?
Question:
The opinion dismissed Cohen’s MWTA claim, citing the absence of statutory authority for civil penalties under that statute. What is the statutory meaning of “provide notice” under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4)? Can a failure to notify an employee of a policy change be actionable, and if so, is it actionable through penalties or merely a wage‑payment claim?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the court affirmed the dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA claim in part because the Minnesota Wage‑Theft Act (MWTA) contains no statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties. The key statutory language that governs notice requirements is found in Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f)(d)(4). That provision requires an employer to:

> “**provide notice** of any change in the employer’s overtime pay policy to all employees who are covered by the act”…

The language is ordinary, unambiguous, and has no secondary interpretation. The statute is a *duty*—only a *failure* to satisfy that duty creates a tort action that is “a wage‑payment claim” rather than a claim for penalties.

### 1.  Statutory meaning of “provide notice”

* Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f)(d)(4) is a plain duty.  The plain meaning of *provide* is to “give,” “afford,” or “affair” something to the intended recipient.  In the context of a notification requirement, *provide notice* means that the employer **must deliver a written communication** (or other reasonable method of conveyance) that informs the employee of the policy change before it takes effect.  The *court* in its opinion said that there is “no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.”  § 181.032(f)(d)(4) does not create a penalty schedule or a duty to pay a fine; it merely sets out a *notice* requirement that, if breached, gives rise to a wage‑payment claim.

### 2.  Whether a failure to notify is actionable

Yes.  § 181.032(f)(d)(4) makes failure to provide notice a *tort* under the MWTA.  The statutory scheme is:

| Element | Source | Effect |
|---------|--------|--------|
| **Notice** | § 181.032(f)(d)(4) | Employer must inform employees of any change to overtime policy. |
| **Failure** | Lack of the above action | Violates the MWTA, creating a tort and a right to recover. |
| **Remedy** | § 181.032(a)(1) | The employee may recover “the wages … that were not paid because of the employer’s wrongful conduct.”  No civil‑penalty language is inserted in the act.

Thus a failure to inform an employee of a policy change is actionable, but the remedy is limited to **wage payment (and, where the MWTA expressly authorizes it, liquidated damages or statutory penalties that are separately provided for).**  The MWTA itself contains no clause that permits the recovery of civil penalties, and the court explicitly states that the statute “provides no statutory penalties.”

### 3.  Penalties vs. wage‑payment claim

* Under the MWTA* – no penalties.

* Under the MPWA* – civil penalties are available **but only if the Commissioner first “serves a demand for payment”** (see § 181.101(a) and the court’s discussion).  If the employee does not go through the Commissioner's demand process, the employee cannot recover penalties under the MPWA.

* Under the MFLSA* – statutory penalties can be recovered **only** when the employer has violated a wage‑payment requirement that the MFLSA explicitly allows for penalties.  The court noted that Cohen’s MFLSA claim potentially could be moot or lack standing for penalties, but the discussion was not resolved.  In no case does the MFLSA create a penalty for a failure to provide notice; it only provides penalties for wage‑payment violations.

**Bottom line for Cohen**

1. The failure to provide notice of the August 2019 overtime policy change *is* a wage‑payment claim under the MWTA.  
2. Because the MWTA does not authorize civil penalties, Cohen cannot recover penalties under that statute.  
3. Penalties could only be pursued under the MPWA **if** Cohen first had the Commissioner's demand served and the Commissioner found a violation, or under the MFLSA **if** a separate wage‑payment violation was proven.

The court’s holding that there is no statutory authority for civil penalties in the MWTA directly supports this conclusion.  The action remains a wage‑payment claim, and any resolution will involve the payment of wages that were unlawfully withheld, not a penalty fee.
