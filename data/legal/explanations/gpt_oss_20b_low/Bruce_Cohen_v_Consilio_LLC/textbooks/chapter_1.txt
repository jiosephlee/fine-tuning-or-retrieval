Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 1: # I. Background and Procedural History

The court’s opinion is anchored in the factual and procedural chronology that led the parties to the district court’s summary‑judgment decision. Understanding the parties’ relationship, the specific employment arrangement, the employer’s policy change, the timing of the lawsuit, the post‑filing settlement, and the agreement that the court decide all claims before discovery is essential for grasping the appellate analysis that follows.

## Parties

Bruce Cohen is a licensed attorney who, in 2018, joined Consilio, LLC, a company that contracts with legal‑service clients to provide document‑review work. Consilio’s Minnesota offices employ a mixture of attorneys and paralegals to read, index, and analyze discovery documents. Cohen’s role was that of an hourly, licensed‑attorney document reviewer. Consilio’s corporate structure is that of a limited liability company, and its employment relationship with Cohen is governed by the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA).

## Employment Facts

Cohen began working in 2018 as an hourly employee whose hourly rate was set by the Minnesota bar licensing regulations. His duties involved reviewing large volumes of legal documents for the firm’s clients, and he was paid at the rate established for licensed attorneys doing document review. On the basis of his status, Cohen was covered by Minnesota’s wage‑and‑hour statutes, which apply to hourly licensed attorneys. Consilio’s policy at the time of Cohen’s hire allowed for a “premium” overtime rate for hours worked beyond normal business hours.

## Premium Overtime Policy Change

In August 2019, Consilio sent an email to Cohen and other Minnesota‑based hourly licensed‑attorney reviewers. The email announced the institution of a new premium‑overtime policy effective that month. The change removed the premium overtime rate; instead, all hours worked—including overtime—would be compensated at the employee’s base pay rate. The message did not contain any statutory language or official policy manual; it functioned as a unilateral change to the employment terms. This change was perceived by Cohen and the other reviewers as a reduction of compensation above the statutory minimum.

## Filing of Suit

In August 2020, Cohen filed suit against Consilio in federal court, bringing claims on behalf of himself and a putative class of similarly situated employees. He alleged that the 2019 policy change violated several Minnesota wage‑and‑hour statutes: the MPWA (including §§ 181.101 and 181.171), the MFLSA (including §§ 177.25 and 177.27), and the MWTA (including §§ 181.032(f), 181.171, and 177.27). The proposed remedies were wage restitution (overtime), liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief requiring the revocation of the policy.

## Settlement Stipulations

Prior to the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling, the parties reached a partial settlement. Consilio agreed to pay Cohen and the other Minnesota licensed‑attorney reviewers a sum of $256,010.01, which the parties agreed would cover all outstanding overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA claim. Cohen’s share of that payment was $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. The settlement contained a stipulation that both parties acknowledged that Cohen had been paid for all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed. The parties could not agree on whether penalties were payable to Cohen; they remained in dispute over: $172,080 in MPWA average‑daily‑wage penalties, $42,000 in MFLSA penalties, and $42,000 in MWTA penalties.

## Agreement on Summary‑Judgment Timing

Both parties convened in a post‑settlement conference and mutually agreed that the court should hear and decide all remaining claims—MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA—on the merits before any additional discovery or litigation moved forward. They understood that a grant of summary judgment in full would resolve the case entirely. This agreement, embedded in the settlement stipulation, directly influenced the district court’s procedural posture: the court was instructed to grant summary judgment on the claims before the parties had a full slate of evidence.

## District Court Ruling

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. The court concluded that (1) Consilio’s policy change was not actionable under the MPWA because it had not been enforced by the Minnesota Commissioner, thereby precluding penalties, (2) the MFLSA claim was moot after the settlement payment was made, and (3) the MWTA claim was dismissed due to lack of statutory penalties. The district court entered judgment for Consilio and allowed the case to be fully resolved.

Bruce Cohen filed an appeal at the Eighth Circuit on October 6, 2025, seeking reversal of the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims and challenging the determination of mootness and standing with respect to penalties and attorney’s fees. This chapter establishes the factual and procedural context that the appellate court must review before applying the law to the specific statutory claims.
