Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 5: # V. Injunctive Relief and Procedural Economy

The appellate court’s decision to leave Cohen’s injunctive‑relief claim unanswered was the result of a strict application of procedural rules rather than a substantive denial of his request.  Below we unpack the facts of the claim, the procedural misstep that led to its dismissal, and the appellate rule that governed the court’s treatment of the issue.

## Cohen’s Original Injunctive Relief Request: Rescind 2019 Policy

When Cohen filed his lawsuit in August 2020, he identified three avenues of relief: (1) unpaid overtime wages and liquidated damages, (2) statutory penalties, and (3) an injunctive order that required Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy.  The 2019 policy, communicated to employees by email in July 2019, eliminated overtime premium pay and stipulated that all hours would be paid at base rate.  Cohen’s complaint alleged that this policy violated Minnesota’s wage‑law statutes and demanded that the court “issue an affirmative injunction directing Consilio to cease the illegal payroll practices.”

This request was a direct irritant to Consilio’s business model; it would require the company to reinstate overtime premiums for a sizable segment of its workforce, a cost the company expressly promised to abandon.  By seeking a court‑mandated change to a policy that had already been in effect for more than a year, Cohen aimed to enforce the statutory rule that employees are entitled to overtime compensation at a rate equal to the base wage plus one‑and‑a‑halftimes.

## Later Change of Relief Sought: Withdrawal of Policy in Summary‑Judgment Brief

Shortly before the district court heard the summary‑judgment motions, the two parties negotiated a settlement.  Consilio agreed to pay Cohen the full amount that Cohen claimed was owed under the MFLSA and the MPWA, and the parties entered a stipulation confirming that “Cohen had been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit.”  The settlement did not touch the injunctive‑relief component, but the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.”

Cohen’s counsel subsequently introduced a new argument in the summary‑judgment briefing—a “withdrawal of the 2019 policy.”  The brief, which appeared as a supplemental motion, did not re‑file a complaint asking for the same injunctive relief that was originally pleaded.  Instead, it simply asked the court to acknowledge that the 2019 policy “has been withdrawn” and that any residual policy changes would be moot.  Because the injunctive claim was no longer the subject of a properly amending action, the appellate court treated the request as an oralally introduced, “new” claim that did not meet the procedural threshold for appellate review.

## Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a): Amendment, New Claims, and the Appellate Rule

Rule 15(a) governs a party’s right to amend pleadings.  It allows a party to file an amendment in a timely manner—or to “add claims, defenses, or parties” that “result in the admission of a material change” to the case.  However, a new claim introduced without a concomitant amendment to the original pleading—whether via amendment or a new complaint—constitutes a procedural error that the court can overlook only if the court deems the claim “patently unfair” under 28 U.S.C. § 1297(c).

In *InfoDeli, LLC* (2025) the 8th Circuit made clear that a request for a new type of relief—particularly an injunctive order that was not pleaded at the start of the action—must arise from an amending complaint or a new complaint, not an opposition brief.  The court held that “a petitioner who introduces a novel claim or a different type of relief without filing a correctly punctuated amendment or new complaint is effectively losing the race to do so.  The pleadings are the one true source of claimable rights, and the appellate court is bound by what the district court had jurisdiction to adjudicate.”

Consequently, Cohen’s brief did not satisfy the procedural requirements for an appellate review of the injunctive relief claim.  The court did not have jurisdiction to decide a claim that was never properly brought in the original complaint or any annexed amendment.

## Attorney’s Commentary on Procedural Economy: InfoDeli, LLC Case Support

The dissenting opinion in *InfoDeli, LLC* emphasized the importance of procedural economy.  The court’s decision to “clamp a new claim that was not properly pleaded” protects the parties from an endless cycle of pleadings and motions at the appellate level, which would erode the finality of judgments and squander judicial resources.  The dissent argued that proper procedural conduct—filing an amending complaint within the statutory period—would have let the appellate court examine whether the injunctive request had merit.  Nonetheless, the court expressed respect for the principle that “things practiced in a lawsuit are best practiced when they are well‑begun,” thereby underscoring that new claims presented only at the appellate level are not admissible unless the parties were “properly offered” the opportunity to bring them earlier in the litigation.

From Cohen’s perspective, the absence of an amending complaint meant that the injunction was a *post‑hoc* change in the nature of the relief sought—moving from an injunction to rescind the policy to a withdrawal that effectively no longer demanded injunctive action.  The appellate court deemed this change insufficient for review because the parties had not formally amended the complaint to reflect the revised relief request.

## Court’s Decision: Issue Not Yet Ready, Not Considered on Appeal

Given these procedural facts, the appellate court held that it could not address Cohen’s injunctive‑relief claim.  In its opinion the court noted that the claim “was not properly pleaded in either the original complaint or a subsequent amendment; it was introduced only in the summarily‑judgment briefing, which does not confer appellate jurisdiction.”  Consequently, the argument was omitted from the appellate analysis, and the district court’s order to dismiss the claim was affirmed.

### Recap

1. **Original Pleadings** – Cohen showed injunctive relief (rescinding the 2019 policy) as a claim in the complaint.
2. **Settlement & Brief** – The parties settled the wage and damages aspects; Cohen introduced a withdrawal of the policy in a brief, not an amended complaint.
3. **Rule 15(a)** – Only an amendment or new complaint may introduce new claims; briefs alone are insufficient.
4. **InfoDeli, LLC** – The appellate court cited *InfoDeli* citing the need for procedural economy and proper pleadings.
5. **Outcome** – The appellate court declined to review the injunctive claim, affirming the district court’s dismissal for failure to amend properly.

By understanding the interplay between the original pleading, the later settlement, and the procedural rules governing amendment and new claims, students can see why the appellate court made its decision and how procedural choices shape the ultimate fate of a claim.
