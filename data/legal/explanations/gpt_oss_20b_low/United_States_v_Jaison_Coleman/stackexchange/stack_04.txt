Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### What is the scope of consent after an officer has obtained entry into a home?
Question:
The district court found the officers acted within the scope of Lisa’s consent. The opinion says the officers’ purpose was to check children’s welfare and, if necessary, approach Coleman. How do courts objectively determine whether the officers’ subsequent actions—such as canvassing the house or performing a protective stop—fall within the original consent?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit upheld the district court’s conclusion that Lisa’s initial, voluntary consent to enter the home was **within scope** and that the officers’ subsequent actions—“a brief canvas of the home” and the protective pat‑down of Coleman—did not exceed that consent.  The court applied the ordinary “objective‑reasonableness” test developed in *Florida v. Jimeno* and *Saucedo* and relied heavily on the video record to evaluate the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa.

---

### 1.  What *scope* means in consent‑search cases

When a third party grants consent to search a dwelling, the search is limited to what the consenting person “reasonably understood the officers could do.”  The Supreme Court explained this in *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte* and the Seventh Circuit has reiterated it in *Saucedo* and *Jones*.  The inquiry is **objective**: “what would a typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange?”  (See *Saucedo*, 688 F.3d at 865, citing *Florida*, 500 U.S. at 251.)

---

### 2.  The officers’ actions after entry

The district court found that, once inside, the officers:  

1. **Canvased the house** – a “quick canvas” to locate everyone and ascertain safety.  
2. **Conducted a protective pat‑down** of Coleman.  

These acts were “necessary to check the welfare of the children and ensure none of the occupants posed an immediate threat.”  The court emphasized that such a “brief canvas” is a standard, routine part of a welfare check and does not in itself transform a consent‑search into a warrant‑required, full search.  (See *Coleman*, 1–2, paragraph 37.)

---

### 3.  How courts evaluate whether such acts fall within the original consent

Courts look to **totality of circumstances** (Bumper, 391 U.S. at 548; *Han*, 105 F.4th at 991) and to the **content of the consent exchange**.  In *Coleman*, the judge examined:  

* The timing and wording of the consent.  Lisa first said “I’d prefer you not,” then – after the officer reiterated and used the word “need” – she said “Okay.”  The court held that “need” was not coercive “in light of the totality of the circumstances” (*Coleman*, 1–2, paragraph 28).  
* The officer’s demeanor—calm, respect‑bearing, no physical pressure (*Coleman*, 1–2, paragraph 20).  
* The nature of the requested activity—“check the welfare of the children” (*Coleman*, 1–2, paragraph 30).  

Because the self‑instructed inquiry to locate the children and to assess whether Coleman posed an imminent threat was a *reasonable* completion of the purpose that Lisa communicated, the court held it stayed within scope.  The officers did not, for example, burrow into private bedrooms searching for contraband; their scan was limited to the publicly accessible areas and to identifying occupants.

---

### 4.  What the opinion says about the objective‑reasonableness test

The opinion repeatedly quotes the Seventh Circuit’s standard:

> “the proper inquiry for evaluating the scope of consent is ‘objective reasonableness,’ with courts asking, ‘what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent?’” (*Coleman*, 1–2, paragraph 19).

The court therefore treated the district court’s finding of voluntary consent and scope as an error *only if the deliberative process singled out a clear deviation from normal, reasonable expectations*.  It found no such deviation, and under *clear‑error* review it affirmed.

---

### Bottom line for you, as a student

When a third party consents, the police may *in principle* conduct any act that a reasonable person would understand the consenting party allows.  Courts assess this objectively, looking at:

1. **The exact words used** in the consent exchange.
2. **The officers’ demeanor and any pressure** (none here).
3. **The purpose communicated** (“check child welfare”).
4. **The conduct that follows** (a brief canvas and protective pat‑down) and whether it aligns with that purpose.

In *Coleman*, the court found that the officers’ post‑entry activities were sufficiently “reasonable” and *aligned* with the purpose Lisa conveyed, so they were within the scope of her consent.  If the officers had, say, turned the lights off and stolen items, the search would *not* have been within scope.  

This objective‑reasonableness framework is the one you’ll cite when discussing scope‑of‑consent cases after the fact.
