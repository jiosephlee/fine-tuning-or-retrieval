Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why does the court allow judicial notice of the copyright registrations in the factual matrix?
Question:
The court says it may take judicial notice of ‘copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.’ I’m unclear about when a court can elect to take judicial notice of copyright registrations and how that affects the fair‑use analysis.

Answer:
**Short answer**

At the pleading level a court may “take judicial notice” of a copyright registration that is **publicly available** in the Copyright Office’s official register.  Judicial notice lets the court treat the fact that a work is registered (and, by extension, that it is copyrighted) as a *proven* element of the factual matrix.  In this case that matters only to the first factor of fair use—the “purpose and character of the use”—because the registration confirms that the videos were *published* on the same day they were created.  The court uses that fact to reason that the new “Jamie Kimmel Live!” segments are not a competing replacement for the original videos but a *commentary* and *parody* that do not transform the work in the manner the law requires.  The registration itself does not erode or enhance fair‑use defense; it simply supplies evidence that the original work was available to the public.

---

### 1. When can a court take judicial notice of a copyright registration?

The Copyright Act gives the Court the same power that other courts have to take judicial notice of facts that are:

* *widely known, or*  
* *verified by reliable sources and available in public record*.

In *Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.* and *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum* the Second Circuit explained that “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry” are prima facie facts that the court may accept without proof.  The registrations themselves are uncontroversial, being issued by the federal government after an examination of the application.

Because the Supreme Court has not distinguished *registered* from *unregistered* works in the context of fair use, the court’s taking judicial notice of the registration does **not** alter the statutory analysis.  It merely gives the court “the evidence that the work was registered and, thus, that it is protected” (see *Hachette Book Group* at 179).  The following holds:

|  • | Requirement |  Example in this case |
|---|-------------|------------------------|
|​1|The fact must be *non‑controversial* and *publicly available.* | The registration is in the U.S. Copyright Office’s online registry. |
|​2|The court may elect to *accept* the fact without the parties having to produce it. | Cote, J. took judicial notice of “the relevant copyright registrations” (para. 13). |
|​3|The fact is relevant to the *state of the world* that the claim is based on. | The registration proves the videos were published. |

---

### 2. What does the registration mean for fair‑use analysis?

A copyright registration is a **fact‑making tool,** not a doctrinal tool.  Fair‑use is a strict statutory inquiry that looks at *four* non‑exclusive factors (U.S.C. § 107).  The registration is relevant to *factor (1)* – the “purpose and character of the use” – because it confirms that the original work’s first market was the public (i.e., it had been made available to potential users).  Without this fact the court would need a party to prove the original was *published*.

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the court relied on the registration when it said:

> “Among other things, the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created” (para. 20).  

The timing matters for two reasons:

1. **Availability to the public.**  A *published* work is free to be used, including for criticism or parody, as long as rest of factors permit.  If the work had not been published the “market” factor would be different (often more favorable to fair use).
2. **Interpretation of “substantial” portion.**  If the work had not been released the court would need to infer whether the segment used could substitute for a non‑published work.  Publication simplifies that question.

But the registration *does not* itself create fair use.  The court still applied the *transformative* test (*Andy Warhol*, *Hachette*), the nature of the work, the amount used, and the market effect.  The registration merely fed data into the *purpose* factor.  The court therefore concluded (para. 19):

> “The use was not transformative because Kimmel … was the designer of the original purpose.”  

That conclusion follows from *TCA Television Corp.* and other precedent, not from the mere existence of a registration.

---

### 3. Judicial notice as a procedural convenience

A procedural benefit is that the plaintiff does not need to prove that the videos were *published* in the lawsuit.  The plaintiff’s own pleadings already reference the registrations, but the court can still avoid formal discovery or testimony solely on that point.  The court notes that it “may also take judicial notice … of ‘copyright registrations’” while reviewing the pleading (para. 13).  The court then treats the registration as **conclusive** for the purpose of that factor, providing a clean factual base for its fair‑use judgment.

---

### 4. Bottom line for you, a law student

- **Judicial notice** is a court’s power to accept public‑record facts—like a copyright registration—without additional evidence.  
- In *Santos*, the court used that notice to confirm the videos were **published**, which supports the *purpose*‑and‑*character* analysis of fair use.  
- The registration does **not** in itself create fair use; it simply supplies a fact (published status) that the court tests against the statutory factors.  
- The court still revisits all four factors; it concluded fair use *at the pleading stage* because the use was not transformative and because the original work had been made public.

See *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025 U.S. 2d Cir. 24‑2196‑cv, § I, paras. 13‑22 (court “may take judicial notice … of copyright registrations…”; fact that “the videos were published on the same day they were created” confirms *factor (1)* and helps the court reject the infringement claim).
