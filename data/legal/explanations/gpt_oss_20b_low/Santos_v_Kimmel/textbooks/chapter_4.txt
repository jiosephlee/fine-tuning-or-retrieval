Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 4: # 4. Integration of Analysis and Holding

The appellate court’s task in this case was twofold: first, to decide whether the pleadings contained an arguable claim for copyright infringement; second, to determine whether the state‑law claims that Santos alleged could be sustained. The opinion’s structure mirrors this dual objective. At the outset, the court performs a de novo review of the dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) and gives judicial notice of the factual basis needed to evaluate fair use. It then follows a logical sequence—geared by the four statutory factors of fair use—to decide that the Division’s denial was correct. Finally, it affirms the district court’s dismissal of the state‑law claims on the basis that they either were preempted or failed to state a claim. The following sections explain how each of these elements is brought together.

## De Novo Review and Permissible Judicial Notice of Copyright Registrations

Under the Second Circuit precedent of *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, the appellate court may review a district‑court ruling under Rule 12(b)(6) de novo. The court re‑examines “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” Consequently, the lower court’s factual findings are not binding; the appellate court can reach different conclusions based on what is plausibly alleged. This standard is particularly important in copyright cases, where the pleadings themselves often contain the core data needed to assess fair use.

Santos’ complaint presented the copyright registrations of the six cameo videos he created for Kimmel. *Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.* and *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum* authorize the court to take judicial notice of those registrations, as they are items published in the Copyright Office’s registry. By doing so, the appellate court confirms that the videos constituted published, copyrighted works. These facts enable the court to address the four statutory factors without needing a formal expert or additional evidence. The court’s reliance on judicial notice clarifies that the appellate panel can, within the confines of the complaint, assess fair use entirely de novo.

## Logical Flow from Facts to Fair‑Use Determination

Once the factual ground is established, the opinion applies the four-metric framework of 17 U.S.C. § 107. The first factor—purpose and character—receives the most substantive analysis. The court treats the purpose question as a matter of transformativity, a term the Second Circuit uses to capture whether a new work merely supersedes the original or adds new value. Citing *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith* and *Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive*, the court explains that the judge’s assessment should be grounded in actual observable impact, not the plaintiff’s hope or subjective intent. The complaint’s own language, which frames the cameo videos as “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” illustrates that the videos are a commentary on Santos rather than his attempt to produce a commercially-derived substitute. Because Kimmel’s use was clearly “insignificant” (the first factor awardee), the court finds it strongly suggestive of transformative use.

The second factor—the nature of the copyrighted work—is less contentious. The videos were creative and expressive, which the fair‑use doctrine traditionally weighs against infringement. Yet, as the opinion notes, the weight of this factor is secondary when balanced against a transformative purpose.

The third factor—amount and substantiality—focuses on the core content of the videos. The court discusses how, despite their publication on the same day, the plaintiff’s complaint clarifies that no portion of the videos was copied without permission. The analysis acknowledges that fair use protects the plaintiff’s use of the entire work, not merely a selective excerpt, but that the entire work was transformed into a new context (satirical commentary). Thus the factor does not weigh heavily against the court’s conclusion.

The fourth factor examines effect on market value. Crucially, *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc.* is cited to emphasize that if a secondary use does not serve as a competing substitute for the original, it cannot be considered a market injury. The court affirms that the cameo videos were not marketed to the same audience the original videos might have been, and that Santos’ own complaint provides no evidence that the use adversely affected the market for the original works. Therefore, even this factor fails to defeat the fair‑use defense.

The court brings these analytical strands together to decide that, even at the pleading stage, fair use applies. It avoids any “rubber‑stamp” on the dismissal and demonstrates how the application of the statutory framework aligned with the details of the case.

## Reaffirmation of District Court’s Dismissal and Conclusion of Appeal

Having concluded that fair use bars the copyright claims, the opinion moves to the state‑law claims. It begins with the breach of contract and breach of implied contract allegations. Here, the court invokes *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.* to support the doctrine that a third party cannot rely on a contract that does not expressly bestow rights upon them. Coupled with *Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Group*, the court finds that Santos neither named an express declaration that the Terms of Service applied to him nor alleged essential terms that would create an implied contract. Hence those claims fail to state a claim under New York law.

The fraudulent‑inducement claim is dismissed on the ground that Santos did not allege any out‑of‑pocket loss, a requirement for a New York fraud claim (see *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*). The court’s strict adherence to the pleading requirements, consistent with the Court’s earlier decision to dismiss the claims for failure to state, leads it to uphold the district court’s ruling.

By tying these disparate analyses into a coherent narrative—first the statutory barrier of fair use, then the procedural non‑merit of the state‑law claims—the court arrives at a single, streamlined conclusion: the district court’s dismissal was proper, and the appeal is denied. The final paragraph, therefore, offers a concise, affirmative summary that “for the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court,” cementing the appellate court’s integration of the factual, statutory, and procedural strands.
