Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Contractual Pitfalls in the Digital Age: Why Non‑Contracting Third Parties Can’t Hop on a Platform’s Terms of Service  

## Why Santos‑v‑Kimmel Matters  
In the sprawling comedy‑copyright cross‑fire that dropped in the Second Circuit in October 2025, former congressman George Santos tried to seize a late‑night host for using his cameo‑made videos on a national broadcast. The parties fought over whether “Will Santos Say It?” was a fair use parody or a trespass‑to‑copyright, but the real legal fireworks emerged from the *state‑law* claims that Santos planted in his complaint—breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement—based on the terms of service (ToS) of the Cameo platform.

A de‑novo dismissal of those claims underscores a doctrine that has become increasingly relevant as influencers, meme‑bakers, and other content creators make deals on the shoulders of digital platforms. The court’s reasoning spells out, in plain terms, why a platform’s ToS rarely extends to non‑signatories and what that means for anyone who tries to piggy‑back on a contract that only the platform and its direct users entered into.

## The Court’s Core Holding  
In short: **State‑law contract claims that Serra’s analog child‑compliant litigation is based on a digital platform’s ToS fail to state a cause of action because 1) the parties never entered into an agreement that covers the alleged breach and 2) there is a strong presumption that a franchise‑type contract does *not* benefit non‑signatories**. The Second Circuit explicitly pointed to Illinois law for the Terms of Service and New‑York law for the implied‑contract argument, but the reasoning is readily extrapolated to other jurisdictions that share similar principles.

## Third‑Party Beneficiary Doctrine and the “Strong Presumption”  
The civil‑law doctrine of third‑party beneficiaries exists to delineate who actually holds the contractual teeth. Under Illinois, a non‑signatory can only enforce a contract if the contract expressly designates them as a beneficiary or if it’s so apparent that the parties intended that the beneficiary be bound. The court cited *Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp.*, which explains that “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” In Santos’s case, there was no such declaration. He was not a party to the Cameo terms, nor did the platform’s language or the “feeling of a meeting of minds” indicate that Kimmel and Santos had entered into an agreement that made Santos a beneficiary.

For New‑York law, the court referenced *Rocky Point Props.*, noting that an implied‑in‑fact contract requires the essential terms of the parties’ agreement to be clear. Santos’ complaint merely listed the fact that the crew had “supplied” the videos and that the platform's ToS had some stipulations—it never spelled out what the parties intended to be bound by the contract or specifically assigned duties that would affect a third party. The court found the claim under the “burden of the initial pleading” turned over to the defendant’s legal team.

## What This Means for Influencers and Content‑Sharing Platforms  
1. **Contracts Are Promissory, Not Mandatory.** If you are a content creator with a brand partnership announced by an influencer via a platform, you’re in a position analogous to Santos: you have not signed the contract between the influencer and the platform. The platform can enforce its ToS against the influencer, but there is no legal mechanism for the brand to complain about the influencer’s breach of the platform’s terms.  

2. **Third‑Party Beneficiaries Must Be Explicit.** Platform policies that talk about “consultants” or “third‑party partners” must craft language that explicitly recognizes those parties as beneficiaries or imposes obligations that extend beyond the two direct parties. Absent that language, any attempt to sue a non‑signatory for breach of the ToS is likely to fail.  

3. **The “Meeting of Minds” Sledstone.** An implied contract doctrine, often invoked in “silent” or “evidential” correspondence, still requires more than a resumé‑style email thread. Courts will look for *essential terms*—obligation, consideration, or a specific promise that a third party would enforce. If it is merely that “Kimmel’s content is of low quality,” that is insufficient.  

4. **Digital Platforms and Creative Economy Dynamics.** In an ecosystem where a platform’s terms govern a flurry of ancillary creators, there is a candled path for “sub‑sub‑contractors” to try to pose as beneficiaries if they stand to suffer in the marketplace. This decision puts an important boundary—platform ToS can be joined through a *direct* relationship, not a *derivative* one.  

## A Case Study in Content‑Leveraging  
Consider a social media influencer who signs a contract with a brand while simultaneously using a platform’s ToS that obligates them to deliver “original content” to the brand. The brand refuses delivery, claiming the influencer’s underlying contract with the platform forced the influencer to create generic mash‑ups that are no longer original. Under this court’s logic, the brand cannot sue the influencer for breach of the platform contract; they can only sue for breach of the brand contract itself. The platform contract holds up against the influencer, but not the brand.  

This is precisely the boundary the Second Circuit set, leaving the brand with no recourse other than the contract they negotiated directly with the influencer.

## Potential Downstream Effects  
1. **Contract Drafting Revisions.** Platforms will likely revisit their terms to expressly carve out certain beneficiary roles or to limit third‑party enforcement. Some may create companion agreements that allow third parties to opt‑in.  
2. **Guidelines for Brands vs. Influencers.** Brands should be mindful when signing agreements that depend on a platform’s policy, ensuring that any “third‑party obligations” are codified somewhere the brand can invoke directly.  
3. **Legal Research for Influencers.** Agencies that manage creators need to counsel clients that they cannot rely on platform ToS to defeat third‑party claims unless the ToS is expressly written to extend.  
4. **Marketplace Transparency.** If platform ToS are obscure, a brand could find itself out of luck. Transparent, signed, and disseminated agreements will be the industry’s new virtue.

## Analytical Perspective  
The court’s reliance on the Illinois “strong presumption” and the New‑York analysis may seem jurisdictional, but they crystallize a long‑standing principle: *contracts are made between consenting parties.* Even a “boilerplate” ToS is no more binding on an outsider than a business list of terms that did not determine a mutual agreement. The Second Circuit’s balancing outline also implicitly reminds us that the purpose of a contract clause is to satisfy an *actual* contractual need—a need that cannot arise for a party who did not sign into the equation.

There is a lingering concern, though, that platforms could advantageously circumvent this doctrine by crafting “program participation agreements” that involve an omnibus statement recognizing a broad group of beneficiaries. The onus would then shift to brands and creators to wire‑tap the fine print. That means greater diligence for all parties in the content chain.

## Bottom Line  
“Santos v. Kimmel” sends a clear message: *Placing a contract clause in a platform’s ToS is not an invisible safety net for everyone who witnesses the end result.* For content creators, brands, and the platform itself, it underscores the need for explicit agreements that define who is bound and who may sue. As the digital economy keeps churning out new biotech and get‑creative ways to monetize influence, the rules that keep the chain unbroken—explicit, signed, and consistently enforced contracts—remain the most reliable safeguard.
