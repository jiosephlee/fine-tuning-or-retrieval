Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Fraudulent Inducement Without Loss: The New York Standard and the Limits of Allegations in Platform‑Related Disputes

## Why This Case Matters

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit held that a plaintiff’s fraud claim can be dismissed for failure to plead an “out‑of‑pocket loss” under New York law, even when a seemingly substantial contractual misrepresentation underlies the dispute. The decision is a rare, yet impactful illustration of how statutory standards for fraud interact with the particularities of modern online marketplaces. As platforms like Cameo, Patreon, and TikTok become the venue for contract formation, the *Santos* ruling signals that courts will scrutinize meticulously whether a non‑contracting user has suffered a concrete, recoverable loss before allowing fraud remedies to flourish.

## The Court’s Analytical Path

### The New York “Out‑of‑Pocket Loss” Doctrine

New York law requires a fraud plaintiff to allege a “had‑to‑pay” loss that vanishes when the defrauded party is refunded or replaced. The Supreme Court of New York and the Court of Appeals have repeatedly emphasized that mere transaction costs, opportunity costs, or speculative damages do not satisfy the requirement. *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* (1996) insists that a plaintiff must demonstrate that an actual, identifiable loss incurred “out of pocket.” *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.* (2017) further clarifies that loss must be “real, present, and specific.”

Santos framed his fraud claim around the fact that Jimmy Kimmel & ABC had violated Cameo’s Terms of Service and breached a presumed non‑contractual relationship. He argued that he was “enticed” to perform the videos based on Kimmel’s lure, implying that the subsequent airing of the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* deprived him of future earnings. However, the Second Circuit found that Santos failed to and could not plausibly allege an out‑of‑pocket loss—a loss that would vanish upon refund or replacement. The court pointed out that the complaint did not specify any actual loss, such as a suspended *Cameo* credit, a reduced fee, or any other concrete detriment. Hence, the fraud claim was barred from pleading.

### Tying Platform‑Specific Claims to General Fraud Standards

Santos relied on an alleged breach of "Cameo Terms of Service" as a vehicle to allege fraud. This approach is noteworthy because the Terms of Service, governed by Illinois law, create an implicit contract involving the user. The court’s analysis underscored that, in the absence of an express declaration that the Terms of Service applied to Santos, there was no basis to treat the interaction as a contract. Moreover, the *Cameo* context does not override New York’s fraud standard: the court’s reasoning was grounded in New York law regardless of the contractual domicile of the platform agreement.

## What the Court Sought to Do (and Why It Fell Short)

Santos’s complaint suggested that he suffered a loss from the “cost of the performance” he undertook under false pretenses. Yet, the complaint omitted any statement that an actual fee was paid, that the performance required a material out‑of‑pocket expense, or that the resulting broadcast harmed his earnings in a specified, calculable way. The court concluded that a plaintiff cannot simply allege that the defendant “is liable for misrepresentations” without showing a tangible loss. This outcome is consistent with established New York rulebook: that the fraud insurer’s obligation to show a loss is foundational to any recovery.

## Implications for Future Platform Litigation

### 1. The “Real and Specific” Loss Requirement Is Not Permissible “Mishap” Proof

The *Santos* decision reaffirms that if a plaintiff is attempting to use a platform’s terms or the nature of a digital agreement to allege fraud (or even breach of contract), they must craft a complaint that pinpoints a concrete loss. Courts will not allow a plaintiff to “arm‑chair” a misrepresentation claim simply because the second party published a video or resold an item. The grievance must detail the financial or tangible cost borne by the plaintiff. In practice, this means that any New York‑based or New York‑plaintiff platform dispute must have a robust factual basis for loss—such as a reimbursement record, an actual withheld fee, or a documented shift in revenue streams.

### 2. The Court’s Emphasis on “Non‑Contractual” Claims Is a Red Flag for Platform Users

Santos treated his claim as a “non‑contracting” relationship with the Cameo platform, assuming a sort of implied third‑party benefit. The court decisively rejected that narrative, underscoring Illinois law’s presumption against non‑contracting third‑party benefits. This doctrine is likely to apply more broadly to online marketplaces where users rarely sign explicit contracts or where contract terms are implicit in user agreements. Plaintiffs who wish to invoke breach of contract or fraudulent inducement must therefore either prove an explicit contract (or a clear, enforceable expectation) or fall back on a different remedy, such as tort-based claims for defamation or verbal agreements.

### 3. Defendants May Argue, and Likely Will, That No Loss Has Occurred in Similar Cases

Given the *Santos* precedent, defendants will be better positioned to argue that a plaintiff’s alleged loss is intangible, speculative, or not “out of pocket.” If they can demonstrate that the plaintiff committed no monetary error or that any damages were purely “qualitative” (e.g., loss of reputation), then a fraud claim will likely be at a disadvantage. For instance, if a content creator alleges that their videos were misappropriated on a platform and that it violated moral rights, the defendant can argue that the plaintiff has not shown a concrete financial loss to satisfy the New York standard.

### 4. The “Platform Agreement” Preemption Tactic Becomes Riskier

Santos’s attempt to invoke preemption of state law by the Copyright Act is textbook for eminent judicial deference to federal law. The court declined to analyze the preemption issue because the factual pleading failed. But it left open the possibility that, under different circumstances, a state‑law contract claim might indeed be preempted by federal copyright or intellectual‑property statutes. This tension will likely magnify in platforms where user‑generated content is both a commercial product and a platform for artistic expression.

## A Critical View of the Court’s Reasoning

While the court’s adherence to New York’s fraud standard is legally sound, it invites a cautionary pause for plaintiffs in other jurisdictions where the “out‑of‑pocket loss” bar is less strict. The *Santos* ruling calls into question the applicability of a purely fact‑based approach when the “loss” could arguably be measured in future earnings or reputational damage—particularly in the gig economy. A more flexible approach might preserve an essential check on fraud claims in digital economies, where actual out‑of‑pocket losses are often hard to quantify.

Additionally, the court did not squarely address the interplay between the “fair use” defense and fraud. By deeming the copyright claims fail at the pleading stage, the court sidestepped an opportunity to elaborate on whether a platform's use of user content, even if considered fair, might nonetheless constitute fraud if the user was misled about the downstream dissemination of her content. That balancing act remains open for future litigation.

## Looking Ahead: Practical Takeaways for Legal Practitioners

1. **Spell Out Losses Early** – In pleadings that rely on platform-based allegations, provide documentary evidence of fees paid, refunds, or any actual financial shortfall. Courts will not “invent” a loss out of thin air.
2. **Shift to State‑Law Claims Where Appropriate** – If a platform’s user agreement is ambiguous, plaintiffs might better focus on state‑law claims such as breach of contract or torts (e.g., defamation) that require less stringent loss requirements.
3. **Avoid “Non‑Contracting” Tribulations** – Do not rely on an implied non‑contractual relationship with a platform unless the user agreement expressly provides for third‑party benefit.
4. **Prepare for Preemption Analysis** – When dealing with IP litigation in platform contexts, be ready to address whether federal copyright law might preempt the state‑law claim.

## Conclusion

*Santos v. Kimmel* is an instructive example of how a traditional tort principle—fraud’s requirement for an out‑of‑pocket loss—survives scrutiny even in the modern context of online contracts and digital content. The case circumscribes the remedial utility of fraud claims in platform disputes, underscoring the need for plaintiffs to anchor their allegations in concrete, measurable losses. As online marketplaces continue to blur the lines between contractual and non‑contractual relationships, courts will inevitably revisit these doctrines, perhaps refining the loss requirement or tightening the definition of “non‑contracting” claims. For now, the *Santos* ruling remains a sober reminder that digital negligence and misrepresentation are not a free pass to a recovery; the law still demands an evidentiary scaffold.
