Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What distinguishes a threat from persecution in asylum cases?
Question:
In the opinion, the court says that threats alone are insufficient to establish past persecution. I’ve read that asylum requires ‘more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty’, but I’m unclear on how the courts determine what level of harm crosses that threshold. What factors are considered when evaluating whether a threat, or a series of threats, constitute persecution?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the court reiterated that *threats alone do not rise to the level of “persecution”* required for asylum.  When the court evaluates whether a threat (or series of threats) amounts to persecution it looks for **(1)** that the threat is **“offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats”** (Karki, § C, ¶ 17‑18), **(2)** that the threat is **part of a broader pattern of mistreatment** that creates a **cumulative risk of harm**, and **(3)** that the threat is **an instrument of state‑level or state‑aligned violence** that the government is either unable or unwilling to curb.  If any of those elements are missing, the court will conclude the applicant has failed the past‑persecution prong.

---

### 1.  Threats are not “persecution” unless they are part of a broader pattern

> “We have held that a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm ... and must entail more **than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty**.” *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (emphasis added) – *Jimenez*, § C paragraph 17.  

The court emphasizes that **individual threats, even if violent in tone, do not automatically meet this threshold**.  In *Karki* the court said:

> “**Threats alone are insufficient** to constitute persecution.” *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013).  

Thus, a single threatening phone call or a brochure saying “death to snitches” does not count as persecution.  The threat must be embedded in a larger context of intimidation, violence, or criminal activity.

### 2.  Cumulative, ongoing threats can be treated as persecution

The court stresses that it looks at **the collection of incidents, not an isolated event**:

> “We do not look at each incident in isolation, but **consider them collectively**, because the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution.” *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citing *Jimenez*, § C, ¶ 18).  

In *Jimenez*, the family documented repeated phone calls, a threatening brochure, a gun‑point assault at the factory, a threat of murder from men “under the order of Don Rafa,” and an attempt to hold one of their children at gunpoint.  While the judge noted no physical injury was reported, the court said the **aggregate of these threats and the ongoing danger they created failed to meet the “offensive” standard** needed for persecution.  The court cited *Chhetri v. Rosen* and *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland* to show that **four telephone threats and one vague in‑person threat, or threats that were contingent on compliance, were insufficient**.

### 3.  The threat must be linked to a protected ground and to state control

Even when threats are cumulative, the court requires that they be **“on account of”** a protected ground and that they be **caused or condoned by the government or a group the government cannot control**:

> “The petitioner must establish that **the persecution was ... on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds**.” *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012).  

In *Jimenez*, the petitioner argued the threats were political in motive.  The court found the predominant motive was a personal dispute with **Don Rafa** (a narcotics‑timed operator aligned with the president), not the petitioner’s political opinion.  The court held that **the political opinion was merely “tangential”** and “subordinate to the main reason” for the harassment (see *Jimenez*, § D, paragraph 3).  Thus the threats could not be tied to a protected ground.

### 4.  A threat must be barred by the state’s unwillingness or inability to protect

If an applicant shows that a threat is part of a pattern of persecution by a government or a law‑enforcement body, the court also checks whether the **government failed or was unwilling to intervene**:

> “Whether a noncitizen has proved past private persecution … is a **fact question**.” *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 859‑60 (10th Cir. 2025).  

In *Jimenez*, the court found the applicant had **not sought help** beyond two informal conversations with police officers, and no evidence that the government had actually responded to the threats.  The court therefore deemed **no proof of government unwillingness or inability** (see *Jimenez*, § E).  Without this element, the threat sequence cannot constitute persecution.

---

### Bottom line for a student

1. **Single threats = not persecution.**  
2. **Multiple, ongoing threats + evidence of retaliation or violence can be persecution,** but only if the pattern is **“offensive and more than sporadic.”**  
3. The threats must be **tied to one of the protected bases (race, religion, nationality, particular social group, political opinion).**  
4. The threats must be **by or associated with the government or a group the government cannot control.**  
5. If the government is **unable or unwilling** to stop the persecution, the threat can also satisfy the *unable‑or‑unwilling* prong for “future persecution.”  

If any of those steps fail, the court, as in *Jimenez*, will reject the claim under the past‑persecution prong of asylum.

### How does the ‘central reason’ test apply to political-opinion persecution?
Question:
Petitioners allege that they were targeted because of their political opinion, yet the court found that the violence was primarily motivated by Jimenez’s conflict with Don Rafa. The court refers to the ‘central reason’ test from Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti. Could someone explain how the central‑reason test is applied when a persecutor has multiple motives, and what evidence is necessary to satisfy that one protected ground is a central reason?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
The “central‑reason” test is satisfied when the record shows that, *at least one* protected‑ground motive was the *dominant* or *primary* driver of the persecution, even if other motives are present.  In mixed‑motive cases the adjudicator must weigh all facts, but if the majority of the evidence points to a different motive, the protected ground is only “tangential” and the applicant fails the nexus prong.  To meet the test, the applicant must demonstrate that:  

1. **There is at least one incident or **a pattern** of incidents** that can be clearly linked to the protected ground.  
2. **The persecutor’s motive in the incident(s) can be proven** to have been, or was, *at least one central reason* for the harm.  
3. **The evidence for other motives is not sufficient to tip the balance** to a different primary motive.  

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the court applied Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti exactly in this way.  The record was “replete with evidence that Jimenez’s conflict with Don Rafa Lancheros—his association with the narcotics‑trafficking group aligned with the president—was the principal reason the attacks were directed at him and his family.”  The court rejected the claim that political opinion was the primary cause because Jimenez’s politically‑motivated threats *pre‑dated* the escalation that followed his involvement in the property dispute, and the “escalated threats” were linked explicitly to Don Rafa’s protest that Jimenez had “inspected” his property.  The adjudicators therefore found that the protected‑ground motive was “tangential, subordinate, or a minor role” in the persecution (see *Rio v. Freedom*, 993 F.3d at 1514‑15).

Below is a concise, step‑by‑step guide on how the central‑reason test is applied in mixed‑motive cases, with the standard–of‑evidence structure that the court used in *Jimenez*.

---

## 1.  Identify the protected‑ground claim

The applicant must first state the protected ground (e.g., political opinion, particular social group, race). In *Jimenez*, the claim was political opinion (“membership in the Colombian Liberal Party”).

---

## 2.  Establish that the protection‑ground was **at least one central reason**

### a.  Look for *ordered or patterned* evidence

The court says the protected ground must be “a central reason” and not “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate.”  The record must contain at least one incident (or a consistent pattern) that can be causally linked to the protected ground.  The “at least one” requirement is *critical*—the existence of a single, clearly connected incident is enough, even if other unrelated incidents also occurred.

### b.  Evaluate *time‑to‑time* sequence

In *Jimenez*, the court noted that the threats and harassment escalated **after** the property dispute with Don Rafa.  The timing was crucial: the contested attacks were not happening *because* of Jimenez’s party work but because he *interfered* with Don Rafa’s illicit operations.  The court therefore found the political‑opinion motive had *no causal role* in the escalation.

### c.  Examine the persecutor’s statements

Statements made by the persecutor (e.g., “Don Rafa sent them”) are a key piece of evidence.  In *Jimenez*, the men who threatened the family explicitly said they had been sent by Don Rafa and were angry that Jimenez had “made trouble” for “snitches.”  That testimony placed Don Rafa’s personal grievance—not political opinion—at the heart of the persecution.

### d.  Consider *corroboration* from third parties and context

- **Other victims**: The court looked at the fate of other Liberal Party members who received threats *before* Jimenez’s dispute.  Their continued campaigning suggested the threat level was not rooted in political affiliation.  
- **Local police**: The officer’s statement that “the Colombian government is contributing to the problem” is *not* evidence of a political‑opinion motive.  
- **Crime‑control context**: The fact that Don Rafa was a narcotics‑trafficking leader aligned with the current president added weight to the “personal grievance” motive.

All of these points helped the court conclude that the political‑opinion motive was “minor” in the context of the specific persecution.

---

## 3.  Weigh the evidence: *Central vs. Peripheral*

The central‑reason test requires a *substantial‑evidence* (not "preponderant") determination that the protected ground was a *principal* motive.  The court therefore asked:

- **Did the majority of the evidence point to Don Rafa’s personal grievance?**  
  Yes.  
- **Are there any facts that show political opinion was the driver?**  
  No.  Threats before Jimenez’s dispute were “politically affiliated,” but they did not link to the *actual* violence that followed.  

Because the evidence tilted decisively toward another motive, the court found the protected‑ground claim was *tangential*.

---

## 4.  Evidence required to satisfy the test

| Evidence | Why it matters |
|---------|----------------|
| **A specific incident or pattern directly tied to the protected ground** | Shows the persecution is rooted in that ground. |
| **Persecutor’s statements or documents explaining motive** | Provides direct evidence of motive. |
| **Temporal sequencing that links the protected‑ground actions to the persecution** | Demonstrates causal relationship. |
| **Contextual data (e.g., other victims, political climate)** | Context can show whether similar actions were politically motivated. |
| **Ruling on other motives being stronger or equal** | If other motives outweigh the protected ground, the test fails. |

---

## 5.  Practical application in *Jimenez*

1. **Claim**: Political opinion (Liberal Party membership).  
2. **Incidents**: Threatening phone calls, vandalization, and armed harassment.  
3. **Persecutor statements**: Men explicitly said they were “sent by Don Rafa” and were angry at Jimenez for “snitching.”  
4. **Timing**: Escalation occurred *after* the property dispute.  
5. **Other motives**: Don Rafa’s business interests, his alignment with the president, and the nativist criminal context.  
6. **Conclusion**: Protected‑ground motive was *not central*; instead, the primary motive was personal grievance with Don Rafa over the property dispute.

Thus, the court rejected the nexus claim.

---

## Bottom line

*If you assert a mixed‑motive case, you must prove the protected ground was *the dominant* or *principal* reason for the persecution.  A single incident clearly linked to that ground suffices, but **all other evidence must not press the persecutor to another motive as the stronger one**.  In *Jimenez*, the evidence overwhelmingly favored Don Rafa’s personal grievance, making the political opinion claim peripheral and insubstantial.*

### What is the standard of review for the BIA’s findings of fact under § 1252(b)(4)(A)?
Question:
The opinion states that the court reviews the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard. I’ve seen the term used in other contexts, but can anyone clarify what “substantial evidence” means in this particular review, and how it differs from the standard used in merits‑review decisions by immigration judges?
Answer:
**Answer**

The 10th Circuit is clear that, under § 1252(b)(4)(A), the court reviews the BIA’s *findings of fact* “under a substantial‑evidence standard.”  That means the court gives the BIA’s factual determinations the benefit of the doubt; those findings are *conclusive* unless the record shows that *any reasonable adjudicator* would have reached the opposite conclusion.  See *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012).

### What “substantial evidence” means in this context

In practice, the substantial‑evidence test is less deferential than the “substantial evidence” standard used in most ordinary administrative appeals, but it is not merely “the same evidence” as the BIA’s.  The standard requires that:

1. **The record actually contain evidence** that a reasonable adjudicator would have found.  
2. **That evidence is not merely circumstantial or weak** but, taken together, supports the factual conclusion.  

The BIA’s facts are not “pre‑certified” or presumed to be beyond question; the court must still look at the evidence the BIA considered.  The court consults the IJ’s “more complete explanation” of the facts when the BIA relied on the IJ (as the opinion notes), but it does not re‑weigh the evidence in a new light.  See *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

### How this differs from the standard used in merits‑review decisions

On a “merits‑review” of the IJ’s decision (the ordinary review of an asylee’s claim), the court also uses a substantial‑evidence standard, but it applies it to *both* factual findings and legal conclusions drawn from those findings.  The difference here is that the 10th Circuit is reviewing the BIA’s *final* order under § 1252(b)(4)(A).  The procedural posture is a *final‑order* review, not a merits‑review:

* **Merits review** – the IJ’s findings of fact are reviewed de novo under substantial evidence, and the court can consider new evidence or re‑evaluate the IJ’s facts.  
* **Final‑order review** – the court reviews the BIA’s findings as part of a single, final administrative decision.  The standard is still substantial evidence, but the court is restricted to the record that supports the BIA’s *final decision*, and it gives the BIA’s determinations the benefit of the doubt.

Thus, although the formal standard is the same word (substantial evidence), the scope in a final‑order review is narrower and the court is expressly directed not to re‑weigh evidence or read the BIA’s findings differently—a “catch‑all” limitation that is not present in a merits review.

### Bottom line

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court correctly applied the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings under § 1252(b)(4)(A).  The court found that the BIA’s findings were supported by the record and therefore were conclusive, and it refused to overturn them.  The standard is the same phrase used elsewhere, but in a final‑order context the court is even more deferential to the agency’s factual conclusions.

### Why did the court refuse to consider the petitioners’ fear of future persecution under the ‘unable or unwilling’ test?
Question:
The opinion disclaims that the petitioners failed to show that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them, citing insufficient reporting. How does the ‘unable or unwilling’ analysis weigh evidence of the government’s response to past persecution, and why is a lack of police reporting not fatal in other cases?
Answer:
**Why the court could not apply the “unable or unwilling” prong to the petitioners’ case**

The “unable or unwilling” test is part of the **well‑founded fear of future persecution** prong of asylum.  
When that prong is asserted the applicant must show that, *if* the authorities were notified of the past persecution, they would **not** be able or would refuse to provide protection.  (See *Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861.)

---

### 1.  What the courts look for

The *Tenth Circuit* acknowledges two kinds of evidence that weigh heavily on the government‑response analysis:

| Evidence | What it signals | How it is applied |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Police action to stop the persecutors.** | That the state is willing to intervene. | If the police actually foiled a threat, the “unwilling” claim collapses. |
| **Offer of protection (e.g., protective custody).** | That the state is able to shield the applicant. | If such a protection was offered, the “unable” claim is negated. |

The court also stresses that the **first question** in the inquiry is whether the applicant denied the state knowledge of the past persecution.  “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped” (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861–62).  The *absence of a reporting* *can* undermine a claim, but it is *not fatal*; the applicant can still satisfy the prong if it can show that filing a report would have been futile or dangerous.

---

### 2.  How the record in *Jimenez v. Bondi* weighs

1. **No formal police report.**  
   The petitioners only spoke informally with two officers who told them they should “not seek trouble.”  No written complaint was filed.  
   The court accepted that this *lack of reporting* makes it hard to assess how the police would respond, but noted that *the fact alone is insufficient to prove the government was unwilling or unable* (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861; *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2*).

2. **Evidence of police intent to act.**  
   The court pointed out that the government had *already* attempted “several searches and seizures” of Don Rafa’s property.  That shows the police were aware of the situation and had taken concrete steps, undermining the petitioners’ assertion that the government would “refuse or be unable to stop the perpetrators.”  The court stated that “the evidence counters Petitioners’ argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government and would be unwilling or unable to stop the persecutors” (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861).

3. **No systemic evidence of state failure.**  
   The court explained that *one unhelpful officer is not proof of systemic unwillingness* (“…a one‑off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” *Osorio‑Mora⁎, 72 F.4th at 743).  The petitioners failed to produce evidence that the Colombian police, or any Colombian authority, routinely failed to protect the cartel’s informants or to investigate crimes against political opponents.

4. **Argument that contacting the state would have been futile is unsupported.**  
   The petitioners’ claim that the government would “not be able to stop the persecutors” because they were aligned with the president is *not proven* in the record.  The court explicitly rejected that proof: “Petitioners cannot show that the Colombian government was, or would be, unable or unwilling to protect Petitioners had it known about the threats they received” (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861). The court treated the threat that “the men told them to move out of the neighborhood” as not extending to the country as a whole, so a government‑wide “unwillingness” could not be inferred.

---

### 3.  Why a lack of police reporting is “not fatal”

The court cites *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and *Singh* to explain that a missing report does not automatically defeat the claim.  A petitioner may still satisfy the “unwilling or unable” prong by showing that *reporting would have been futile or dangerous* (e.g., “the state would have turned a blind eye” or “the police would have protected the trafficker”).  In *Jimenez*, however, the record *does not contain* an inference that reporting would have been futile: the police had *already* tried to intervene in the property dispute, and the petitioners did not show that the authorities would have protected them if they had come forward.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for the petitioners

Because the record lacks evidence that:

* The petitioners *tried* to involve the state in a formal way (no police filing), **and**  
* The state *would not have acted* successfully or was *systemically unwilling* to protect them,

the court concluded that the “unable or unwilling” analysis could not be satisfied.  Consequently, the petitioners could not establish a well‑founded fear of future persecution.  This conclusion, coupled with the earlier denial of past persecution, led the court to deny review of the immigration judge’s decision.

In sum, the court refused to consider the petitioners’ fear under the “unable or unwilling” test because the record did not support the key elements—lack of a police report *and* evidence that a state‑based intervention would have failed—necessary to satisfy that part of the asylum standard.

### What does ‘safe internal relocation’ mean in asylum law, and why did the court reject the petitioners’ claims?
Question:
The opinion dismisses the petitioners’ argument that they cannot safely relocate within Colombia, citing that they lived safely with relatives for three weeks. How is ‘safe internal relocation’ evaluated, what evidence is required, and why did the court find relocation to be ‘reasonable’ despite the petitioners’ claims of insecurity?
Answer:
**Answer**

**Short answer:**  
“Safe internal relocation” is a *requirements‑test* that the asylum applicant must satisfy to rebut the de facto presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution. The test asks whether the applicant could **reasonably** (i.e., based on facts available in the record) move to *another part of the same country* and avoid the threat posed by the persecutor(s). In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the court found that the petitioners could have “safely and reasonably relocated” to the countryside (where they stayed for three weeks) and that their alleged threats were directed only at a particular neighborhood, not at the country as a whole. The court therefore concluded that safe internal relocation was possible and that the petitioners’ claims of insecurity were insufficient to override the ordinary presumption that a person who can relocate safely is unlikely to face future persecution.

---

### 1. What the standard actually asks

The Court repeats the *Rivera‑Barrientos* framework:  

> “The government may rebut the presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution… *if, by a preponderance of the evidence, the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.*” ( *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646, citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Thus, **two elements** are required:  

1. **Feasibility** – is there a *practical* place where the applicant could live that is outside the persecutor’s influence?  
2. **Reasonableness** – does the record show that the applicant *could actually* make that move; i.e., do they have family ties, economic means, or credible evidence that they would *safe* there?

The Court’s analysis is *fact‑specific*; the record must show that the applicant *could* mitigate the threat by moving.

---

### 2. Evidence the Court considered

| Evidence | How it helped the Court find relocation *reasonable***
|----------|-------------------------------------
| **Three‑week stay in the Bogota countryside** | Shows the petitioners *actually lived* there safely after the last threat; demonstrates that the countryside is a viable, **non‑threatened** area. |
| **Family ties (mother, father, relatives)** | The presence of family in the country, and no reports that those relatives had the same political opinions or were under threat, indicates that the petitioners would have local support if they returned. |
| **Threats were localized** | The threats came from men tied to “Don Rafa” and were *directional* to a neighborhood, not the entire country; the men never told the family to “move out of the country.” |
| **No evidence of broad government cruelty** | The petitioners never claimed the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them; the Court noted that “the government had attempted several searches and seizures,” suggesting at least *some* level of control over the perpetrators. |
| **Petitioners’ own action** | They voluntarily went to their mother’s house after the last threat. Their decision to leave for the U.S. happened only after they **tried** a domestic refuge that proved inadequate. |

Because the record *demonstrated* that a **safe place existed** and that the petitioners had *actually used* that place, the Court concluded that safe internal relocation was **available and reasonable**.

---

### 3. Why the court rejected the petitioners’ claim of insecurity

* The Court emphasized that *“security” is a *function* of the specific threat, not an assumption of country‑wide indiscriminate danger.* The plaintiffs’ own evidence—the three‑week stay—showed that the area they chose was safe.  
* The Court noted that **parking a threat on the whole country is not a prerequisite** for a refusal under the safe‑relocation bar. It is enough that the threat could be *avoided by moving to a safer region*, which the record supports.  
* The petitioners’ narrative that the persecutors were “closely aligned with the Colombian government” was contradicted by the fact that *the police had already made several searches at the property* and that the petitioners themselves were *not able to get help* from a single “unhelpful” officer—an isolated incident that the Court did not find sufficient to establish that the government was “unwilling or unable” to protect them.

In sum, the Court held that the **jurisprudence requires the record to show a *reasonable plan* for relocation; the petitioners proved exactly that**—they lived safely in the countryside, had family support, and had no evidence that relocation would be impossible or unsafe.

---

### 4. Bottom line for you

If you are a student looking at this ruling, remember:

* **Safe internal relocation is *not* an automatic bar**; it is a *fact‑specific* test that focuses on whether the applicant can *avoid future persecution* by moving elsewhere in the country.  
* The Court expected *concrete evidence* that the applicant *could and would* relocate safely (family ties, transient stays, economic resources).  
* In *Jimenez*, the record showed that the petitioners *actually did* move to a safe place, rendering their fears of state‑linked persecution irrelevant to the safe‑relocation bar.

That’s why the court dismissed the petitioners’ argument and concluded that safe internal relocation was indeed “reasonable” despite their claims of insecurity.

### How are asylum and withholding of removal interrelated in terms of the burden of proof?
Question:
The court notes that if a petitioner fails to meet asylum criteria, they also fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. Could someone walk me through the logic linking these two reliefs, especially the preponderance of evidence burden for asylum vs. the well‑founded fear standard for withholding?
Answer:
**Answer to “How are asylum and withholding of removal interrelated in terms of the burden of proof?”**

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the court lays out the epistemic relationship between the two forms of relief. The key points are:

| Relief | Who bears the burden? | Standard | How the standard differs |
|--------|-----------------------|----------|--------------------------|
| **Asylum** | Applicant | **Preponderance of the evidence** (POTE) | The applicant must prove that, on the balance of probabilities, the facts show 1) past persecution, 2) a nexus to a protected ground, and 3) that the government **is or would be unwilling or unable** to protect him. |
| **Withholding of Removal** | Applicant | **Well‑founded fear** (a higher, “well‑settled” standard) | The applicant must show that the fear of future persecution is **well‑founded** (i.e., a reasonable person in the same situation would conceive of persecution) **and that the government is or would be unwilling or unable** to protect him. |

The court makes three doctrinal points that tie the two together:

1. **Withholding is a “higher” standard.**  
   The Ninth‑Circuit principle that “previously satisfied asylum standards automatically satisfy withholding” is reflected verbatim in *Jimenez*: “Because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well‑founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.” (Conclusion). Thus, if proof is insufficient under the **preponderance** standard, it will also be insufficient under the **well‑founded** standard. The court treats the two reliefs as *nested*; the fear prerequisite for withholding is not an *additional* fact but a *higher* threshold for the same facts that support asylum.

2. **The same factual elements are evaluated for both reliefs.**  
   The asylum framework (Niang v. Gonzales; Rivera‑Barrientos) requires (i) past persecution, (ii) nexus to a protected ground, and (iii) government unwillingness/unability. The withholding framework (“Government Protection” section) “considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution…whether the government … offered protection…or is unwilling or unable.” (Sub‑section **E**). *Jimenez* re‑states that the “well‑founded fear” analysis hinges on the same factual questions: did the government know, respond, and would it have helped? The court applies the *preponderance* standard to discuss whether there *is* a refusal or inability on the government's part; the *well‑founded* standard simply requires that the asylum burden of proof succeed.

3. **Failure to satisfy asylum’s evidentiary bar precludes withholding.**  
   The court explicitly applies a “preponderance” test to the record and finds substantial evidence that the petitioners lacked past persecution and a political‑opinion nexus. *Jimenez* notes that because the asylum burden was not met, “they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal” (Conclusion). The logic is that one cannot satisfy a standard that is *strictly* higher when the lower standard cannot be met.

### Illustration from the opinion

1. **Past persecution** – The IJ and BIA found “petitions’ harm, even in the aggregate, does not amount to past persecution” (Sub‑section **D**). Under a *preponderance* analysis of the evidence, those facts are not enough.
2. **Nexus** – Petitioners could not show that their political opinion was the “central reason” for the harm; the evidence indicated that the perpetrators were motivated by the defendant’s property dispute (Sub‑section **D**).
3. **Government protection** – Because the petitioners “failed to report” wrongdoing and the government “was aware” of the property, the record did not show that the government was unwilling or unable to protect them (Sub‑section **E**).

> “In sum, there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s findings that Petitioners do not qualify for asylum. **And because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well‑founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.**”

So the logic is straightforward: the *preponderance* burden is the “gatekeeper”; once it is not satisfied, the *well‑founded* burden, which demands the same facts be more substantial, cannot be met.

---

**Bottom line for a law student:**

* If you cannot meet the asylum criteria on a preponderance of the evidence, you automatically fail withholding because the latter requires a *higher* standard (well‑founded fear) built on the same facts. The court’s language in *Jimenez* codifies this logic and illustrates it with the same evidentiary questions used for asylum.

### What role does a provisional law license play in asylum proceedings?
Question:
Jimenez is described as a provisional lawyer who investigated a property dispute. He claims his role might have made him a target. Does having legal credentials or the perception of being a law‑student influence an asylum applicant’s credibility or affect how the court analyzes persecution rooted in professional activities?
Answer:
**Answer**

A provisional law license (or the perception that a person is a law‑student or a “provisional lawyer”) does not automatically give an asylum applicant any special credibility or a different analytical framework.  The courts evaluate *past persecution* and *future persecution* under the same statutory test, regardless of the applicant’s professional status.  In *Jiménez v. Bondi*, the 10th Circuit made it clear that Jiménez’s career position was simply part of the factual background that the immigration judge (and later the BIA) considered, but it played no privileged role in the asylum analysis.

---

### 1. The “provisional lawyer” is only a factual element

The opinion repeatedly refers to Jiménez as a *provisional lawyer* and notes that he was working on a property dispute in 2022.  The court treats this as an event that helped explain *why* the threat‑givers—namely Don Rafa’s associates—targeted him and his family:

> “...the men accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and considered him responsible for the searches and seizures at the property.”  
> *See* the court’s discussion in § D. “Nexus” (pp. […]).

There is no indication that the courts considered Jiménez’s provisional license or the mere fact that he had “legal credentials” as an *admissible* or *more credible* piece of evidence.  The court’s focus remains on whether (i) the conduct constitutes persecution, (ii) it was tied to a protected ground, and (iii) the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect the family.

---

### 2. Perception of legal training is not a protected ground

Persecution on account of *political opinion* is a protected ground under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i).  The court’s analysis of the nexus requirement was strictly framed around political opinion:

> “To show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish that the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.”  
> *See* Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641 (10th Cir. 2012).

The opinion held that Jiménez’s professional engagement with Don Rafa and his subsequent threats were *not* “at least one central reason” for the persecution.  The court found that the predominant motive was the threat to Don Rafa’s business interest, not the political affiliation of Jiménez or his wife.  Thus, even if the court had entertained the possibility that Jiménez’s status as a provisional lawyer made him “look like a lawyer” or “more credible,” that status was not a protected ground.

---

### 3. Credibility is addressed under the “substantial‑evidence” standard

The standard for reviewing the IJ and BIA findings is *substantial evidence*.  The court affirmed the findings that Jiménez’s assertions did not rise to the level of persecution because “the threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution” and because there was no accompanying “motive based on a protected ground.”  Credibility and the presence of “legal credentials” are extraneous to that standard. The court gives weight to the IJ’s more complete explanation:

> “Because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision, we review the BIA’s decision, but 'we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.'”  
> *See* Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848 (10th Cir. 2025).

No part of that explanation hinges on Jiménez’s provisional license.  It was simply one of many factual elements considered.

---

### 4. The law‑student perception has no effect on the government‑unwillingness analysis

When evaluating whether the Colombian government is “unwilling or unable” to protect the applicants, the court notes:

> “…whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.”  
> *See* the court’s discussion in § E. “Government Protection.”

Jiménez briefly explained that he reported threats to a police officer and was told that nothing could be done.  The court considered this as “minimal effort” rather than a demonstration that a lawful professional would be given special consideration.  The evaluation was factual—did the police try to help? Did the general rule of government failure apply?—and did not factor in Jiménez’s provisional status.

---

### Bottom line

In *Jiménez v. Bondi*, the court treated Jiménez’s provisional law license as a detail that helped explain the *facts* of the case, not as a variable that altered the legal standards or analytical framework.  It does not give an asylum applicant enhanced credibility nor does it change how courts weigh past or future persecution.  The standard is the same: (1) was there persecution (above merely “threats”); (2) was it tied to a protected ground as a “central reason”; (3) is the government unwilling or unable to protect the applicant.  Provisional legal credentials do not shift any of those prongs.
