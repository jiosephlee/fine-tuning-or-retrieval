Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 2: # 2. The Governing Legal Standards for Asylum

The United States furnishes asylum as a discretionary form of relief under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Every denial of asylum that flows from a Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) order is susceptible to appellate review. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit reaffirmed the doctrinal framework that a claimant must satisfy in order to survive such a denial: the claimant must first prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he or she is a refugee; then the BIA must find that the claimant suffered past persecution; that the persecution was on account of a statutorily protected ground; and that the government of the claimant’s country is either unwilling or unable to protect the claimant from future persecution. Where all those elements are met, the BIA has found that the claimant has a well‑founded fear of future persecution. Finally, the claimant must show that safe relocation within the country is either not available or would be unreasonable.

Below that brief: (1) the discretionary nature of asylum under § 1158(b)(1); (2) the burden of proof; (3) the statutory definition in § 1101(a)(42)(A); (4) the three‑step past‑persecution framework; (5) the well‑founded fear and “unable or unwilling” test; and (6) the safe‑relocation test.

## Discretionary Nature of Asylum under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) states that the Attorney General may grant asylum to qualified noncitizens. The statute makes no substantive requirement for eligibility beyond the refugee definition (see § 1158(b)(1)). Rather, it places the power entirely in the hands of the Executive. Consequently, the BIA, the final administrative adjudicator, is empowered to apply the same discretion that the Attorney General does: even if a claimant meets every statutory criterion, the Board can deny asylum if it believes, based on the record, that awarding asylum is not in the public interest. *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) affirms that the BIA’s discretion is a property of the statute and not subject to review unless the Board’s findings of fact are not supported by substantial evidence.

## Burden of Proof and Preponderance of Evidence Standard

The homogenized standard in asylum matters gives the claimant the burden to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she is a refugee. The *preponderance* test requires that the claim be more likely than not to be true. The *Jimenez* opinion reiterated that the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the appellate court finds that the administrative record, viewed by a reasonable adjudicator, compels a different conclusion. *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012). Thus, discretion is not merely a “means” but a “burden”-bearing exercise: the claimant must convince the Board, and the Board’s decision is only reviewable for lack of substantial evidence.

## Section 1101(a)(42)(A) – Definition of a Refugee

Section 1101(a)(42)(A) is the statutory yardstick by which the BIA measures asylum. The statute reads:

> **“A refugee is a person who is outside the country of his or her nationality who is unable or unwilling to return there because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.”** *INA § 1101(a)(42)(A).*

Two things matter. First, a claimant must be **outside** his or her country of nationality at the time of the asylum claim (i.e., not present in that country). Second, the claimant must establish that he or she is **unable or unwilling** to return because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on a protected ground.

In *Jimenez*, the Court noted that this standard is the gatekeeper for the entire asylum process. Without satisfying this definition, the claimant is dismissed entirely, and no further analysis of past persecution or government protection is required.

## Three‑Step Framework for Past Persecution

The BIA’s determination of past persecution follows a triad established by the case law. A claimant must show:

1. **An Incident (or Incidents) that Rise to the Level of Persecution.** The incident must be such that the recipient suffered physical, emotional, or psychological harm. Courts distinguish simple threats or police harassment from persecution, requiring that the harm be **offensive** and more than a mere restriction of liberty or the mere threat of violence. *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004). The *Jimenez* opinion relied heavily on *Karki v. Holder* and *Brandy v. Holder* to clarify that solitary threats, absent accompanying physical harm or a pattern of harassment, do not constitute persecution in isolation. The court introduced the idea of a **cumulative** analysis: even if no single incident rises to the level of persecution, the aggregation of multiple incidents may. *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (2011).

2. **That the Incident is on Account of a Statutorily Protected Ground.** The claimant must demonstrate that the perpetrator’s motive was—at least in part—linked to one of the protected grounds in § 1101(a)(42)(A): race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. A protected ground may not be merely incidental or superficial. *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d 646, 647 (quoting § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In *Jimenez*, the Court found that the claimant’s political activity was a **tangential** rather than **central** reason for the alleged persecution. The Board’s analysis of why the Muslims were targeted—whether because of political activism or because of the landlord’s crime—was decisive.

3. **That the Persecution was Committed by the Government or by a Group the Government Controls or is Unable or Unwilling to Control.** The protected incident must have involved the state (directly prosecuting, arresting, or executing any wrongdoing) or a group that the state controls or fails to restrain. In *Jimenez*, the Court turned to the alleged involvement of *Don Rafa*, an informally described leader of a narcotrafficking guerrilla aligned with the president. The question then was whether this group was under the government's aegis. The grievance “withholding of removal” anchored the Board’s request that the government be rendered “unwilling or unwilling” to protect the claimant.

Because the BIA’s decision is summary, the appellate court, in *Jimenez*, affirms that the Board did not err where the BIA’s findings of fact were corroborated by substantial evidence.

## Well‑Founded Fear and the “Unable or Unwilling” Test

Once past persecution is established, the claimant is then judged for a **well‑founded fear of future persecution**. The “well‑founded fear” is a higher bar than past persecution; it requires more than a vague fear or a desire for safety. The statutory statute lumps this step together with the “unwilling or unable” test: *“It is presumed that the government is unwilling or unable to protect the applicant from persecution.”*

The Court has identified two key strands:

1. **Government Response to Past Persecution.** The Board must weigh evidence regarding whether the government tried or would have tried to prevent the actual persecution. Elements include law‑enforcement response, prosecution of the perpetrators, and protective measures. *Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861. The presence of a police attempt to raid the property or investigations into the landlord’s conduct weighed against a finding that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect the claimant. Likewise, *Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland* noted that the testimony that a police officer told the claimant “you should not seek trouble” did not constitute proof of a systemic unwillingness.

2. **Reporter of Persecution to the State.** If a claimant reports persecution, the government may rebut the presumption that it is unwilling or unable to protect the claimant. The court held that the mere failure to report, as long as it is not outweighed by clear evidence that reporting would be futile or dangerous, can kill the claim. *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, id. at *2. In *Jimenez*, the claimant’s failure to file a formal police report coupled with the absence of evidence that a formal report would have been futile made it impossible to locate a factual basis for the “unwilling or unable” conclusion.

If the BIA finds that a claimant has a **well‑founded fear of future persecution** and the government is **unable or unwilling** to protect him, then asylum is a viable path. In *Jimenez*, the Board and the Court concluded that the claimant’s fear was **not well‑founded** because the threat was incidental to the landlord’s drug‑related activity and not directly tied to the claimant’s political stance.

## Safe Relocation (Fundamental Change/Test) and its Evidentiary Burden

The “safe relocation” exception is a **positive assurance** that a claimant will not suffer future persecution in any part of his or her country. It is triggered in two contexts:

- **Fundamental Change**: The claimant demonstrates that circumstances have altered in a way that removes the basis for future persecution. 
- **Relocation Test**: The claimant can show that he or she can reasonably relocate to a region in which the threat does not exist.

Both require **preponderance of the evidence**. *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646. The Board can negate the well‑founded fear *only* if it proves that future persecution is avoidable by relocation. In *Jimenez*, the Court found that the claimant, who had family residencies in Bogotá and previously lived in the countryside for weeks, demonstrated that relocation in Colombia was possible. The Court also noted that the threat was exclusively from a neighborhood recruiter, not an organized, state‑backed entity, so the claimant's social environment remained unchanged by relocation.

In sum, the safe‑relocation test is a stringent hurdle that often falls short for claimants whose threat is of a localized, non–governmental nature. *Jimenez* recognized that the presence of family does not automatically provide safety, but that safe relocation, fortified by records, can create a valid defense. However, due to the inadequate evidence in the *Jimenez* record, the Board could not find any risk that exempted the claimant.

---

**Take‑away for the Law Student:** In a denial of asylum, the BIA’s analysis starts with the rubric in § 1101(a)(42)(A). If the claimant fails the refugee definition, the denial is immediate. If the claimant satisfies “unable or unwilling” and the three steps for past persecution, the BIA may give the claimant a *well‑founded fear of future persecution*. The BIA must also address whether the claimant can relocate safely. Only when *all* those analyses culminate in a finding—and when the record supports each element—does a refugee status excuse the removal. In *Jimenez*, the Court found that the claimant failed to meet the refugee definition because he did not establish past persecution on a protected ground connected to a government‑controlled threat; consequently, the Board’s denial was upheld, and the “well‑founded fear” and safe‑relocation arguments were not legally actionable.
