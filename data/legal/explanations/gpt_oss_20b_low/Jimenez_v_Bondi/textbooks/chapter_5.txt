Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 5: # 5. Government Protection, Safe Relocation, and Final Holding  

In this chapter we trace how the Tenth Circuit applied the statutory framework for asylum and withholding, focusing on the policy that requires an applicant to show that a persecutor is “unable or unwilling” to protect her or him. We then examine how that “lack of protection” analysis fed into the safe‑relocation inquiry and ultimately produced the court’s denial of the petition for review.  

## Government Protection Analysis: Notice, Response, Systemic Failure  

The INA requires the applicant to demonstrate that the government has a “notice of the past persecution” and that it failed or could not provide protection. The Seventh Circuit’s *Osorio‑Mora­les* and the Ninth Circuit’s *Aviles‑Gonzalez* decisions teach that evidence of a single unhelpful police officer is insufficient; the court looks for a pattern of systemic failure.  

The Board’s record offers only two incidents. First, Jimenez reported a threatening brochure to a police patrol in Bogotá. The officer answered that nothing could be done and advised the petitioners to leave the city. Second, the petitioners claimed that two men, who later said they were “sent” by Don Rafa, held a factory worker at gunpoint and demanded that the petitioners leave the neighborhood. In each case the petitioners sought help from the local law‑enforcement apparatus. The courts, however, see this as a private encounter with an officer whose remarks do not reflect the Colombian government’s overall policy or capacity. *Osorio‑Mora­les* stresses that a single officer’s statement must be weighed against evidence of a wider institutional failure. The petitioners failed to offer any evidence of prosecutions, protective custody, or systematic police indifference in Bogotá or elsewhere in Colombia.  

Because the petitioners did not file any police report of persecution, the courts regard the record as lacking sufficient “notice.” The *Aviles‑Gonzalez* decision makes clear that, absent notice, the government’s willingness or ability cannot be evaluated. Accordingly, the Tenth Circuit found no “systemic evidence” that the Colombian authorities had either stopped or attempted to stop the persecutors, nor did they show a pattern of refusing to protect political opponents. The record merely confirms the petitioners’ fear that “the government was closely aligned with the criminals,” an assertion that the court also deems unsubstantiated.

## Application of *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and *Osorio‑Mora­les* Precedent  

The court’s treatment of the petitioners’ claims mirrors the reasoning in *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and *Osorio‑Mora­les* in two ways:

1. **Notice Requirement** – In *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, the BIA rejected an asylum claim because the applicant had not reported persecution to the police. The Tenth Circuit followed that logic, noting the petitioners’ one‑off conversation with the patrol officer did not provide the necessary notice.

2. **Systemic Failure Standard** – *Osorio‑Mora­les* urged that the burden is on the applicant to show repeated systemic lapses by the government. The petitioner’s evidence is limited to isolated anecdotes; the court rejected the claim as “insufficient to prove an unwilling or unable government.”

By applying these precedents, the court affirmed that the petitioners lacked the evidence required to establish a “willing or able” Colombian state.

## Evaluation of Safe Relocation  

Safe‑relocation is a secondary obstacle that a denial of asylum or withholding must satisfy. The Board and, therefore, the appellate court, look for a “principal, permanent, and perpetually risky regional threat” that precludes internal movement. The petitioners attempted to argue that they could not relocate safely because of “administrative and economic burdens” and because their persecutors have ties to the president. The courts rejected these claims for three reasons:

* **Stable Family Network** – The petitioners have relatives who remain safely in Bogotá and in the countryside for at least three weeks after the assaults, demonstrating that a temporary relocation is feasible.

* **Prior Rural Residence** – Jiménez’s mother’s house in the Bogotá countryside served as a safe refuge for the family’s three sons. This prior safe residence suggests that political persecution was not intolerably widespread in other Colombian districts.

* **Lack of a Credible Future Persecution Claim** – Since the court found no well‑founded fear of future persecution, the safe‑relocation inquiry is moot. In *Carias‑Mejia*, the court emphasized that a denial of asylum that precedes a safe‑relocation judgment falls outside the scope of its review.

The court concluded that the petitioners could relocate without enduring continued persecution and that the government was not complicit in preventing such relocation.

## Implications of Lacking a Well‑Founded Fear of Future Persecution  

A well‑founded fear is a prerequisite for both asylum and withholding. The court’s failure to find a well‑founded fear in *Jimenez v. Bondi* had a twofold effect:

1. **Denial of Asylum** – Because the petitioners could not demonstrate past persecution, nexus, or a well‑founded fear, the asylum grant was denied under the “three‑prong” test in *Niang v. Gonzales*.

2. **Denial of Withholding of Removal** – Withholding is “higher‑handed” than asylum. The court ruled that, since the petitioners did not satisfy the asylum “past persecution” criterion, the BIA was also correct in holding that they lacked the “well‑founded fear” required under *Carias‑Mejia*.

In both cases, the absence of well‑founded fear implied that the petitioners could be returned to Colombia without danger, negating the need to apply the safe‑relocation standard or seek police protection.

## Court’s Final Decision and Its Reasoning Hierarchy  

The appellate decision culminated in the order to deny the petition for review. The court articulated a clear hierarchy in its reasoning:

1. **Review of the BIA’s factual findings** – Under the substantial‑evidence standard, the court found the BIA’s findings regarding past persecution, nexus, and government protection to be supported by the record.

2. **Legal assessment of the asylum framework** – The court applied *Niang*, *Rivera‑Barrientos*, and *Karki* to conclude the petitioners could not establish past persecution or a nexus to a protected ground.

3. **Withholding determination** – Leveraging *Carias‑Mejia* and the absence of well‑founded fear, the court held that the petitioners failed to meet the higher standard for withholding.

4. **Safe‑relocation footnote** – Because the key prerequisites for asylum and withholding were missing, the safe‑relocation inquiry was effectively precluded.

5. **Summary denial** – The court, therefore, denied the petition for review, affirming the BIA’s denial.

The decision underscores a strict adherence to the legal framework for asylum relief and demonstrates that, absent a robust record showing both past persecution and a well‑founded fear tied to a protected ground, applicants cannot rely on the government’s willingness or inability to protect them. The Tenth Circuit’s use of *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and *Osorio‑Mora­les* signals that future petitioners will need a more comprehensive evidentiary showing of systemic state failure and concrete reports of persecution to succeed.
