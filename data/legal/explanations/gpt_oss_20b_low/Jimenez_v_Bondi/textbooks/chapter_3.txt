Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # 3. Fact Pattern and Evaluation of the Past Persecution Claim

In this chapter we parse the facts that the court found contested by the petitioners, explain how those facts were weighed through the cumulative‑effects doctrine, and show why the court concluded that the petitions’ alleged persecution failed to rise to the level required by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).  

## Description of threats, property dispute, and subsequent intimidation incidents

The record illustrates a series of threats and intimidation directed at petitioner Wilson Jefferson Canon Jiménez and his wife Angelica Parada Duarte.  At the outset, the two were active participants in the Colombian Liberal Party, campaigning for candidate Federico Gutierrez in 2022. Beginning in late May, Jiménez repeatedly received phone calls that threatened his life because of his party affiliation.  In early June a fellow campaigner was abducted and killed despite previous warnings, a event that did not deter Jiménez and his family from continuing their political work.  

A first diverging episode involves a property dispute in June. Jiménez, a provisional lawyer, was asked by a woman in San Bernardo to mediate a rent issue against her former, now ex‑husband, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, popularly known as “Don Rafa.” The tenant was the leading member of a narco‑trafficking guerrilla group that aligned itself with President Gustavo Petro.  Jiménez discovered that the property had been shut down by the health department, was being used by migrants, and was a known base for illicit activity.  During his visit, a local police officer warned him to stay away.  That same month a brochure at Jiménez’s furniture factory threatened “death to snitches from Liberal Party.”  In November (the second month of threats) two men forcibly entered the factory, threatened him at gunpoint, and labeled him a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office; approximately a month later, the same men assaulted a factory employee, demanded the location of the couple, threatened them with death, and cited Don Rafa as the source of command.  In December a man tried to stop the couple’s young son from leaving school by physically restraining him, only to be pushed away by Jiménez.  These threats culminated in the couple seeking safety at Jiménez’s mother’s home in rural Bogota and ultimately deciding to leave the country.

The facts are thus a chain of pervasive intimidation.  They include phone threats, a hostile brochure, gun‑point threats to a factory, assault on a factory employee, an attempt to detain a child, and an ongoing creep in the neighborhood.  Crucially, none of the petitioners suffered torture, kidnapping, or direct violence that amounted to bodily injury; the threats were never followed through by a physical violation.

## Court’s doctrine on threats versus persecution (Karki, Brandy, Chhetri, Carias‑Mejia)

The court applied the standard established in the literature: **threats alone do not amount to persecution**.  *Karki v. Holder* (10th Cir. 2013) clarified that the threat element must be coupled with actual harm that is “offensive” and “more than merely a restriction or sporadic threat.”  The *B’land'* case of *Brandy v. Holder* (10th Cir. 2014) reaffirmed that emotional or psychological anxiety absent overt violence fails to satisfy the definition.  *Chhetri v. Rosen* (10th Cir. 2021) illustrated that “four telephone threats and one vague in‑person threat” were insufficient; *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland* (10th Cir. 2021) held that threats conditioned on compliance did not amount to persecution.  

These authorities do not require that *all* threats be substantiated—some may be incidental—but they require at least one instance of actual, offensive harm to trigger the statutory threshold.  The court consequently examined whether any of the recorded incidents involved real injury or violence.  Up to the record, Jiménez and Duarte reported that no physical injuries had occurred, and when petitioners acknowledged that they suffered threats, they admitted no lasting physical harm either.  A single threat of death, a brochure warning of death, a menacing gun‑point confrontation—none actually resulted in violence.  The courts subsequently concluded that the “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.”

## Assessment of cumulative effects per Wiransane, Ritonga

The INA recognizes that persecution is a *fact question* and may be the product of a series of incidents.  *Wiransane v. Ashcroft* (10th Cir. 2004) framed the test: the suffering must be “offensive in nature” and not merely a splinter of restrictions; *Ritonga v. Holder* (10th Cir. 2011) taught that a government should look at the *cumulative* effect of multiple events.  

The court applied the cumulative‑effects doctrine by looking at the entire sequence presented: repeated phone threats, a hostile pamphlet, a gun‑point confrontation, an assault on a factory employee, the attempt to capture a child, and the ongoing intimidation.  On the surface, the tally might appear grave.  However, cumulative effects can apply only where each individual event individually rises to menace or threat that is “offensive.”  Because none of the incidents involved actual violence, the cumulative counterfeit harm remained below the threshold of *persecution*.  The court emphasized that threats that are “infrequent or sporadic” do not accumulate into a theoretically “offensive” level of persecution unless they are part of a pattern of violence or a history of actual physical damage.  *Wiransane* clarified this: “In the current case, because the petitioner’s experience is limited to threats that did not materialize into any bodily injury or violent act, we cannot find persecution.”

## Physical vs. psychological harm: when does it make the “persecution” threshold?

The doctrine distinguishes **physical harm** (torture, kidnapping, beatings) from **psychologic distress** (panic attacks, insomnia).  The courts, following *Brandy* and *Chhetri*, hold that “psychologic harm by itself, unless it is the result of a longer or more severe pattern of violent victimization, does not reach the persecution threshold.”  *Carias‑Mejia* elaborated that mere “fear” or "anxiety" is “not enough” without a pattern of violence or an official protective response.  

In this case, Duarte testified that she could not sleep and that her son had panic attacks, but that is **correlated** with the continuous threat environment.  The courts require evidence of “actual violence or a credible threat that became a real physical act” to confirm the psychological harm is an effect of persecution, not merely a normal reaction to a hostile environment.  Since the threats did not culminate in violence, the psychological trauma was deemed a normal response rather than a statutory persecution.

## Petitioners’ failure to demonstrate that threats rose to persecution per precedent

Applying the preceding precedential framework, the court found that Jiménez and Duarte's record constituted purely *threats* without accompanying violence.  The threats were “incidental” in the sense that they did not culminate in actual harm.  The court noted:

1. The impugned threats (phone calls, brochure, gun‑point confrontation, assault on a factory employee, intimidation of a child) were unsustained; none resulted in physical injury or direct violence.  
2. The record provided **no evidence** of an actual assault that resulted in bodily harm or enforceable injury.  
3. The cumulative effects doctrine, invoked through *Wiransane* and *Ritonga*, was not satisfied because the individual events did not individually reach the “offensive threshold.”  
4. The psychological distress reported by Peter's wife and son could not be linked to violence; thus, it fell short of *persecution* as defined in the INA.  

Hence, the court concluded that the alleged past persecution failed to meet the statutory definition.  Under the substantial‑evidence standard, the BIA’s finding that the petitioner’s experiences did not constitute persecution was conclusive, and the appellate court upheld that determination.

By carefully applying the doctrinal framework, the court demonstrated that the line between lawful intimidation and “persecution” is drawn not merely by the presence of threats but by the presence of actual, offensive harm that is verified either by physical injury or a credible pattern of violence.  In the absence of such harm, the asylum claim cannot advance.
