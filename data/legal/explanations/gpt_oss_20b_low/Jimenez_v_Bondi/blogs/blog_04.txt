Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Safe Relocation in the Shadow of the Lore: How the 10th Circuit Leverages Internal Migration to Deny Asylum

## Why This Case Matters

The 10th Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* is more than a verdict for a single Colombian family; it is a crystallizing point in an ongoing, nationwide tightening of asylum standards. The court’s treatment of the “safe‑relocation” defense—arguably the most skeptical measure of a country’s internal refuge capacity—offers a window into how the federal courts are redefining the interplay between “protection” and “migration” when the homeland suffers localized violence. For applicants from states rife with asymmetrically spread cartel activity, gang violence, or regional human‑rights abuses, the ruling signals a shift toward an almost systematic dismissal of the internal migration argument, unless the record is strikingly conclusive on a *fundamental* change of circumstances. In a period where the epidemiology of violence in Colombia (and similar countries) is unevenly distributed, the 10th Circuit’s razor‑thin standard threatens to sideline a large swath of asylum seekers who could credibly escape to safer provinces.

---

## The Legal Landscape: Safe Relocation Under the INA

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) is clear that applicants may be denied asylum if either (a) a “fundamental change” in circumstances removes their well‑founded fear of persecution or (b) they can avoid persecution by relocating to a different part of the country. The appellate standard—“substantial evidence”—follows from *Singh v. Bondi* and *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*. By “substantial evidence” the courts mean that any reasonable adjudicator would find the facts so credible that he or she might adopt the same conclusion. This is an “agency‑friendly” standard that, in practice, demands a demonstrable shift in facts or the discovery of new evidence.

Critically, *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder* provided the two relevant tiers: **(1)** a substantial change in circumstances that would put the alternative lifestyle or relocation in question into a different category of danger; **(2)** proof that the individual could avoid future persecution by moving to another region. The 10th Circuit has historically been on the fence about the first tier, preferring to stick to the in‑depth factual verification that an applicant actually “could” relocate safely. *Rivera‑Barrientos* is merely procedural guidance; the courts insist on “preponderance of the evidence” that a relocation would be safe and **reasonable**.

---

## The Record in *Jimenez*: Family, Resources, and “Unattached” Threats

Petitioners in *Jimenez* claimed that they had a **network of family** in the countryside—“safety to go back to” if the violence in Bogotá escalated. The court, however, described the record as showing only a remote, relatively lightly documented “some relatives in unaffected Colombian regions.” The key facts:

1. **Presence of Relatives** – The family’s mother and father lived elsewhere in Colombia, and the petitioners themselves spent a short period in the countryside before deciding to flee. The court dismissed the idea that a mere “family presence” establishes safety.  
2. **Economic Resources** – The petitioners’ acknowledgment of “access to resources” was seen as nominal. A prison staff member’s “minimal” check and a modest deposit of money did not prove a practical safety net.  
3. **Threats’ Genuineness** – Threatening men cited Don Rafa for the “von‑Colombia” relationship. But the court found “the threat was tethered to the property dispute, not to political opposition.” This nuance, insignificant for the baselines, undermined the *“fundamental change”* assertion that the threats had spontaneously morphed into geographically specific dangers: *“the men threatened them to leave a particular neighborhood, not the country.”*

Thus, even with family and resources abroad, the court dismissed the safe‑relocation argument because it failed to cross the threshold of **“a fundamental change in circumstances”** or **“reasonable relocation”**—the two guardrails set by *Rivera‑Barrientos* and reinforced by subsequent case law.

---

## Distinguishing “Geographic Relocation” From “Fundamental Change”

The doctrine hinges on two conceptual moves:

1. **Geographic Migrate** – Any shift in the applicant’s place of living.  
2. **Fundamental Change** – A structural, sociopolitical shift that alters the *nature* of the threat; a difference that could not have existed in the previous context.

The 10th Circuit leans heavily toward the second. The court repeatedly signals that “migrating to another city or region *does not automatically* reduce risk.” The party must argue that *conditions of those regions differ substantially* from the circumstances that warranted protection in the first place—i.e., the threats are *no longer* individually tailored because the new location lacks the same power structure or looming danger.

In *Jimenez*, the court saw no evidence that the Colombian government had lost policing capacity or that the “Violent Rafa” network had drained its local influence. The record showed that the same forces that threatened the petitioners remained active elsewhere; the men’s motive persisted—advancing a personal vendetta over a property. Consequently, the court deemed “no (or only marginal) fundamental change” in the threat landscape.

---

## The Evolving Trend: A Shift Toward Stronger Safe‑Relocation Circumstances

Across the circuits, safe‑relocation analysis has tightened:

- **7th Circuit** (*Osorio‑Moras v. Garland*) emphasized the need for *systemic* rather than *individual* failure data. A single punitive or unhelpful officer does not suffice.  
- **3rd Circuit** (*Chhetri v. Rosen*) highlighted the requirement that an applicant’s basis for relocation must demonstrate the *actual possibility* of *safe* and *reasonable* refuge *outside the current environment*.  
- **9th Circuit** (*Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland*) further required evidence that *government intervention* would not have been futile or hazardous, a criterion that the 10th Circuit also invoked by questioning the extent of Colombian police backing.

The new pattern is that safe relocation is no longer an automatic bar once the applicant is considered to have a home region. Instead, the court now requires **substantial evidence** that the new region truly lacks the *specific* risks that led to the applicant's persecution. In practice, this means that applicants from countries with wide‑scale, uneven violence must provide a **robust, fact‑heavy narrative** documenting:

- Distinctive law‑enforcement gaps in their current region versus the alternate region.  
- A verifiable record of *actual* protection provided by local authorities or community networks.  
- Evidence that the new region lacks the “localized power structures” that enable persecution, such as organized crime or gang dominance.

These demands narrow the asylum window for many and tilt the judicial scale toward *“no safe place in the country”* conclusions—exactly what the 10th Circuit did in *Jimenez*.

---

## Impact on Applicants From Localized Violence Hotspots

Potential asylum seekers emerging from countries where violence is geographically concentrated will find it increasingly difficult to survive under the *internal migration* doctrine. The trend carries two clear implications:

1. **Internal Precaution** – Applicants will be pushed to meticulously map out safe zones, perhaps arriving at the migration stage already prepared with verifiable relocation plans.  
2. **Government-Centric Evidence** – Instead of relying solely on factual threats, applicants must dig into the background of local authorities: corruption indices, enforcement capacity, and even direct evidence of police involvement with criminal elements. A single “less helpful” officer or the existence of a family member in the police force will *not* outweigh a lack of verifiable systemic safety.

We expect this relentless refinement to render the asylum claim increasingly a matter of *proof‑provider* rather than *credible narrative*. In a climate where immigration officials prefer the objective math of “fewer risks” over the subjective “fear” of persecution, the internal migration rubric provides a convenient target for denial.

---

## Lessons for Counsel and Immigration Advocates

1. **Collect Detailed Local Intelligence** – Show cancer‑grade evidence that a localized threat cannot be mitigated by any relocation. In *Jimenez*, the presence of a “family connection” was routinely seen as a *hint* that there *could* be a threat beyond the immediate environment, but not *proof* that relocation would be safe.  
2. **Recruit Third‑Party Corroboration** – Witnesses, NGO reports, and independent facts that link the threat to a localized entity, separate from the individual’s political orientation, were crucial. The court gave weight to the “perpetrator’s motive was tied to property, not politics.”  
3. **Amplify Distinctness Between Current and Target Regions** – Bring statistical or qualitative evidence of differential violence indicators, security apparatus, or community resilience. If a threat article cites a *generalized violation* of human rights, the court will still check for *whether a region has the specific capacity* to enforce or protect.  
4. **Show the Government’s Response** – Even a single officer’s unfriendly comment is not enough. The record needs to reflect a *systemic* reluctance or inability. Acceptance of the 10th Circuit’s *“lack of notification”* test means demonstrating a *systemic* unwillingness or failure to provide protection even if a threat is reported.  

Above all, counsel should adopt a *state-of‑the‑art* compliance strategy: start with a pre‑migration site‑assessment, then “plot” a route that is at least *documented* to be risk‑free, and finally gather evidence that any threat specifically targets the individual’s *new* home.

---

## Conclusion: The 10th Circuit’s Safe‑Relocation Doctrine Is a Signal

The *Jimenez* decision signals that the safe‑relocation exception is no longer a blanket shield for indigents in disordered regions. Courts are insisting on a more sophisticated analysis that verifies a *fundamental change* in threat conditions. By requiring demonstrable differences in protection and risk profiles between the current and potential relocation sites, and by demanding a robust governmental willingness or inability to intervene, the 10th Circuit has tightened the relief filter.

For immigration attorneys, that means a sharper focus on building *district‑specific* and *government‑related* evidence. For asylum seekers from countries whose violence is geography‑heavy, the practical democratic reality is that **they will either pass the internal‑migration barrier successfully or risk their asylum claim failing**. The *Jimenez* ruling may not abolish the doctrine, but it has engineered a higher bar that reshapes the asylum landscape for anyone facing localized persecution.
