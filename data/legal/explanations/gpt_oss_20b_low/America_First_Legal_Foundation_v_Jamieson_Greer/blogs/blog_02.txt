Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# OSC’s Enforcement Limits: FOIA versus Personnel‑Practice Investigations  

## Why This Case Matters  

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* is a succinct reminder that the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) power to investigate alleged Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations is not a blanket mandate. The court held that OSC’s discretion in section 1216(c) – the remedial provision that lets OSC investigate "arbitrary or capricious withholding" of records – makes the foundation’s claim of procedural injury untenable. In a climate where agencies routinely deny FOIA requests on narrow privacy grounds or in the name of internal policy, the decision sharpens the lines between FOIA enforcement and the traditional civil‑service “prohibited personnel practice” track that OSC normally follows. The implications ripple for civil‑service employees, agency lawyers, and litigants who seek to hold agencies accountable when FOIA and personnel grievances overlap.

## The Legal Framework: FOIA, §1216, and the Civil‑Service Toolbox  

FOIA (5 U.S.C. §552) obliges federal agencies to disclose records “unless” one of its nine exclusions applies. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) adds a distinct procedural safeguard: when a court orders a record’s production, awards attorneys’ fees, and “specifically questions” whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to withholding, OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted. The statutory language, worked out by Congress, is therefore dual‑purpose: it tailors the OSC’s fire‑power to FOIA misconduct while tethering that power to particular court facts.

At the same time, 5 U.S.C. §§1215–1216 govern OSC’s broader civil‑service mandate. Section 1214 authorizes OSC to conduct investigations into "prohibited personnel practices" (like discrimination or retaliation) and to seek corrective or disciplinary action against offending employees. Section 1216’s sub‑paragraph (c) indicates that, just as for personnel practices, OSC may seek corrective action *in the same way* if a "prohibited personnel practice" is involved. The text does not, however, eliminate OSC’s discretionary handling of the procedural elements of a §1216 investigation. The D.C. Circuit’s brief opinion rests, essentially, on the idea that the statutory provision is silent on whether OSC must follow the stricter procedural checklist of §1214 when it is investigating a FOIA withholding under §1216.

## The Court’s Analysis of “Discretion” under §1216(c)

The foundation’s central argument was – roughly – that OSC has a “procedural right” to investigate whenever a FOIA withholding might be arbitrary or capricious. The court rejected that theory by stressing that a procedural right “in vacuum” does not confer Article III standing. The dish‑on-the‑plate reasoning unfolds in three steps:

1. **Procedural rights must protect a concrete, particularized interest**. The Supreme Court has repeatedly hammered out that an interest in not having to wait for a redaction or a denial is a *becoming* right, not a *standing* right.

2. **OSC’s §1216(c) grants, not imposes, procedural discretion**. When the court, in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, noted that OSC “decides whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in a §1216 investigation, it was underscoring that the section does not compel OSC to treat FOIA investigations as if they were personnel investigations. The law is ambiguous, and the court chose to read it conservatively: OSC’s role is to investigate, not to shepherd the complainant through the procedural baggage that shipped with §1214.

3. **Proving injury requires more than a factual tie**. Even if OSC had “undertaken” an investigation, the foundation would have to show that any resulting notices or disciplinary orders were *tied* causally to its own FOIA request and that an adverse resolution of its FOIA claim actually hinged on OSC’s investigative outcome. The court found that chain too speculative, citing *FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.* and *Hecate Energy* to remind us that a plaintiff’s injury must be “proximate” to the court’s decision. OSC operates among a tri‑agency ecosystem (DOJ, MSPB, FOIA relevant agencies) whose internal motions are out of the litigation’s view.

This doctrine is not a new contribution. The jury has heard similar arguments in *Food & Water Watch v. Vilsack* and *Citizen’s for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau*. Those decisions underscored that one must link the court’s judgment to a specific, enforceable right—otherwise the claim is boilerplate. *AFL v. Jamieson Greer* merely applies that well‑trod principle to a very specific statutory nexus.

## Mobility Between FOIA and Personnel Investigations

Because OSC’s §1216 investigations do not automatically carry the procedural machinery of §1214, the decision clarifies a technical but consequential feature of civil‑service enforcement: a FOIA‑oriented investigation is fundamentally a *civil‑service* investigation in that it seeks punitive or corrective action against personnel, but it does not oblige OSC to adopt §1214’s notice and evidentiary standards. The court did not question whether OSC can, in principle, impose disciplinary measures against a DOJ employee based on a FOIA violation. It simply said that the *procedural path* to that sanctions remains discretionary. In practice, that means agencies cannot rely on a procedural right to compel OSC to tell them precisely how to conduct their FOIA requests or how to draft their internal traffic‑control policies.

The decision also signals that “procedural injury” under FOIA does not automatically extend to a more expansive “information injury.” An agency’s failure to provide records is arguably an injury in its own right; *American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.* confirms that informational FOIA claims can satisfy the “concrete injury” requirement. However, the foundation’s claim hinged on OSC’s *failure* to tell them what it would do, not on the failure of DOJ to produce the records. That’s a different kind of injury, one that the court deemed too indirect to trigger standing.

## Practical Consequences for Agencies and Whistleblowers

For agencies, the ruling reaffirms that OSC’s “enforcement” power is bounded. A DOJ employee who improperly withholds records may still face disciplinary sanctions under §1215 or corrective actions under §1214, but only if OSC exercises its discretionary investigative authority. Agencies cannot compel OSC to adopt a strict procedural template, and conversely, employees seeking to hold agencies accountable for alleged FOIA violations must keep in mind that OSC’s response is not compulsory.

For whistleblowers and those dependent on FOIA transparency, the implications are more subtle. The decision does not preclude FOIA litigation when the underlying bar is a purely privacy‑or consent‑issue; it merely clarifies that OSC may not be an infallible bridge to compel agency disclosure. Whistleblowers who rely on OSC’s investigative engine must anticipate that it might well choose not to probe an agency’s policy unless it meets the narrow criteria under §552(a)(4)(F)(i). Therefore, FOIA plaintiffs may still need to mount a direct administrative appeal or a federal court case that pursues more direct statutory remedies (e.g., alternative remedy statutes, executive privilege claims, or, in exceptional cases, a §1981 discrimination claim).

One advantage—in a less than obvious way—is that the decision clarifies that the “information injury” route is less likely to be litigated on the basis of OSC’s discretionary powers. In other words, a party can plead to the statutory FOIA remedies without peppering the complaint with hypothetical speculation about whether OSC will execute a note‑less investigation of the agency’s hold policy. The process becomes cleaner: if the agency refuses to comply, the plaintiff can simply seek a court‑ordered disclosure, rather than waiting on OSC’s internal discretion.

## Codifying the Ambiguity (What Might Come Next)

The *AFL* decision sharpens the interpretation of §6216(c) but does not fully eliminate uncertainty. Future cases could flesh out whether OSC could be compelled to use §1214’s procedural safeguards when it chooses *not* to initiate a corrective‑action lawsuit under §1215. Alternatively, Congress could respond by tightening the statutory language: for instance, explicitly requiring OSC to adopt §1214’s notification regime in all §1216 investigations, or empowering OSC to issue notice *interim* to complainants who file a FOIA assertion of unlawful withholding. That legislative path would remove the “discretion” question altogether.

Another line of development concerns the *“arbitrary or capricious”* trigger. The *AFL* decision hinged on the fact that the court had not set that term in motion. If a FOIA claim is brought that *does* specifically demand an investigation into an agency’s withholding policy as “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC might be in a tighter spot. It remains to be seen how much discretion the agency reviewer will be forced to exercise. The “phrase” of the FOIA statute might be expanded or clarified in future court decisions or in legislative package amendments to the FOIA Act, perhaps under the FTC’s scheme for privacy‑focused “Arbitrary or Capricious” provisions within the federal privacy framework.

## Bottom Line

The *AFL v. Jamieson Greer* ruling is not a sweeping denial of OSC’s enforcement powers. It is a caution that OSC’s enforcement authority under FOIA is *discretionary*—you cannot rely on OSC to impose the same procedural certainty that applies to personnel‑practice investigations. That subtle but real limitation reminds agencies that they are ultimately responsible for procedural compliance. It also reminds litigants that the path to remedial action will be determined by OSC’s discretionary assessment of the FOIA allegations rather than by a statutory “must‑investigate” engine. 

In the broader constitutional landscape, the decision reinforces the wall of procedural posture: a league of “procedural rights” is insufficient to pierce an agency’s discretion or to carve a new analytic category of standing. For any FOIA litigant or civil‑service worker, the message is clear: the legal system will not automatically turn on OSC’s discretion; rather, it will respect statutory ambiguity and require concrete, plausible injury that can be directly remedied by the court’s order.
