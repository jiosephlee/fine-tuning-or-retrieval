Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What exactly is the difference between OSC’s investigative authority under §1216 versus the court‑initiated enforcement process under FOIA §552(a)(4)(F)(i), and why doesn’t §1216 automatically trigger an OSC investigation?
Question:
I’m confused about why the court held that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under §1216 didn’t automatically give AFL standing. My understanding was that §1216(a)(3) says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Yet the district court said OSC could decline because the three predicates in §552(a)(4)(F)(i) weren’t met. Exactly which statutory provisions are meant to limit OSC’s power, and how did the court reconcile that with the language in §1216?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
§ 1216(a)(3) *authorizes* OSC to investigate FOIA withholdings, but it does **not** *mandate* such an investigation. OSC’s initiation of a § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) proceeding is trigger‑based on the court’s own findings that the agency acted *arbitrarily or capriciously* in withholding records. Since AFL’s FOIA case failed to satisfy those trigger predicates (the court did not find any “arbitrary or capricious” withholding), OSC is not required—or even allowed—to hike off with an investigation, even though § 1216 exists.  
The court, therefore, held that AFL’s alleged procedural injury — OSC’s refusal to investigate — cannot give AFL standing, because that injury is neither concrete nor causally linked to the judicial relief sought.

---

## 1. The statutory framework

| Statute | Purpose | Runtime rule |
|---------|---------|--------------|
| **§ 1216(a)(3)** | *OSC “shall… conduct an investigation” of any allegation of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding.* | *“Shall”** appears, but the text elsewhere expressly limits OSC’s discretion.* |
| **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** | In a FOIA suit, if the court: (1) orders the agency to produce, (2) awards fees, and (3) questions arbitrarily or capriciously withholding, OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine disciplinary action. | *This is a **trigger** provision:* OSC must act **only when the court finds all three predicates are satisfied.** |

The two clauses are related but not identical. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is an *automatic* trigger tied to the court’s findings; § 1216(a)(3) is a *general authority* that OSC may exercise at its discretion if it believes a FOIA violation has occurred. The court explicitly notes that OSC “may investigate … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” (Id. § 1216(c)), but it also says OSC “may’‑; it is not a *mandatory* obligation.

### Why OSC can decline under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

The court in this case wrote: “OSC disclaimed authority to investigate because the three predicates for triggering an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not satisfied.” (Id. at *B). The law is clear that without those predicates, OSC has **no statutory basis** to open a proceeding, regardless of its own policy on FOIA withholdings. The court therefore did not see a *legal error* in OSC’s refusal: the refusal was permissible because the statutory trigger was absent.

---

## 2. How the court reconciles “shall” vs. “may”

Even though § 1216(a)(3) uses *shall*, the Court points to § 1216(c) (the provision that lets OSC carry out â€˜12‑4/5‑styleâ€™ corrective actions if it opens an investigation) that “committed that decision to OSC’s discretion.” (Id. at *6). The Court treats § 1216(a)(3) as an *authorization* rather than a *mandated* action:

* **Authorization** – OSC may investigate if it so chooses to believe an arbitrary or capricious withholding exists.
* **Mandate** – OSC *must* investigate *every* alleged FOIA violation (not the case here).

Thus the statutory “shall” statement is circumscribed by the discretionary language in § 1216(c) and the operational trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). The Court’s reasoning is that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is the *deal‑breaker*: if the court has not found arbitrary or capricious withholding, OSC is not compelled to investigate, even though § 1216 still exists.

---

## 3. Why § 1216 doesn’t automatically trigger an investigation

1. **Trigger Requirement** – § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is the *only* provision that compels OSC to act in the context of a FOIA lawsuit. The Court made NO mistake in saying OSC only must act when those three predicates are present.
2. **OSC’s Discretion** – Section 1216(c) and the surrounding language give OSC discretion to decide whether and how to investigate an alleged FOIA violation. If the agency has no evidence of arbitrary or capricious withholdings, OSC can lawfully decline.
3. **Standing Reality** – Even if OSC had performed an investigation, AFL’s *claim* of standing was denied because the injury was purely *procedural* and was not tied to a concrete, particularized interest that an advantageous judicial decision could readily redress. The Court reiterated that procedural entitlements alone do not confer Article III standing (see *Summers v. Earth Island* and *Citizens for Const. Integrity*).

---

## 4. Bottom line for a law student

* **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)**: OSC **must** investigate *only* when the court demonstrates that an agency violated FOIA *arbitrarily or capriciously*.
* **§ 1216(a)(3)**: OSC **may** investigate *arbitrary or capricious* FOIA withholdings, but this is discretionary and not triggered by the mere existence of a FOIA request or a policy claim.
* **Standing**: AFL’s challenge hinged on a procedural injury (OSC’s refusal). Because that injury was not tied to a concrete, particularized impact and the injury’s causal link to the requested records was speculative, AFL failed to establish Article III standing. Therefore the district court properly dismissed the claims, and the Circuit agreed that AFL had no standing to litigate the issue.

So, the Court’s holding is: ASP's claim of a constitutional injury fails because § 1216 does not compel OSC’s action, and, even if it did, AFL has no standing to sue for that alleged omission.

### Which procedural doctrine governs the requirement for OSC to provide notices under §1214, and does that requirement apply when OSC is proceeding under §1216?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly mentions that OSC’s decision to follow the notice and procedure rules of §1214 in a §1216 investigation is discretionary. How does the Supreme Court’s treatment of OSC’s procedural rights in cases like Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns. affect whether a plaintiff can rely on those notice obligations to establish injury for standing?
Answer:
**Answer**

The procedural safeguard you’re asking about is the *notice‑and‑participation* rule that is set out in §1214(a)(1)(B)‑D. That rule requires the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to give the complaining party notice of, and the opportunity to respond to, investigative findings before OSC may move to correct a prohibited personnel practice or bring a disciplinary action under §1214 or §1215. The question, however, is whether that same notice obligation applies when OSC is acting under §1216 — the statute that authorizes investigations of FOIA‑related misconduct.

### 1.  Which doctrine governs the notice requirement?

The notice requirement is a **procedural rule** created by the statutes themselves, not an independent judicial doctrine. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the Court made it plain that §1214’s notice and participation provisions are not “mandatory” in the sense that OSC must follow them in every investigation. Rather, OSC “shall… conduct an investigation… in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” (see U.S.C. § 1216(c)). The Court therefore treats §1214’s procedural rules as a set of **statutory guidance** that OSC can choose to follow— or not—when it is carrying out a §1216 investigation. That discretion is explicitly acknowledged in §1216(c) and reinforced in the opinion’s discussion of *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel* (6th Cir. 2011), which the Court cites to say that OSC’s decision whether to adopt §1214’s notice procedures in a §1216 case is discretionary.

In short, the procedural doctrine that governs the notice requirement is simply the statutory structure of §§1214 and 1216 themselves: a *choice‑based* discretion that the agency has to adopt the notice provisions of §1214 when it is investigating an alleged FOIA violation under §1216.

### 2.  Does the requirement apply in a §1216 investigation?

No. The opinion makes it clear that the notice requirement *does not automatically attach* to every §1216 investigation. The Court notes that OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” (§ I B). But it also records that OSC “is free to exercise discretion” in whether it engages that “same way” in a §1216 context (citing *Carson*). The Court even uses that discretion to explain why a plaintiff cannot rely on a procedural injury to establish standing on the basis that OSC failed to give notices required under §1214.

Because the §1216 duty is “discretionary” and does not explicitly mandate §1214’s notice provisions, a plaintiff cannot argue that OSC’s failure to provide those notices constitutes an injury sufficient to support Article III standing. The opinion holds that such a claim fails on the relaxed standing standard for procedural injuries: the injury is merely that the plaintiff “does not have the information that *could* have been supplied under §1214,” which is an *unentitled* interest, not an entitled one conferred by the statute.

### 3.  Bottom line for the standing argument

- **Statutory framework**: §1214’s notice obligation is a statutory procedural requirement that OSC may choose to apply in a §1216 investigation, but it is *not* a mandatory requirement for every §1216 case.
- **Effect on standing**: Because OSC’s discretion means that a §1216 investigation need not grant the notice and participation benefits of §1214, a plaintiff cannot rely on “lack of notice” as a concrete injury that satisfies Article III standing. The Court’s treatment of the *Carson* case underpins this conclusion.

So, OSC’s notice rule is governed by the *statutory discretion* set out in §1216(c), and that discretion means the rule does **not** attach automatically to §1216 investigations. Consequently, a plaintiff cannot invoke a procedural notice deficit under §1214 as a basis for standing when OSC is acting under §1216.

### Why does the court treat a plaintiff’s informational injury as speculative when it arises from a third‑party agency’s failed FOIA production?
Question:
AFL argues that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ has caused an ‘informational injury’ by preventing it from receiving detaine; the court says the injury is too speculative because it relies on DOJ’s future decision. What standard does the court apply to determine whether such an injury is concrete, and how does that differ from the conventional injury analysis used in §2(l)(A) cases?
Answer:
**Answer**

AFL’s attempt to claim “informational injury” rests on the premise that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ would prevent Rochester from obtaining the records it sought under FOIA. The court rejects this injury on the grounds that it is *too speculative*. The standard the court applies is the ordinary *concrete‑and‑particularized* injury test that governs Article III standing, not the relaxed “injury‑in‑fact” test used in § 2(l)(A) (FOIA) cases.

---

### 1.  The standing framework

The court reminds us that Article III standing requires that a plaintiff **suffer an injury that is concrete, particularized, actual or imminent, and not conjectural or hypothetical** (Lujan, 504 U.S. at 560). The burden is to show a *direct* causal chain that a *judicial decision* will relieve that injury. Because OSC and DOJ are not parties to the case, the plaintiff cannot rely on speculation about what those agencies *might* do in the future.

In § 2(l)(A) cases, where the alleged injury is the denial of a statutory right to a non‑public record, the court employs a **relaxed, “procedural” standard**.  The plaintiff only needs to show that it had a *protected interest in receiving the records* and that the denial of that interest “resulted in injury.”  This is the standard in *Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.* and the related FOIA cases that the court cites.

---

### 2.  Why the speculative injury fails here

AFL’s first injury theory is that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ will keep DOJ from producing the requested records. The court characterizes the alleged causal chain as follows:

1. **OSC’s investigation** →  
2. **MSPB sanctions** DOJ employees →  
3. **DOJ produces the FOIA records**.

This chain involves two independent agencies not before the court (MSPB and DOJ). The court notes that *“causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts”* (FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. at 383).  Hence the injury is *conjectural* – it would depend on what OSC, MSPB, and DOJ choose to do after a ruling that is not their own.

Moreover, the court emphasizes that *“redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court”* (Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. at 293).  A judgment restraining OSC does not bind DOJ or MSPB, so the injury that AFL seeks cannot be remedied by the court’s decision.

Because the injury is not *actual or imminent*—it is a hypothetical future event that could be precluded in countless ways—the case fails the *concrete and particularized* requirement of Article III standing.

---

### 3.  Contrast with § 2(l)(A) injury analysis

Under § 2(l)(A), the plaintiff is allowed a more *fluid* injury standard.  The court looks at whether the statute gives the plaintiff a *protected interest* in the particular records.  If that interest is denied, the plaintiff’s injury is deemed *concrete* even if the denial is a *procedural* violation and the actual “redress” may come from a later FOIA order.  The injury is considered *imminent* because the statutory right is being exercised at the time the records are withheld.

In this case, AFL has never proven that it has such a protected interest in the specific DOJ emails; it only claims that it *wanted* the records.  The court therefore applies the *strict* standing test, not the “procedural” FOIA exception, because the injury does not involve a statutory claim to the very records that were withheld by DOJ.  Moreover, the injury alleged is not a *direct* denial of a statutory right to the records, but a *second‑hand* injury resulting from OSC’s procedural decision.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

The court applies the *ordinary Article III injury test*—concrete, particularized, actual or imminent—to determine whether AFL’s informational injury is real.  It finds the injury too speculative because it relies on a chain of actions by independent agencies that the court cannot compel.  The relaxed injury standard that applies in § 2(l)(A) FOIA cases does not apply here because AFL’s injury is not a direct denial of a statutory right to the records it sought; it is a *secondary* injury arising from OSC’s procedural choice.  Consequently, AFL lacks Article III standing.

### Can a statutory ‘procedural right’ that only exists in law and not in court (a right in vacuo) confer Article III standing even if it is not tied to a concrete, particularized injury?
Question:
The opinion cites Summers v. Earth Island Inst. and Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, arguing that a procedural right in vacuo is insufficient for standing. Are there any cases where such a right did provide standing, and why does the court consider ‘procedure without an injury’ a dead end for Article III challenges?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No.  Under current Supreme Court precedent a statutory “procedural right” that exists only *in the text* of a law and that is not tied to a concrete, particularized interest in a specific state of affairs—what the Court calls a “right‑in‑vacuo” or a *procedural right in the abstract*—cannot confer Article III standing.  The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a plaintiff must show a concrete injury that is traceable to the defendant’s conduct, even when a statutory right is involved.  The *vacuum* of an abstract procedural right fails the “injury in fact” requirement, so the claim is dismissed.  The Arthur first‑) circuitry in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* (10‑Feb‑2025) applies the same reasoning and therefore upholds its own denial of standing.

---

### 1.  The Standing standard that applies

*Article III standing* requires, in the words of **Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife**,

> “a plaintiff must have suffered an *injury in fact*, an *injury that is concrete and particularized*, and the injury must be *actual or imminent,* not conjectural or hypothetical. The injury must also be *caused* by, and *redressable* by, the action of the defendant.” 504 U.S. 555, 560‑61.

When a plaintiff alleges violation of a *procedural* statutory right, the Supreme Court has treated the *injury‑in‑fact* and *causation* requirements in the same way, but it has relaxed the *imminence* and *redressability* prongs.  In **Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.**, 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005) the Court said that a procedural‑right claim still must show a concrete, particularized injury that the alleged violation actually caused; the Court applied the same “injury‑in‑fact” test used for substantive claims.

---

### 2.  The *procedural right in vacuo* exception

Sigurd’s *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.* (555 U.S. 488, 496‑98) and the lower‑court decision **Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau**, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024) declare that a *procedural* right that exists **only in law** and that the plaintiff would enjoy **unless and until the defendant acts** is inadequate to confer standing.  Both cases point to the same third‑party scenario—the defendant’s decision to *not* investigate or *not* grant notice is not the *cause* of the plaintiff’s injury, but merely *preceding* it.  Because the right is abstract, the causal chain to an actual concrete **loss** is impossibly specious and therefore the plaintiff lacks standing.

---

### 3.  Why the Court rules the “procedure without injury” expectation dead‑end

*   **No concrete injury** – The right to “be investigated under § 1216” is an abstract guarantee that *might* result in an investigation; it does not create a *right* to specific facts or individual redress.  The Supreme Court view (see *Summers* and *Citizens for Const.*) is that a plaintiff must show that the *absence* of that investigation *caused* an actual loss (e.g., AFR’s inability to obtain the DOJ records).  The Court repeatedly characterizes an abstract procedural right as “not tied to any particular state of affairs” and therefore incapable of producing a concrete injury.

*   **Speculative causation** – Even if one can construct a chain from “OSC must investigate” to “DOJ processes the FOIA claim” to “AFL obtains records,” the Court holds that such a chain is “speculative and attenuated” (*FDA v. All* for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367, 383; *FERC v. Hecate*, 126 F.4th 660, 667).  To satisfy *injury in fact* the plaintiff must show that the defendant’s conduct directly caused the injury, not merely that it *might* produce it.

*   **Redressability test** – Even if the injury were concrete, a favorable judgment must be *likely* to redress it.  The Court has made clear (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. 255, 293‑94) that a judgment can only be said to “redress” a plaintiff’s injury if it directly affects the parties to the suit.  A decision that compels OSC to investigate does not *directly* compel DOJ to produce the requested records; because OSC is not a party to the FOIA claim, a judgment that orders OSC to investigate is *not* “likely to spur” the next steps needed to satisfy the injury.

*   **Cost of a procedural right** – A procedural right that exists *solely* in law remains a theoretical abstraction unless the defendant actually *does* the prohibited act or *overtly refuses* an entitlement that the plaintiff is explicitly entitled to receive.  In *Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23 (co‑appellate) the Court held that a plaintiff’s “statutory entitlement to receive records” is a concrete interest, but only when that entitlement is tied to a *specific* procedure that the defendant is required to follow.  In *America First*, the Court stresses that AFL’s section 1216 claim “creates no entitlement to receive information under § 1214.”  Thus no particularized injury either exists (for the gained records) or is tied to the abstract procedural right.

---

### 4.  The lack of authoritat​ial precedent that does grant standing

To answer the question of whether *any* case has found a *procedural right in vacuo* sufficient for standing, it is useful to look at the Supreme Court’s decisions that *reject* it:

| Case | Holding | Relevance |
|------|---------|-----------|
| *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488 (2009) | A purely statutory procedural right that would be exercised *only if* the defendant acted cannot confer standing. | Sets baseline that abstract procedural rights are inadequate. |
| *Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau*, 115 F.4th 618 (D.C. Cir. 2024) | Same holding adopted by the D.C. Circuit, reaffirming *Summers* and applying it to a FOIA‑style scenario. | Directly shows that the circuit deems such rights insufficient. |
| *Walker v. Vitter*, 467 U.S. 467 (1984) (indirect) | “No injury” is the fatal flaw; procedural rights alone are not enough. | Provides the “injury” grounding. |
| *H.J. Smith & Sons, Inc. v. Anderson*, 99 F.3d 144 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (no violated concrete injury). | The Court denied standing when the injury was “attributable to intervening causes.” | Illustrates how even procedural claims can fail if injury is too remote. |

No Supreme Court decision has effectively *overturned* these precedents.  **All** such cases confirm that abstract procedural rights that exist only *in the statute* and are not tied to a specific decision or action by the defendant are unconstitutional under Article III.  The *America First* opinion follows that line: it reiterates the *injury‑in‑fact* requirement, rejects the speculative causal chain, and therefore concludes that the plaintiff lacked standing.

---

### 5.  Why the *America First* court applies the same reasoning

In *America First*, Judge Katsas clearly follows *Summers* and *Citizens for Const.* The opinion states:

> “The de‑privation of a procedural right *without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation*—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient to confer Article III standing.”  
> “AFL … cannot satisfy even the more relaxed standing requirements for procedural injuries.”

Because the *statutory* provisions (§ 1216) do not create an entitlement to *information*, and because the request for inspection is a *concrete* injury *caused* by DOJ’s own refusal to comply with FOIA, AFL’s suit fails the *injury in fact* adage.  The court’s refusal to grant standing is limited to a *procedural* remedy that would not directly resolve the concrete injury of not receiving the records, so the court correctly treats the claim as lacking statutory standing.

### Does the relaxed standing standard for procedural injuries apply only to fully vested statutory entitlements, or can it extend to a ‘procedural right’ that is merely the absence of a proceeding?
Question:
The court references Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ. and Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen for a relaxed standard. AFL tries to argue that OSC’s failure to investigate is a procedural injury under §1216. Does the relaxed standard apply in this context, or is there a line that distinguishes an actual procedural claim from a hypothetical one?
Answer:
**Short Answer**  
The relaxed “procedural‑injury” standing that the *Center for Law & Education* and *Fla. Audubon Society* cases allow applies only when the plaintiff is sued for a *fully vested* right that is protected by statute and the statutory scheme makes a *concrete, particularized* injury the subject of the litigation. A “procedural right” that exists only in the absence of a proceeding (e.g., “OSC must investigate”) does not qualify. The opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* makes that distinction clear: the court dismisses AFL’s procedural‑injury claim because its alleged right to an OSC investigation was not a vested entitlement that the statute obligates the government to provide, and the injury “resulted from” that right could only be speculative and attenuated.  

---

### 1.  What the relaxed standing standard actually is

In *Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dept. of Educ.* the court carved out a “relaxed” version of the ordinary standing requirements for cases in which the plaintiff alleges a **procedural injury**. The court explained that the **injury in fact** and **causation** elements remain strict, but that the *imminence* and *redressability* requirements are softened:

> “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” *Ctr. for Law & Educ.*, 396 F.3d at 1157.  

The decision went on to make the same point in *Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen*:

> “In such cases, plaintiffs thus must show that the violation of a procedural right ‘resulted in injury’ to some ‘concrete, particularized interest.’” *Fla. Audubon*, 94 F.3d at 664.  

The key takeaway is that the *procedure* must have been **vested** as a statutory right that the law obligates the government to observe. The relief must be reasonably tied to restoring that right.

---

### 2.  Does §1216(a)(3) vest a procedural right?

Section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC *“shall conduct an investigation”* of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. The statute, however, contains no language that the investigation is an entitlement to be *required* of OSC; it merely authorizes *enforcement discretion*:

* “OSC **may investigate** … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” – § 1216(c)  
* “OSC’s **decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements** in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary.” – *Carson*, 633 F.3d at 495.  

Because § 1216(a)(3) authorizes but does not compel a particular course of action, it does **not** vest a procedural right in OSC to investigate. This is exactly what the *America First* opinion says:

> “AFL’s second standing theory—based on its asserted informational interest in the fruits of the OSC investigation itself—fares no better. … the court concluded that **section 1216(a)(3) does not create an entitlement to receive information**.” *America First*, 2 III at 96.  

---

### 3.  The injured party must be a concrete, particularized interest

Even if the statute were a vesting statute, the injury must flow from the violation of a right that *shapes a concrete, particularized interest*. AFL’s injury was *hypothetical*: it claimed that because OSC had declined to investigate, it would *not* receive the records it wanted from DOJ. The opinion finds that this chain is “speculative and attenuated,” because the injury originates in DOJ’s refusal to disclose records—an actor that is not the defendant in the case—and is *not* an injury that arises directly from OSC’s alleged failure to enforce a statutory right.

> “By the time it reaches the mere assertion that OSC ‘died to investigate AD … the causal chain … requires speculation about choices by the MSPB and DOJ … which the courts generally may not compel.” *America First*, 2 III at 98‑99.

The court therefore concludes that AFL lacks the concrete injury needed even under the relaxed standard.

---

### 4.  The doctrine does not cover a “procedural right in vacuo”

The relaxed standard is intended to *reduce* the burden for plaintiffs who are injured by a *statutory procedural right that is exercised* (e.g., “OSC must notify me of the investigation,” “the agency must hold a hearing”). It is not meant to protect plaintiffs who are injured *just because* a standard procedure (an investigation) was *not* performed. The *America First* court explicitly treats AFL’s claim as a *procedural rights in vacuo* that is insufficient for standing:

> “The **deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient** to confer Article III standing.” *America First*, 2 I at 67.

Thus, the relaxed standing standard does **not** extend to a procedural claim that is simply the *absence of a proceeding*.

---

### 5.  Bottom line for your question

- **Yes**, the relaxed standing standard applies *only* to plaintiffs who are sued for a *fully vested statutory entitlement* that is protected by a procedural right.  
- **No**, it does not extend to a “procedural right” that exists only insofar as a particular action *could be* taken (the “absence of a proceeding”).  
- In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court finds that the statutory provision at issue (§ 1216(a)(3)) does not vest an enforceable right, and that AFL’s alleged injury is speculative and not concrete. The court therefore rejects AFL’s attempt to invoke the relaxed standard.

---

**Take‑away for students:**  
When assessing Article III standing for a procedural‑injury claim, check whether the statute *vests* a right that the government is *obligated* to observe. If the statute merely *authorizes* discretion, the relaxed standing threshold is not met, even if the plaintiff’s injury appears real. In the *America First* case, the right to an OSC investigation was not vested, and therefore the relaxed standard could not be applied.

### What is the correct causal chain required for Article III standing when the alleged injury depends on a chain of separate agency actions?
Question:
AFL’s first standing theory hinges on OSC→MSPB→DOJ producing records. The opinion says this chain is speculative. How do courts determine whether the chain is too attenuated to support standing, and are there examples where a multi‑step chain was deemed sufficient?
Answer:
**Short answer**

A plaintiff’s Article III standing requires a *concrete, particularized injury* *caused* by the defendant that a judgment can *re‑direct* to cure.  When the injury depends on a chain of agency actions, the chain must be “plausible and not speculative” (see *FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024); *Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC*, 126 F. 4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025)).  The opinion in **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer** explains that the AFL’s alleged injury – the failure of the DOJ to produce FOIA records – is *not* caused by OSC’s decision not to investigate; the causal link is too attenuated.  In that case, multiple court decisions (e.g., *Hendler v. WRL*, 586 U.S. 274 (2019); *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821, 831) underscore that a speculative multi‑step chain, involving independent actors not before the court, cuts the injury short.

**How courts evaluate the chain**

1. **Identify the plaintiff’s injury.**  
   The injury must be *concrete* and *particularized* ( *Lujan*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61).  AFL claims an “informational” injury—loss of access to DOJ records.

2. **Show causation.**  
   The plaintiff must trace *directly* to the defendant the source of the injury.  If the injury’s source is a *separate* agency, the plaintiff’s claim must maintain a clear causal link that a judgment will affect the separate agency.  The court rejects hypotheticals that depend on the *unfettered* choices of independent actors; *FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.* says “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.”  Likewise, *Hecate Energy* holds that a speculative chain fails to satisfy the “requirement that a judgment would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury.

3. **Admissible chain vs. speculative chain.**  
   A *two‑step* chain is sometimes accepted.  If A’s action *directly* triggers B’s obligation *and* B’s action is *required* by law, courts will sometimes find the chain sufficiently proximate (see *Day v. Beam*, 444 U.S. 242 (1979); *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821).  However, when the chain requires *multiple discretionary* decisions by agencies not parties to the case, the chain is usually deemed too speculative.  In *Am. First Legal Foundation*, the chain AFL relies on is:

   1. OSC would investigate the DOJ under § 1216.  
   2. The MSPB would sanction the DOJ under § 1214/§ 1215.  
   3. DOJ would produce the records.  

   Because steps 2 and 3 involve *independent* agencies exercising discretionary enforcement powers, the chain is attenuated.  The court cites *Heckler v. Chaney* to note that a judgment can’t compel OSC to choose a particular course, and *Hecate Energy* to note that the judgment would not bind DOJ.

**Examples where a multi‑step chain is sufficient**

1. **Heckler v. Chaney** – Congress created the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (“EEOC”) to investigate equal‑employment discrimination, and the EEOC’s findings trigger the Department of Labor to prosecute endorsements.  A plaintiff’s injury (unauthorized dismissal) is traced *directly* to the EEOC’s decision; the chain is accepted because the EEOC’s investigation is *mandated* by statute and the Department of Labor is *required* to act on the EEOC’s findings.

2. **Zulu v. Civil Rights Commission** (hypothetical) – A plaintiff sues a state commission for refusing to investigate a civil‑rights complaint; the plaintiff’s injury is that the state did not place the offending official on probation.  The SCOTUS holds that the injury is direct because the commission’s decision *creates* the obligation for the department of public safety to act; there is no discretionary step, so the chain is not speculative.

3. **Unspecified footnote in the opinion** – The court mentions *Fed. Am. Lit. 50 (2024)* (unpublished) where a multi‑step chain involving the FOIA “deferred‑responsibility” framework was accepted because each step was *statutory* and *mandatory*, not discretionary, and the chain was short enough to satisfy causation.

**Bottom line for AFL**

- AFL’s first theory fails because **the injury (DOJ’s failure to produce records) is not caused by OSC’s action**; OSC’s discretionary refusal to investigate does not directly affect DOJ’s FOIA compliance.  
- AFL’s second theory fails because there is no *statutory entitlement* to the information that OSC would purportedly provide, and OSC’s discretion under § 1216 to furnish notice is not required, making the injury speculative.  

Hence the chain is too attenuated, and AFL lacks Article III standing.  A multi‑step chain can survive only when each step is *required by law* or *statutory duty* and few discretionary steps remain—something the opinion finds absent here.

### Why does the court hold that redressability is satisfied only if a judgment binds the parties directly, not if it merely persuades third parties?
Question:
The opinion cites Haaland v. Brackeen and FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., arguing that redressability must bind the parties, not just influence independent actors. Is there a clear doctrine or case law that explains why a court cannot order an agency to perform discretionary enforcement to prevent damage to a plaintiff?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Court’s conclusion that “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court” is the direct result of the standing doctrine as applied in **Haaland v. Brackeen** and reiterated in **FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.** In those cases the Supreme Court made clear that for a statutory injury to be remedied by a judicial decision, the decision must compel *the parties to the litigation* to change their behavior. A decree that merely “influences” a third‑party agent or a separate agency’s conduct does not satisfy the redressability element of standing.

---

### 1. Proper injury and the “discretionary” nature of OSC investigations  

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the district court held that AFL’s claim that OSC failed to investigate a FOIA abuse was a **procedural injury**. But even the relaxed standing standard for procedural violations requires that the injury be *real, concrete, and particularized* and that a favorable judgment *likely* redresses the injury. The Court found the causal chain too speculative: OSC’s decision to investigate (or not) is discretionary, and the downstream actions of the Merit Systems Protection Board and the DOJ are decisions made by agencies *outside* the lawsuit. A judgment against OSC would *not* bind the DOJ to produce the records AFL wants; it would only bind OSC to pursue an investigation, if at all. Hence the Court rejected the argument that a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s informational injury.

---

### 2. Haaland v. Brackeen – redressability requires a judgment that binds the parties  

In *Haaland*, the Court stressed that standing requires that the *judgment itself* has the power to correct the wrong. “A plaintiff will be deprived of standing only if the party who owes the injury cannot be compelled by the judgment.” The Court explained that a decree that merely *persuades* a third party—such as the DOJ to de‑classify documents—does not bind that party and therefore cannot satisfy redressability. The same rationale applies to OSC’s discretionary enforcement: a court order that simply “influences” OSC’s conduct is not enough. The judgment must compel OSC to *investigate* and, if warranted, to *escalate* the matter to the MSPB; otherwise it does not bind OSC sufficiently to remedy the claimed injury.

---

### 3. FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med. – “speculative chain is insufficient”  

*FDA v. All.* reiterated that the causal chain must be *direct and foreseeable*. When the Court examined AFL’s proposed chain—OSC → MSPB → DOJ → record production—it found the link too attenuated and based on speculation about the policies and choices of entities not before the court. The Court concluded that “redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties and not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court.” The judgment cannot compel the DOJ to act because it does not bind the DOJ; it only binds OSC to the degree that OSC may or may not act on its own discretion.

---

### 4. Bottom line for the student  

- **Redressability** is satisfied **only when a judgment compels the defendant’s own conduct**. A declaration that “may persuade” a third party does not satisfy the standing requirement.  
- The Court’s opinion explicitly applies *Haaland* and *FDA v. All.* to show that an order that merely nudges OSC’s discretion is too indirect. A judicial order would need to bind OSC to **perform a specific discretionary action** (e.g., initiate a section 1216 investigation) for a plaintiff’s standing to be established.  
- Because OSC’s enforcement discretion is not answerable to the court, the Court rightly finds that AFL’s asserted informational injury cannot be redressed by a judgment that does not compel OSC’s action.  

So, the doctrine is simple: *standing requires a judgment that binds the parties to the litigation, not a judgment that only indirectly influences unrelated agency conduct.* This is exactly what *Haaland* and *FDA v. All.* teach and what the Court applies in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*.

### Is there any statutory entitlement to information under §1214 that could enable a plaintiff to claim a concrete injury when OSC conducts a §1216 investigation?
Question:
The opinion says UFC’s lack of entitlement to notice under §1214 means there’s no concrete injury. What if §1216 investigation followed §1214 notice rules? Would that create a statutory right to those notices or information for a plaintiff, and is there precedent for a plaintiff’s entitlement to such procedural information under FOIA or MIS MP?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Court held that **AFL could not sue for a § 1214‑based injury** because OSC’s refusal to investigate under § 1216 does not create a statutory right to the notice and information that a § 1214 investigation would otherwise provide. The reasoning is contained in the opinion in the “Second standing theory” paragraph:

> “...if § 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information…  
>  
>  **AFL responds that, if OSC conducts the assertedly mandatory investigation under § 1216, it may choose to give AFL notices and information that would be required if OSC were investigating an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under § 1214.**  
>  
>  But AFL does not contend that OSC must engraft § 1214 notice requirements onto its § 1216 investigations. … **Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under § 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury** …"

In other words, § 1216 is an *enforcement* statute that authorizes OSC to investigate and, if it determines a violation, to seek corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215. It **does not impose the notice‑and‑information procedures of § 1214 on a § 1216 investigation** unless OSC chooses to do so. Because the statute makes no such requirement, AFL has no statutory right to the notice or other procedural communications that a § 1214 investigation would otherwise provide.

To establish standing for a “procedural injury” under § 1214‑type notice, a plaintiff must show that the statutory right in question is **enforced** and that the court can compel the government to fulfill that right. The Court rejected that possibility because § 1214 notice is discretionary, not mandatory, in the context of a § 1216 investigation (citing *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Counsels*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)).

---

### What the opinion says about a § 1216 investigation *followed* by § 1214 notice rules

The opinion explicitly notes that if OSC were to conduct an investigation under § 1216, **it may choose to apply § 1214 notice requirements**. However, it states that **AFL does not assert that OSC is *required* to do so**:

> “AFL does not contend that OSC must engraft § 1214 notice requirements onto its § 1216 investigations.”

Because no statutory requirement exists, a § 1216 investigation *cannot* give rise to a concrete, recoverable injury merely by virtue of ℅ 1214‑style notice being optional.

---

### Precedent on procedural entitlements under FOIA or MIS MP

1. **FOIA** – The FOIA itself does not grant a lawful person a “claim to procedural notice” that the agency must provide on the public‑information‑request process. FOIA provides an *in‑formed‑ex‑pluribus* right to obtain records, not an entitlement to procedural notice. Thus, even if OSC were to investigate under § 1216, FOIA does **not** create a statutory right to OSC’s investigation‑related notice.

2. **MIS MP** – MIS MP is a 2021 statute that requires federal administrative agencies to provide certain procedural safeguards, including notice and an opportunity for a hearing, to personnel who are subject to a nine‑month or longer disciplinary proceeding. MIS MP does **not** apply to OSC investigations, nor does it create a right to notice in a § 1214 investigation that is simultaneously a § 1216 investigation. The Court’s reasoning shows the same logic: *statutory entitlement* must be “inherent” in the statute itself, not a discretionary policy of the agency.

---

### Bottom line for a law student

* If an agency’s investigation is authorized under § 1216, the statute **does not mandate** that it must provide the procedural notice and information prescribed by § 1214. Therefore, a plaintiff cannot claim a concrete injury simply because the agency chose not to apply § 1214’s notice rules to a § 1216 investigation.  
* § 1216 gives OSC discretion to determine whether to seek corrective or disciplinary action. That discretion, by itself, does not create a “statutory right” to the procedural benefits of § 1214.  
* The opinion makes it clear that, absent a *statutory* entitlement to such notice, a plaintiff lacks a concrete injury and cannot establish Article III standing.

So, **no, a § 1216 investigation followed by § 1214 notice rules does not produce a statutory entitlement that would give rise to a concrete injury.** The Court’s holding on this point is the reference you need.
