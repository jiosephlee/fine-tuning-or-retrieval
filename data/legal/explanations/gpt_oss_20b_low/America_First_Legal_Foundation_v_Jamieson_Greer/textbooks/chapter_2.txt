Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 2: # II. Governing Law: OSC’s Authority and FOIA Enforcement

## Civil Service Reform Act §§ 1214–1215 & 1216

Section 1214 of the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) governs the investigation of prohibited personnel practices and the corrective actions that may result. It sets out the procedural steps the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) must follow, including the obligation to notify the complaining party of investigative progress (§ 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D)). Section 1215 authorizes OSC to pursue disciplinary action against individual employees who on a “prohibited personnel practice” are found liable. Together, these provisions constitute OSC’s core investigative and enforcement toolkit with respect to personnel conduct within the federal service.

Section 1216 expands OSC’s jurisdiction beyond traditional personnel matters. Subsection (a)(3) specifically authorizes OSC to investigate “any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, FOIA‑related withholdings. Paragraph (c) of that subsection clarifies that OSC may pursue the same corrective actions outlined in § 1214 or the same disciplinary measures in § 1215 in the event of a FOIA‑related finding. Thus, § 1216(a)(3) gives OSC an investigative mandate that parallels its personnel enforcement powers but is tailored to FOIA violations.

## OSC’s Discretionary vs. Mandatory Investigative Duties

The statutory text distinguishes between duties that are obligatory and those that are encompassed in OSC’s discretion. When OSC is compelled under § 1216(a)(3) to investigate a claimed FOIA withholding, the statute does not itself prescribe how the investigation must be conducted beyond the general statutory framework. OSC retains discretion under § 1216(c) to determine whether to initiate the investigation at all, and if it does, whether to proceed under the procedural regimes of § 1214 or to rely on its own “internal procedures.” The district court, in *America First Legal v. Kerner*, held that OSC’s failure to investigate was a legal error because OSC had failed to satisfy the statutory triggers for a § 1216 investigation, yet it still left the ultimate decision to investigate to OSC’s discretion. That decision was later reiterated in the present case when OSC declined to investigate, leading the plaintiffs to argue that OSC had a statutory duty.

## FOIA §552(a)(4)(F)(i) and its Triggers

Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) of FOIA explicitly creates an enforcement mechanism that applies only after a court has already taken action. If a court orders the production of records that were improperly withheld, awards attorneys’ fees, and queries whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously, then OSC must “promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the individual chiefly responsible. This clause establishes a post‑judgment triggering mechanism that is strictly procedural within the context of FOIA enforcement. It does not, by itself, confer OSC with a pre‑judgment obligation to investigate every alleged FOIA violation.

## Contrast between §1216(a)(3) and §552(a)(4)(F)(i)

While both provisions address FOIA violations, they differ in scope and trigger. § 1216(a)(3) is a proactive investigative authority granted to OSC before any court involvement; it allows OSC to investigate arbitrary or capricious withholdings detected by the agency itself or by external parties (e.g., the plaintiffs). In contrast, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is reactive, activating only after a court has already adjudicated a FOIA dispute and found that a record was withheld arbitrarily or capriciously. The statutory architecture thus intends (1) for OSC to conduct its own investigations under § 1216, and (2) for OSC to follow up on court judgments under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

In *America First Legal v. Kerner*, the court concluded that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s pre‑judgment investigative power under § 1216. Consequently, OSC’s decision not to investigate was deemed a legal error, but the court ultimately rejected an order compelling OSC because § 1216(c) retains the discretion to conduct or forego investigation. The present case affirms that distinction while emphasizing that the statutory trigger of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is not required for OSC to act under § 1216.

## Prior Leading Decisions

- **Frazier v. MSPB (1982)**: Established that the OSC is an investigative and prosecutorial agency dedicated to maintaining a fair, efficient, and lawfully conducted civil service. It reinforced the statutory framework of § 1214 and § 1215 in protecting personnel rights.

- **America First Legal v. Kerner (2023)**: Clarified that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cap or exclude OSC’s investigatory authority under § 1216(a)(3). The district court found OSC’s refusal to investigate a procedural error to be a legal misstep, yet recognized that OSC’s discretion under § 1216(c) determines whether an investigation commences. This case sets the factual context for the current appeal, where AFL sought OSC’s investigation of DOJ’s FOIA policy.

These precedents, together with the statutory provisions, frame the legal landscape that the appellate court must navigate when assessing whether OSC's discretionary decision to forego investigation aligns with the enforceable obligations imposed by the CSRA and FOIA.
