# Federal Criminal Securities Fraud: The Statutory Landscape

This chapter examines 18 U.S.C. § 1348—the principal federal criminal securities‑fraud statute enacted in the wake of the corporate scandals of the early 2000s—and situates it in the larger ecosystem of federal fraud law and securities regulation. The chapter explains the statute’s text and elements, its relationship to the older mail and wire fraud statutes, its overlap and contrasts with Exchange Act § 10(b) and Rule 10b‑5 (the principal civil anti‑fraud regime), and its interaction with the Exchange Act’s market‑manipulation rules. Drawing on recent appellate guidance (including the Fifth Circuit’s decision reversing dismissal of a “pump‑and‑dump” indictment), the chapter develops the doctrinal tests courts use to identify a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud,” and discusses the practical consequences that follow at charging, trial, and sentencing stages.

## Origins of § 1348 in Sarbanes–Oxley and congressional purpose

Congress enacted 18 U.S.C. § 1348 as part of the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002. Sarbanes–Oxley responded to high‑profile corporate collapses (Enron, WorldCom, and others) and to perceived gaps in prosecutors’ tools for addressing securities fraud. Prior to § 1348, prosecutors commonly charged securities fraud by shoehorning conduct into general fraud statutes such as the mail and wire fraud statutes (18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343) or by using the Exchange Act’s criminal provisions. Section 1348 was a targeted congressional effort to provide a standalone criminal statute focused expressly on fraudulent schemes “in connection with any security” and to bring the law closer to a comprehensive instrument for prosecuting modern securities fraud.

The statute is thus best read as both substantive and remedial: substantive in that it delineates criminal conduct specific to securities markets, and remedial insofar as Congress sought to give federal law enforcement a clear, flexible vehicle to reach contemporary securities schemes—whether traditional misrepresentations, face‑to‑face investment scams, or the novel, technology‑driven manipulations of social media and electronic trading. From a prosecutorial perspective § 1348 is a breadth‑oriented statute; it reaches schemes that affect securities markets and that are executed by deceptive means to obtain money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. Courts have therefore looked to the abundant jurisprudence under mail and wire fraud for analytical guidance when parsing § 1348’s elements, but they remain attentive to the statute’s securities‑specific focus.

## Text of § 1348(1) and (2): schemes to defraud and to obtain money or property

Section 1348, in its most operative formulation, criminalizes two related types of conduct. One clause penalizes schemes “to defraud any person in connection with … any security,” while the other penalizes schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of … any security.” Reading those clauses together is essential to understanding the statute.

First, the “scheme to defraud” element focuses attention on deception: a defendant must have engaged in a plan or course of conduct that employed materially deceptive statements or omissions, or other fraudulent artifices, to induce others to act. Courts commonly ask whether the alleged misrepresentations were material and whether they were intended to deceive recipients about information that the recipients would find relevant to their investment decisions. Materiality is judged by whether the misstatements would have a natural tendency to influence, or were capable of influencing, a reasonable investor.

Second, the “to obtain” clause introduces the property dimension. Section 1348 targets schemes in which the defendant used deception to secure money or property in the securities context. The statute therefore borrows an analytic frame familiar from fraud law: deception plus an object of the deception that is money or property. Important doctrinal questions arise at the interface of these two clauses: what sorts of “things” count as property, what level of causation between deception and transfer is required, and whether a defendant’s intent must be to cause a victim net economic loss. Recent Supreme Court and circuit decisions have substantially clarified these issues.

Two doctrinal markers stand out. First, a “scheme to defraud” typically requires that the defendant’s deception was calculated to induce action by the victim (e.g., purchase of a security). Second, the property that the defendant seeks need not be a novel or exotic interest; it is the conventional categories of money or property (or a proprietary interest) that § 1348 protects. Thus, a case in which a fraudster uses false statements to trick investors into handing over cash to buy securities presents the paradigmatic § 1348 claim: deception, a victim’s transfer of money or property, and the securities connection.

The Fifth Circuit’s recent reversal of a district court dismissal in a “pump‑and‑dump” indictment illustrates how these elements operate in practice. There, the indictment alleged that social‑media influencers made false and misleading posts about particular stocks with the purpose and effect of inducing their followers to purchase those stocks, thereby allowing the influencers to sell at inflated prices for a profit. The court concluded that those allegations plausibly pled a scheme to defraud and an intent to obtain money or property because the followers were induced to part with funds to buy securities—classic “obtain” conduct under § 1348.

## Relationship to the mail and wire fraud statutes (18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343)

Section 1348 did not arise in a vacuum; legal analysis of § 1348 has been heavily informed by the long doctrinal history of the mail and wire fraud statutes. Those statutes criminalize schemes to defraud that use the mail or wire communications facilities. Because § 1348 tracks the device‑or‑scheme orientation of the mail and wire fraud statutes, courts frequently apply analogous interpretive principles: material misrepresentation, intent to defraud, and the notion that a “scheme” can encompass an array of deceptive devices.

The practical consequence of this borrowing is twofold. First, courts will often look to mail/wire fraud precedents to define what it means to have devised a scheme to defraud and to determine the mens rea required for criminal liability. For example, tests developed in mail/wire fraud cases—requiring a specific intent to deceive and an intention to deprive another of money or property, plus a showing that the scheme involved material misrepresentations—are applied to § 1348 claims. The Fifth Circuit opinion explicitly notes this line of reasoning: it treats § 1348 as “borrowing key concepts” from mail and wire fraud jurisprudence and applies those familiar concepts to the securities context.

Second, prosecutions often charge overlapping statutes. Many securities‑fraud prosecutions couple § 1348 counts with mail/wire fraud counts (and with other federal offenses), especially where the scheme used interstate communications. Historically, prosecutors charged mail/wire fraud when a clear mailing or interstate communication was present, and used § 1348 when the scheme had a clear securities nexus. The overlapping coverage creates both redundancy and tactical flexibility for prosecutors, and it also raises careful pleading concerns—courts remain attentive to whether the indictment alleges each statutory element for each charged offense.

However, a critical limitation emerged from the Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli: the mail and wire fraud statutes (and by extension § 1348) cannot be used to criminalize every deceit that amounts merely to withholding valuable economic information necessary for discretionary decisionmaking. In Ciminelli, the Court rejected a “right‑to‑control” theory that would have treated a victim’s right to information as a property interest under the fraud statutes. That decision forced prosecutors to articulate more precisely the property interest alleged. The Fifth Circuit’s pump‑and‑dump decision shows how prosecutors can navigate Ciminelli’s limitation: by grounding the fraud in an inducement that causes a victim to transfer money (purchase stock), prosecution properly alleges deprivation of money or property rather than mere deprivation of information.

## Relationship to Exchange Act § 10(b) and Rule 10b‑5 (civil vs. criminal frameworks)

Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and the Securities and Exchange Commission’s Rule 10b‑5 are the principal instruments of civil securities fraud enforcement and the basis for private actions by investors. Rule 10b‑5 makes it unlawful to employ any device, scheme, or artifice to defraud in connection with the purchase or sale of any security, to make any untrue statement of a material fact, or to engage in any act that operates as a fraud or deceit upon any person in connection with the purchase or sale of a security.

At first blush § 1348 and Rule 10b‑5 look similar: both are concerned with fraud “in connection with” securities transactions and both use the language of schemes and deceptive devices. But careful attention to procedural and doctrinal differences is essential.

First, burdens of proof and consequences differ. Rule 10b‑5 civil enforcement and private suits proceed under a preponderance standard; criminal prosecutions under § 1348 require proof beyond a reasonable doubt and typically require proof of a defendant’s specific intent to defraud. This heightened mens rea in criminal cases means prosecutors must build a more robust factual record to show purposeful deception and the intent to obtain money or property.

Second, doctrinal elements diverge in practices courts emphasize. In private Rule 10b‑5 suits, reliance and causation are central. An investor bringing a private action ordinarily must show reliance on the defendant’s misrepresentation (or that the misstatement was of the kind that would be relied upon) and that the misrepresentation caused the investor’s loss (proximate causation). In criminal § 1348 cases, reliance is not always an explicit element; rather, the government focuses on the scheme and the defendant’s intent to effectuate transfers of money or property. The Supreme Court’s recent decision in Kousisis (addressing the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory) underscores that criminal statutes can reach deceptive inducements that result in transfers of money or property regardless of whether the defendant expects the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss. In short, a rival between Rule 10b‑5 and § 1348 is not a contest of mutual exclusivity but a difference in function: Rule 10b‑5 supplies civil remedies and private causes of action built around reliance, while § 1348 supplies criminal liability built around intent to obtain money or property in the securities context.

Third, the source of authority differs and sometimes determines available remedies. SEC enforcement under Rule 10b‑5 can seek disgorgement, injunctions, and civil penalties; private plaintiffs seek damages. Criminal prosecution under § 1348 can result in imprisonment, criminal fines, forfeiture, and, in appropriate cases, orders of restitution. The choice of civil or criminal forum influences investigative tools (e.g., use of grand juries and subpoenas), evidentiary standards, and sentencing consequences.

Finally, there is enforcement overlap. Major securities scandals often spawn parallel civil and criminal proceedings. Prosecutors and the SEC frequently coordinate such efforts, but the distinct legal standards and remedies remain important for practitioners and courts.

## Overlap and differences between § 1348 and market manipulation statutes (e.g., Exchange Act § 9)

The Exchange Act contains provisions that address market manipulation more directly than § 1348. Section 9 of the Exchange Act and other rules and regulations target specific market‑manipulative acts—such as wash sales, matched orders, or other artifices designed to create the illusion of activity or price movement. Those provisions have both civil and criminal enforcement pathways under the Exchange Act, and the SEC promulgates ancillary rules defining manipulative conduct.

The relationship between § 1348 and market manipulation provisions is complementary but distinct. Section 9’s domain is manipulation—conduct that creates false or misleading appearances concerning the price or trading activity of a security. It is often focused on conduct that affects the market’s informational integrity and price discovery mechanisms. Section 1348, by contrast, focuses on deceptive schemes in connection with securities transactions that are designed to obtain money or property. A single fact pattern can implicate both regimes. For example, a trader who arranges wash trades to inflate trading volume and then sells into the artificially generated demand may violate both the Exchange Act’s anti‑manipulation provisions and § 1348’s general fraud prohibitions. Which statute is the most appropriate vehicle for enforcement often depends on facts, available evidence, and prosecutorial strategy.

Doctrinal differences matter. Market manipulation provisions sometimes do not require the same proof of induced transfers of money or property; they look to whether the defendant’s conduct affected market pricing or trading activity in a deceptive way. Section 1348 typically requires proof that the defendant intended to obtain money or property by deception, or that the defendant’s scheme to defraud had as an object the obtaining of money or property. The Supreme Court’s Ciminelli decision—which curtailed a “right‑to‑control” theory under the fraud statutes—does not foreclose manipulation prosecutions under the Exchange Act’s anti‑manipulation provisions, because those provisions do not hinge on a freestanding property interest in information.

In practice, prosecutors will often charge both manipulation and § 1348 counts, or they will select the statute that best maps to the government’s proof. Market manipulation statutes can be especially useful where the evidence supports a claim that the defendant’s conduct independently distorted market prices, while § 1348 is well suited where the core of the wrongdoing is an attempt to induce victims to part with funds—such as the classic pump‑and‑dump scenario.

## Jurisdictional hook and federal interests in securities markets

Federal jurisdiction over securities fraud is grounded in multiple sources. The Securities Exchange Act itself confers authority to regulate transactions in national markets. Section 1348’s “in connection with any security” language is broader than many state fraud statutes; it creates an express federal interest in policing deception that affects securities. Moreover, the federal interest is both substantive—protecting the integrity of national securities markets—and procedural—protecting interstate commerce and the means of communication used in modern markets.

In practice, jurisdictional hooks for federal prosecutors include: the national character of securities trading (stocks listed on national exchanges are traded across state lines), the interstate transmission of messages or orders (email, social media, wire transfers), and the use of national clearinghouses and brokerage networks. The pump‑and‑dump indictment the Fifth Circuit reviewed illustrates the jurisdictional picture: social‑media posts cross state and national lines, trading occurs on national exchanges, and the alleged deception targeted a dispersed, interstate investor base. Accordingly, federal courts have little trouble asserting jurisdiction when the scheme implicates nationally traded securities and interstate communications.

The federal interest in maintaining orderly securities markets informs prosecutorial choices. Securities fraud can produce systemic harms—investor losses, reduced market confidence, and the undermining of price discovery—that justify robust federal enforcement. That policy rationale is part of why Congress enacted § 1348 and why courts read it to reach a wide range of fraudulent schemes that exploit securities markets.

## Penalty structure and sentencing considerations under the Guidelines

A criminal conviction under § 1348 exposes a defendant to criminal penalties—imprisonment, fines, forfeiture, and potential orders of restitution. From a practical perspective the likely punishment is informed less by the statutory maximum than by the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines and by restitution rules.

Sentencing under the Guidelines for fraud offenses is driven principally by the loss amount. The guidelines provision most commonly applied to fraud is U.S.S.G. §2B1.1 (Fraud and Deceit), which sets a base offense level and then increases that level according to the amount of actual loss or, where appropriate, intended loss attributable to the offense. The calculation of loss in securities cases repeatedly raises contentious issues: should loss be measured by the victims’ out‑of‑pocket losses, the decline in market value following disclosure, or some measure of the defendant’s gain? Courts have wrestled with whether intended loss (the loss the defendant sought to cause) suffices when actual loss is hard to calculate, and whether market‑wide effects should be counted.

Kousisis and related decisions have implications here. The Supreme Court’s recognition that a defendant can be guilty under a fraudulent‑inducement theory even if the victim ultimately receives market‑value securities clarifies criminal culpability; it does not, however, resolve whether restitution or loss calculations for sentencing require a showing of net pecuniary harm. Restitution under the Mandatory Victim Restitution Act (MVRA) and under 18 U.S.C. § 3663A requires identifiable victims and an accounting of victims’ losses. In many securities schemes, establishing restitution may require granular proof of who paid what price when and who realized losses—an often difficult evidentiary task in widely traded securities.

Other guideline considerations commonly affect sentencing in securities fraud cases. If the offense involved a large number of victims, the offense level may increase. Use of “sophisticated means” (e.g., complex layering of transactions, use of shell companies, or technical devices to conceal the scheme) can add offense‑level enhancements. A leadership role in a criminal organization or group involved in the fraud may add an enhancement under §3B1.1. Conversely, acceptance of responsibility and cooperation can reduce the offense level and, importantly, may lead to a motion for a downward departure if a defendant provides substantial assistance to prosecutors.

Criminal forfeiture can be sought to strip a defendant of ill‑gotten gains. Forfeiture is a remedial and punitive device that complements sentencing and restitution. The government typically seeks forfeiture of the proceeds traceable to the offense; the computation of those proceeds is often coextensive with the loss analysis but must stand on its own evidentiary footing.

Finally, plea bargaining is a dominant feature of securities enforcement. Prosecutors weigh the strength of the proof on intent, causation, and loss; defense counsel weigh exposure under the Guidelines and the risk/reward of trial. The criminal framework thus interacts with civil regulatory actions, investor civil suits, and parallel investigations by the SEC.

Conclusion

18 U.S.C. § 1348 occupies a central place in federal securities‑fraud enforcement. It is a statutory expression of Congress’s decision to give federal prosecutors a tailored tool to attack deception in securities markets, and it operates in close dialogue with the legacy mail and wire fraud doctrines as well as the Exchange Act’s civil and criminal provisions. Recent appellate decisions illustrate two important limits and clarifications for practitioners and students: Ciminelli’s restriction on treating the mere withholding of economic information as a cognizable property deprivation, and Kousisis’s affirmation that a fraudulent‑inducement theory—where victims are tricked into transferring money or property—comfortably fits within the fraud statutes. Understanding § 1348 therefore requires both a firm grasp of traditional fraud doctrine and an appreciation of securities‑specific policy concerns about market integrity and investor protection. The statute’s breadth and the complexity of modern markets ensure that § 1348 will remain a focal point for doctrinal development and for prosecutorial practice.

# Elements of § 1348: Scheme to Defraud and Intent to Defraud

Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to defraud “in connection with” securities and schemes to obtain “money or property” by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises. Because § 1348 is a relatively recent statutory addition, courts in this circuit and others commonly import doctrinal concepts developed under the mail and wire fraud statutes to give those statutory terms content. That practice yields helpful tests and vocabulary—“scheme to defraud,” “fraudulent inducement,” materiality, and intent to deceive and obtain—but the imported concepts must be read in light of recent Supreme Court decisions that cabin certain theories of wrongdoing (most notably Ciminelli) while affirming others (most notably Kousisis). The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in the pump-and-dump prosecution provides a useful, contemporary illustration of how those elements are pleaded and assessed: the government alleged that defendants used material misrepresentations on social media to induce followers to purchase securities, thereby obtaining money or property when the defendants sold their positions at inflated prices.

The analysis below sets out the core elements the government must plead and eventually prove under § 1348, explains the governing standards and tests, and shows how those legal principles operate in contexts such as pump-and-dump schemes.

## Element 1: A scheme to defraud—what counts as a scheme

A “scheme to defraud” under § 1348 is a plan or course of conduct that involves deception or trickery designed to cause another to part with something of value. Courts describe the scheme element with two complementary emphases. First, the scheme must involve deception—a falsehood, a half-truth, an omission that is deceptive in context, or some other artifice calculated to mislead. Second, the deception must be undertaken in order to obtain something of value (typically money or property) or to effect some other unlawful objective in connection with a security.

The scheme can take many forms. Traditional examples include false representations about the character or quality of an investment that induce purchases, contracts procured by lies, or the use of phony paperwork to obtain funds. Modern examples include coordinated social-media campaigns—“pump” posts that misrepresent trading positions or prospects to induce followers to buy, followed by secret sales (“dump”) by those who originated the misrepresentations. The factual variety is broad, but the legal question is stable: did the defendant engage in a course of deception intended to induce others to act in a way that produced a transfer of money or property (or otherwise effected the proscribed result)?

Important analytical limits on what counts as a protectable scheme follow from recent Supreme Court precedent. In Ciminelli, the Court rejected the so-called “right-to-control” theory of fraud—an attempt to treat the denial of valuable economic information (the victim’s “right to control” a discretionary decision) as a property interest protected by the fraud statutes. Ciminelli makes clear that not every deceit that affects someone’s economic decisionmaking is a federal fraud; the deception must target money or property in the sense long recognized by fraud law. By contrast, the Supreme Court in Kousisis clarified that the fraudulent-inducement theory remains valid: a defendant may be prosecuted for using a material misstatement to trick a person into a transaction that causes the victim to part with money or property, even if the victim ultimately receives something of comparable value and even if the defendant’s objective was profit rather than to make the victim worse off. Thus, schemes that trick victims into buying securities (or otherwise transferring money or property) through materially false representations fall squarely within § 1348’s reach.

In assessing whether an alleged course of conduct constitutes a scheme to defraud, courts look to the substance of the alleged misrepresentations and the intended (and actual) effect on victims. The Fifth Circuit’s pump-and-dump opinion exemplifies this approach: the indictment alleged a recurring pattern—acquire a position, disseminate false or misleading information to induce purchases by followers, then sell into the inflation—together forming a scheme because the misrepresentations were calculated to cause followers to purchase securities, thereby enabling the defendants to realize profits.

## Material misrepresentation or fraudulent pretenses: definitions and scope

A material misrepresentation is an affirmative statement of fact that is false, or a representation that is misleading because it omits or conceals information necessary to make what is said not misleading. Fraudulent pretenses encompass both affirmative false statements and representations that are “pretenses” designed to induce a transfer—falsehoods about past or present facts, promises about future conduct made with no intention to perform, and other dishonest statements framed to deceive.

Materiality, discussed separately below, is the threshold concept that separates actionable lies from nonactionable puffery or inconsequential errors. But before materiality, the basic threshold is deceptiveness: the communication must be capable of misleading a target audience. Courts routinely emphasize that a defendant’s intent and the surrounding context matter: a factual misstatement that would not mislead the reasonable recipient in context is not the core of a federal fraud charge. Conversely, a misleading half-truth is as actionable as an affirmative falsehood when the partial disclosure creates a materially misleading impression. The law treats half-truths seriously because the speaker who chooses to inject some truthful facts into the conversation may thereby assume a duty to provide the non-deceptive context; failure to do so can be the basis for criminal liability.

Fraudulent pretenses also include schemes that use deceptive mechanisms rather than formal spoken or written lies: sham contracts, counterfeit documents, or bogus corporate arrangements designed to create the illusion of legitimacy. The mail/wire fraud jurisprudence has long allowed convictions based on schemes that employed such artifices when they were part of a calculated plan to induce transfers of money or property.

The Fifth Circuit opinion stresses that the indictment adequately alleged deception: defendants posted false and misleading information about securities on social media with the intention of inducing purchases. Under settled doctrine, such allegations, if proven, describe classic material misrepresentations or fraudulent pretenses—even though the forum was social media rather than a printed prospectus or a direct conversation.

## Money-or-property requirement: protected property interests

A central threshold for § 1348 liability is that the fraud must target money or property. That requirement distinguishes prosecutable fraud from mere interference with an individual’s autonomy to make informed economic choices. Ciminelli explains this limitation most starkly: federal fraud statutes protect “traditional” property interests; they do not create a federal crime for depriving another simply of valuable economic information (the “right to control” theory). Put differently, you cannot convict someone under the federal fraud statutes simply because they deprived someone of information that would have informed a discretionary decision, unless that deprivation also induced a transfer or otherwise interfered with a property interest.

That said, the fraudulent-inducement theory protects money or property that a victim parts with as a result of the fraud. If a defendant uses materially false statements to induce someone to enter a contract, buy a security, or otherwise give up money or property, the fraud statutes reach that scheme because the defendant sought and obtained “money or property.” The Supreme Court in Kousisis reaffirmed that fraudulent inducement—where the misrepresentation causes the victim to hand over money or property in a transaction—is a core form of the fraud that federal statutes punish, even if the victim received something in exchange and even if there was no net pecuniary loss.

In securities contexts, the “money or property” requirement is often satisfied when investors purchase securities as a result of deceptive statements. The purchase transfers money or other assets from the investor to the market or to other market participants. Even more indirect profits—such as the defendant’s later sales into an artificially inflated market created by their misrepresentations—can supply the “money or property” that the fraud statutes protect when the government alleges that the defendant’s fraudulent inducement caused the market transfers that enabled the profit. The Fifth Circuit’s pump-and-dump indictment alleges exactly this: the defendants caused followers to purchase securities, which generated market price increases enabling the defendants to sell at a profit—money obtained by the defendants through a fraudulent inducement.

Careful attention must be paid, however, to the nature of the alleged property interest. Courts will not stretch the concept of “property” to cover an abstract or purely informational interest. Thus, while the right-to-control (i.e., the right to possess economic information) is not protected under federal fraud statutes, a transfer of cash or a contractual right created by a transaction induced by deception is protected.

## Element 2: Intent to defraud—intent to deceive and to obtain

Intent under § 1348 has two components that courts commonly describe: (1) an intent to deceive (the mental disposition to mislead another by false or misleading statements or omissions) and (2) an intent to obtain money or property (the defendant’s objective in using deception to secure a transfer or to accomplish a property-related objective). Historically some precedent described a second prong as intent “to cause some harm” to result from the deceit, but the Supreme Court in Kousisis clarified that the fraud statutes criminalize schemes to obtain money or property even if the defendant did not specifically seek to cause the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss. The critical focus is on the defendant’s objective to obtain money or property through deception—not on a separate requirement that the defendant intend the victim to be worse off than before.

Intent is rarely admitted or proven by direct evidence. Courts therefore infer intent from circumstantial evidence: the scheme’s design and execution, contemporaneous misrepresentations, the defendant’s pattern of concealment, secret sales or transfers timed to exploit those misrepresentations, large profits realized in a way consistent with a deceptive plan, and internal communications showing knowledge and deliberate tactics. In the pump-and-dump indictment, for example, the government alleged a series of acts showing a pattern—purchase the security, craft and disseminate misleading social-media posts, and sell secretly to realize gains—that, if proven, would permit a jury to infer both the intent to deceive and the intent to obtain money.

Kousisis also clarified the scope of permissible inferences about intent. It rejected the argument that fraud requires a showing that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. Under Kousisis, it suffices that the defendant used deception to induce a transaction whereby the victim parted with money or property—even if the defendant provided something of value in return or did not aim to inflict net loss. That principle answers a frequent defense theme in securities cases (including pump-and-dump defenses): the claim that the victims “got securities for their money” or that the defendant simply sought to profit but did not want victims to lose. Under Kousisis, such defenses do not negate the existence of intent to obtain; they may go to the defendant’s theory of why there was no crime, but they do not negate the core allegation that the defendant intended to induce transfers by deception.

Finally, the mens rea inquiry requires courts to distinguish innocent endorsements or expressions of opinion from deceptive misrepresentations made with wrongful intent. Subjective puffery, enthusiastic predictions honestly held, or general market commentary do not evidence the intent required for § 1348. By contrast, knowingly false statements about concrete facts, sham trades designed to conceal true positions, or coordinated deceptive campaigns aimed at manipulating prices will support the required intent.

## Materiality standard and the reasonable investor

Materiality is the gatekeeper for transforming ordinary mistaken or irrelevant statements into actionable misrepresentations. The materiality inquiry asks whether the misrepresented fact or omitted information is “material,” meaning that it is sufficiently likely to affect a reasonable recipient’s decisionmaking. In the securities context courts typically employ the standard articulated in TSC Industries v. Northway: an omitted or misstated fact is material if there is a substantial likelihood that a reasonable investor would consider it important in making an investment decision or if it would have significantly altered the total mix of available information.

Although § 1348 is phrased in language borrowed from mail and wire fraud statutes, courts applying § 1348 in securities cases have borrowed the securities-law conception of materiality because the statute expressly reaches “in connection with” securities. Thus, when a prosecution charges securities-related misstatements under § 1348, courts will often ask whether the misrepresentation or omission had the tendency to influence a reasonable investor. The Fifth Circuit opinion relies on this lineage: it frames the scheme element to require a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value.

Materiality is a question of fact for the jury in most cases, but it may be resolved as a matter of law if the misstatement is so trivial that no reasonable investor would treat it as important. On the other hand, where the alleged misrepresentation involves concrete aspects of a company’s business, finances, or prospects—areas investors commonly rely upon—materiality will usually be a live factual issue for resolution at trial.

## Half-truths and omissions: duty not to mislead once speaking

A speaker who makes partial disclosures or presents facts in a way that creates a misleading impression can be criminally liable for the omission even though the omitted information is true. The law treats such half-truths as equivalent to affirmative falsehoods when the incomplete statement is materially misleading. The duty principle is straightforward in the securities context: once a market participant chooses to speak about a material topic, the speaker may have a duty to provide truthful and complete information necessary to prevent deception. The duty can arise from a fiduciary or statutory duty (for example, in registered offering documents), from a special relationship, or simply from the speaker’s decision to make representations that create a materially misleading impression.

In the social-media pump-and-dump context, the duty to correct or to disclose arises particularly when influencers present themselves as informed, disclose partial trading information, or portray positions and motives in a way that cultivates trust. The Fifth Circuit observed that defendants “hold themselves out to be skilled stock traders and frequently posted their trading activities on social media.” Those circumstances, if proven, make plausible the inference that their selective disclosures were intended to mislead and that the omissions were actionable because they created a materially false impression.

Defenses that rest on the “no duty to disclose” mantra can succeed in limited circumstances where the speaker did not say anything or had no special relationship and the omitted fact would not have been expected by the recipient. But where the speaker undertook active communications about material matters and those communications were false or incomplete in a way that tended to mislead reasonable investors, omission-based liability is a well-established principle.

## Causation and reliance: what is (and is not) required in criminal fraud

A recurring question in criminal fraud prosecutions is whether the government must prove that victims relied on the deceit and that the deceit caused their loss. Historically, many mail and wire fraud cases required some showing that the deceptive scheme was reasonably calculated to deceive and that the deception caused the victim to part with money or property; in other words, proof of a causal nexus between deception and transfer has long been central.

The shape of that requirement differs across contexts. Under a classic fraudulent-inducement theory, the government must show that the defendant used materially false statements to induce the victim to hand over money or property. That showing is usually (but not invariably) accomplished with evidence that the victim acted in response to the deception—i.e., reliance. In practice, criminal prosecutions often proceed without direct testimonial evidence of reliance because circumstantial evidence (timing, contemporaneous trading, statements made, the structure of the scheme) can establish that the deception caused the transfers. The Fifth Circuit’s decision reflects this pragmatic approach: the indictment alleged that the defendants’ false posts induced their followers to purchase securities; that allegation, if proven, supplies the required causal link between deception and transfer.

Importantly, Kousisis clarified that the government need not show that the defendant intended to cause a net pecuniary loss to the victim. The fraud is complete when the defendant obtains the victim’s money or property under materially false pretenses—even if the defendant expects to provide something of value in return or if the victim’s ultimate economic position is not made worse by the deception. The legal consequence is that reliance and causation remain relevant to connect the deception to the transfer, but proving that the deception left the victim worse off is not required. Thus, showing that victims purchased securities they otherwise would not have bought, or that they purchased at inflated prices because of the deception, will typically suffice even if in the end some victims sold at a profit.

Two practical corollaries flow from this principle. First, prosecutors will plead and prove causation through the structure of the scheme, timing of communications, and trading patterns that reflect inducement. Second, defendants may still challenge causation at trial by demonstrating that the victims acted for reasons unrelated to the defendant’s statements or that the causal link is too attenuated; such arguments go to the weight of the evidence, not to a categorical bar on the fraudulent-inducement theory.

## Use of mail/wire fraud case law to interpret § 1348 (e.g., Greenlaw; Evans)

Because § 1348 was drafted to reach securities-focused frauds but uses language closely akin to the mail and wire fraud statutes, courts often look to the extensive mail/wire fraud jurisprudence for interpretive guidance. The Fifth Circuit has explicitly endorsed this methodology, observing that § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and that courts frequently rely on mail/wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges.

Cases such as United States v. Evans and United States v. Greenlaw supply elaborated statements of the elements—scheme to defraud, material misrepresentation, and intent—along with tests for materiality and intent that courts applying § 1348 can adapt. Evans, for example, described the scheme element in terms of material misrepresentations intended to deceive to obtain something of value; Greenlaw recognized that § 1348 requires a scheme and intent and that analysis often parallels mail/wire fraud doctrine.

At the same time, reliance on mail/wire jurisprudence is not mechanical. Courts must apply developments from higher courts that limit certain doctrines. Ciminelli is a prime example: earlier mail/wire fraud decisions had sometimes sustained convictions based on a “right-to-control” theory, but Ciminelli rejected that theory as inconsistent with the statutory focus on property. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion carefully distinguishes right-to-control cases from fraudulent-inducement allegations and imports Kousisis to revalidate fraudulent-inducement reasoning in the face of Ciminelli’s limits.

In practice, this means that prosecutors will frame § 1348 charges using established mail/wire fraud categories—fraudulent inducement, schemes to obtain property, misrepresentations and omissions, materiality, and intent to deceive—but they must avoid theories that rest solely on depriving a victim of an informational advantage. Defendants and courts will litigate whether particular misrepresentations fit the fraudulent-inducement mold (targeting a property transfer) or the prohibited right-to-control theory (targeting intangible informational control), and whether the alleged conduct satisfies Kousisis’s instruction that obtaining money or property is the focal point of the offense.

Conclusion

Section 1348 prosecutions require careful articulation of the two central elements: a scheme to defraud (involving material misrepresentations or deceptive practices) and an intent to defraud (an intent to deceive and to obtain money or property). Recent Supreme Court decisions have refined these doctrines by excluding purely informational deprivations from the scope of federal fraud law (Ciminelli) while affirming that fraudulent inducement—using deception to cause a victim to part with money or property—remains an actionable crime even when the victim receives something of value in return or does not suffer a net pecuniary loss (Kousisis). The mail and wire fraud body of law continues to supply practical tests and evidentiary principles for § 1348 cases, but those authorities must be applied in light of Ciminelli and Kousisis. The Fifth Circuit’s pump-and-dump opinion neatly illustrates these themes: allegations that influencers used material misrepresentations on social media to induce purchases and thereby realized large profits fall within § 1348 because they allege both a deceptive scheme and the required intent to obtain money or property.

# Property After Ciminelli and Kousisis: Drawing the Line

This chapter examines how the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis recalibrated the outer boundaries of federal fraud prosecutions that rest on alleged deprivations of “economic information” or control over another’s decisionmaking. Taken together, those decisions draw a clearer line between (1) impermissible “right‑to‑control” theories that attempt to cabin fraud liability to the withholding of valuable information alone, and (2) permissible “fraudulent‑inducement” theories that reach misrepresentations used to procure money or property. The Fifth Circuit’s recent reversal of the district court dismissal of a pump‑and‑dump securities indictment provides a useful vehicle for illustrating how those decisions operate in practice. This chapter explains the doctrinal elements at stake, how to plead and defend fraud cases in light of the new doctrine, and how courts have begun to apply the rulings across different fact patterns—especially procurement and securities fraud.

## Ciminelli v. United States: rejection of the right‑to‑control theory

Ciminelli is the doctrinal inflection point from which the current contours of property for fraud purposes are drawn. In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court confronted a prosecution that relied principally on what has been called the “right‑to‑control” theory: the government treated a victim’s “right to control” its own discretionary decisionmaking (for example, how to award state contracts) as a cognizable property interest protected by the federal fraud statutes. The Court rejected that theory. It held that the federal fraud statutes reach schemes that deprive victims of “traditional” property interests—money, tangible property, or well‑recognized intangibles like choses in action—but do not reach the mere withholding or manipulation of valuable economic information necessary for discretionary decisionmaking.

Two features of the Ciminelli opinion deserve emphasis because they set limits on prosecutorial theories. First, the Court insisted on a historical and conceptual boundary: the fraud statutes protect interests analogous to property as traditionally understood. Second, the Court rejected any theory that would convert every deceptive access to another’s decisionmaking into a property offense simply because the deception affects the victim’s choices or the “value” of a decision. Thus, Ciminelli foreclosed convictions premised solely on a defendant's alleged scheme to deprive the victim of information that would have influenced discretionary decisions; such schemes, standing alone, do not satisfy the “money or property” requirement.

Although Ciminelli narrowed the government's ability to argue property deprivation in some contexts, the opinion did not collapse all fraud theories into the right‑to‑control frame. Indeed, Ciminelli left open other ways of pleading fraud—most importantly fraudulent inducement—so long as the indictment alleges a scheme that obtains money or property from the victim, rather than mere access to or control over information.

## Traditional property interests vs. valuable economic information

Understanding why Ciminelli mattered requires distinguishing between two concepts that sometimes blur in complex financial or procurement settings: (1) traditional property interests—money, goods, ownership rights, contractual entitlements, and recognized intangible property such as accounts receivable—and (2) valuable economic information that affects how a person chooses to use or dispose of their assets.

Traditional property interests are paradigmatic objects of fraud statutes because they are susceptible to being “obtained” by deception in an immediately tangible sense: the victim parts with currency, the defendant takes title to an asset, or the victim conveys a contractual right. Historically, federal mail, wire, and bank fraud jurisprudence has treated such deprivations as the prototypical fraud target.

By contrast, valuable economic information—forecasts, inside knowledge, or strategic advice—is not ordinarily treated as property in the same sense, and Ciminelli held that the mere deprivation of that information does not equate to depriving someone of property for purposes of federal fraud statutes. The distinction is functional: depriving someone of information alters their decision calculus but does not, by itself, transfer money or property to the defendant. The law therefore requires prosecutors to show that the defendant’s scheme aimed at obtaining an interest that the law recognizes as property, rather than merely manipulating the victim’s access to information.

A practical consequence: prosecutions that rest on showing only that the defendant skewed the victim’s decisionmaking by providing false information (for instance, concealing a conflict of interest so that a public official awards a contract) must demonstrate that the fraud resulted in the defendant obtaining, or attempting to obtain, money or property from the victim. If the indictment cannot tie the deception to an “obtained” property interest, Ciminelli suggests the indictment will be vulnerable.

## Kousisis v. United States: recognizing fraudulent inducement

Kousisis clarified the permissible alternative to the right‑to‑control theory: the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Under this theory, the gravamen of the offense is the defendant’s use of a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into entering a transaction that requires handing over money or property. The classic example—considered and approved in Kousisis—is a contractor who lies to obtain a government contract and thereby causes the government to part with funds or contractual rights. Kousisis confirmed that fraudulent inducement targets property interests because it centers on the victim’s parting with money or property as the object of the deception.

Kousisis is important for two reasons. First, it explicitly disavows the idea that fraudulent inducement is merely a repackaging of right‑to‑control. Instead, it grounds liability on the acquisition of property—an interest squarely within the fraud statutes—thereby preserving many traditional fraud prosecutions. Second, Kousisis clarified the mental‑state inquiry: the government need not prove that the defendant intended to make the victim worse off in net terms; it is sufficient that the defendant intended to obtain money or property by means of a misrepresentation.

In other words, Kousisis saved a large set of fraud prosecutions from Ciminelli’s narrowing effect by permitting convictions where the government alleges and proves that the defendant used lies to induce victims to hand over money or property—even if the victims received something of value in return and even if they were not left economically worse off in the long run.

## ‘Object of the fraud’ and the requirement to obtain money or property

Post‑Ciminelli and Kousisis, a central doctrinal question in mail, wire, or securities fraud prosecutions is whether the indictment alleges an “object of the fraud” that is the obtaining of money or property. This requirement has two interrelated aspects: (1) the offense must describe a scheme to defraud, often by material misrepresentations or omissions; and (2) the scheme must be aimed at obtaining money or property as an objective.

Courts analyze that duality by parsing the indictment to identify where the deception was intended to culminate in a transfer of money or property. If the indictment shows that the defendant used false statements to induce victims to enter transactions that required a payment, or to transfer securities, contractual rights, or other property interests, the “object of the fraud” element is satisfied. The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in the pump‑and‑dump case is instructive: the indictment alleged that social‑media promoters purchased securities, posted false statements to induce followers to buy, which caused the followers to part with money to buy the securities—thereby providing property (money) that the promoters could liquidate at a profit. By alleging a fraudulent inducement to purchase securities, the government tied the deception to the obtainment of money or property rather than to merely influencing followers’ information set.

Careful pleading therefore requires specifying the economic transfer the deception caused or sought to cause. Money is the clearest example, but property includes securities, contractual interests, and other recognized intangibles. The “obtain” element requires more than showing market effects or changes in the non‑defendant’s decisionmaking; it requires an allegation that the scheme was designed to and did cause a victim to part with a property interest that the defendant sought to acquire or exploit.

## No net‑loss requirement: why economic harm to the victim is not an element

A particularly consequential clarification in Kousisis is that the fraud statutes do not require the government to show that the defendant intended to make the victim economically worse off, nor that the victim actually suffered a net pecuniary loss. Kousisis holds that fraudulent inducement is complete once the defendant induces the victim to hand over money or property under materially false pretenses, even if the defendant returns “equivalent” value or the victim is not in the long run poorer.

This principle flows from the object‑of‑the‑fraud focus: fraud statutes are designed to protect property interests against schemes intended to obtain them by deception. Whether the defendant intended to inflict a net loss on the victim is analytically separate from whether the defendant sought to obtain the victim’s money or property. A classic example is a “pass‑through” subcontractor scheme: a prime contractor lies to obtain a government contract that requires subcontracting to a disadvantaged business, but the prime contractor simply passes payments through the subcontractor while retaining profits. The government may charge fraud because the prime contractor obtained the contract and payments as a product of deception, whether or not the disadvantaged business received payments in some form.

In practice this means prosecutors need not prove (and defenders cannot insist upon) a conviction‑ending requirement that the victim be left economically worse off. Instead, focus turns to whether the deception induced a transfer of a protected interest and whether the defendant intended to obtain that interest. The Fifth Circuit applied that principle in the pump‑and‑dump case, rejecting an argument that because followers received securities and did not necessarily net‑lose, no fraud had occurred. Because the indictment alleged defendants induced purchases (transfers of money) to enable the defendants’ profitable sales, the object‑of‑the‑fraud requirement was met even if victims later held market‑priced securities.

Counsel must therefore evaluate whether alleged deception resulted in an induced transfer. If so, the absence of net economic loss is not an automatic defense to the sufficiency of the indictment.

## Pleading around Ciminelli: alleging deprivation of money or property

Ciminelli’s constraint requires prosecutors to be deliberate in how they plead fraud. To survive a motion to dismiss or to present a jury case, the indictment or information should:

- Clearly describe the misrepresentations or omissions and how they were material;
- Allege concretely that the misrepresentations were used to induce a transaction or transfer in which the victim parted with money or property (for example, “defendant posted false statements encouraging purchases of Security X and thereby induced Purchasers to pay money to brokers to buy Security X”);
- Identify the property interest obtained or sought (money, securities, contractual payments, etc.);
- Allege the defendant’s intent to obtain that property interest (motive, pattern of trades, concealment of sales, contemporaneous transfers of funds, traces to defendant, or other circumstantial indicators);
- If possible, include traceable economic effects (e.g., dates and amounts of defendant’s purchases and sales, profits realized, and contemporaneous customer purchases) to connect the deception to material transfers.

Particularity matters. An indictment that alleges only that the defendant “deprived victims of accurate information” or “influenced their decisions” without pleading that the defendant induced the victims to hand over money or property is vulnerable under Ciminelli. Prosecutors should plead the victim’s transfer as the concrete consequence of the lie.

Defenders, conversely, can attack the indictment by arguing that it alleges only informational deprivation and lacks allegations tying misrepresentations to an obtainment of property. Defense motions to dismiss should insist upon the distinction Ciminelli established and press the indictment to identify a clear property interest.

## Applying Ciminelli and Kousisis beyond procurement fraud to securities cases

Although Ciminelli arose in the procurement context and Kousisis addressed procurement fraud as well, the doctrinal principles apply across the spectrum of fraud prosecutions, including securities, commodities, and other commercial settings. The key inquiry—whether the defendant’s deception sought to obtain money or property—translates naturally to securities fraud because purchases and sales of securities necessarily involve transfers of property (money for securities).

In a securities “pump‑and‑dump” scenario, for example, social‑media promoters who post false bullish statements to induce followers to buy a thinly traded stock effectuate transfers of money: followers give money to brokers to buy shares, and promoters who already hold shares can sell at an inflated price and realize profits. Allegations that promoters made material misrepresentations to induce purchases therefore fit squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement framework. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion reversing the dismissal of a pump‑and‑dump indictment demonstrates this application: the court concluded that the indictment cannot be dismissed simply because the followers received securities (i.e., not necessarily a net loss); what matters is that the promoters obtained money (in the sense of proceeds from sales) by inducing others to transfer funds to buy securities.

Securities prosecutions thus have a natural path around Ciminelli. The complaint or indictment should anchor the misrepresentation to the purchase transaction (the induced transfer of money for securities), show that the defendant intended to profit from that induced trading, and, where possible, include trading chronology and profit figures tying the deception to realized proceeds.

One caveat: not every misleading analyst report or puffery about a company will suffice. The government must still allege material misrepresentations that are capable of inducing a reasonable investor to part with money. Boilerplate or vague claims about misleading the market will be vulnerable absent particular allegations of inducement and obtainment.

## Comparative approaches in the circuits (e.g., Seventh Circuit’s Smith)

Courts have approached the post‑Ciminelli landscape with variations in emphasis, but the decisive thread is applying Kousisis to preserve fraudulent‑inducement prosecutions while policing attempts to plead around Ciminelli by couching property as control or information.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision in Smith (decided after Kousisis) is illustrative. In Smith, the Seventh Circuit applied Kousisis to uphold charges where the defendants allegedly used false statements to induce purchases or procure payments. It concluded that Kousisis’ reasoning extends beyond procurement settings to any context in which the government alleges that the defendant used misrepresentations to obtain money or property. Smith aligns with the Fifth Circuit’s approach: post‑Ciminelli courts will distinguish information‑based theories from inducement theories and will sustain indictments that track the latter.

Other circuits have similarly recognized Kousisis as permitting convictions where the government alleges a fraudulent inducement to part with money or property. The common thread is that Ciminelli’s prohibition on the right‑to‑control theory does not prevent enforcement when the victim actually parted with tangible property or an identifiable intangible as a direct product of deception.

That said, some circuits remain vigilant against broad or imaginative attempts to convert every fraud claim into an “obtained property” claim. Courts continue to require concrete allegations tying deception to an actual or intended transfer. The precise level of required specificity varies by court, and practitioners should monitor evolving precedents in their circuit.

## Litigation strategy: prosecutorial and defense considerations

The doctrinal developments invite distinct strategic responses.

Prosecutorial strategy:
- Draft indictments that recite the elements of a fraudulent‑inducement theory plainly. Describe the misrepresentations, the inducement to transfer money or property, and the defendant’s profit motive and realized gains.
- If possible, provide transaction‑level detail: dates, trading amounts, traceable transfers, and evidence of concealment (e.g., secret sales, undisclosed ties between promoters and sellers).
- Anticipate Ciminelli challenges by contrasting the pleading with informational deprivation; emphasize that the victims parted with money or property.
- Use circumstantial evidence to plead intent: pattern of trading, timing of posts and subsequent sales, coordinated behavior, and measures to conceal holdings or sales.

Defense strategy:
- Move to dismiss where the indictment alleges only informational deprivation or fails to identify a transferred property interest.
- If the indictment survives, focus discovery on showing that any transfers to the market did not flow to the defendant or that the alleged misrepresentations were immaterial or mere opinion/puffery.
- Attack the sufficiency of evidence of intent. Kousisis lowers the bar for showing economic harm is absent, but prosecutors still must show that obtaining money or property was an object of the scheme.
- Where appropriate, file a motion for a bill of particulars seeking exact allegations tying specific transfers to the defendant’s misrepresentations.

Both sides should remember that an indictment’s sufficiency (for dismissal) turns on whether it alleges each material element; courts accept the allegations as true at that stage. The sufficiency inquiry is not a merits determination.

## Synthesis and practical checklist

Ciminelli and Kousisis together create a two‑step gate for fraud prosecutions:
1. Exclude theories that rest only on deprivation of valuable economic information or control over discretionary decisionmaking (Ciminelli).
2. Permit prosecutions that allege deception used to induce a victim to part with money or property—the fraudulent‑inducement theory—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net economic loss (Kousisis).

For prosecutors drafting an indictment under post‑Ciminelli/Kousisis law:
- Allegation of scheme: identify specific misrepresentations or omissions and why they were material.
- Allegation of obtainment: specify the money or property the defendant sought to obtain and how victims were induced to transfer it.
- Allegation of intent: plead facts showing the defendant acted with the intent to deceive and to obtain the money or property (motive, pattern, concealment).
- Particularity and chronology: provide dates, amounts, and transactional connection when feasible.

For defenders challenging such indictments:
- Test whether the pleading relies on informational deprivation alone.
- Demand particulars that tie transfers of money/property to the alleged deception.
- Press the distinction between inducing a decision and inducing a transfer.

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the dismissal in the pump‑and‑dump case illustrates the practical conclusion: a well‑pleaded fraudulent‑inducement allegation that links misrepresentations to induced purchases of securities—and to profits realized by the defendants—survives a Ciminelli attack. Conversely, broad or amorphous claims that a defendant “manipulated” decisionmaking without alleging that victims parted with money or property will remain vulnerable.

In doctrinal terms, the current state of the law preserves robust protection for traditional property interests from deceptive schemes, while preventing overreach where the supposed injury is merely to a victim’s capacity to make informed choices. Courts will continue to police the boundary between these categories; practitioners should structure pleadings and defenses with the distinction firmly in mind.

# Pump-and-Dump, Scalping, and Market Fraud in the Social‑Media Era

This chapter examines contemporary pump‑and‑dump schemes and related misrepresentations in securities markets, emphasizing how social media and influencers have changed both the mechanics of fraud and the law’s response. The recent Fifth Circuit opinion reversing the dismissal of a social‑media–based pump‑and‑dump indictment provides the factual and doctrinal anchor for the discussion. That opinion applies (and is informed by) the Supreme Court’s recent decisions on the scope of federal fraud statutes—most notably Ciminelli and Kousisis—and demonstrates how the fraudulent‑inducement theory operates in the criminal context when promoters use false social‑media posts to induce purchases of securities. This chapter explains the classic mechanics of pump‑and‑dump schemes, the particular problem of scalping (undisclosed proprietary positions), the doctrinal distinction and overlap between misrepresentations and market manipulation, how social platforms amplify risk and complicate proof, and the parallel but distinct paths of SEC civil enforcement and Department of Justice criminal prosecution. It then surveys practical indicators prosecutors and civil enforcement attorneys use to establish fraud and how defendants can and do litigate these cases.

## Classic pump‑and‑dump mechanics: acquisition, hype, and exit

A pump‑and‑dump is an archetypal market manipulation scheme that unfolds in three phases. First, a promoter or group of promoters quietly accumulates a position in a target security, typically one with low public float and thin trading volume (a “microcap” or “penny stock”). The low liquidity and small float make the security unusually sensitive to buying pressure: modest purchases can cause substantial price increases. Second, the promoters “pump” the price by generating promotional activity that creates demand—through positive (but false or misleading) statements about the company, its prospects, or imminent news expected to drive the price up. Historically promoters used newsletters, cold calls, or paid advertisements; today, social media, private chat groups, and influencer posts serve the same function but at far greater speed and scale. Third, after the artificial demand lifts the price, the promoters “dump” their accumulated shares—selling into the heightened liquidity at much higher prices—and realize profits. Prices typically collapse once selling begins or the absence of fundamental support becomes obvious, leaving late buyers with losses.

From a legal perspective, the essence of a classic pump‑and‑dump is not merely aggressive marketing but deception that causes others to part with money or property. Under federal securities‑fraud analogues—criminally, 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud), and civilly, Exchange Act § 10(b) and Rule 10b‑5—the government or regulator must show a scheme that involves material misstatements or omissions and scienter: an intent to deceive, manipulate, or defraud. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in the social‑media pump‑and‑dump case illustrates how an indictment grounded in allegations of acquisition, coordinated promotional misrepresentations on social media, induced purchases by followers, and secret sales can satisfy the “scheme” and “intent” elements if pleaded with sufficient specificity.

The practical lesson for investigators and prosecutors is to document the three phases. Proof that defendants acquired sizeable positions before promotional activity, that they disseminated false or misleading claims about the company or their positions, and that they sold into a subsequent price spike supports both the factual narrative and the legal theory. For students analyzing fact patterns, always map factual allegations onto those three phases: (1) what was acquired and when; (2) what statements or activity purported to increase demand and whether those statements were false, misleading, or omitted material facts; (3) when and how did the accused dispose of holdings and realize gains?

## Scalping and undisclosed trading positions

Scalping is a subset of fraudulent conduct commonly associated with touting: an individual touts or praises a security in public fora while simultaneously holding an undisclosed position that will benefit if the price rises. The legal problem is the promoter’s undisclosed conflict of interest. In many contexts—broker‑dealer conduct, analyst conflicts, or statements by registered persons—failing to disclose a material conflict is an actionable omission or misstatement under securities laws. In the social‑media context, an influencer who posts glowing commentary about a stock while concealing that she owns or is buying shares is creating a classic scalping scenario.

Doctrinally, scalping can give rise to liability under multiple theories. Under Section 10(b) and Rule 10b‑5, a material omission—failing to disclose a relationship or position that would significantly alter the total mix of information available to investors—can render otherwise truthful statements misleading. For criminal prosecution, scalping fits comfortably within the fraudulent‑inducement paradigm discussed in Kousisis: an influencer’s grave misstatement or omission induces victims to part with money (by buying stock), enabling the influencer to obtain money or property (the proceeds of the covert sale). The Fifth Circuit’s pump‑and‑dump decision highlights scalping as an integral part of the scheme: promoters allegedly misrepresented their trading positions and thereby induced purchases.

Despite the doctrinal fit, proof problems arise. Defendants may argue they had a legitimate expectation of gain and were providing merely opinion, or that their holdings were de minimis and not material. Conversely, prosecutors and civil plaintiffs seek evidence of contemporaneous trading records (e.g., brokerage statements), communication among promoters, and timing to show that the promoter’s posts were tied to undisclosed buying or selling. The market’s objective indicators—transaction timestamps, volume, and price patterns—are crucial to proving scalping practically; the law requires demonstrating both a material omission and the requisite scienter.

## Misrepresentation vs. manipulation: doctrinal overlaps and distinctions

Legal analysis of misconduct in securities markets can rest on two overlapping frameworks: misrepresentation (untrue statements or omissions) and manipulation (conduct designed to distort prices or trading volume irrespective of specific false statements). Understanding the distinction and overlap is critical for choosing prosecutorial and civil strategies.

Misrepresentation theory (most familiarly Section 10(b)/Rule 10b‑5 claims and analogous provisions of § 1348) focuses on false statements of material fact or omissions that make statements misleading. The central elements are falsity or misleadingness, materiality, scienter (intent or recklessness in civil private actions), reliance (for private damages), causation, and loss. In criminal contexts, the government must show a scheme to defraud and an intent to obtain money or property by false pretenses. The fraudulent‑inducement theory—endorsed recently as valid—captures the case where a materially false statement or omission induces a victim to enter a transaction in which she transfers money or property.

Manipulation theory targets transactional conduct intended to create a false or deceptive appearance of active trading or to artificially affect price, volume, or market conditions. Manipulative devices include wash trades, matched orders, spoofing, and layering. Manipulation can be proved without a specific false statement; the emphasis is on intentional conduct that affects the market mechanism. Criminal statutes and Rule 10b‑5 encompass schemes to manipulate the market as well as misrepresentations, and many prosecutions combine both theories.

In practice, pump‑and‑dump prosecutions often plead both misrepresentation and manipulation theories because the promotive false statements and the coordinated trading are both central to the scheme. The Fifth Circuit opinion treats the social‑media conduct as a “scheme to ‘pump’ the price by posting false and misleading information” (a misrepresentation theory) and then ‘dump’ by secretly selling (a manipulative sales tactic). Ciminelli’s limitation—that the fraud statutes do not criminalize the mere deprivation of valuable economic information—applies primarily to “right‑to‑control” theories that convert a victim’s “right to control” as the property interest. But fraudulent inducement that causes victims to surrender money or property is squarely within the fraud statutes’ protection. The upshot: when drafting charges or civil complaints, pleaders should carefully articulate whether the alleged wrongdoing deprived victims of money/property (fraudulent‑inducement) or relied on conduct distorting market mechanisms (manipulation), and gather evidence accordingly.

## Role of social media, chat rooms, and influencer culture

Social media has transformed how promotional narratives spread. In previous eras, pump‑and‑dump operations depended on mailings and paid advertisements dispersed slowly. Now, an influencer’s tweet, short video, or chat‑room post can reach thousands or millions of potential investors in seconds. That amplification reduces the number of promoters required to achieve a price spike and complicates tracing causation: a post may reverberate across platforms, be copied by bots, and spur purchases by investors who do not directly follow the promoter.

From an enforcement standpoint, social media changes the evidentiary landscape. Positive features for prosecutors include dramatic digital footprints: timestamps on posts, follower counts, records of private messages, screenshots, and platform metadata can establish sequence and connection. Coordinated posting can be revealed through similarities in content or timing: many accounts posting the same claim within a short window is circumstantial evidence of coordination. Conversely, the ubiquity and velocity of social media complicate causation and reliance arguments—many retail investors act on snippets of information, third‑party reposts, or broader market sentiment rather than a single tweet. The defense can argue that purchases were motivated by independent research, third‑party news, or market momentum rather than the defendant’s statements.

Influencer culture raises additional regulatory concerns. Unlike registered brokers or analysts, social‑media influencers often lack formal disclosure obligations but may nonetheless be required under securities laws to avoid fraudulent statements and to disclose conflicts when making investment recommendations. The SEC has increasingly focused on influencer conduct; civil enforcement actions and guidance have called for clearer disclosure of paid promotions and proprietary positions. Criminal enforcement, as shown in the Fifth Circuit case, treats social‑media pump‑and‑dumps as traditional schemes to defraud when the classical elements are present.

For students, the key analytic task is to map the social‑media facts onto the statutory elements: was there a material misstatement or omission, was it disseminated in a way calculated to reach and induce purchases by followers, and did the defendant intend to obtain money or property through that deceit? The prominence of social media makes some evidentiary predicates (posting dates and follower reach) easier to prove, but it also requires careful handling of proof about causation and investor reliance.

## SEC civil enforcement vs. DOJ criminal prosecution

Enforcement for pump‑and‑dump and scalping can proceed along two tracks, often in parallel: the SEC’s civil enforcement authority under the Securities Exchange Act and the Department of Justice’s criminal prosecution authority under statutes such as 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and the mail and wire fraud statutes. Each path has different legal standards, burdens, remedies, and strategic considerations.

The SEC commonly brings civil actions under Section 10(b) and Rule 10b‑5, under Section 17(a) of the Securities Act for offers or sales of securities, or under specific rules addressing market manipulation. In civil cases, the SEC can seek injunctions, disgorgement, and civil monetary penalties, and it can obtain equitable relief such as trading bars. Although private damages actions under Section 10(b) typically require proof of scienter (intent or severe recklessness) as interpreted by the courts, the SEC’s enforcement posture can be more flexible: the Commission can obtain relief by showing statutes or rules have been violated, and settlements often avoid admissions of scienter. The SEC also has regulatory tools—such as administrative proceedings and bar authority—that are unavailable in criminal court.

Criminal prosecution requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt and—especially after Ciminelli and Kousisis—careful pleading to show that a defendant sought to obtain money or property by false pretenses (fraudulent inducement) or engaged in scheme or device to defraud in connection with the purchase or sale of securities (18 U.S.C. § 1348). The DOJ typically pursues criminal charges when the facts show deliberate deception intended to obtain funds or property, large-scale or repeat conduct, or conduct featuring aggravating features (blatant concealment, use of fraud to obtain massive sums, victim vulnerability). The Fifth Circuit opinion illustrates how an indictment alleging acquisition, false social‑media posts, induced purchases, and subsequent sales, together with substantial alleged profits, can survive a facial challenge and proceed to criminal prosecution.

There are strategic interplays. SEC investigations frequently produce evidence (trading records, communications, testimony) that can inform or be shared with criminal prosecutors. Defendants often face parallel enforcement with both agencies; the remedial focus differs (disgorgement and injunctive relief vs. incarceration and criminal fines), and that difference affects defense strategy, settlement dynamics, and collateral consequences (e.g., reputational harm, loss of professional licenses). Students should note that elements and burdens differ: criminal prosecution requires proof of intent to defraud, but Kousisis clarified that proving a desire to obtain money/property—without proving a desire to leave the victim worse off—can be enough.

## Disclosure obligations when touting securities: avoiding half‑truths

Touting a security is not unlawful per se; investors and influencers may offer opinions and predictions. The legal problem arises when statements are materially false, misleading, or omit facts that render them deceptive. The law treats half‑truths—statements that are literally true but omit material information that would make the statement materially misleading—as potentially fraudulent.

A frequent example in the social‑media context is the failure to disclose a beneficial ownership or compensation arrangement: an influencer posts “I just bought X and expect it to spike” without revealing she owns a large hidden position or is being paid to promote the stock. That omission creates the risk that listeners will rely on the statement to make investment decisions, unaware of the speaker’s conflict. Under Rule 10b‑5, an omission can be actionable if disclosure is necessary to make the disclosed information not misleading and if the omitted fact is material. In criminal prosecutions invoking § 1348 or mail/wire fraud analogues, the government pleads that the omission was part of a scheme to defraud and that the defendant intended to obtain money or property through the resulting inducement.

The duty to disclose depends on context. Registered brokers and analysts often have express regulatory obligations to disclose ownership or compensation; unregistered influencers may lack such express text‑book duties but still cannot avoid liability by labeling an invitation to buy as “opinion” or “not investment advice” in a way that masks a material undisclosed conflict. The safe practice—both legally and ethically—is plain disclosure: state clearly whether one has a financial interest, whether the recommendation is paid, and any other fact that would alter the total mix of information.

For lawyers counseling clients in the influencer economy, practical advice includes documenting all positions, avoiding blanket endorsements without disclosing conflicts, and ensuring promotional content contains clear, prominent disclosure of ownership or compensation. In litigation or enforcement, the presence or absence of contemporaneous disclosures is a central factual issue.

## Investor susceptibility and market microstructure in thinly traded stocks

Pump‑and‑dump schemes are especially effective in thinly traded securities. Market microstructure variables make these securities highly responsive to relatively modest buying interest: small orders move price significantly, bid‑ask spreads are wide, and available liquidity is limited. Low public float—few shares available for trading—means that a promoter who buys and then induces others to buy can drastically change supply/demand balance.

Investor susceptibility also matters. Retail investors who follow social media influencers may have limited resources or experience, and they may be prone to “fear of missing out” when they see upward price movement amplified by social signals (likes, re‑posts, testimonials). The mixture of social proof and rapid price movement creates a feedback loop: posts cause buying; buying creates price momentum visible to others; momentum draws more buyers.

From an enforcement perspective, thin markets and susceptible retail buyers provide both motive and means. Prosecutors look for evidence that promoters targeted audiences likely to amplify the pump; economists’ or market experts’ testimony can be used to explain microstructure effects to juries (e.g., how volume changes would anticipate price movement). Defendants may counter that market forces, legitimate news, or other traders—not the alleged misrepresentations—explained price changes. Thus, experts and transactional records become critical to bridge the gap between promotional conduct and market outcomes.

When analyzing hypotheticals, students should think about how microstructure amplifies the causal chain: in a thin market, a single actor’s trades can cause outsized movement, making the link between promotional conduct and subsequent trading outcomes easier to establish. In thicker markets, establishing causation and loss becomes harder.

## Evidentiary signatures of manipulation (volume spikes, coordinated posts, dump timing)

Successful prosecution or civil enforcement of pump‑and‑dump schemes depends on assembling a constellation of circumstantial and direct evidence that, taken together, proves a fraudulent scheme and intent. The following types of evidence commonly appear in enforcement files and are often persuasive to factfinders:

- Transactional timing and pattern evidence. Detailed brokerage records showing purchases by the alleged promoter before promotional activity and sales immediately after the price spike are powerful. The timing of buys relative to posts and of sells relative to peak prices demonstrates the three-phase scheme and supports inference of scienter.

- Volume spikes and price run‑ups. Sharp increases in trading volume and price without corresponding public news or fundamental developments is an indicator that promotional activity and purchases—not new information—drove the move. In thinly traded stocks, small dollar amounts can produce large percentage moves; pattern analysis can distinguish legitimate organic buying from suspicious concentrated buying by accounts connected to promoters.

- Coordinated posts and messaging. Identical or highly similar promotional messages across multiple accounts, or posts clustered in time, signal orchestration. Platform metadata (timestamps, IP addresses, or administrative communications) may corroborate coordination. Evidence of compensation or communications among promoters strengthens the inference that the coordination was intentional and fraudulent.

- Dump timing and realized profits. Evidence that defendants sold shares during or immediately after the artificial price rise and realized substantial profits ties the deceptive conduct to the economic gain that fraud statutes criminalize. The Fifth Circuit opinion emphasized this classic fact pattern: buying, false promotion, induced purchases, and secret sale producing profit.

- Concealment tactics. Use of burner accounts, false identities, deletion of posts, routing trades through straw accounts, or use of private chat platforms to coordinate sales are classic indicia of fraudulent intent. Evidence of efforts to conceal, such as deleting messages or using anonymized accounts, can be admissible to show consciousness of guilt.

- Lack of fundamental catalyst. Expert testimony showing an absence of material company developments that would justify the price movement supports the inference the movement was manufactured. Conversely, a legitimate press release or fundamental improvement can undermine manipulation claims.

- Communications showing intent. Direct evidence—text messages, emails, or chats in which defendants explicitly discuss plans to “pump” the stock and “dump” for profit—is often decisive, though rarely available. When present, it dramatically reduces the need to rely on circumstantial inference.

A prosecutor will typically combine multiple strands of this evidence to meet the beyond‑a‑reasonable‑doubt standard in criminal cases and the preponderance or civil standards in SEC matters. Defendants will attack each link: challenge the timing inferences, contest the materiality of the statements, point to independent market factors, and present alternative explanations for coordinated posts (e.g., organic re‑posting, meme culture).

## Applying the doctrines: indictment sufficiency and the fraudulent‑inducement theory

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the district court is instructive about pleading standards and doctrinal boundaries. An indictment need not prove the case; it must allege each material element of the offense with sufficient detail to (1) inform the defendant of the charges, (2) allow preparation of a defense, and (3) protect against double jeopardy. When the underlying crime is securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, that requires allegations of (a) a scheme to defraud in connection with any security and (b) an intent to defraud. The scheme element often incorporates allegations of material misrepresentations or omissions intended to deceive, and the intent element requires an intent to deceive and to obtain some benefit (such as money or property) through the deceit.

In Ciminelli (the “right‑to‑control” case), the Supreme Court restricted the scope of fraud statutes by holding that they do not reach schemes that merely deprive a victim of valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary decisions. That decision foreclosed prosecutorial reliance on a theory that someone simply sought to deprive another of control over an asset by concealing information about it. But Kousisis clarified the boundaries: a fraudulent‑inducement theory—wherein a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires handing over money or property—is valid. Kousisis also confirmed that the government need not allege the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off; it is sufficient that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property.

Against that doctrinal backdrop, the Fifth Circuit held that an indictment alleging promoters purchased stock, posted false and misleading information on social media to induce followers to buy (thereby surrendering cash or securities), and secretly sold to realize profits adequately alleged a scheme to defraud and intent to obtain money. The emphasis was on the allegation that followers were induced to purchase securities—i.e., to part with money or property—rather than on denying them mere access to information. That pleading satisfies the fraudulent‑inducement theory and avoids the Ciminelli barrier.

For students and practitioners, the practical takeaway is that charging instruments should allege the causal link between false statements and the victim’s surrendering of money or property. Pleadings that merely allege deception without tying it to induced transfers risk dismissal in light of Ciminelli; pleadings that allege induced purchases or transfers fall squarely within Kousisis’s approval of fraudulent inducement. Defense strategies often will attempt to reframe the allegations as merely informational deprivations or to challenge causation and scienter; prosecutors will shore up pleadings with transactional detail, contemporaneous communications, and demonstrable profit windows.

## Conclusion: theory, proof, and evolving enforcement

Pump‑and‑dump and scalping schemes are technologically old crimes dressed in new platforms. The legal framework—fraudulent inducement, misstatement/omission doctrine, and anti‑manipulation rules—remains the controlling law, but doctrinal contours continue to be refined by recent Supreme Court decisions. Ciminelli narrowed theories that rely solely on deprivation of information; Kousisis reaffirmed that fraudulent inducement and schemes to obtain money or property by deception remain criminally and civilly actionable even if victims ultimately receive something of value in return.

In practice, the shift to social media makes certain proof elements easier (timestamped posts, follower metrics, platform metadata) and others harder (establishing direct reliance and causation). Effective enforcement combines rigorous market‑structure analysis, careful digital forensics, and classic transactional tracing. For students, the analytical skills most rewarded are the ability to map facts onto statutory elements, to distinguish theories of liability, and to identify the evidentiary signatures that connect deceptive promotional conduct to transfers of money or other property.

# The Case Study: United States v. Constantinescu et al.

This chapter uses the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Constantinescu et al. as a vehicle to explore how federal securities-fraud prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 are constructed and tested at the indictment stage. The defendants in Constantinescu were social‑media traders alleged to have run a classic “pump‑and‑dump” scheme: they posted misleading statements about securities to induce their followers to buy, causing prices to rise, then secretly sold their holdings at a profit. The district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that the government had alleged only a deprivation of economic information—not a traditional property interest—but the Fifth Circuit reversed, holding that the indictment adequately alleged a fraudulent‑inducement theory and the requisite intent to defraud. The opinion provides a compact lesson on the elements of § 1348 prosecutions, the interplay between the “right‑to‑control” and “fraudulent‑inducement” theories, and the standards for challenging indictments under Rule 12.

## Allegations: false posts about positions and price potential; induced purchases; secret sales

At the factual heart of the indictment were a set of factual allegations tailored to the archetypal pump‑and‑dump. Each defendant maintained large followings on social platforms and publicly portrayed himself as a skilled trader who regularly disclosed his trading activity. The indictment alleges a consistent pattern: defendants would first purchase a target security, then “pump” the price by posting false and misleading information—misstatements about their positions in the security and about the security’s prospects—to induce followers to buy, thereby artificially inflating the market price. Once the price rose, the indictment alleges, defendants would “dump” by secretly selling their holdings for profit.

For purposes of an indictment challenge, those kinds of allegations are significant because they tie the misrepresentations to a concrete economic act by the victims: purchasing securities (transferring money or property). The alleged sequence—acquisition by the schemer, public misrepresentations designed to cause followers to buy, and secret sales by the schemer—maps directly onto the fraudulent‑inducement theory: the misstatement induces a transaction in which the victim parts with money or property. In assessing an indictment’s sufficiency, courts accept such allegations as true and ask whether the indictment alleges the offense’s material elements; thus carefully pleaded chains of conduct like these are often dispositive at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage.

## Charges: § 1349 conspiracy and § 1348 securities fraud, aiding and abetting

The indictment charged the defendants with substantive securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349. The indictment also invoked aiding and abetting liability under 18 U.S.C. § 2 for acts by co‑conspirators. Understanding these statutes—and the overlay of conspiracy and aider/abettor doctrines—is essential.

Section 1348 makes it unlawful to “engage in any fraudulent activity in connection with” securities or to obtain money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with a security. Section 1349 criminalizes conspiracies to commit offenses like § 1348; it requires an agreement and an overt act in furtherance of the conspiratorial plan. Aiding and abetting under § 2 is a theory of derivative liability: a defendant who willfully causes the commission of a crime by another—by intentionally facilitating, encouraging, or assisting—can be convicted even if not the principal actor.

At the indictment stage the government need only allege the elements of these offenses with sufficient factual particularity to inform defendants of the charges and to provide a double jeopardy bar against future prosecutions. For a § 1348 count in a pump‑and‑dump case, the necessary allegations ordinarily include (1) false or misleading statements or omissions (material misrepresentations), (2) in connection with the purchase or sale of a security, (3) made as part of a scheme to defraud and (4) with the requisite scienter—i.e., intent to defraud. Conspiracy counts must allege an agreement and at least one overt act undertaken to effectuate the scheme.

## Procedural posture: Rule 12 dismissal; de novo appellate review

Procedurally, the Constantinescu defendants moved to dismiss the superseding indictment before trial. A pretrial dismissal of an indictment is governed by Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 12(b) (challenges to sufficiency of indictment), and an appellate court reviews such dismissals de novo. When assessing an indictment challenge the court “must take the allegations of the indictment as true” and determine whether the allegations, so taken, allege every material element of the charged offense. The Fifth Circuit reiterated the well‑established tripartite function of an indictment: it must (1) set out each prima facie element of the charged offense, (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges, and (3) provide a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. Those standards are drawn from the court’s prior precedents—Gaytan, Guzman‑Ocampo, Fontenot—and shape the analysis at the pleading stage.

It is critical to appreciate that sufficiency review is not a mini‑trial. The court does not weigh evidence, nor does it resolve factual disputes. If the indictment, construed in the light most favorable to the government, alleges the elements, dismissal is inappropriate; conversely, if the indictment fails to allege an essential element, dismissal is proper.

## District court’s rationale: deprivation of information vs. property

The district court granted the defendants’ Rule 12 motion, concluding that the indictment alleged only a deprivation of valuable economic information rather than a deprivation of a property interest protected by the federal fraud statutes. Its analysis invoked Ciminelli v. United States, where the Supreme Court held that the federal wire‑fraud statute could not be used to punish a scheme that sought merely to deprive a public entity of the “right to control” information necessary to make discretionary decisions; the Court emphasized that the property protected by the fraud statutes must be a traditional property interest, and that the “right to valuable economic information” is not such an interest.

Applying that principle, the district court viewed the government’s allegations as a variant on the rejected right‑to‑control theory: defendants had, the court concluded, sought to control the flow of information to their followers rather than to obtain their followers’ money or property. Under that view, the asserted harm was informational—followers were deprived of truthful information they might have used in choosing whether to trade—so the indictment did not state a § 1348 offense. This was the pivotal legal error the Fifth Circuit addressed on appeal.

## Issues on appeal: scheme to defraud and intent to defraud sufficiency

Two legal issues framed the appeal: first, whether the indictment adequately alleged a “scheme to defraud” as required by § 1348, given the limitations imposed by Ciminelli; and second, whether the indictment alleged the requisite “intent to defraud.” Both questions turn on how to distinguish the disallowed “right‑to‑control” theory from permissible fraudulent‑inducement theories and on the meaning of “obtaining” money or property under the statute.

On the first issue, the government advanced what the Supreme Court has characterized as the “fraudulent‑inducement theory”: a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into entering a transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. The key difference from the right‑to‑control theory is that the fraudulent‑inducement theory targets the victim’s transfer of money or property as the object of the fraud, rather than a defendant’s control of information needed for the victim’s discretionary judgments. The Supreme Court’s subsequent decision in Kousisis v. United States reinforced that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is a valid basis for conviction and is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli.

On the second issue—intent—the defendants argued the indictment showed only that they sought to profit, not that they intended to injure followers financially. The question thus became whether § 1348 (as interpreted in light of Kousisis) requires proof that the fraudster intended the victim to suffer a net monetary loss. Kousisis answered that question: the fraud statutes do not require that the defendant have sought to leave the victim worse off on the bottom line. It is sufficient that the defendant intended to obtain the victim’s money or property by deception; whether the victim in fact suffers a net loss, or whether the victim receives market‑value consideration, does not negate the existence of a fraud if the deceit induced the transfer.

## Legal doctrine applied: scheme to defraud; fraudulent‑inducement vs. right‑to‑control; intent

Constantinescu clarifies how the doctrinal contours settled in Ciminelli and Kousisis play out in practice. Three doctrinal points are especially important for students and practitioners.

First, “scheme to defraud” requires an affirmative allegation of deceptive conduct—a material misrepresentation or omission—intended to deceive in order to obtain something of value. The Fifth Circuit relied on its precedent that treatment of § 1348 often borrows concepts from mail/wire fraud jurisprudence. Thus, the indictment must allege both materially deceptive conduct and that the deception aimed to obtain value.

Second, courts distinguish between (a) a right‑to‑control theory that seeks to criminalize the manipulation of information flow (which Ciminelli rejected because the “right to valuable economic information” is not a traditional property interest) and (b) the fraudulent‑inducement theory that makes the transfer of money or property the object of the fraud. If an indictment alleges that deceptive statements induced victims to transfer money or property (for example, by purchasing securities), it invokes the latter theory and therefore falls outside Ciminelli’s proscription. Constantinescu’s indictment, by alleging induced purchases that transferred money/property to effectors of the scheme, was thus a fraudulent‑inducement case.

Third, “intent to defraud” under § 1348 requires an intent to deceive and an intent that some harm result from the deceit. But Kousisis clarified that the “harm” element does not require the defendant to intend the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss; it suffices that the defendant intended to obtain the victim’s money or property through deception. Consequently, a defendant who induces a victim to enter a transaction by false statements—even if the defendant provides something of ostensibly equal value in return—can be guilty if the object or purpose of the deceit was to obtain the victim’s money or property. The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu relied on this reasoning to reject the defendants’ contention that their desire to profit, rather than to injure followers, negated intent to defraud.

Together these points yield a practical rule for pleading: if the government sufficiently alleges that deceptive communications caused targeted investors to part with money or property, and that the deception was designed to obtain that money or property, the indictment will ordinarily survive a motion to dismiss under § 1348 even if the investors later received securities at market value or otherwise were not left economically worse off.

## Holding: indictment states an offense; reversal and remand

Applying the foregoing principles to the factual allegations, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment adequately alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. The court emphasized that the indictment did not rest on the right‑to‑control theory; instead it pleaded a fraudulent‑inducement theory—defendants induced their followers to purchase securities through materially false and misleading posts and then sold the securities secretly for profit. Those allegations tied the deceptive conduct to the followers’ transfers of money/property and to the defendants’ object of obtaining money. The court further relied on Kousisis to hold that the government need not allege that the defendants intended their followers to suffer a net pecuniary loss. Because the indictment, read as a whole, sufficiently alleged each material element of the charged offenses, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal and remanded for further proceedings.

The decision thus provides a textbook example of when a pump‑and‑dump indictment will survive a Rule 12 challenge: there must be allegations that the misrepresentations were material, were intended to deceive, and were employed to induce victims to transfer money or property in a securities transaction.

## Concessions: defendants concede deception; dispute centers on property and intent

Notable in the briefing and opinion is the parties’ narrow focus. The defendants expressly conceded that the indictment alleged deceptive conduct—that is, material misrepresentations were pleaded. The contested questions, therefore, were limited to whether the alleged scheme sought to obtain a protected property interest and whether the defendants possessed the requisite intent to defraud. This narrow framing is helpful for doctrinal analysis because it isolates the legal consequences of Ciminelli and Kousisis: once deception is conceded, the question becomes whether the deception is alleged to have been used to induce transfers of money or property (fraudulent inducement) rather than to mislead decision‑makers by controlling information (right‑to‑control). It also directs attention to the mens rea question (intent to obtain money or property), which the Fifth Circuit resolved in the government’s favor under Kousisis.

For practitioners, the lesson is practical: when defending a § 1348 indictment that alleges deceitful communications, focus your Rule 12 attack on whether the indictment links the deception to an alleged transfer of money or property and whether the indictment pleads intent to obtain that money or property. Conversely, prosecutors should ensure indictments explicitly allege the causal link between deception and transfers and allege that the object of the defendant’s misconduct was to obtain money or property.

## Applying the lessons: drafting, challenging, and trying pump‑and‑dump cases

Several practical implications flow from Constantinescu.

- Drafting indictments: In a pump‑and‑dump context, a successful indictment should include concrete allegations of (1) the defendant’s misrepresentations (with times and general content), (2) the defendant’s own trading activity (purchases before the posts, secret sales after the posts), (3) the contemporaneous reactions of followers (increased purchases or volume), and (4) the defendant’s pecuniary motive or the proceeds realized. These allegations show that deception was used to induce transfers of money or property and that obtaining money was the object of the scheme—two points that defeat a Ciminelli‑style defense.

- Pretrial challenges: When moving to dismiss, defense counsel should stress any insufficiency in tying misstatements to an induced transfer of money or property, and argue (if possible) that the government’s theory is really about manipulating information, not obtaining property. After Kousisis, however, arguing that the defendant did not intend to leave victims worse off will not, by itself, make the indictment deficient; courts now focus on the object of the scheme (obtaining money/property), not on net loss.

- Trial strategy: At trial, provenance of intent remains central. The government will try to show through circumstantial evidence—timing of purchases and sales, patterns of posting, communication among co‑conspirators, and realization of profits—that defendants intended to induce purchases to enable profitable sales. Defense proof that posts were opinions or predictions rather than knowingly false factual statements may be decisive; materiality and scienter remain fact issues.

- Aiding & abetting and conspiracy: Because the government often cannot prove who sold what to whom at the individual level in complex online markets, conspiracy and aiding‑and‑abetting counts allow prosecutors to pursue liability for coordinated conduct. Indictments should thus allege agreement and overt acts for conspiratorial liability and specific acts of assistance or facilitation for § 2 liability.

## Conclusion: doctrinal summary and teaching points

United States v. Constantinescu et al. exemplifies how modern securities‑fraud law accommodates deceptive conduct in digital markets within the constraints set by the Supreme Court. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal teaches several clear rules: an indictment that alleges deceptive communications used to induce victims to transfer money or property states a scheme to defraud under § 1348; Ciminelli does not bar prosecutions premised on fraudulent inducement; and Kousisis makes clear that the government need not prove an intent to cause a victim’s net pecuniary loss—only an intent to obtain money or property by deception.

For law students, the opinion is a useful study in statutory interpretation (what does “property” mean in the fraud statutes?), mens rea (what does intent to defraud require?), the relationship between Supreme Court precedent and circuit practice, and the mechanics of indictment‑stage review. For practitioners, it is a reminder to plead the causal link between deceit and transfers carefully, to plead intent with illustrative facts, and to tailor Rule 12 arguments to distinctions that remain open after Ciminelli and Kousisis.

# Applying the Elements: What a § 1348 Indictment Must Allege

This chapter uses the Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu (the Constantinescu opinion) to illustrate how prosecutors may (and must) plead a securities‑fraud charge under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, and how defense counsel should evaluate whether an indictment states the elements. The opinion is a useful teaching vehicle because it compresses recent doctrinal developments—most notably the Supreme Court’s limitation of the “right‑to‑control” theory in Ciminelli and its clarification of the fraudulent‑inducement theory in Kousisis—into an application of classic mail/wire‑fraud concepts to a modern “pump and dump” scheme conducted over social media.

An indictment under § 1348 must do the familiar three things: plead each material element of the offense, fairly inform the defendant of the charge, and protect against double jeopardy. Those requirements, applied in the Fifth Circuit, mean the indictment must set out a reasonably detailed narrative of the alleged scheme, identify the defendant’s false or misleading statements or omissions, allege the victims and the way they were induced to part with money or property, and include sufficient allegations of criminal intent. The Constantinescu indictment—alleging that social‑media influencers purchased securities, “pumped” prices by false posts, and “dumped” shares in secret—illustrates what a sufficient charging narrative looks like and shows how prosecutors can frame allegations to avoid the pitfalls identified in Ciminelli.

## Alleging a scheme: coordinated conduct and the narrative of fraud

A scheme to defraud is not a single misstatement standing in isolation; it is a plan or course of conduct designed to mislead and obtain money or property by deception. In practice, an indictment should narrate how multiple acts—purchases, posts, trades, concealment, and the timing of sales—fit together to achieve the fraudulent object.

The Constantinescu indictment does this by describing a sequence: defendants bought target securities, posted false and misleading content to generate buying interest among their followers, thereby inflating prices, and then sold their holdings at a profit. That kind of chronology matters at the pleading stage because courts evaluate whether the allegations, taken as true, describe coherent, coordinated conduct directed at the victims’ inducement and the perpetrators’ gain. The Fifth Circuit stressed that courts must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and determine whether those allegations, not the evidence, state the offense.

When drafting or evaluating a charging narrative, prosecutors should include: who participated and what role each defendant played; the temporal sequence of relevant conduct; the means of dissemination (platforms, accounts); the content and timing of statements in relation to purchases and sales; and specific overt acts showing concealment or deception (e.g., secret sales, use of aliases, or coordinated timing). Such detail supports the inference that the conduct was more than mere churning or market commentary—it was a scheme targeted at inducing transactions.

For the defense, the lesson is to look for gaps in that narrative. Does the indictment connect the alleged misstatements to actual trades by alleged victims? Does it allege contemporaneous sales by the defendants that plausibly achieved the claimed object (financial gain)? If the narrative lacks a link between misleading conduct and the defendants’ obtaining money or property, it risks collapsing into the forbidden “right‑to‑control” theory condemned in Ciminelli.

## Material misrepresentations: positions, timing, and price claims

Materiality is central in fraud—only misrepresentations that a reasonable investor would consider important to his decision to buy or sell a security qualify. In a § 1348 context, the indictment should identify the alleged misstatements with enough specificity to show they were both false (or misleading by omission) and material.

Constantinescu typifies the kinds of misrepresentations that support a fraud charge in a pump‑and‑dump: false assertions about personal trading positions (“I’m buying X at $Y”), misstatements about imminent events that will materially raise price, or deceptive price‑target claims presented as fact rather than opinion. Distinguishing puffery and protected opinion from actionable factual misstatements is therefore critical at the pleading stage. An indictment should allege that the challenged statements were presented as statements of present fact or concrete expectation, not mere subjective opinion. Where defendants held themselves out as expert traders and repeatedly posted “trade updates,” an indictment can plausibly allege that such representations carried the implied assertion of current holdings and intentions—representations that are capable of being true or false and thus material.

Timing allegations make materiality and intent more probative. Pleading that a defendant posted bullish statements immediately after acquiring a position and sold shortly after the market moved up supports the inference that the statements were used to manipulate price. Similarly, allegations that defendants failed to disclose conflicts of interest—e.g., that they had undisclosed short positions or were being paid by the security’s issuer—can transform omissions into material misrepresentations when disclosure was necessary to make other statements not misleading.

Practical drafting guidance: identify the exact language alleged to be false or misleading (or summarize with illustrative examples); state when and where it was posted; explain why an investor would find it important; and, if possible, connect that content to contemporaneous trading activity.

## Property interest: victims parting with money to purchase securities

Ciminelli held that the federal fraud statutes protect “traditional” property interests and that deprivation of valuable economic information alone—a “right‑to‑control” theory—is not a protected property interest. The Constantinescu opinion shows how to avoid that defect: allege that the defendant’s misrepresentations induced victims to part with money (or other property) in purchasing securities. That is the classic fraudulent‑inducement theory: the victim conveyed money or property in exchange for the security because of the defendant’s deceit.

An indictment that alleges victims bought securities as a direct result of the defendant’s misrepresentations states a property deprivation. The victims’ money used to purchase securities is a traditional property interest; their loss of that money (or its transfer to the market and ultimately to the defendant) is the prototypical fraud victimization. In Constantinescu, the government alleged that followers were “induced to purchase the security” by false posts—an allegation that puts money squarely at issue, not merely information.

At the pleading stage, it is therefore important to allege (1) that the victims were actual or prospective purchasers, (2) that the misrepresentations were causally linked to their decision to buy, and (3) that the defendant obtained proceeds (directly or indirectly) as a result. Quantifying gains, alleging specific trades by victims, or showing transfers into accounts controlled by defendants strengthens the allegation that money or property was obtained.

## Fraudulent inducement vs. right‑to‑control in charging language

The Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli rejected the right‑to‑control theory as a basis for fraud convictions because the “right to valuable economic information” is not a traditional property interest protected by the statutes. But Kousisis made clear that the fraudulent‑inducement theory—obtaining money or property by inducing a contractual or transactional exchange through misrepresentation—remains valid and is not a disguised right‑to‑control claim.

The practical difference at the pleading stage is simple but critical. A right‑to‑control charge alleges that the defendant deprived the victim of information needed to exercise discretion over assets; it does not allege that the victim parted with money. A fraudulent‑inducement charge alleges that the defendant’s false statements caused the victim to enter a purchase or sale transaction and therefore to hand over money or property. The Constantinescu indictment is careful to plead fraudulent inducement: it specifies that followers purchased securities as a result of defendants’ false posts. It does not rely on asserting that the victims were deprived of information needed to control their preexisting assets.

Drafters must avoid language suggesting the offense is simply the manipulation of informational flow (e.g., “deprived the public of truthful information about X”). Instead, the indictment should anchor the deceit in a transaction in which the victim conveyed money or property as part of the market exchange.

## Intent to defraud: profit motive as intent to obtain victims’ money

The mental‑state element of § 1348 requires intent to defraud. The courts commonly break that down into an intent to deceive and an intent that the deception cause some harm or deprivation. After Kousisis, the indictment need not allege that the defendant sought to make the victim economically worse off; it is sufficient to plead that the defendant intended to obtain the victim’s money or property. Put plainly, intending to profit from a deception directed at inducing trades is sufficient evidence of intent to defraud.

Constantinescu demonstrates this clearly. The indictment alleged that defendants posted misleading content so “other investors were induced to purchase the security” and that defendants secretly sold their holdings for substantial profits ($114 million alleged). Those allegations support the inference that defendants had “money in mind” and intended to obtain it by deception. The Supreme Court in Kousisis emphasized that providing something of value to the victim in return does not negate the fraud if the defendant’s object was to obtain money or property through the deceit.

At the pleading stage, prosecutorial allegations of intent are typically circumstantial: timing of posts and sales, internal communications revealing deceptive tactics, patterns of repeated behavior, and the scale of profits all support an intent inference. Indictments should therefore include factual allegations pointing to those inferences—limits on the period during which defendants sold after posting, use of hidden accounts, payment records, or coordinated conduct among participants.

Defense counsel should look for alternative, innocent explanations offered by the indictment itself (e.g., an alleged post that closely matches contemporaneous trading commentary or a small, unilateral sale inconsistent with a dump). But courts evaluate intent allegations on the face of the indictment by accepting them as true; thus an indictment that plausibly alleges deceptive intent will survive a facial sufficiency challenge.

## ‘In connection with’ the purchase or sale: nexus via inducement to trade

Section 1348 criminalizes fraud “in connection with” a purchase or sale of a security. The “in connection with” requirement is met when the fraud has at least some relationship to a securities transaction—that is, when the deceptive conduct influences, or is intended to influence, the purchase or sale. In other words, the misrepresentation need not be embedded in a formal contract; it need only have played a role in causing the trade.

The Constantinescu indictment satisfies the nexus by alleging that defendants’ posts were designed to induce followers to purchase the securities at issue. When an indictment alleges that victims bought securities in reliance on misrepresentations, the “in connection with” element is plainly met: the deception prompted the purchase and therefore had a direct nexus to a securities transaction.

Pleading practice: make the nexus explicit. Allege that each misrepresentation was made with the intent to induce purchasers (or sellers), and, where possible, identify particular trades that occurred after specific posts or communications. Allegations that the misrepresentations targeted a class of followers or prospective investors who then engaged in market trades are sufficient; prosecutors need not show every individual trade was caused by the misrepresentation, only that the scheme was linked to purchase or sale activity.

## Damages not required: victims’ receipt of market‑value securities is not a defense

A recurring defense argument in pump‑and‑dump cases is that victims were not harmed because they received the securities they paid for, or because the market ultimately traded at efficient prices. Ciminelli posed a different but related concern—avoiding convictions based solely on informational control—but Kousisis clarified that the fraud statutes do not require that the victim be left economically worse off for liability to attach under the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

The Constantinescu court therefore rejected the idea that it matters if victims received market‑value securities or obtained some value in return. The crime is complete when a defendant uses materially false pretenses to induce a victim to part with money or property—even if the defendant provided something in return that had value. The proper inquiry is whether the defendant intended to obtain money or property by deception and whether the deception induced the transfer.

Practically, prosecutors should anticipate and rebut the “no net loss” defense by focusing on the inducement and the defendant’s object, not on the victim’s ultimate economic position. Pleadings that allege clear causal links between misrepresentations and purchases, together with defendants’ secret sales that realized the inflated prices, demonstrate the classic pump‑and‑dump harm regardless of complex, ex post market movements.

## Use of profits and trading records to show the object was money or property

Evidence of realized profits and contemporaneous trading records is highly probative at both the pleading and proof stages because it supplies a tangible link between the fraud and the property obtained. In Constantinescu, the allegation of $114 million in profits performs that role in the indictment: it alleges the object of the scheme and the fruits obtained by defendants.

At the pleading stage, the indictment should, where possible, allege representative trading sequences (for example, dates of purchases by defendants, subsequent posts, dates and sizes of sales, and the financial gains realized). Allegations that defendants used shell entities, concealed transfers of proceeds, or coordinated sales among multiple accounts further support the inference that the predicate misrepresentations were deployed with the object of obtaining money or property.

For the defense, close scrutiny of trading allegations can reveal weaknesses. Are the alleged profits tied to particular false statements, or are they aggregate figures pooled over long periods? Does the indictment allege contemporaneous sales sufficient to constitute a “dump,” or does it rely on conjecture about profit taking? Defense counsel may move to dismiss if the indictment fails to allege a plausible nexus between alleged misstatements and concrete transfers to the defendant.

Concluding practical checklist

Taken together, Constantinescu teaches how to plead—and how to evaluate—a § 1348 indictment in a pump‑and‑dump context. At a minimum, an indictment should:

- Describe the scheme as a coordinated course of conduct: who did what, when, where, and how.
- Identify specific false or misleading statements (or actionable omissions), with enough detail to show materiality (e.g., alleged positions, price targets, or claims of impending events).
- Allege that victims were induced to purchase (or sell) securities—that is, that they parted with money or property as a result.
- Frame the theory as fraudulent inducement—not as the deprivation of informational control.
- Include facts supporting intent to obtain money or property (timing of trades, secret sales, profit figures, use of false identities, coordination among actors).
- Show the nexus “in connection with” purchase or sale by alleging inducement or influence over trading decisions.
- Anticipate the “no damages” defense by pleading the fraudulent inducement itself and the defendant’s object to obtain money or property.
- Where available, allege specific trading records and profit amounts to tie the scheme to tangible property gains.

Constantinescu confirms that modern, social‑media‑facilitated schemes can be charged under § 1348 when the indictment pleads that the defendant used material misrepresentations to induce market transactions that transferred money or property. The decision also illustrates how careful drafting—attending to causal nexus, materiality, and the object of the fraud—avoids the pitfalls identified in Ciminelli and leverages Kousisis’s doctrinal clarification about fraudulent inducement. For prosecutors, this is a blueprint for robust pleading; for defense counsel, it highlights the precise vulnerabilities to test by motion and at trial.

# Pleading and Pretrial Practice: Sufficiency of the Indictment

An indictment is the defendant’s first classroom: it defines the charges, frames the issues for pretrial litigation, and—if properly drafted—protects the accused from surprise or future prosecutions for the same conduct. Federal courts have long required that an indictment do more than recite a statutory label; it must state an offense with sufficient particularity to inform the defendant of the charged crime, enable preparation of a defense, and secure double jeopardy protection. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Constantinescu (and companion authority discussed below) illustrates how these longstanding principles apply in complex fraud prosecutions, including those involving modern platforms such as social media and classic schemes like “pump and dump.” This chapter uses that opinion to teach the controlling pleading rules, the scope of pretrial challenges under Rule 12, and practical drafting choices prosecutors and defense counsel should understand.

## Rule 7(c)(1) requirements: a plain, concise, and definite statement

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 7(c)(1) requires that an indictment be “a plain, concise, and definite written statement of the essential facts constituting the offense charged.” That deceptively simple command has three principal goals: (1) to provide the defendant fair notice of the charges so she can prepare a defense; (2) to identify the essential legal elements of the offense so that the accused and the court know what the prosecution must prove; and (3) to afford protection against future prosecutions for the same conduct.

Practical application of Rule 7(c)(1) balances two competing concerns. On the one hand, the indictment must allege the material elements of the offense with sufficient factual grounding—naming the statute, time frame, parties, and the core criminal conduct alleged. On the other hand, courts do not require the government to plead its entire evidentiary case. The indictment is not a bill of particulars or a substitute for discovery. As the Fifth Circuit reiterated in Constantinescu, the “indictment must allege each material element of the offense,” but it need not include evidentiary detail beyond what is necessary for notice.

When prosecutors comply with Rule 7(c)(1), they typically identify (a) the statutory provisions charged, (b) the acts or omissions constituting the offense, (c) the time period, and (d) the victims and objects of the scheme where relevant. In fraud prosecutions, a well-drafted count will plainly allege the scheme (what misrepresentations or deceptive practices were used), the victims (who were targeted), and the object of the scheme (what money or property the defendant sought to obtain). The Constantinescu indictment, as described by the Fifth Circuit, exemplifies this approach: it identified the “pump and dump” scheme, the false social-media statements, and the alleged object—profits obtained when followers were induced to purchase securities.

## The Gaytan test: elements, fair notice, and double jeopardy protection

Fifth Circuit precedent applies a tripartite test to assess an indictment’s sufficiency: (1) Does the indictment enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense? (2) Does it fairly inform the defendant of the charges against him (notice)? and (3) Does it provide a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions? United States v. Gaytan synthesizes these concerns into a workable rule: allege the elements; give fair notice; and lock in the contours of the charge.

First, enumerating the elements requires the indictment to allege the controlling legal components of the offense. In the securities-fraud context (18 U.S.C. § 1348), courts treat the statute’s elements analogously to mail and wire fraud: the existence of a scheme to defraud and a fraudulent intent. The “scheme” element, in turn, typically requires an allegation of a material misrepresentation or deceptive practice intended to deceive. The “intent” element requires allegations that the defendant acted with the purpose to deceive and to obtain money or property (or otherwise cause harm flowing from the deception).

Second, fair notice demands that a defendant be able to understand from the face of the indictment what conduct the government intends to prove. That does not mean the indictment must include minutiae (e.g., trial exhibit lists), but it must be specific enough to eliminate unfair surprise. The Gaytan test thus rejects hyper-technical sufficiency requirements that would permit the government to rest on bald statutory recitations.

Third, double jeopardy protection is a structural constraint on the indictment’s scope: the indictment should be sufficiently specific so that the accused is not later prosecuted for a different offense arising out of the same facts without clear notice. That requires careful pleading of counts and elements so the statute of conviction and the factual allegations are aligned.

In practice, attorneys and courts apply the Gaytan factors by reading the indictment as a whole. If the government has alleged the elements plainly, identified the targets and objects, and described the means of the fraud sufficiently to prevent surprise, the indictment will satisfy Rule 7(c)(1) under Gaytan even if it leaves details for trial and discovery.

## Taking allegations as true: no evidentiary weighing at the motion stage

A foundational principle in pretrial motions to dismiss for failure to state an offense is that courts must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and decide whether those allegations, so taken, state an offense. The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu reiterated that rule: on a sufficiency challenge the court is not to weigh evidence, assess credibility, or resolve factual disputes. That limitation flows both from the text of Rule 12 and from separation-of-powers and procedural fairness concerns: a pretrial dismissal for factual insufficiency would require the court to invade the jury’s role.

This “assume-the-truth” rule has several concrete consequences. First, proffers of conflicting evidence are generally inappropriate at the Rule 12 stage; the government need only plausibly allege the elements. Second, factual predicates that would be contested at trial—such as whether a social-media post was materially false or whether a follower actually purchased securities in reliance—are not decided on a Rule 12 motion unless the indictment fails to allege the necessary facts even when credited. Third, the indictment must contain more than mere legal conclusions; courts will reject counts that recite the statutory language alone without factual support. The balance is that factual allegations are credited, but legal conclusions must be linked to facts.

The Constantinescu decision shows the operation of this principle. The district court dismissed the indictment based on an interpretive legal conclusion (that the alleged deprivation was only of “economic information” and thus outside the scope of the fraud statute). The Fifth Circuit reversed because the indictment, taken as true, alleged that followers were induced to purchase securities—i.e., to part with money or property—satisfying the statutory object of the fraud. The court did not probe the sufficiency of proof that followers actually bought securities; it asked whether the indictment’s allegations, if true, would state an offense.

## Enumerating material elements vs. evidentiary detail

Law students often conflate “material elements” with “proof of every detail.” The law distinguishes the two. Material elements are the legal components the government must prove to secure a conviction (e.g., scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and nexus to a security under § 1348). Evidentiary detail consists of facts showing how those elements will be proven (e.g., the full text of each social-media post, timestamps of trades, or witness testimony). An indictment must allege the elements and factual bases that put the defendant on notice, but it need not preview the government’s full evidentiary playbook.

For fraud cases, courts expect the indictment to identify: (1) the fraudulent scheme in general terms, including the deceptive misrepresentations or omissions; (2) the who/when/where of the scheme in sufficient breadth (meaning the actors, the time period, and the affected securities or transactions); and (3) the object—what the defendant sought to obtain (money or property) and from whom. The indictment in Constantinescu did precisely that: it alleged a “pump and dump” scheme, described the use of social-media posts to “pump,” and alleged that followers were induced to purchase securities and the defendants “dumped” their holdings to realize profits. Those allegations provide defendants notice of the legal theory and the nature of the proof required, without listing every instance of fraud the government intends to introduce.

This distinction also governs motions for a bill of particulars. When an indictment is sufficiently specific about the elements, a defendant seeking particulars must show how the indictment is so vague that it prevents preparation of a defense. Courts will not use the bill of particulars to force the government to disclose all evidentiary specifics; rather, they will require particulars only where the indictment’s generality would cause unfair surprise.

## Common pitfalls: overreliance on economic-information theories post-Ciminelli

The Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli reshaped the landscape for right-to-control theories of fraud. Ciminelli held that federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests; they do not criminalize the mere deprivation of potentially valuable economic information. That ruling rejected prosecutions relying solely on a “right-to-control” theory—i.e., alleging that the defendant deprived a victim of the ability to make an informed economic decision without showing the defendant obtained money or property.

A common pitfall for prosecutors after Ciminelli is to draft an indictment that only alleges the victim was deprived of economic information (for example, by a lobbyist’s failure to disclose relationships or by withholding knowledge). Such an indictment risks dismissal because, as the Supreme Court explained, economic information standing alone is not a traditional property interest protected by the fraud statutes.

Constantinescu demonstrates the appropriate remedial approach: when the conduct involves inducing victims to act with their money or property—such as urging followers to buy shares—plead the fraudulent-inducement theory explicitly. The fraudulent-inducement theory differs from right-to-control because it alleges the defendant used misrepresentations to cause the victim to transfer money or property (or to enter a contract that transfers money or property). The Supreme Court later confirmed (in Kousisis) that the fraudulent-inducement theory stands even if the defendant did not seek to leave the victim worse off overall. The critical focus for pleading is therefore the alleged transfer of money or property as the object of the deception.

Defense counsel should be alert to government indictments that emphasize omission of economic information without alleging that the victim parted with money or property. If the indictment rests solely on the right-to-control theory, a Rule 12 motion to dismiss may succeed. After Ciminelli and Kousisis, prosecutors must take care to allege the fraud’s property-object component when that is the government’s theory.

## Strategic drafting: focusing on money-or-property and fraudulent inducement

The most effective fraud indictments post-Ciminelli identify the property interest sought and frame the scheme as fraudulent inducement when appropriate. Practically, that means alleging the defendant knowingly made material misrepresentations or omissions with the intent to induce victims to transfer money or property (or enter contracts effecting such transfers).

Key drafting techniques include:

- Explicitly identify the object of the scheme. Rather than describing only deceptive conduct, plead that the scheme’s object was to obtain money or property—profits from sales, proceeds of trades, or contractual payments—from named victims or classes of victims (e.g., “followers,” “investors,” or “the public”).
- Plead the mechanism of inducement. Describe how misrepresentations were used to induce the transfer (e.g., social-media posts touting a stock’s prospects, false claims about the speaker’s positions, or fabricated trading results designed to prompt purchases).
- Allege who was deceived and how they were deceived. While the indictment need not list every victim, identifying the category of victims (retail investors, subscribers, a city agency) and the nature of the misrepresentations gives defendants notice and anchors the charge to concrete actors.
- Include temporal and platform identifiers. For conduct tied to communications technology, naming the platform(s), approximate dates, and the sequence (buy, pump via posts, sell) helps show the scheme’s structure.
- Quantify when reasonable. Alleging the amount defendant allegedly obtained—profits, receipts, or the aggregate benefit—adds specificity and supports venue, sentencing, and restitution considerations.
- Consider pleading alternative theories. If there is a plausible fraudulent-inducement theory and an aiding-and-abetting or conspiracy theory, set those out. Pleading alternative statutory bases preserves prosecutorial options without prejudicing the defendant, but avoid pleading inconsistent charges that would violate notice or double jeopardy protections.

Constantinescu exemplified many of these drafting choices: the indictment alleged a “pump and dump” method, named the platforms and the pattern of conduct, identified the victims (followers/investors), and alleged the object (profits realized by selling during inflated prices). Those allegations sufficed to frame the fraud as a property-deprivation offense rather than a mere withholding of economic information.

## Appellate review: de novo standard; harmless error considerations

Challenges to an indictment’s legal sufficiency are reviewed de novo on appeal. The appellate court asks whether, taking the indictment’s allegations as true, it states an offense. The Fifth Circuit stated this rule expressly in Constantinescu and, like other circuits, applies it uniformly to Rule 12 dismissals that raise pure legal questions.

What follows from de novo review is that factual controversies are generally not entertained on appeal where the district court’s dismissal would have required weighing evidence. If the lower court dismissed an indictment for legal error—such as misinterpreting the statute or applying inapplicable precedent—appellate reversal is likely when the indictment’s allegations, so read, state the elements.

Harmless error doctrine has limited application in the indictment-sufficiency context. If an indictment omits an essential element, dismissal is not a matter of harmless error because the defendant’s constitutional and statutory protection requires the government to allege and prove all elements. Conversely, if the dismissal was based on a non-jurisdictional procedural error that does not undermine the defendant’s notice or the elements alleged, reversal may be unnecessary. In practice, however, appellate courts treat dismissal for failure to state an offense as an error that affects the merits of the prosecution, and reversible error is common where the indictment, properly read, states the offense (as in Constantinescu).

One practical appellate point: district courts sometimes dismiss a count sua sponte or based on a novel reading of Supreme Court precedent (e.g., Ciminelli) without permitting the government to amend. Because the indictment stage is not the opportunity for evidentiary resolution, appellate courts will often give the government the chance to amend or reallege if the dismissal is based on curable pleading defects. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal in Constantinescu remanded for further proceedings rather than foreclosing amendment.

## Conspiracy counts (§ 1349): pleading agreement and object

Conspiracy counts require special attention. Section 1349 criminalizes conspiracy to commit securities fraud; conspiracy is a distinct offense with its own elements. The core elements of conspiracy are an agreement between two or more persons to commit an unlawful act (or to commit a lawful act by unlawful means) and the defendant’s knowing participation in that agreement with the intent to further its object.

An indictment charging conspiracy under § 1349 should therefore allege (1) the existence of an agreement to commit securities fraud, (2) the identity or description of the conspirators (or at least their roles), (3) the object(s) of the conspiracy (e.g., the underlying securities-fraud scheme), and (4) the defendant’s knowing participation and intent. While some conspiracy statutes (such as 18 U.S.C. § 371) require allegation of an overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy, § 1349 is an agreement offense tied to § 1348, and circuit practice varies regarding overt-act allegations. Pleading one or more overt acts—purchases, posts, trades, transfers of proceeds—provides additional specificity and avoids disputes about the sufficiency of the agreement allegation.

Practical drafting suggestions for conspiracy counts include:

- Plead the common plan or purpose. Describe the shared objective (e.g., to pump share prices by issuing false statements and to sell shares for profit).
- Identify the means of the agreement. While the full communications need not be pleaded, specify the means by which conspirators coordinated (direct communications, joint postings, or use of a platform).
- Allege at least some concrete conduct taken in furtherance of the conspiracy, especially where the statute or local practice expects overt-act allegations. Even if an overt act is not strictly required, including such acts strengthens the indictment and provides fair notice.
- Distinguish between defendants’ roles. Indicate who made public statements, who purchased shares initially, who sold shares at inflated prices, and who profited; these allegations will matter for aiding-and-abetting, culpability, and sentencing analyses.

The Constantinescu indictment alleged both substantive securities-fraud counts under § 1348 and a conspiracy count under § 1349; the Fifth Circuit’s decision to reinstate the indictment implicitly approved pleading a conspiracy based on an agreement to pursue a fraudulent-inducement scheme to obtain money or property.

## Practical defense and prosecution perspectives at the pleading stage

From the government’s perspective, the briefing and drafting strategy should be to tie deceptive conduct to a tangible object—money or property—and to allege the causal mechanism of inducement. Prosecutors should anticipate Ciminelli and be proactive: plead fraudulent inducement where victims are alleged to have transferred money; describe the mechanism of deception; and include specific dates, platforms, and amounts where reasonable. Preserve alternative theories (substantive fraud, conspiracy, aiding-and-abetting), but ensure the indictment’s counts are consistent with the elements and give clear notice.

From the defense perspective, the pretrial motion to dismiss remains a high bar but can succeed when the indictment fails as a matter of law—most commonly in Ciminelli-type scenarios where the government’s theory rests solely on economic-information deprivation. Defense counsel should scrutinize whether the indictment alleges the defendant obtained money or property or whether it alleges only misleading information. If the latter, a Rule 12 motion may force dismissal. Even where the indictment survives, a defensible approach includes seeking a bill of particulars to pin down the government’s theory, motions to exclude prejudicial evidence, and careful cross-examination plans to show lack of intent to deceive or absence of causal inducement.

## Conclusion

An indictment must be a clear map: it must state the legal elements of the offense, provide fair notice of the charges, and prevent double jeopardy. In complex fraud cases, that requires careful pleading of the scheme and, crucially after Ciminelli and Kousisis, clear identification of money-or-property as the object when the prosecution’s theory rests on fraudulent inducement. At the Rule 12 stage courts take the allegations as true and do not weigh evidence; appellate review of dismissal for failure to state an offense is de novo. For prosecutors, the lesson is concrete: plead the property interest and the inducement mechanism. For defense counsel, the lesson is equally concrete: look for any pleading gap between alleged deception and actual deprivation of money or property, and press Rule 12 when the government’s theory is legally insufficient. The Fifth Circuit’s reinstatement of the Constantinescu indictment is a useful modern example of these principles in action.

# Mens Rea in Fraud: Intent to Harm vs. Intent to Obtain

This chapter examines the mens rea required for criminal fraud after the Supreme Court’s recent decision in Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit’s application of that decision in a securities “pump and dump” prosecution. The doctrinal issue presented by these cases is deceptively simple: what mental state must the government prove to convict someone of fraud? That question breaks into several related inquiries. Is the government required to prove that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off? Does a defendant’s desire to profit negate an intent-to-defraud charge if he did not intend to harm the victim? How do concepts such as “right‑to‑control,” “fraudulent inducement,” good faith, willful blindness, and disclaimers fit into the element of intent? The answers matter acutely in modern online marketplaces and social‑media trading schemes, where actors can make public statements and profit without necessarily causing an obvious net loss to every investor.

The leading decisions we use to structure our analysis are Ciminelli v. United States (which rejected a right‑to‑control theory), Kousisis v. United States (which clarified and endorsed the fraudulent‑inducement theory), and the Fifth Circuit’s recent application of those principles in the pump‑and‑dump indictment discussed above. Those cases, together with preexisting Fifth Circuit formulations, yield a practical framework for assessing intent to defraud in contemporary fraud prosecutions.

## Defining intent to defraud: intent to deceive and to cause harm (or obtain property)

Traditional formulations in this circuit describe “intent to defraud” as a two‑part requirement: (1) an intent to deceive; and (2) an intent that some harm result from that deceit. See United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018); United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 (5th Cir. 2023). That language captures the intuition that most frauds involve both falsehood (or omission) and the purposeful use of that falsehood to secure a benefit or advantage at the expense of another.

But two important qualifications must be borne in mind after the recent Supreme Court decisions. First, Ciminelli held that not every deprivation of an intangible interest is the sort of “harm” the federal fraud statutes were meant to remedy: the statutes protect “traditional property interests,” and a mere deprivation of valuable economic information—the “right to control” one’s assets—cannot, standing alone, sustain a fraud conviction. Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 316 (2023). Second, and crucially, Kousisis clarified that the “harm” required for the intent element does not, in the fraudulent‑inducement context, require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off overall. In Kousisis, the Supreme Court explained that a fraudster who uses a material misstatement to induce a victim to enter a contract that requires handing over money or property can be liable even if the victim receives something of value in return and may not be left worse off in net pecuniary terms. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1388–92 (2025). The fraudulent‑inducement theory therefore protects the victim’s money or property itself as the object of the fraud, not merely the victim’s informational autonomy.

Reconciling these principles: an indictment (or proof) that alleges a defendant intentionally deceived others for the purpose of obtaining money or property states a valid fraud charge, provided the alleged object of the scheme is a protected property interest (money or property), and not merely an informational advantage. Thus, when a social‑media influencer posts false trading positions to induce followers to buy, causing those followers to transfer money in exchange for securities, the element of intent to defraud can be met by alleging that the influencer intended to deceive in order to obtain followers’ money or to dispose of securities for personal gain. The government must still prove intent to deceive, but it need not show that the defendant sought to cause the victim a net pecuniary loss.

Put differently: the statutes require that deception be used to obtain something of value—traditionally money or property. The harm component can be satisfied by the act of inducing the victim to part with that money or property; the law does not always require a showing that the victim’s net worth decreased as a result.

## Profit motive as evidence of intent to obtain victims’ money

The fact that a defendant profited from a transaction is powerful circumstantial evidence that she intended to obtain victims’ money or property. In pump‑and‑dump prosecutions, the typical pattern—(1) acquire securities privately, (2) post false or misleading favorable statements, (3) induce purchases by followers, and (4) sell the holdings at an elevated price—maps precisely onto the fraudulent‑inducement theory: the object of the scheme is to obtain money via induced purchases.

Kousisis expressly recognizes that profit motive and the “object” of obtaining money can establish intent: a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses, and the defendant “had money in mind.” 145 S. Ct. at 1394 n.5, 1397. The Fifth Circuit’s recent reversal of the dismissal in the pump‑and‑dump indictment relied on those same points: the indictment alleged that the defendants “posted false and misleading information . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” and that the defendants profited—allegations that sufficiently plead intent to obtain money.

But the prosecutor should not assume that profit alone is dispositive of intent to defraud. Profit motive is probative, not conclusive. It must be paired with evidence of deception and a causal link between the deception and the transfer of money or property. Timing (when the defendant sells relative to public statements), undisclosed conflicts of interest or payments, and corroborating contemporaneous communications (messages that show coordination or acknowledgment of the misleading nature of the statements) all strengthen an inference of intent.

## No “mirror‑image” requirement linking victim loss to defendant gain (Baker)

Some defense theories rest on the premise that, to prove fraud, the government must show a mirror‑image transfer: the defendant’s gain must equal the victim’s loss, or at least the defendant must have intended to leave the victim worse off. Fifth Circuit precedent rejects that approach. United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390 (5th Cir. 2019), explained that the fraud statutes do not impose a “mirror image” requirement where the victim’s loss supplied the defendant’s gain. Kousisis reinforced that reasoning at the Supreme Court level: the fraudulent‑inducement theory criminalizes schemes to obtain money or property by material misstatements regardless of whether the fraudster sought to leave the victim financially worse off. 145 S. Ct. at 1392–93.

This doctrine is particularly important in contexts where the alleged victims received something on paper (for example, securities purchased at market price) and might not suffer an identifiable out‑of‑pocket loss if they later sell at market value. The government need only allege that the defendant used deception to obtain the victims’ money or property at the time of the transaction. The focus is on the inducement and the transfer, not on whether the victim’s ultimate financial position declined. This removes an otherwise significant defense that would allow ubiquitous manipulative conduct so long as each buyer acquired a tradable asset that could be resold.

## Good faith and belief‑in‑value defenses

A defendant’s good faith—an honest belief that a statement was true or that the security had the value claimed—negates the specific intent to deceive and is a complete defense to fraud. Good faith under criminal fraud doctrine typically means an honest belief in the truth of one’s statements, even if that belief is mistaken. If the defendant truly believed the representation, there is no intent to deceive.

That said, the government may overcome assertions of good faith with evidence of knowledge, recklessness, or conscious disregard for the truth. The line between mere negligence and culpable mental states (recklessness or deliberate falsehood) is often litigated. Reckless disregard for the truth—where a defendant made statements despite a high probability of falsity and failed to investigate—may be sufficient to infer intent depending on the statute and the circuit’s precedents. After Kousisis, prosecutors commonly seek to prove that social‑media promoters did not hold an honest belief in their public claims by pointing to contemporaneous communications, undisclosed sales, and profit‑seeking patterns that belie any genuine belief in value.

A defendant’s subjective belief about value is different from a belief about truth. Saying “I believed the stock would go up” is not the same as “I believed the statement I made about my position was true.” Courts frequently require the defendant to have understood the falsity of the representation or at least to have acted with reckless disregard to that falsity. Good‑faith belief in value may be a plausible defense to charges alleging fraud based on deceptive price predictions, but it will be fragile when the government possesses objective evidence that the defendant knew the statements were false or deliberately withheld material information that undercut the claimed basis for the predictions.

Practical note for defense counsel: present documentary and testimonial evidence that supports a genuine investment thesis, show contemporaneous analyses or models used to form the opinion, and highlight any communications that reveal uncertainty or hedging language contemporaneous with the statements.

## Relevance of disclaimers and “not financial advice” language

Disclaimers such as “not financial advice,” “do your own research,” or other boilerplate language are frequent in online communications. They can be relevant to intent, but they rarely provide an absolute shield.

Disclaimers tend to be probative on the question of whether a speaker intended the audience to rely on the statement as a factual representation about the speaker’s own trading positions or as a prescriptive recommendation. If a promoter consistently uses careful disclaimers and follows them up with substantive explanations, those facts can help demonstrate lack of intent to deceive. On the other hand, a single form disclaimer in the presence of coordinated messaging, secret sales, and contemporaneous conversations encouraging purchases will carry little weight. Courts look to the totality of the circumstances: the substance of the messages, the speaker’s role and reputation (did followers reasonably expect trading guidance?), the presence or absence of disclosure of conflicts or positions, and whether the disclaimers were genuine or merely perfunctory.

A defendant cannot, by adding a disclaimer, convert a fraudulent inducement into lawful speech if the rest of the conduct demonstrates an intent to deceive and to obtain money or property. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the pump‑and‑dump indictment rests in part on allegations that go beyond mere promotional speech—allegations of secret sales timed to exploit the induced purchases, and large profits traced to the scheme. In such contexts, a boilerplate disclaimer will be of limited persuasive value.

## Willful blindness and conscious avoidance in online schemes

Willful blindness—also called conscious avoidance or deliberate ignorance—is an established doctrine courts allow to prove knowledge where direct evidence of actual knowledge is lacking but the defendant deliberately avoided confirming what she suspected. The test typically asks whether the defendant (1) subjectively believed there was a high probability that wrongdoing was occurring and (2) took deliberate steps to avoid learning the truth.

In online fraud (including pump‑and‑dump schemes), willful blindness can be a practical tool for the government. For example, if a promoter receives payments tied to posting favorable comments but intentionally avoids asking whether counterparties are selling into the induced demand, or if the promoter instructs associates to funnel orders through opaque accounts to hide sales, such conduct can support an inference of willful blindness. Similarly, structured attempts to block or delete incriminating messages, use burner accounts to sell at opportune times, or coordinate sales through intermediaries can demonstrate deliberate avoidance.

Defense counsel must be prepared to show reasonable steps the defendant took to ensure compliance or to demonstrate a lack of a high probability of wrongdoing. The presence of innocuous explanations for suspicious behavior (e.g., reliance on third‑party advice, automated posting systems, or mere negligence) can cut against a willful‑blindness inference, but the doctrine remains a potent prosecutorial tool when other evidence of knowledge is absent.

## Jury instructions on intent to defraud and the object of the scheme

Careful jury instructions are essential. A properly worded charge will (1) define the offense elements with sufficient clarity, (2) explain the intent requirement in light of current precedent, and (3) instruct on defenses such as good faith and conscious avoidance.

Recommended core elements to include in a jury instruction for a fraudulent‑inducement securities fraud charge:
- The existence of a scheme or artifice to defraud, consisting of a plan or course of conduct to accomplish the unlawful objective;
- A material misrepresentation, omission, or deceptive practice used in furtherance of the scheme;
- That the defendant acted knowingly and with intent to defraud—that is, with an intent to deceive or to cause a deprivation of money or property by means of the deception (explain that, under current law, “deprivation” is satisfied when the defendant induces the victim to hand over money or property in reliance on materially false information; it does not require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim worse off overall);
- That the scheme was executed in connection with the purchase or sale of a security (if charged under the securities fraud statute);
- That the defendant’s acts were the proximate cause of the alleged transfer or inducement.

Because jury confusion about “harm” has been common, the instruction should expressly distinguish Ciminelli’s limitation (no protection for a bare right‑to‑control or mere deprivation of information) from Kousisis’s holding (fraudulent inducement targeting money or property is actionable even if the victim receives something of value in return). The instruction should further explain willful blindness as a substitute for actual knowledge when warranted, and affirm that an honest belief in the truth of a representation is a defense. Finally, the court should provide a clear definition of “material” and give concrete examples drawn from the factual record.

## Mens rea proof: contemporaneous chats, DMs, and trade confirmations

Proof of intent to defraud is often circumstantial. For online and social‑media schemes, certain categories of evidence are particularly probative:

- Contemporaneous private messages (DMs, texts, chats) in which the defendant admits misleading practices, coordinates timing of posts with sales, or discusses plans to “pump” the stock. Such messages can show both fraudulent intent and knowledge of wrongdoing.
- Trade confirmations, brokerage records, and account statements showing that the defendant or affiliated accounts sold securities at or near the peak coincident with promotional activity. The timing of purchases and sales, especially when inconsistent with the defendant’s public statements, supports an inference of intent to obtain proceeds.
- Payment records or bank transfers demonstrating that a promoter received compensation tied to promotional activity (e.g., paid promotions for particular tickers).
- Evidence of undisclosed conflicts of interest (e.g., ownership of controlling shares, secret agreements to sell holdings) that render public statements misleading.
- Internal analytics showing the defendant tracked follower purchases or price movements and used that information to time sales.
- Deletion of posts, use of burner accounts, or instructions to avoid written records—all of which can be relevant to willful blindness or consciousness of guilt.

The value of each item depends on the surrounding context. For example, a single trade confirmation that shows a small sale shortly after a recommendation may be consistent with legitimate trading. But multiple trades across accounts, coordinated with private messages and payments, create a pattern that jurors will find persuasive. The government’s burden is to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt, and these circumstantial proofs often form the core of a fraud case.

## Practical takeaways and litigation strategies

For prosecutors:
- Draft indictments to allege a fraudulent‑inducement theory when the scheme involves inducing victims to transfer money or property; avoid reliance on a right‑to‑control theory.
- Plead specific facts showing deception, the defendant’s intent to obtain money or property, and the causal link between the misrepresentations and the transfers.
- Rely on contemporaneous digital evidence, payment trails, and trade records to establish intent and to rebut good‑faith defenses.

For defense counsel:
- Emphasize the absence of intent to deceive by producing contemporaneous documentation of honest belief—analyses, models, and communications showing uncertainty or hedging language.
- Challenge the sufficiency of circumstantial evidence where timing or sales patterns are ambiguous.
- Highlight disclaimers, lack of control over followers’ trades, and the possibility that defendants provided only opinions (although courts will scrutinize whether the speakers presented statements as fact or as advice).
- Contest willful‑blindness inferences by demonstrating reasonable steps the defendant took to verify sources or by showing lack of awareness of third‑party sales.

For judges and jury instructors:
- Carefully define “intent to defraud” and the object of the scheme in light of Kousisis and Ciminelli.
- Prevent juror confusion by explaining that the law protects money or property, not mere information, but that inducing the transfer of money or property by deceptive means suffices even if the victim receives something of value in return.
- Include instructions on willful blindness and good faith, tailored to the case facts.

Conclusion

Modern fraud prosecutions, particularly those arising in online and social‑media contexts, often turn on nuanced distinctions among deception, profit motive, and the nature of the property interest alleged to have been taken. The law today—after Ciminelli and Kousisis and as applied by the Fifth Circuit—draws three clear lines: (1) the federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests (money or property), not bare informational rights; (2) fraudulent inducement of a transfer of money or property is actionable even if the victim receives something of value and is not left worse off in net terms; and (3) proof of intent to defraud can rest on circumstantial evidence, including profit motive, contemporaneous communications, timing of trades, and evidence of conscious avoidance. Students and practitioners should analyze these cases together to ensure indictments and defenses are framed to the current doctrinal landscape.

# Materiality, Opinions, and Half-Truths in Investor Communications

This chapter examines how the law determines whether statements made to investors — especially on social media — are “material” and therefore capable of supporting civil or criminal securities-fraud charges. It focuses on three recurring problems: statements of opinion and prediction, statements that are literally true but misleading because important facts are omitted (the “half-truth” problem), and undisclosed trading intentions or positions. Although the doctrinal roots lie in civil securities law, recent criminal cases, including the Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu and companions, and the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, show how materiality and intent operate in prosecutions that allege “pump and dump” schemes conducted over social platforms. Students should leave this chapter able to (1) identify when a communication crosses the line from permissible puffery or opinion into actionable misrepresentation, (2) apply the half-truth doctrine, and (3) evaluate whether undisclosed trading intentions or positions can be the type of information that a reasonable investor would find material.

## Materiality: the reasonable investor standard

Materiality is an objective inquiry: would a reasonable investor consider the information important in making an investment decision? That formulation, which governs civil securities litigation, is the touchstone for criminal prosecutions under statutes that “borrow key concepts” from mail and wire fraud doctrines, and now under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 as well. In TSC Industries v. Northway, the Supreme Court explained that a fact is material if there is a substantial likelihood that a reasonable shareholder would consider it important; courts use that standard to decide whether non-disclosure or a false statement is actionable. Criminal courts examining allegations of securities fraud under § 1348 and analogous statutes apply a similar materiality lens, while recognizing the separate mens rea requirement.

Materiality is context-sensitive. The reasonable investor is not an atomized, uninformed person in the abstract; the inquiry asks whether, in the particular transaction and given the particular audience and relationship between speaker and listener, the omitted or misrepresented fact would have been viewed as significant. Courts therefore look not only at the content of a statement, but at its context: the sophistication of the audience, the role of the speaker (expert, tipster, advisor), and the market setting (illiquid microcap stock versus widely traded blue chip). In social-media pump-and-dump cases, the presence of a large, engaged following amplifies the significance of materiality: a false claim about a purchased position or a misleading prognosis can induce many retail investors to buy an otherwise thinly traded security, magnifying the likelihood that a reasonable follower would rely on the communication when deciding to purchase.

Evidence that a statement was material often comes from market reaction and transaction data. A sudden spike in trading volume and price immediately following a post, coupled with contemporaneous purchases by the speaker or their associates, can support an inference that the communication was important to investors. Likewise, content showing targeted calls to action (e.g., “buy now,” “this will go to X”) or representations of insider knowledge weighs in favor of materiality. But courts remain mindful that materiality is not simply whether a statement moved a market price; it is whether the misrepresented or omitted fact would have been viewed as important by a reasonable investor making an informed decision.

## Opinions and predictions vs. objective misstatements

Statements that express opinions, beliefs, or predictions occupy a different doctrinal space than statements of historical fact. The Supreme Court in Omnicare established that a statement of opinion can be actionable under the securities laws when (1) the speaker did not actually hold the expressed belief, or (2) the speaker omitted facts that, if known, would have rendered the opinion materially misleading to a reasonable investor. In short, opinions are not categorically exempt from liability, but they receive special treatment: the inquiry centers on the sincerity of the speaker’s belief and on whether undisclosed facts subvert the opinion’s apparent basis.

Applying that framework in the social-media context requires careful parsing. A generic promotional assertion — “this company has great upside” — often reads as non-actionable puffery; it is vague, not readily verifiable, and unlikely to induce reliance in the way a specific factual claim would. By contrast, a precise forecast tied to specific facts — “I just bought 20,000 shares because management told me they will announce a deal that will double revenues next quarter” — is a mixed assertion: it combines an opinion/prediction with an implied factual predicate (that management made certain representations). If that factual predicate is false or omitted, the combined statement becomes actionable because it is no longer an honest belief based on disclosed facts.

Omnicare’s two-part test thus guides analysis in both civil and criminal settings. To assess whether an alleged misstatement of opinion supports prosecution, prosecutors must show not merely that the speaker expressed optimism, but that the speaker lacked belief in the statement at the time, or that omission of underlying facts made the opinion misleading to a reasonable investor. In pump-and-dump prosecutions, this often manifests as when an influencer asserts confidence in a stock’s prospects while secretly liquidating a position or lacking any reasonable basis for a bullish forecast. Under Omnicare, an opinion shared to induce purchases can be criminally actionable if the speaker was knowingly misrepresenting the basis for that opinion or concealing adverse facts that would have undermined the expressed belief.

## Half-truth doctrine: when speaking triggers a duty to tell the full story

The half-truth doctrine is an indispensable tool for police, prosecutors, and courts confronting modern communications. The rule is straightforward in principle: when a defendant makes an affirmative statement, even a literally true one, the speaker may have a duty to correct or supplement that statement if the omitted information renders the overall communication misleading. In securities jurisprudence this idea appears in several guises — as the misleading-nature principle in Rule 10b‑5 cases and in the Supreme Court’s discussion in Omnicare regarding omissions that render opinions misleading.

The doctrine is operationalized through two inquiries. First, whether the affirmative statement or representation induced a material impression that omitted facts would have significantly altered. Second, whether the speaker had a duty to disclose the omitted facts — which can arise from the nature of the statement (a partial disclosure), a pre-existing fiduciary or statutory duty, or from a scheme to defraud where the speaker created a misleading impression and then remained silent to perpetuate it. In the social-media pump-and-dump context, a post such as “I just bought shares and I’m bullish” can be a half-truth if the speaker actually sold shares before posting, or if the speaker failed to disclose that their prior position was a short or that they stood to personally benefit from an immediate price movement. The literal truth of some component does not inoculate the speaker if the net impression conveyed to reasonable followers is materially misleading.

Courts look to the totality of the message. A defendant cannot insulate an omission by couching the message as opinion or by burying a qualifier in small print. Conversely, the presence of a broad, explicit cautionary statement will sometimes blunt the misleading effect of an omission; however, cautionary language is not a talisman when the omitted facts would have changed the reasonable investor’s assessment. In criminal prosecutions, demonstrating that a defendant knowingly used a half-truth to induce investment helps establish both materiality and the requisite intent to defraud.

## Undisclosed intent to sell and undisclosed positions as material facts

One of the most fact-intensive and important questions in pump-and-dump prosecutions is whether a speaker’s undisclosed intent to sell a security, or undisclosed contrary trading positions, is a material fact that must be revealed. The short answer — as the Fifth Circuit recognized in United States v. Constantinescu and the Supreme Court confirmed in Kousisis — is that undisclosed intent to obtain money or property through inducing purchases can be material and criminally actionable under a fraudulent-inducement theory. An influencer who publicly urges followers to buy while secretly planning to sell (dump) misleads those followers about a critical fact relevant to their investment decision: the speaker’s incentive structure and likely future trading behavior.

That materiality justification rests on two principles. First, traders often rely on the perceived alignment of interest and on the belief that an influencer’s recommendation is motivated by genuine conviction derived from disclosed information. A secret intention to sell undermines that belief because it changes the economics: the speaker’s plan is to extract value from induced purchases rather than to share a long-term market view. Second, information about positions and intentions is the sort of fact a reasonable investor would consider important in deciding whether to trade with the influencing party’s implied trust.

Prosecutors should therefore pursue evidence that a speaker’s communications were designed to induce purchases while the speaker secretly planned to sell: contemporaneous sell orders, timing of sales relative to posts, private messages revealing intent, and internal or third-party communications showing coordination. Conversely, defendants will argue that mere failure to disclose a passive position or an ordinary sale does not automatically suffice, particularly where the statements were broad opinions or where the trading was open and market-based. The critical question is whether the non-disclosure was part of a scheme to deceive and obtain money or property — the fraudulent-inducement framework approved in Kousisis squarely addresses that question: inducing a person to hand over money or property by means of material misrepresentations or misleading omissions is an object of fraud, regardless of whether the victim later ends up with a market-value asset.

## Puffery and hyperbole in social media: limits and risks

Social-media discourse is rife with promotional language, hyperbole, and “puffery.” In commercial and securities law, puffery refers to vague, subjective statements that no reasonable person would take as factual representations. Courts routinely treat puffery as non-actionable: phrases like “this company is a game-changer” or “I love this stock” usually fall on the permissive side of expression. But the casual, informal venue of social media complicates the analysis because followers, especially unsophisticated ones, may ascribe technical expertise or factual content to a popular poster’s declarations. The law thus distinguishes between generalized, non-verifiable praise and specific, verifiable assertions.

The line is practical and doctrinal. A post that contains quantifiable, verifiable statements — “I just bought 50,000 shares,” “Company X will announce a merger next week,” or “I own no position in this stock” — is not puffery and can form the basis for liability if false or misleading. Conversely, a sweeping bullish slogan without factual predicates is likely puffery. The speaker’s credentials and prior conduct matter: when an influential trader with a documented history of precise trade reports or a reputation for reliable tips makes a concrete claim, courts are more likely to treat that claim as a factual representation rather than mere advertising.

Social-media dynamics create particular risk for would-be influencers. Small differences — the use of a specific number, a past-tense statement about a trade, or an assertion about possession of nonpublic information — can transform a harmless endorsement into a potentially criminal misrepresentation. Law enforcement and private plaintiffs therefore focus on the specificity and verifiability of language rather than its tone. For practitioners and influencers alike, the practical lesson is simple: avoid precise representations about trading activity, positions, or intents unless they are true and documented; do not rely on a general “this is my opinion” label to shield objectively verifiable statements.

## Context and audience: sophistication and reliance expectations

Materiality and the line between opinion and misstatement are both sensitive to the communicative context and the sophistication of the audience. The reasonable investor standard is objective, but courts consider the actual circle of recipients and the relationship between speaker and audience when assessing whether a statement was likely to influence investment decisions. A post directed to a small, specialized group of professional traders will be analyzed differently than a broadcast to thousands of retail followers.

Sophistication influences two inquiries: whether the misrepresentation is material and whether reliance is reasonably foreseeable. A communications platform that markets itself as financial advice to unsophisticated investors raises greater expectations of disclosure and accuracy. Conversely, communications in contexts where readers expect bluster or opinion — chatrooms where posting norms emphasize entertainment over instruction — may attenuate the inference that a reasonable recipient relied on the statements as a factual basis for investment. That said, modern social-media pump-and-dump cases show that popularity can itself heighten the duty: a person with a large following who projects investment expertise will be held to a higher standard because followers plausibly rely on that persona.

Courts also consider whether the speaker’s statements create reasonable expectations of accuracy or exclusivity. An influencer who purports to act as an advisor, posts trade confirmations, or offers paid subscription services promising “insider-like” picks invites reliance and thus a greater duty to avoid misleading omissions. The Fifth Circuit’s Constantinescu opinion is illustrative: defendants spoke to large followings, portrayed themselves as skilled traders, and posted allegedly false statements about trades and price prospects — facts that made it plausible that their followers would rely on the statements when buying securities.

## Documenting materiality: market reactions, follower engagement, and timing

In any enforcement or defense strategy, the sufficiency and source of evidence proving materiality and intent matter. Prosecutors must build a record demonstrating not only that a statement was false or misleading, but that it was likely important to investors and that the speaker intended to induce purchases. Several types of empirical and documentary evidence are particularly probative.

First, contemporaneous market data: abrupt spikes in price and trading volume right after a post, particularly in thinly traded microcap stocks, support the inference that the post influenced investor behavior. Correlating timestamps of posts to transaction records can show that purchases by followers trended immediately after the communication. Second, follower engagement metrics — likes, shares, comment threads, and direct messages asking whether to buy — can be marshaled to demonstrate that followers read and acted on the content. Third, the timing of the speaker’s own trades in relation to the posts is crucial: sales executed immediately after bullish posts create a strong circumstantial inference of a pump-and-dump pattern. Fourth, internal communications (e.g., private messages indicating secret sales or coordination with brokers), screenshots of claim-and-response exchanges, and subscription service records can reveal intent to deceive.

Defendants will challenge causation; correlation is not causation. A vigorous defense will attempt to show independent buying pressure, unrelated market-moving news, or pre-existing trends that explain the price movement. Thus prosecutors should seek to triangulate: show that the speaker’s posts were the proximate cause of the buying surge, that followers lacked other information sources, and that the speaker’s transactions were timed to extract profit from the induced buying. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion highlights this evidentiary work: the indictment alleged a pattern of purchases by defendants, public posts designed to induce purchases, and profits of $114 million — a pattern sufficiently concrete to overcome the district court’s dismissal at the indictment stage.

## Comparisons to civil standards while noting criminal proof burdens

Doctrinally, criminal prosecutions under § 1348 and civil actions under Rule 10b‑5 share many concepts — materiality, deceptive conduct, and the misleading nature of omissions — and courts frequently borrow reasoning from civil cases. Cases such as TSC Industries (materiality) and Omnicare (opinions) are therefore instructive in criminal cases. But there are important differences.

First and most obvious, the burden of proof is higher in criminal cases: the prosecution must prove every element, including mens rea, beyond a reasonable doubt. That elevation of proof affects how prosecutors frame charges and select evidence. Demonstrating that a defendant intended to deceive — not merely that a defendant negligently misstated a fact — is essential in criminal cases. Hearsay and circumstantial evidence can suffice to prove intent, but the inferences must be reasonable and supported by a robust evidentiary mosaic: private communications, contemporaneous trading patterns, evidence of concealment, and inconsistent public statements.

Second, civil litigation often requires a showing of reliance (in private actions) and loss causation, whereas criminal liability under the fraudulent-inducement theory endorsed in Kousisis does not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim financially worse off or that the fraud caused net pecuniary loss. Kousisis clarified that inducing a contract or purchase by material misrepresentation is itself an object of fraud, even if the victim receives something of equal value in return. Thus criminal prosecutions can proceed where civil claims based on net-loss theories might fail. Nevertheless, criminal defendants can use civil standards substantively to contest materiality or to argue reasonable belief in the truth of a statement (defenses that judicially mitigate culpability).

Third, remedies and disclosure standards differ. Civil regulators and private litigants can seek monetary and equitable relief, and the SEC has administrative tools for enforcement that do not require criminal mens rea. Criminal prosecutors must be selective, mindful of constitutional safeguards and the public interest in preserving vigorous but honest public debate. For this reason, courts have developed guardrails — distinguishing puffery from factual misstatements, safeguarding protected opinions — while ensuring that fraud designed to extract money through deception remains prosecutable.

Conclusion: the Fifth Circuit’s reversal in the Constantinescu line of cases exemplifies the contemporary approach. The indictment alleged defendants with large social followings made deceptive statements designed to induce purchases, while secretly planning to sell. Applying the reasonable investor materiality standard, Omnicare’s and half-truth principles, and the fraudulent-inducement rationale recognized in Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the allegations sufficiently pleaded a scheme to obtain money or property. For students and practitioners, the lesson is practical: in the age of social media, specificity, verifiability, and the speaker’s intent and relationship to the audience determine whether promotional speech crosses the legal line into fraud. Robust documentation of market reaction, detailed trade timing, and internal communications can transform a collection of posts into a prosecutable fraud; conversely, generalized opinion and puffery remain unlikely to support criminal liability absent a dishonest or opaque factual predicate.

# The ‘In Connection With’ Requirement

The “in connection with” language in the federal securities-fraud provision, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, is the statutory bridge that links deceptive conduct to securities transactions. That phrase is the linchpin in deciding whether deceptive conduct—whether an insider’s lie, an issuer’s misstatement, or an influencer’s social‑media post—falls within the criminal prohibition on schemes to defraud in the context of securities. This chapter explains how courts treat that statutory nexus: how the textual parallel with Rule 10b‑5 shapes analysis, how courts determine whether deception functionally induced purchases or sales, what degree of temporal and causal linkage is required, and how those principles play out in modern fact patterns such as online promotion and pump‑and‑dump schemes. The discussion uses the Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cayce and related Supreme Court precedents (notably Ciminelli and Kousisis) to illustrate the doctrinal contours students will encounter on exam and in practice.

## Textual comparison: § 1348 and Rule 10b‑5’s ‘in connection with’ language

Section 1348 prohibits, among other things, schemes “to defraud any person in connection with any security” and schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.” Rule 10b‑5, promulgated under Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act, contains parallel language: it prohibits any device, scheme, or artifice “in connection with the purchase or sale of any security” from employing fraud or deceit. Because § 1348 tracks the language and policy concerns of Rule 10b‑5, courts frequently borrow the interpretive decisions of the 10b‑5 line when construing § 1348. The Cayce opinion follows that approach, observing that the securities-fraud statute “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and that courts often consult mail/wire and 10b‑5 decisions to define statutory terms such as “scheme to defraud” and the “in connection with” nexus.

The textual parallel matters for two reasons. First, it grounds an interpretive presumption that “in connection with” requires some relationship between the fraudulent conduct and a securities transaction—more than a mere factual coincidence. Second, it indicates that courts should evaluate whether the deception affected the market or the transaction in a way that made the purchase or sale a practical objective or consequence of the deception. But the parallel does not mean that § 1348 simply imports every nuance of 10b‑5 jurisprudence unchanged; courts read § 1348 with attention to its statutory context and to the criminal-law elements of scheme and intent.

## Functional nexus: inducing purchases or sales through deception

A central question under the “in connection with” requirement is whether the deception operated to induce—or was designed to induce—a purchase or sale of securities. The Cayce panel emphasizes a functional approach: the government may proceed under a fraudulent‑inducement theory when it alleges that the defendant used material misstatements to “trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property,” i.e., to cause the victim to buy (or sell) a security. The Supreme Court’s recent decision in Kousisis clarified and reaffirmed this path: the fraudulent‑inducement theory is a valid way to show the required nexus because it protects money or property that the fraudster obtains by inducing a transaction.

This functional test is distinct from other theories that courts have rejected or treated skeptically. Ciminelli rejected a “right‑to‑control” theory that attempted to treat the victim’s loss of valuable economic information as the protected property. That decision means the government cannot rely solely on a theory that the fraudster deprived a decisionmaker of information that would influence discretionary judgments unrelated to the surrender of money or property. But when the deceit directly causes a person to hand over money or property in the form of a securities purchase, the functional nexus is present: the deceptive communication is an instrumentality by which purchases or sales are obtained.

In practical terms, a prosecutor (or civil plaintiff) must plead and later prove that the deception either (1) was intended to induce the purchase or sale and (2) had some operative effect on the decision to trade. In pump‑and‑dump cases like Cayce, the indictment typically alleges that the defendant posted false and misleading statements to induce followers to buy, thereby inflating price, and then sold shares. That sequence—misrepresentation followed by induced purchases and then defendant sales—maps cleanly onto the fraudulent‑inducement theory and satisfies the functional nexus.

## Temporal proximity and causal linkage to trading activity

How close in time and how direct a causal link must the deception have to the trading activity? Courts have resisted mechanical rules. The required linkage is contextual and flexible: the deception need not be simultaneous with the trade, and it need not be the sole cause of the trade. Instead, courts ask whether the misrepresentation was sufficiently related in time and causation to the transactions so that it can fairly be said to have been “in connection with” them.

Temporal proximity helps but is not dispositive. A misstatement communicated well before a transaction but reasonably calculated to influence trading decisions can satisfy the nexus if the other circumstances demonstrate that the statement was intended to and did affect purchases or sales. Conversely, a close temporal coincidence without evidence of intent or causation (for example, a misstatement uttered after the execution of a trade) will not suffice.

Causation is likewise treated in practical rather than metaphysical terms. The government must show that the defendant’s deception was part of a scheme that made obtaining money or property from securities purchases or sales an object or reasonably foreseeable consequence of the scheme. The Cayce court applied this view: the indictment need only allege that defendants posted false statements “so that other investors were induced to purchase” the security. That allegation ties the misrepresentations to the act of purchasing—satisfying the “in connection with” nexus even though courts do not require proof that the fraudster intended the victims to be worse off.

Students should note that proving causation at trial often relies on circumstantial evidence: timing of posts relative to buy orders, messaging that asked followers to “buy now,” coordination among accounts, and sales that realize profits after the price rise. The flexible temporal/causal standard accommodates these evidentiary realities.

## Market‑wide vs. victim‑specific connections

The “in connection with” requirement accommodates both market‑wide manipulations (schemes that affect many market participants or prices generally) and victim‑specific inducements (deceiving a particular person or group to induce a particular purchase or sale). The distinction matters for pleading and proof but not for the existence of the statutory nexus.

Market‑wide schemes—classic market manipulation and pump‑and‑dump schemes—operate by spreading false information or trading activity designed to inflate prices so that the schemers can sell into the artificially high market. The deception is typically broadcast to a broad audience (investors generally or followers of a promoter), and the causal link is established by showing that the deceptive messaging affected market demand and price. Courts have recognized that large-scale deception that foreseeably influences market participants satisfies the “in connection with” requirement even though there is no single identifiable victim.

Victim‑specific schemes align more closely with the fraudulent‑inducement model (as in Kousisis): a defendant makes a material misstatement to a discrete decisionmaker knowing it will induce that person to enter into a transaction—such as awarding a contract or purchasing a security. The “in connection with” link is straightforward where the deception directly precedes and induces a particular transaction.

Cayce illustrates that both pathways are viable under § 1348: social‑media promoters may be indicted for inducing their followers (a discrete class of investors) to buy a security, thereby creating a market effect that lets the promoters sell at inflated prices. The indictment does not have to choose a single “market‑wide” or “victim‑specific” label so long as it pleads a plausible linkage between deception and trading.

## Online promotion and trading: establishing transactional linkage

Digital platforms and social media have complicated the question of how to show that online promotion was “in connection with” purchases or sales. Modern fraud cases involving influencers, message‑board posters, or coordinated online campaigns require concrete methods to show that deceptive content was intended to, and did, produce trading activity.

Courts look to several indicia of transactional linkage in online contexts:

- The content of the communications: explicit calls-to-action (“buy now,” “accumulate shares”), false statements about control of shares, fabricated earnings claims, or fake endorsements are strong evidence that posts were designed to induce trades. Cayce’s indictment expressly relied on posts that portrayed defendants as actively trading and touted securities in ways likely to prompt purchases.

- The timing of posts relative to trades: posts that precede spikes in buying activity and are followed by defendant sales provide compelling circumstantial evidence that the posts prompted trading.

- Audience and reach: the size of an influencer’s following, engagement metrics, and the demographics of followers (retail traders, subscribers to paid trading services) help show that the messaging was aimed at prospective purchasers.

- Coordination and pattern: repeated postings across multiple accounts, coordinated messaging, or use of multiple platforms to amplify the same falsehoods suggests a plan to generate buying pressure.

- Direct channels of communication: private messages, subscriber newsletters, or paid subscription content targeted at followers may demonstrate an intent to induce particular purchases.

Taken together, these factors allow prosecutors to plead and, later, prove that online promotion was not mere puffery or market chatter but a deliberate instrumentality of a scheme to obtain money or property by inducing purchases or sales.

## Proof at trial: trade blotters, timestamps, and content archives

Because the “in connection with” inquiry is often a question of fact, trial proof commonly relies on documentary and digital records that can track the sequence and effect of deceptive communications and trading. The typical evidentiary tools include:

- Broker statements and trade blotters: these show purchase and sale dates, sizes, and prices for both defendants and particular cohorts of investors (for example, subscribers to an influencer’s channel). Matching the timing of purchases by followers to promotional posts supports an inference of inducement.

- Timestamps and metadata: platform-generated timestamps on posts, edits, and metadata can establish that a post preceded a surge of trading. Metadata also can establish that content was posted or removed strategically to conceal a scheme.

- Content archives and communications logs: archived versions of social‑media posts, screenshots, private messages, emails, newsletters, and promotional materials establish the substance of the deceptive communications.

- Platform analytics: engagement metrics—views, likes, comments, clickthroughs—can help quantify the reach and likely impact of promotions on potential buyers.

- Financial records showing realized gains: bank records, securities-account liquidation records, and profit-and-loss schedules demonstrate whether and when the defendant converted inflated prices into cash.

- Market data: order‑book snapshots, price and volume series, and comparators (similar securities, index movements) enable experts to opine whether observed price movements were plausibly linked to the defendant’s activity rather than to independent market news.

At trial, the government stitches these materials into a chronology: post → investor purchases → price increase → defendant sales. That chronology provides both the “in connection with” nexus and proof of the defendant’s objective to obtain money or property by deception.

## Defensive arguments: intervening market forces and price volatility

Defendants typically raise a range of defenses to break the “in connection with” link, many of which focus on causation and temporal proximity. The most common defensive arguments are:

- Intervening market forces: defendants may argue that independent news, analyst reports, legitimate market interest, or macroeconomic events explain price movements and investor purchases, severing the causal link between their statements and trades. To counter this, the government must show that the deceptive messages were a material contributing cause of the transactions or that the defendants’ conduct was a reasonably foreseeable source of the trades.

- Price volatility and coincidence: especially in small‑cap or penny markets, prices move rapidly for many reasons. Defendants contend that any purchases by followers were coincidental or part of ordinary market churn. Courts assess the totality of the evidence—timing, content, reach, and coordination—rather than rely on price movements alone.

- Lack of intent to induce trades: defendants may admit making misstatements but claim they never intended to influence anyone’s buying or selling decisions, asserting the statements were opinions, jokes, or non‑actionable puffery. Whether a statement is a nonactionable opinion is a mixed legal and factual question; courts examine the specificity and factual nature of the statement, whether it was presented as a material fact, and the speaker’s relationship to the audience.

- Absence of proximate causal link to a discrete victim: in some cases, defendants argue that because the purported victims received marketable securities of at least equal value (or because the defendants did not intend to make victims worse off), there is no actionable deprivation. Kousisis resolves the legal side of that argument—actual net pecuniary loss is not required—so the defense must instead undermine the claimed inducement or prove independent causation.

A successful defense strategy often requires undermining the government’s chronology, presenting alternate explanations for trading, and demonstrating that the challenged statements were too vague, rhetorical, or speculative to have been reasonably relied upon by investors.

## Applying the doctrine: how to evaluate new fact patterns

When confronting a new fact pattern, construct the “in connection with” analysis by asking a series of practical questions:

1. What was the content of the communication? Was it a factual claim likely to affect investment decisions, or mere puffery/opinion?
2. To whom was it communicated? A broad public, a group of retail followers, or a targeted institutional buyer?
3. What is the timing relationship between communication and trades? Is there credible evidence that trades followed the communication and that defendant trades followed the induced buying?
4. Was there an intent to induce trades? Do the communications, pattern of conduct, or defendant’s trading behavior suggest that obtaining money or property by prompt selling was an objective?
5. Are there plausible alternative market explanations for the trading and price movement? If so, how strong are they relative to the evidence of inducement?
6. What documentary and digital records exist (timestamps, trade blotters, analytics) to prove the sequence and effect?

If the answers point to material false or misleading statements directed at potential buyers, followed in time by purchase activity and defendant sales that realize gains, the “in connection with” requirement is likely satisfied under § 1348. Conversely, if misstatements are remote in time, too speculative to be material, or demonstrably unrelated to trading activity, the government’s theory will be weaker.

## Conclusion

The “in connection with” requirement under § 1348 is satisfied when deception is functionally tied to the purchase or sale of a security—when misrepresentations are intended to, and reasonably calculated to, induce trading and the defendant obtains money or property as a consequence. Courts apply a flexible, fact‑sensitive standard that examines the substance and timing of communications, the audience reached, and circumstantial evidence demonstrating that trading followed the deception. Recent decisions—most notably Ciminelli and Kousisis, and as applied in Cayce—clarify two important limits: the government cannot rest a fraud charge on a theory that only deprived a decisionmaker of economic information (the right‑to‑control theory), but it can proceed under a fraudulent‑inducement theory that protects money and property obtained by causing purchases or sales. In the age of digital platforms, careful pleading and rigorous use of trade records, timestamps, and platform analytics are essential to bridge the statutory nexus and prove that online promotion was “in connection with” securities transactions.

# Comparative and Emerging Authority

This chapter surveys recent circuit decisions and prosecutorial trends that shape criminal securities-fraud practice in the wake of the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli v. United States and Kousisis v. United States. Those cases clarified the limits of fraud prosecutions built on theories that deprive victims of merely “valuable economic information” (the so‑called right‑to‑control theory) and reaffirmed the viability of a fraudulent‑inducement theory that targets schemes aimed at obtaining money or property through material misrepresentations. The post‑Ciminelli/Kousisis landscape is best understood as a recalibration: prosecutors must frame securities fraud charges to allege the obtaining of money or property by deceit, and courts must distinguish between cognizable property deprivations and informational injuries that fall outside the reach of the criminal fraud statutes. The leading circuit authorities, recent applications to inducement cases (including social‑media “pump and dump” prosecutions), the Department of Justice’s evolving charging practices, the interplay between civil and criminal securities doctrines, and the particular evidentiary and jurisdictional problems raised by technology‑driven schemes are all developing in response to those doctrinal guardrails.

## Fifth Circuit guidance: Greenlaw; Evans; Baker

The Fifth Circuit has been the principal source of circuit guidance in this area, and its jurisprudence provides the textual and analytic building blocks that the courts use to assess whether an indictment pleads the “scheme to defraud” and the requisite “intent to defraud.” Three district and circuit decisions—United States v. Greenlaw, United States v. Evans, and United States v. Baker—illustrate how the Fifth Circuit treats materiality, deception, and the relationship between the defendant’s gain and the victim’s loss.

Greenlaw (84 F.4th 325) articulates the black‑letter formulation the panel relied upon in the pump‑and‑dump appeal: section 1348 “borrows key concepts” from the mail and wire fraud statutes, and a securities fraud charge requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” The “scheme” element turns on a material misrepresentation intended to deceive in order to obtain something of value—most commonly money or property—from the deceived party. Greenlaw’s reiteration of the two‑part framework provides the doctrinal baseline that other courts apply when assessing post‑Ciminelli claims.

Evans (892 F.3d 692) focuses on the first element—what counts as deception—and emphasizes that the government must plead a material misrepresentation or omission calculated to deceive. Evans also shows how courts analyze materiality in the corporate and securities contexts: the misrepresentation must be such that it would be likely to affect a reasonable investor’s decisionmaking. Evans thus supplies the material‑misstatement standard that anchors a fraudulent‑inducement theory: the government must identify the misstatements or deceptive omissions and tie them causally to the victim’s act of delivering money or property.

Baker (923 F.3d 390) is especially important because it addresses how courts treat the symmetry (or lack thereof) between a defendant’s gain and a victim’s loss. Baker explains that the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” loss—i.e., that the defendant’s gain be precisely equal to the victim’s loss. Instead, what matters is whether the defendant sought to obtain money or property through deceit; the victim’s ultimate net pecuniary position is not dispositive. That principle became critical after Kousisis clarified that a defendant may be guilty even if the defendant furnishes something of equal apparent value, so long as the victim was induced to transfer money or property pursuant to materially false pretenses.

Taken together, these Fifth Circuit precedents provide the analytical scaffolding adopted in the pump‑and‑dump opinion discussed earlier. They show that a properly pled indictment must (a) identify the material misrepresentations or deceptive omissions, (b) allege that the misstatements were intended to induce the transfer of money or property, and (c) allege the defendant’s intent to obtain that money or property—even when the victims later receive marketable securities or some other benefit.

## Seventh Circuit’s application of Kousisis (Smith) to inducement cases

The Seventh Circuit was quick to apply the Supreme Court’s Kousisis decision to cases involving social‑media‑driven inducement. In United States v. Smith (No. 23‑2840 et seq., 2025 WL 2406104), the court confronted facts similar to those in many modern pump‑and‑dump cases: social‑media influencers touted securities, followers purchased, and the influencers then sold positions. Smith applied Kousisis to validate criminal charges premised on fraudulent inducement even where victims may have received securities in exchange for their money and may not have suffered net pecuniary loss.

Smith demonstrates several practical points. First, it endorses the Kousisis distinction between the right‑to‑control theory (invalid under Ciminelli) and the fraudulent‑inducement theory (valid under Kousisis): where the government alleges that the defendant used misrepresentations to induce the purchase of a security, it is alleging the obtaining of money or property, not merely informational deprivation. Second, Smith affirms that a victim’s receipt of marketable securities does not automatically defeat a fraud indictment, provided the government pleads that the victim purchased those securities in reliance on a material misstatement and that the defendant intended to obtain money or property thereby. Third, Smith clarifies evidentiary contours: courts will expect prosecutors to show temporal and causal connections between misstatements and subsequent purchases, and to produce corroborating transactional records (brokerage statements, timing of trades, profit realizations).

Smith thereby functions as a model for other circuits that must decide how to apply Kousisis in contexts where communications are broadcast to a wide audience via social platforms. The Seventh Circuit’s reasoning makes clear that courts will look to the mechanics of the transaction—who bought, when they bought, what the influencer knew and failed to disclose, and whether the influencer reaped identifiable profits—instead of relying on amorphous theories that treat information itself as the victim’s property.

## Post‑Ciminelli charging trends and DOJ guidance themes

After Ciminelli and especially after Kousisis, the Justice Department adjusted charging practices and messaging. Several themes recur in post‑Ciminelli DOJ guidance and in charging decisions:

- Framing the theory: USAOs and Main Justice now focus on pleading fraudulent inducement when the facts support it. That means carefully framing allegations to show that the defendant used material misrepresentations to induce transfers of money or property (e.g., purchases of securities), rather than alleging an abstract interference with decisionmaking or the “right to control” assets.

- Particularization of misstatements and timing: Indictments increasingly specify (i) the exact misrepresentations; (ii) the platform and audience; (iii) the timing of purchases by followers; and (iv) the defendant’s secret sales or profit‑taking. Prosecutors recognize that Ciminelli invites scrutiny of generic allegations, so more precise factual pleading helps survive pretrial challenges.

- Reliance and causation: Charging memos and grand jury presentations emphasize evidence showing that the defendant’s communications were seen and relied upon by purchasers—screenshots, subscription lists, timestamps, brokerage records, and communications indicating that followers bought in response to posts.

- Mens rea evidence: Because criminal securities fraud requires intent to defraud, prosecutors focus on circumstantial proof of intent—immediate sales after promotional activity, lack of disclosure of positions, use of anonymous or coordinated accounts, concealment of compensation, and contemporaneous incriminating communications. Willful blindness and knowledge inferred from a pattern of conduct are often highlighted.

- Coordination with civil enforcement: DOJ increasingly coordinates with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and state authorities, sharing evidence and aligning charges where possible. That coordination is useful for securing civil remedies, disgorgement, and preserving evidence, but DOJ retains prosecutorial independence and must adhere to criminal standards of proof and mens rea.

- Technology and investigative tools: DOJ guidance has acknowledged the need for specialized technological tools—data analytics to trace transaction patterns, subpoenas to platform operators, geolocation and IP logs—to support allegations that online statements caused particular financial movements.

In short, the Department’s charging posture evolved from earlier eras when prosecutorial theory might be more elastic, toward particularized, evidence‑heavy indictments that tie misrepresentations to actual transfers of money or property.

## Civil‑criminal cross‑pollination: lessons and limits

Civil securities enforcement and criminal prosecution often draw on the same factual matrix—misstatements to investors, trading records, and internal communications—but the doctrines, standards, and objectives differ. Understanding what each regime borrows from the other and where the lines must be drawn is crucial for practitioners on both sides.

Lessons that transfer from civil to criminal practice include materiality analysis, the importance of chronology, and the utility of transaction records and communications evidence. Civil securities suits inform the criminal understanding of what statements would be material to an investor and what circumstantial evidence supports an inference of scienter. For example, Rule 10b‑5 civil litigation has developed extensive standards on materiality and causation that criminal prosecutors look to when drafting indictments and presenting cases to grand juries.

But limits are significant. Criminal law requires a higher mens rea—intent to defraud—than many civil claims, which can be grounded on negligence or recklessness depending on the theory. Civil liability can be remedial and regulatory in nature; criminal law focuses on punishment and requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Importantly, the Ciminelli line demonstrates a doctrinal divergence: civil remedies under securities laws have been interpreted more expansively in some contexts, whereas criminal statutes must hew to the Supreme Court’s narrowing construction of what constitutes “property” for fraud purposes. Thus, theories viable in civil suits (e.g., claims based on misleading disclosures affecting the market or investor decisionmaking) may not be sustainable as criminal charges absent proof that the defendant sought to obtain money or property.

Cross‑agency cooperation is another area of cross‑pollination and tension. The SEC’s investigations frequently supply facts and leads to prosecutors, and the SEC will often bring parallel administrative or civil actions. Practitioners must recognize the strategic interaction: a civil settlement or administrative finding can be persuasive in a criminal case but cannot substitute for the government’s burden to prove criminal intent. Likewise, executives should not assume that success in an internal or administrative proceeding precludes later criminal exposure if prosecutors can marshal evidence of fraudulent inducement.

Finally, civil discovery regimes can materially aid criminal defense but are not identical; issues of privilege, Fifth Amendment protections, and timing differ. Criminal practitioners must navigate these institutional differences while drawing useful lessons where appropriate.

## Technology‑driven schemes: bots, group chats, and signal services

Technology has changed the mechanics of securities fraud and accordingly has pushed courts and prosecutors to adapt doctrinally and tactically. Common modern features include coordinated bot networks, encrypted or ephemeral messaging on platforms like Telegram and Discord, subscription‑based “signal” services promising trade tips, and algorithm‑driven amplification. Each raises particular legal and evidentiary challenges.

First, the multiplicity and anonymity of accounts complicate attribution. Proving that a named defendant controlled accounts that amplified promotions or that a particular post was seen by a particular cohort of investors requires combining platform metadata, IP address logs, device fingerprints, and corroborating witness testimony. Courts are increasingly receptive to metadata and behavioral signatures as evidence of control or coordination, but defense counsel frequently attack the chain of custody, the integrity of logs, or the sufficiency of platform responses to subpoenas.

Second, coordinated bot activity and synthetic engagement can be used to create the illusion of a market consensus or to inflate perceived demand. Prosecutors often seek to demonstrate that defendants steered or paid for such activity, and also to show a temporal linkage between the artificial promotion and follower purchases. The law treats use of bots as one form of deceptive conduct; when bots are used to influence trading and money changes hands, that behavior is squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement frame.

Third, subscription signal services and private chatrooms raise doctrinal questions about “in connection with” the purchase or sale of securities. Courts look for whether the defendant’s communications were reasonably calculated to reach investors who would act—whether through public broadcasts, targeted solicitations, or closed communities where members can be shown to have traded in response. A message in a closed paid chatroom that explicitly instructs members to buy, and where members’ trading records show purchases shortly thereafter, fits comfortably within the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

Fourth, encryption and ephemeral messaging create evidence collection problems. Investigators must use lawful surveillance, preservation requests, and cooperative subpoenas to obtain messages before deletion. Where platforms are uncooperative or data is stored overseas, prosecutors resort to mutual legal assistance treaties, civil discovery in parallel cases, or creative use of device forensics to recover deleted material.

Finally, the prevalence of algorithmic trading and high‑frequency trades raises the question whether certain trading patterns constitute manipulation rather than fraud by inducement. Market‑manipulation statutes and SEC rules address spoofing, layering, and wash trading, and prosecutors will sometimes pursue overlapping counts (fraud and market manipulation) when the technology was used to generate artificial price movement that translated into profits obtained by deception.

The upshot for practitioners is that technology both enlarges the opportunities for fraud and complicates proof: prosecutors must assemble multidisciplinary evidence to show that online activity caused people to part with money or property, and defense counsel have multiple avenues—metadata reliability, lack of causation, or legitimate market speech—to dispute those allegations.

## Venue, jurisdiction, and multi‑district coordination in online schemes

Online schemes spread across state and national borders, creating complex venue and jurisdictional issues. The traditional venue doctrine in fraud cases focuses on where an element of the offense occurred—where the scheme was planned, where the mails or wires were used, where the effects were felt, or where the victims resided. In the social‑media context, those lines blur.

Courts have applied an “effects” analysis: a federal prosecution can be venued where the defendant’s conduct had a foreseeable and substantial effect—where victims lost money or where significant events (sales, transfers, executions) occurred. In practice, prosecutors frequently charge in districts that combine investigative convenience (where evidence or witnesses are located), where significant losses were concentrated, or where the grand jury can most easily assemble corroborating testimony. Defense counsel routinely challenge venue, pointing to dispersed victims and to the fact that the defendant may never have physically been present in the forum. Those challenges succeed only when the court finds a lack of a sufficient connection between the defendant’s conduct and the forum.

Large‑scale online fraud often touches multiple districts; criminal cases may be coordinated through transfer motions under 28 U.S.C. § 1404 (change of venue in criminal cases is rarer but still possible in the interests of justice), or more commonly through investigative coordination by the U.S. Attorney’s Office and Main Justice. Unlike civil cases, criminal cases are not consolidated through MDL panels, but parallel prosecutions sometimes lead to coordination agreements among USAOs to avoid duplicative charges, to preserve scarce witness testimony, and to manage discovery efficiently. In complex frauds involving many defendants and many affected jurisdictions, prosecutors and defense counsel may litigate severance, consolidation for trial, and discovery logistics.

For practitioners, the key remedies to venue and jurisdictional problems include early venue challenges, motions for a bill of particulars (to force more precise allegations about where predicate acts occurred), and fact‑intensive discovery into the reach and effect of the defendant’s communications. Prosecutors should expect such litigation and should draft indictments with careful attention to jurisdictional predicates—identifying specific wires, transactions, or victims tied to the chosen forum.

## International dimensions: cross‑border promotion and extraterritoriality questions

Transnational promotion of securities—whether via foreign influencers, offshore brokers, or international chatrooms—raises extraterritoriality and evidence‑gathering issues. Two doctrinal axes structure the analysis: (1) whether U.S. criminal statutes reach foreign conduct; and (2) how investigators obtain evidence located abroad.

On the first axis, the touchstone is conduct that has a substantial and intended effect in the United States. Courts will apply a targeting analysis: did the defendant direct conduct toward U.S. investors or do acts that foreseeably caused injury in the United States? While Morrison v. National Australia Bank constrains extraterritorial application of SEC civil claims and emphasizes transactions in U.S. markets, criminal statutes can be applied extraterritorially when Congress clearly intends or where the statutes reach conduct that has domestic effects—particularly when communications were directed at U.S. platforms, U.S. brokerage accounts were used, or U.S. investors were harmed. Nevertheless, foreign defendants not present in the United States raise practical and legal hurdles, including extradition limits and comity concerns.

On the second axis, obtaining evidence abroad is often slow and uncertain. Mutual legal assistance treaties (MLATs), Letters Rogatory, and cooperative relationships with foreign regulators and law‑enforcement agencies are the primary mechanisms. Prosecutors commonly coordinate parallel investigations with foreign authorities; sometimes the result is simultaneous charges abroad and in the United States. Where platform data or exchange records are hosted overseas, prosecutors must weigh the feasibility of obtaining that evidence against alternative domestic proof—such as aggregated trading records, U.S. witnesses, or the defendant’s domestic contacts.

International enforcement can also raise questions about deference to foreign regulatory schemes and the possibility of double jeopardy when foreign prosecutions precede U.S. charges. Practitioners should anticipate diplomatic and evidentiary frictions, and tailor charging decisions to the strength of domestically obtainable evidence and the likelihood of effective international cooperation.

Practical defense considerations include early motion practice to contest jurisdictional reach, pressing for specific factual allegations tying defendant conduct to the United States, and challenging the sufficiency of foreign evidence. Prosecutors should document the nexus to the United States carefully in indictments and be prepared to show that the defendant’s conduct was aimed at or had foreseeable consequences in the United States.

Conclusion: Bringing the strands together

The post‑Ciminelli and post‑Kousisis jurisprudence establishes a disciplined framework for criminal securities fraud: courts and prosecutors must distinguish between protected property deprivations (money or property obtained through deceit) and non‑cognizable informational injuries, and prosecutors must plead and prove deceptive inducement of transfers of value with sufficient particularity. Circuit authorities—most prominently the Fifth Circuit’s Greenlaw, Evans, and Baker decisions and the Seventh Circuit’s application in Smith—illustrate how that framework operates in practice. DOJ charging adjustments reflect a posture that favors specificity, demonstrable causation, and robust mens rea proof, while technology, venue realities, and international reach demand novel evidence‑gathering strategies and careful choice of forum.

For students and practitioners, the practical takeaways are straightforward: when evaluating or crafting a criminal securities fraud case in the modern, digitally mediated marketplace, (1) frame the theory as fraudulent inducement when the evidence supports it; (2) particularize the misstatements, the audience, the timing of trades, and the defendant’s profits; (3) collect and preserve the digital trail (platform metadata, brokerage records, and device evidence); (4) anticipate venue and jurisdictional disputes and plan accordingly; and (5) recognize both the utility and the limits of civil enforcement parallels. Those steps will help ensure that complaints and indictments survive threshold scrutiny and that prosecutions focus on the classic criminal fraud target: obtaining money or property by deceit.

# Significance and Takeaways of Constantinescu

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu is a pivotal post-Ciminelli treatment of fraud prosecutions built around social-media “pump-and-dump” campaigns. The opinion confirms—and explains how to plead—what prosecutors must allege to charge fraudulent conduct under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 when defendants use publicity and social influence to induce others to buy securities. It does so by embracing the fraudulent-inducement theory recognized by the Supreme Court in Kousisis and by delineating the doctrinal boundaries separating protected speech and actionable fraud. For students and practitioners this opinion is consequential in three ways: it restates what § 1348 requires elementally; it explains how profit-seeking promoters can supply the requisite mens rea; and it offers practical guidance on how to plead and defend cases that arise in influencer-driven markets. The remainder of this chapter teases these strands apart and explains their doctrinal and practical implications.

## Key doctrinal move: embracing fraudulent inducement to satisfy property

At the heart of Constantinescu is the court’s doctrinal move to reject a right-to-control theory and instead embrace a fraudulent-inducement theory as a proper foundation for a § 1348 prosecution. Under the right-to-control approach—rejected in Ciminelli—the government attempts to frame a victim’s “right to control” assets or access to valuable economic information as the protected property. The Supreme Court in Ciminelli held that deprivation of merely valuable economic information, standing alone, does not constitute the type of traditional property interest protected by the federal fraud statutes. That decision forecloses prosecutions that rely solely on a theory that the victim was deprived of the information necessary to make discretionary decisions.

Constantinescu makes explicit that fraud prosecutions grounded on fraudulent inducement are conceptually and legally distinct. A fraudulent-inducement theory alleges that a defendant made material misrepresentations (or omissions in certain circumstances) to cause a victim to enter a transaction that required handing over money or property. The key difference from Ciminelli is that the object of the deceit is the victim’s money or property itself—the purchase price paid for securities—rather than a nebulous “right to control” decisionmaking. The Fifth Circuit therefore holds that an indictment that alleges defendants used social-media misrepresentations to induce followers to buy securities adequately alleges deprivation of a protected property interest. That approach hews to the Supreme Court’s confirmation in Kousisis that fraudulent inducement “protects money and property,” and it squarely situates promoter-driven schemes within the traditional conception of property-based fraud.

Doctrinally, then, Constantinescu reaffirms that the fraud statutes are not limited to classic embezzlement or conversion; they cover schemes that induce others to transfer money or property by means of material misstatements. The proper pleading strategy after Constantinescu is to frame the misconduct as having induced victims to transfer money or property—explicitly alleging the transactional mechanism by which the defendant’s statements caused victims to part with funds—rather than as abstractly depriving victims of information.

## Clarifying mens rea: profit-seeking as intent to obtain victims’ money

Constantinescu also clarifies how to satisfy the mens rea element of § 1348 in influencer-based schemes. The indictment in that case alleged that defendants purchased shares, made misleading posts intended to “pump” demand, and then “dumped” by secretly selling into the inflated market. Defendants argued this was only profit-seeking conduct without an intent to harm victims. The Fifth Circuit, relying on Kousisis, explains why that argument fails: the fraud statutes require an intent to deceive and an intent to obtain money or property as an object of the scheme. Criminal liability does not hinge on a goal to leave the victim economically worse off; instead, liability attaches when the defendant intends to induce the victim to hand over money or property by deceit.

Kousisis is central here. There the Supreme Court made clear that a fraudster who induces a counterparty to contract and thereby obtain the counterparty’s money or property may be criminally liable even if the fraudster provides equally valuable goods or services or does not seek to create a net negative economic outcome for the victim. The Fifth Circuit reads Kousisis to mean that a promoter’s plan to profit—if accomplished by materially deceptive inducements that lead followers to hand over purchase funds—meets the “intent to obtain” element. Thus, allegations that defendants “had money in mind” by orchestrating purchases and sales after making false or misleading statements are sufficient to plead intent.

For practitioners, the doctrinal implication is straightforward: a well-pleaded indictment should allege facts showing that the defendant intended to induce purchases and to capture the proceeds of those purchases. Temporal allegations (purchases before promotion; sales after price spikes), coordinated posting and trading activity, and concealment of trading intent or positions are all classic means of pleading intent to obtain victims’ money.

## Charging guidance: how to structure § 1348 indictments in promotion schemes

Constantinescu is instructive about the structure and contents of a charging document. An indictment under § 1348 should do more than quote statutory language; it must allege the material elements with factual particularity sufficient to give fair notice. Post-Constantinescu prosecutors should allege (1) the fraudulent scheme’s mechanism—how the promotional messages were disseminated and what they said; (2) the specific securities and transactions at issue—what was bought and sold, when, in what quantities, and at what prices; (3) the causal link between the misrepresentations and the victims’ purchases—market reaction in temporal proximity to defendant statements, spikes in volume and price following posts, and statements from victims or aggregated trading patterns; (4) the defendant’s deceptive intent—circumstantial evidence such as timing of trades (buy before, promote, sell after), concealment of holdings or sales, private communications showing coordinated plans, and unusually large profits; and (5) the results—monetary gains traced to the campaign and numbers of victims affected.

It is especially important to frame the allegations as fraudulent inducement rather than as a right-to-control case. The indictment should allege that the defendant’s misstatements caused followers to transfer money (as purchase payments) in exchange for securities. Particularized descriptive allegations—direct quotations from posts, screenshots, and platform identifiers—help establish the content and falsity of the representations. Specific transactional data (brokerage records, transaction timestamps, profit calculations) strengthen the nexus between the deception and the transfer of money. Where possible, pleading that victims were induced to buy and thereby lost the opportunity to retain the funds they used to purchase securities will assist in satisfying the property requirement without invoking the prohibited right-to-control theory.

The Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of pleading sufficiency reminds prosecutors that indictments need not prove the case, but they must adequately allege each material element. Careful pleading of the mechanism of the scheme and the object of the fraud—money or property—avoids the Ciminelli pitfall.

## Boundaries: speech vs. fraud and the role of disclaimers

One of the trickiest doctrinal questions in influencer prosecutions is the line between protected expression and criminal deception. Constantinescu does not dissolve that tension, but it clarifies boundaries. Mere puffery, opinions, and hyperbolic endorsements are generally not actionable. Statements that are non-factual, subjective expressions of belief about a security’s prospects are often protected speech. By contrast, knowingly false factual statements—about a promoter’s holdings, trading activity, the involvement of market insiders, or fabricated buying interest—can form the basis of fraud.

Disclaimers and declarations of non-reliance complicate this terrain. The presence of a boilerplate disclaimer on a post or channel—“not investment advice” or “I’m just sharing my trades”—will not automatically shield promoters if their statements are materially false and intended to deceive. Similarly, a disclosure that the promoter holds a position does not excuse affirmative misstatements about the size of holdings, the sequencing of trades, or the existence of coordinated purchasers. Courts will examine the overall context: whether disclaimers were conspicuously placed, contemporaneous with the promotional content, truthful, and adequate to cure misleading impressions created by the rest of the message.

Omissions are particularly thorny. A duty to disclose material facts typically arises from a statutory or fiduciary relationship, or when an omission renders other statements misleading. Most social-media promoters do not occupy fiduciary roles to their followers. Nonetheless, an omission can be actionable where the promoter makes statements that would be misleading without disclosure of certain facts—most commonly, a failure to disclose that the promoter is secretly selling into the market while urging purchases. Constantinescu suggests that concealment of the promoter’s sales plans can be probative of deceitful intent when paired with affirmative misrepresentations that induce purchases.

In sum, authenticity and context matter: whether speech is actionable turns on whether the communicator asserted false factual claims (as opposed to opinions), whether the audience reasonably relied on the communicator’s factual representations, and whether omissions or misleading framing rendered the overall message deceptive. Disclaimers are a relevant but not decisive factor.

## Practical implications for influencers, promoters, and trading communities

The practical impact of Constantinescu is immediate for anyone who uses social media to discuss securities. Influencers should recognize that statements about securities can cross into criminal liability if they knowingly or recklessly disseminate materially false representations to induce purchases. Best practices from a risk-management perspective include avoiding false or misleading claims about one’s own trades; disclosing conflicts of interest and trading positions prominently and contemporaneously; abstaining from touting a security while effecting private sales in the same security; and keeping clear, contemporaneous records of trade intent and trading activity to demonstrate good-faith conduct.

Promoters who are paid to advertise or who coordinate with issuers or brokers should document compensation relationships and avoid making factually false claims about a company’s business prospects or their own trading. Trading communities and platforms may face increased attention; administrators and moderators should consider policies that require disclosure of material conflicts and that prohibit coordinated pump-and-dump activity. Market participants who rely on influencer content must be aware that apparent “insider” claims or too-good-to-be-true promotional narratives may mask manipulation.

Regulators and intermediaries will likely increase monitoring of cross-platform promotion and the use of private channels (direct messages, closed groups) to coordinate market-moving messages. For sophisticated users, Constantinescu underscores that the absence of a formal contract or payment does not immunize a promoter if the underlying conduct demonstrates an intent to induce purchases and capture the proceeds.

## Open questions: materiality of trading intent, extent of disclosure duties

While Constantinescu clarifies some issues, it leaves open multiple doctrinal questions that will be litigated in the coming years. First, the materiality of a promoter’s undisclosed trading intent is not definitively resolved. Is a promoter’s failure to disclose that he intends to sell at a certain price a material omission in every circumstance, or does materiality depend on the nature of the statement and the reasonable expectations of the audience? Materiality remains a fact-sensitive inquiry: courts will likely ask whether a reasonable investor would have considered knowledge of the promoter’s trading plan important in making an investment decision. The answer will turn on context—platform norms, the prominence of the promoter’s claims, and how the omitted information interacts with the promotional message.

Second, the scope of disclosure duties for non-fiduciary promoters is unclear. Traditionally, duties to disclose arise from a special relationship or statutory mandate. Constantinescu suggests that omissions can be actionable when they make affirmative statements misleading, but it does not establish a per se duty for every influencer to disclose positions, compensation, or timing. Judges and juries will need to reconcile general principles about omissions with market realities where social-media posts often function as solicitations without formal investor relationships.

Relatedly, the line between puffery and materially misleading statements will continue to be contested. Aggregated market data, the sophistication of followers, and the format of communications (threaded tweets, livestreams with chat) are all contextual factors that bear on whether a statement is a provably false factual claim or a non-actionable opinion.

## Anticipated defenses and evidentiary battles at trial

Defense strategies in cases like Constantinescu will predictably focus on several fronts. First, defendants will assert that their posts were opinions or puffery, not materially false factual representations. To succeed, defendants will press for jury instructions emphasizing the difference between opinion and false statement and challenge the sufficiency of government proof about falsity. Second, defendants will argue lack of intent, offering contemporaneous evidence of genuine belief in their statements—screenshots showing internal analysis, expert reports, or testimony about trading strategies—to rebut circumstantial inferences from trading patterns.

Causation and reliance present further battlegrounds. Defendants will contend that price movements resulted from independent market forces and that followers’ purchases were not caused by the defendant’s posts. The government will respond with market microstructure analysis showing temporal correlations between posts and volume spikes, declarations from affected investors, and platform analytics indicating engagement and flow-through. Expect extensive battles over expert testimony: market impact, attribution of trades to particular posts, and traceability of proceeds will all require experts to explain complex trading data to juries.

Discovery disputes will be fierce. Prosecutors will seek private communications (direct messages, group chats), brokerage records, and platform metadata; defendants will litigate the scope of compelled disclosures and may assert privacy or First Amendment concerns about compelled production of speech-related records. The admissibility of social-media posts (authentication, hearsay exceptions, and context) will be litigated, as will the relevance of disclaimers and contemporaneous disclosure. Finally, the applicability of SEC regulatory findings and parallel civil enforcement actions will affect evidentiary strategies and motions in limine.

## Policy considerations: investor protection and market integrity in the social era

Constantinescu locates the criminal law at the intersection of investor protection and new communications technologies. On the one hand, the decision signals robust enforcement priorities: regulators and prosecutors may treat social-media-driven manipulation as a serious threat to market integrity and retail investors. The amplification effects of large followings, coupled with the speed of execution in modern markets, make pump-and-dump schemes particularly potent and potentially destructive. Criminal enforcement serves deterrence goals and complements regulatory tools that the Securities and Exchange Commission and self-regulatory organizations employ.

On the other hand, heavy-handed prosecution risks chilling legitimate speech. Many market commentators provide valuable analysis and share trading ideas that contribute to market efficiency. Prosecutors and courts must calibrate enforcement to avoid penalizing ordinary market commentary and to preserve robust public discourse. That calibration requires careful doctrinal work—distinguishing factual misrepresentations (or misleading omissions) made with deceptive intent from protected opinion and bona fide analysis.

Policy makers also face resource-allocation questions. Social-media surveillance is technically demanding and can result in enormous caseloads if pursued aggressively. The public policy balance may favor targeted enforcement against demonstrable, coordinated campaigns that use deception to harvest investor funds, rather than broad policing of influencer speech. The opinion therefore suggests that enforcement should be guided by evidentiary strength: clear false statements, coordinated action, demonstrable transactional links, and traceable illicit gains merit criminal attention.

Conclusion: Constantinescu is a doctrine-shaping decision. It confirms that post-Ciminelli fraud prosecutions grounded in fraudulent inducement—where defendants use deceptive messages to cause transfers of money or property—are legally viable. It clarifies the mens rea inquiry by recognizing profit-seeking, if achieved through deceitful inducement, as the requisite intent to obtain victims’ money. The decision offers concrete drafting and evidentiary lessons for prosecutors, a roadmap for defense counsel to anticipate and counter charges, and a policy template for balancing enforcement with free expression. The most significant enduring lesson for market participants is simple: when public commentary is paired with private trading and a plan to reap the benefits of induced purchases, criminal liability is a real risk.
