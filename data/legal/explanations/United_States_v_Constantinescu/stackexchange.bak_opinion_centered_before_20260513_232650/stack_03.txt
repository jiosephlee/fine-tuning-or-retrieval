Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### What practically distinguishes the rejected right-to-control theory from the permitted fraudulent-inducement theory here?
Question:
The district court dismissed because the indictment allegedly targeted deprivation of valuable economic information, which Ciminelli forbids. The panel says this case instead alleges fraudulent inducement—misstatements that prompted followers to hand over money for securities. Where is the line? If the government alleges that deceptive posts deprived investors of accurate information and thereby induced purchases, is that enough to avoid Ciminelli, or must the indictment do more to show that obtaining money or property (not just controlling decisions) was the object?
Answer:
Short answer: The difference is the object of the alleged scheme. Ciminelli forbids prosecutions that rest only on depriving someone of valuable economic information (a “right-to-control” theory). By contrast, the fraudulent‑inducement theory alleges that the defendant used material misstatements to trick victims into handing over money or property (e.g., buying securities). If the indictment pleads that the misrepresentations induced purchasers to part with money or property (or that obtaining money/property was the object), it avoids Ciminelli; an allegation that the defendant merely withheld or corrupted information without alleging inducement to transfer money or property does not.

Why the Fifth Circuit reached that result in Constantinescu
- Ciminelli held that the fraud statutes protect “traditional property interests” and rejected a right‑to‑control theory that treated the “right to valuable economic information” as property. Constantinescu acknowledges that rule and says the indictment here does not rely on that theory. (Constantinescu § III.A; citing Ciminelli.)
- Instead the indictment alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants posted false and misleading statements to induce followers to purchase securities (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14). That is precisely the sort of scheme that targets victims’ money or property, so it “protects money and property” and is not a repackaged right‑to‑control claim. The panel relied on Kousisis for that distinction. (Constantinescu § III.A, quoting Kousisis.)

What the government must plead to survive a Ciminelli challenge
- Allegations that identify the object as obtaining money or property. In Constantinescu the court pointed to allegations that defendants “induced their followers . . . to purchase securities” and profited (the alleged $114 million); that shows the object was obtaining money/property. (Constantinescu § I & III.)
- Allegations that the defendant used material misstatements to cause victims to enter transactions that required handing over money or property (the core of the fraudulent‑inducement theory). The court emphasized that fraudulent inducement “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” (Constantinescu § III.A, quoting Kousisis.)
- The indictment must allege the requisite mental state (intent to deceive and an intent related to the scheme). But note: under Kousisis, the government need not allege that the defendant sought to leave victims economically worse off; it is enough that the defendant intended to obtain money/property (Constantinescu § III.B).

Practical line to draw (how to apply)
- If the government’s theory is: “defendant lied/misled so victims lost access to accurate information about how to exercise discretion” and it rests only on that loss of information → Ciminelli problem.
- If the government alleges: “defendant lied/misled and, as a result, victims purchased securities (or transferred money/property) that they would not have purchased but for the misrepresentations” or otherwise alleges that obtaining money/property was the object → fraudulent‑inducement theory that survives Ciminelli.

So to your hypotheticals: alleging deceptive posts that deprived investors of accurate information is not enough by itself. To avoid Ciminelli the indictment must also tie those deceptions to induced transfers — i.e., allege that the misrepresentations caused purchases or other transfers of money/property (or that obtaining money/property was the scheme’s object). Constantinescu held exactly that pleading sufficed where the indictment alleges inducement to buy and a profit motive (the “pump and dump” allegations).
