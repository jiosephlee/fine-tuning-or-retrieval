Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### What gives the Fifth Circuit appellate jurisdiction over the government’s appeal of a pretrial dismissal of the indictment?
Question:
The district court granted a Rule 12 dismissal for failure to state an offense, and the opinion says, "The government timely appealed." How does the Fifth Circuit have jurisdiction to hear the government’s interlocutory appeal in a criminal case before trial? Is this based on 18 U.S.C. § 3731, and how does that statute avoid any Double Jeopardy concerns when the indictment is dismissed before jeopardy attaches?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the Fifth Circuit’s authority to hear the government’s pretrial appeal comes from the federal government‑appeal statute, 18 U.S.C. § 3731. That statute authorizes the United States to appeal certain pretrial orders, including dismissal of an indictment, and does so in a way that does not run afoul of the Double Jeopardy Clause because a dismissal prior to trial (and prior to the attachment of jeopardy) is not an acquittal and therefore does not bar a merits appeal and retrial if the appeal succeeds.

Explanation and authorities (concise):

- Statutory basis for jurisdiction. The government’s interlocutory appeal of a dismissal of an indictment is governed by 18 U.S.C. § 3731. Section 3731(a) permits the United States to appeal, within the time limits the statute sets, certain pretrial orders, including an order “dismissing an indictment or information.” The opinion’s statement that the government “timely appealed” signals that the government invoked that statutory right to appeal the district court’s Rule 12 dismissal.

- Why this does not violate Double Jeopardy. The Double Jeopardy Clause forbids retrial after an acquittal (or other circumstances where jeopardy has attached and been terminated in favor of the defendant). But a district court’s dismissal of an indictment before trial is not necessarily an acquittal and does not always mean jeopardy has attached. Section 3731 expressly contemplates government appeals of pretrial dismissals; Congress provided that procedure because interlocutory dismissal is not the sort of final acquittal that Double Jeopardy protects against. If the appellate court reverses the dismissal (as the Fifth Circuit did here), the government may proceed to trial; if the dismissal was in form an acquittal on the merits, the statute and courts recognize limits on appeals so that an actual acquittal cannot be undone.

- How that fits with the opinion. The opinion treats the matter as a routine, timely government appeal of a Rule 12 dismissal (the panel reverses and remands). The court does not treat the district court’s order as an acquittal, and the panel addresses only sufficiency of the indictment (de novo review). That posture — a statutory government appeal of a pretrial dismissal raising legal sufficiency questions — is precisely the situation § 3731 was designed to cover and is consistent with Double Jeopardy doctrine because jeopardy had not attached by trial.

Practical takeaway: When the government believes a court’s pretrial dismissal of an indictment is legally erroneous, it may invoke § 3731 to appeal; that procedure is permissible because a pretrial dismissal (absent a true acquittal) does not trigger Double Jeopardy protections that would bar appellate review and retrial if the dismissal is reversed.

### Why does the court review the sufficiency of the indictment de novo and accept the allegations as true?
Question:
The panel cites Rafoi and Fontenot for de novo review and says the court must take the indictment’s allegations as true at the motion-to-dismiss stage. Can someone explain why factual disputes about whether the posts were actually misleading or whether followers relied on them can’t be resolved now? Is this because Rule 12(b)(3)(B) only tests legal sufficiency, not evidentiary sufficiency?
Answer:
Short answer: Because on a motion to dismiss for failure to state an offense the court asks only whether the indictment, taken as true, alleges the necessary legal elements — not whether the government can prove those allegations. That is why the panel reviews sufficiency de novo and accepts the indictment’s allegations as true; competing factual proofs about whether posts were actually misleading or followers relied on them are matters for trial (or an evidentiary motion), not for a Rule 12-type sufficiency challenge.

Why (legal doctrine and how the opinion applies it)
- The applicable standard is set out in Part II of the opinion: the circuit “reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo” (citing Rafoi) and “[t]he court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated” (quoting Fontenot, and Crow). That is the classic pleading-sufficiency rule: ask whether the charging document alleges each material element of the offense, fairly notifies the defendant, and protects against double jeopardy (Gaytan; Guzman-Ocampo cited in the opinion).
- The panel therefore confines itself to legal sufficiency: does the indictment allege a “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” as required for § 1348? It answers that question by construing the indictment’s allegations (e.g., that defendants posted false and misleading information to induce purchases, Indictment ¶ 13–14) and applying the legal tests from Ciminelli/Kousisis and this circuit’s precedent (Part III).
- That framework explains why factual disputes are not resolved now. The court’s role at this stage is not to weigh evidence or resolve credibility. It decides whether, if the indictment’s allegations are assumed true and reasonable inferences drawn in the government’s favor, the elements of the charged crime have been pleaded. If they have, dismissal is improper and factual disputes remain for trial (or a properly supported pretrial evidentiary ruling).

On Rule 12(b)(3)(B) (your suggestion)
- The opinion does not heavily discuss the federal rules, but its approach aligns with the familiar rule that pretrial motions attacking the sufficiency of an indictment test legal sufficiency — not the government’s evidence. So your intuition is correct in substance: a Rule 12(b) motion that attacks the indictment’s pleadings tests whether the indictment alleges the elements of the offense; it is not an evidentiary vehicle to resolve factual contests about what actually happened or who relied on what. Those factual disputes go to proof at trial (or to a different kind of pretrial proceeding that addresses evidence).

Illustration from the opinion
- The panel takes the indictment’s allegations (e.g., they “pump[ed] the price . . . by posting false and misleading information . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” Indictment ¶ 13) as true and asks whether those allegations, under controlling law (Ciminelli/Kousisis and this court’s precedents), are sufficient to plead a fraudulent-inducement scheme and intent to obtain money or property. Because they are, the court reverses dismissal. It does not decide whether those posts actually were false or whether particular followers in fact relied — those are questions for proof.

Bottom line: De novo review + accepting allegations as true is the correct standard for a pleading-stage sufficiency challenge. It preserves separation between pleading (legal sufficiency) and proof (evidentiary questions reserved for trial or other factual proceedings).

### How does the court reconcile Greenlaw/Evans’s ‘intent to cause harm’ formulation with Kousisis’s rule that no net pecuniary loss is required?
Question:
The opinion quotes Greenlaw/Evans that intent to defraud includes intent to deceive and to cause some harm, but then relies on Kousisis for the proposition that a fraudster need not seek to leave the victim economically worse off. What counts as the legally cognizable ‘harm’ here— is it the induced transfer of money itself, even if the victim receives securities of equal market value at the time?
Answer:
Short answer
They are reconcilable: the “harm” Evans requires is satisfied when the defendant causes the victim to part with money or property by deception — i.e., the induced transfer itself is the legally cognizable harm — even if the victim receives something (like market‑value securities) in return and therefore suffers no net pecuniary loss.

Explanation and doctrinal support from the opinion
1) Different formulations are describing the same core idea. Greenlaw/Evans describe intent to defraud as an intent “to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” That “harm” is not limited to leaving the victim economically worse off in the net; it is the harm of being deprived of money or property through fraudulent inducement.

2) Kousisis supplies the bridge. As the opinion explains, Kousisis held that the fraudulent‑inducement theory criminalizes using a material misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that requires handing over money or property “regardless of whether the fraudster … seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The Fifth Circuit opinion quotes Kousisis: a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off,” and that a fraud is “complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses.” (Opinion §§ III.A–B; citing Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1392, 1394 n.5.)

3) Applied to the indictment here. The court points to the specific allegation that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. That allegation pleads a fraudulent‑inducement scheme in which the defendants’ object was to obtain money by inducing purchases. Under Kousisis, the fact that followers received securities (even of equal market value at the time) does not negate that the followers were induced to part with money; that induced deprivation is the legally cognizable injury/“harm” for intent to defraud purposes. (Opinion § III.B; also citing United States v. Baker and United States v. Smith.)

Takeaway for analysis of similar cases
- If the indictment (or proof) shows the defendant intentionally deceived someone into a transaction that required handing over money or property, that satisfies the “cause some harm” prong even if the victim received something of value in return.
- Ciminelli’s rejection of a right‑to‑control/info‑only theory remains good law: mere deprivation of information is not enough. But Kousisis confirms that a fraudulent inducement that causes the victim to transfer money/property is within the statutes’ reach even without net pecuniary loss.

### Why is the pump-and-dump theory framed as ‘fraudulent inducement’ rather than the ‘right-to-control’ theory rejected in Ciminelli?
Question:
The district court dismissed on the theory the indictment alleged deprivation of economic information, which Ciminelli says is not a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit says this is actually a ‘fraudulent-inducement’ theory because the misstatements induced followers to part with money for securities. What facts transform a case from a barred right-to-control theory (deprivation of information) into a valid fraudulent-inducement theory (deprivation of money or property)?
Answer:
Short answer
Because the indictment here alleges that the defendants used false statements to cause followers to hand over money (by buying securities) and that defendants obtained that money by secretly selling their positions, the theory is framed as fraudulent inducement — not a right‑to‑control theory. The right‑to‑control theory (rejected in Ciminelli) is about depriving victims of valuable economic information; the fraudulent‑inducement theory is about inducing victims to transfer money or property by means of material misrepresentations. The opinion draws that distinction at several points: the indictment alleges inducement to purchase securities (Indictment ¶¶13–14) and a $114 million profit, which is the sort of “money or property” the securities‑fraud statute protects (see discussion drawing on Kousisis and Ciminelli).

What transforms a barred right‑to‑control case into a valid fraudulent‑inducement case
1. A misrepresentation (or omission) that is alleged to have induced a transfer of money or property
   - Fraudulent inducement requires that the defendant’s lie or misstatement tricked the victim into a transaction that required handing over money or property. As the panel says, the indictment alleges defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” (Indictment ¶13). That is a classic fraudulent‑inducement fact pattern: deception + induced purchase = transfer of money/property.
2. Allegations that the defendant obtained money or property as an object of the scheme
   - The opinion emphasizes that §1348 targets schemes to obtain “money or property” and treats fraudulent inducement as protecting such interests (citing Kousisis). Here the indictment alleges defendants “artificially inflat[ed] the securities’ prices and allow[ed] defendants to profit” and even quantifies the proceeds ($114 million). Those allegations show the defendants sought to obtain money/property — not merely to control information.
3. Allegations tying deception to a concrete transaction, not merely to control of decision‑making
   - Ciminelli forbids using the fraud statutes to police deprivation of “valuable economic information” (the “right to control” another’s decision). The Fifth Circuit contrasts that with the present indictment: it “does not mention the right‑to‑control theory” and instead alleges defendants induced purchases (opinion §III.A). So the key factual difference is allegation of induced purchases/transfers rather than only withheld or distorted information that left the victim to make discretionary choices.
4. Intent to obtain (not necessarily to cause net loss)
   - The opinion notes (drawing on Kousisis) that the government need not allege that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss; it is enough that the fraudster intended to obtain the victim’s money/property by deception. Thus, facts that the defendant intended to and did obtain money/property by the deception (e.g., secret dumping and realized profits) convert the theory into fraudulent inducement even if victims received marketable securities in return.

Concrete contrasts (based on the opinion)
- Facts typical of a barred right‑to‑control theory (Ciminelli): payments to a lobbyist that affect discretionary government contracting decisions; the government relied solely on showing deprivation of access to information/decision control. The Court said the “right to valuable economic information … is not a traditional property interest” and cannot support a fraud conviction (Ciminelli, quoted at pp. 310–316 of the opinion).
- Facts that support fraudulent inducement (this case): public misrepresentations that cause followers to buy securities (Indictment ¶¶13–14), defendants secretly selling to realize profits ($114M alleged), and the indictment’s allegation that the object of the scheme was to obtain money/property. The panel treats that as a classic fraudulent‑inducement claim (opinion §III.A–B, relying on Kousisis).

Bottom line for pleading or evaluation
- To avoid Ciminelli’s defect, allege (and support through facts) that the deception induced a transfer of money or property (for example, purchasers sent money to buy securities because of the misstatements), and that the defendant sought to obtain that money/property (including allegations of realized proceeds). Mere allegations that the defendant deprived victims of information needed to make discretionary choices (without alleging induced transfers or obtaining money/property) will look like the forbidden right‑to‑control theory and fail under Ciminelli.

### Does § 1348 require that the defendant obtain money specifically from the deceived person, or is it enough that obtaining money was the object of the scheme?
Question:
The opinion cites Kousisis and Baker to say there’s no ‘mirror image’ requirement. In a public-market pump-and-dump, the defendants may sell into the market and not necessarily to the particular followers who read the posts. Under § 1348, is it sufficient that the object of the deception was to obtain money in connection with the sale of a security, even if the money does not provably come from the very person deceived by the misstatement?
Answer:
Short answer: It is enough that obtaining money was the object of the scheme. § 1348 does not require the government to prove the defendant took money from the very person who read the misstatement; it is sufficient that the defendant fraudulently induced others to part with money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of a security.

Why — legal doctrine and how the opinion applies it
- The court frames § 1348 through the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory (not the “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in Ciminelli). Under that theory the protected interest is money or property obtained by inducing a victim to contract or purchase — i.e., to hand over money or property by reason of the misrepresentation. (Opinion, III.A, citing Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388–98.)
- The Fifth Circuit relied on Kousisis to hold that the fraud statutes punish schemes to “obtain” money or property “regardless of whether the fraudster… seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The Court explained a fraud is complete when the defendant has “induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses.” (Opinion, III.B, discussing Kousisis at 1391–1394.)
- The opinion expressly rejects a “mirror‑image” requirement (i.e., that a victim’s loss must match the defendant’s gain or that the money must come from the particular deceived person). It cites United States v. Baker for the proposition that the statutes do not impose such a mirror‑image rule where the victim’s loss supplied the defendant’s gain. (Opinion, III.B, citing Baker, 923 F.3d at 403–05.)
- The indictment here (Indictment ¶ 13) alleges defendants posted materially false information to induce followers to buy securities—i.e., to part with money—thereby inflating prices so defendants could sell at a profit. The panel held those allegations adequately plead a scheme to obtain money in connection with the sale of securities under § 1348. (Opinion, III, A–B.)

Practical takeaway for a pump‑and‑dump scenario
If the indictment pleads that the defendant made material misrepresentations designed to induce purchases (so people would part with money or property) and that obtaining money was the object, that pleading satisfies § 1348 even if the defendants sold into the public market and cannot be shown to have taken money directly from the individual follower who saw the post. The key is alleged inducement to part with money/property in connection with the purchase or sale of a security, not a literal one‑to‑one transfer from the deceived person to the fraudster.

### Why is the receipt of ‘market-value’ securities by the followers irrelevant to whether a fraud offense is stated?
Question:
The defendants argue no victim was harmed because buyers received securities at then-market prices. The panel says Kousisis forecloses a ‘no net loss’ defense. Can someone clarify doctrinally why an exchange of equivalent value does not defeat the ‘scheme to obtain money or property’ element under § 1348?
Answer:
Short answer: Because under the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory the protected interest is the victim’s money or property that the victim is tricked into handing over, it is the defendant’s fraudulent inducement to part with money/property that matters — not whether the victim later received something of equal market value or was left worse off. The Fifth Circuit relies on Kousisis and Ciminelli to explain that the receipt of market‑value securities does not negate a scheme “to obtain” money or property under 18 U.S.C. § 1348.

Why, doctrinally

- Ciminelli drew a bright line that the fraud statutes do not criminalize mere deprivation of valuable economic information (the “right‑to‑control” theory). The Court held the government cannot base a fraud conviction on the victim’s loss of the right to make an informed discretionary economic decision because that right is not a traditional property interest. (Opinion ¶ III.A summarizing Ciminelli.)

- Kousisis, however, distinguishes a separate theory: fraudulent inducement. Under that theory the defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that causes the victim to hand over money or property. The Supreme Court held that fraudulent inducement “protects money and property” and that the fraud statutes reach schemes that “obtain” money or property even if the fraudster provides something in return or the victim is not left economically worse off. (Opinion ¶¶ III.A–B; quoting Kousisis 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1392, 1394 n.5.)

How that controls this case

- The indictment here alleges defendants posted false information to induce followers to buy securities — i.e., to cause followers to part with money/property — and then sold their own holdings for profit. (Opinion ¶ I, Indictment ¶¶13–14.)

- The panel applies Kousisis to hold that a defendant violates § 1348 when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Thus, the fact that followers received securities at market prices (or that the fraudster also transferred something of value back) does not defeat the element that the defendant sought to obtain money/property by deceit. (Opinion ¶ III.B, quoting Kousisis.)

- The court further notes precedent rejecting a “mirror‑image” requirement (victim loss must equal defendant gain): inducing a transfer of money/property under materially false pretenses completes the fraud even if the victim later receives something of value. (Opinion ¶ III.B; citing United States v. Baker and Seventh Circuit decision applying Kousisis.)

Bottom line

Under the fraudulent‑inducement doctrine the relevant wrong is inducing the victim to hand over money or property by means of material misrepresentations. That inducement — the obtaining of money/property by fraud — is the statutory harm under § 1348. Whether the victim ends up with market‑value securities (or whether the defendant caused a net pecuniary loss) is therefore irrelevant to whether the indictment pleads a “scheme to obtain money or property.” The Fifth Circuit relied on those principles to reverse the dismissal. (Opinion ¶¶ III.A–B, IV.)

### Why does the court import mail and wire fraud doctrine into § 1348 securities fraud analysis?
Question:
The panel relies on Greenlaw to ‘borrow key concepts’ from mail/wire fraud for § 1348. What is the textual or precedential basis for treating § 1348 as coextensive with §§ 1341/1343 on concepts like ‘scheme to defraud,’ materiality, and intent? Are there any doctrinal limits to this borrowing given differences in statutory text (e.g., ‘in connection with’ a security)?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel treats § 1348 as drawing on mail/wire-fraud doctrine because § 1348 is a federal “fraud” provision that uses the same core language and protects the same kinds of interests (money/property), and this circuit’s precedent (Greenlaw) and Supreme Court decisions (Ciminelli and Kousisis, as the panel applies them) authorize importing the operative concepts (scheme to defraud, materiality, intent) so long as the borrowed doctrines are applied consistently with the text of § 1348—most importantly the statute’s “in connection with”/purchase-or-sale requirement and the limitation that the government must allege deprivation of money or property (not merely information).

Why the court borrows mail/wire doctrine (textual/precedential basis)
- Textual similarity: § 1348 criminalizes schemes “to defraud any person in connection with … any security” and schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses … any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of … any security.” Those formulations parallel the mail and wire fraud statutes’ focus on schemes to obtain money or property by deception. The opinion notes this parallel and explicitly says § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” (Op. § III, first paragraph).
- Circuit precedent: The court cites United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023), for the proposition that courts “have given the terms similar treatment” and that § 1348 “borrows key concepts” — i.e., an established practice in this circuit of looking to mail/wire fraud cases to define elements like “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” (Op. § III).
- Supreme Court guidance: The panel frames its analysis through the lens of Ciminelli and Kousisis, using those cases to set doctrinal limits (what theories are permissible) while treating the core mail/wire fraud elements as applicable to securities fraud. For example, after quoting Greenlaw’s elements, the opinion then tests the indictment against Ciminelli’s rejection of the “right-to-control” theory and Kousisis’s approval of the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory (Op. § III.A–B).

What concepts are borrowed and how the opinion defines them
- Scheme to defraud: The court uses the mail/wire-fraud formulation: a scheme requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived” (quoting Greenlaw and Evans) (Op. § III, para. 1–2).
- Intent to defraud: Borrowed definitions require intent to (1) deceive and (2) cause some harm/result from the deceit (again via Greenlaw/Evans) (Op. § III).
- Materiality and whether the object is money/property: The opinion applies Ciminelli to reject theories premised on deprivation of mere valuable economic information, and applies Kousisis to confirm that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is valid because it protects money/property even when the victim is not left economically worse off (Op. § III.A–B).

Doctrinal limits on borrowing (what cannot be imported)
- You cannot use mail/wire fraud doctrine to convict for deprivation of mere economic information: the panel emphasizes Ciminelli’s holding that the fraud statutes protect “traditional property interests” and that the “right-to-control” theory (deprivation of valuable economic information) is not a valid basis for fraud liability (Op. § III.A, discussing Ciminelli, 598 U.S. at 316).
- § 1348’s “in connection with” / purchase-or-sale language matters: borrowing is permissible only insofar as the mail/wire doctrinal concepts are applied consistent with § 1348’s securities nexus. The panel enforces that by distinguishing the present “fraudulent‑inducement” allegation (inducing purchases—i.e., obtaining victims’ money/property) from the rejected right‑to‑control theory. In short, the court borrows the elements but insists the government still allege the required nexus to securities transactions (Op. § III, first paragraph; § III.A).
- The object of the fraud must be money or property (not just information): Even when applying Kousisis, the opinion stresses that the fraudulent‑inducement theory succeeds because it alleges defendants induced followers to purchase securities (part with money/property). The panel repeatedly rejects the idea that a defendant can be convicted for merely depriving someone of market information (Op. § III.A–B).

Practical application to this opinion’s facts
- The indictment alleged a classic fraudulent‑inducement/pump‑and‑dump: defendants made material misrepresentations on social media to induce purchases, then sold for profit. The court treats that as a scheme to obtain money/property “in connection with” securities—so the borrowed mail/wire elements (material misstatements, intent to deceive, intent to obtain money/property) fit and sustain the indictment (Op. § I, III.A–B).
- The panel rejects defendants’ arguments that the indictment only alleges deprivation of information or that profit motive means no intent to defraud: Ciminelli bars right‑to‑control theories but does not bar fraudulent inducement; Kousisis confirms intent can be shown by an object to obtain money even if the victim is not left worse off (Op. § III.A–B).

Bottom line
The court imports mail/wire fraud doctrine into § 1348 because (1) the text and purpose of § 1348 align with the fraud statutes’ protection of money/property by deception; (2) circuit precedent has long relied on mail/wire fraud cases to define the operative concepts; and (3) Supreme Court decisions (Ciminelli and Kousisis) set doctrinal boundaries that the panel enforces. The borrowing is not slavish—courts must still respect statutory differences (especially the “in connection with” requirement and the limitation against treating mere informational deprivation as property). In this case the indictment fits within those bounds because it alleges fraudulent inducement to purchase securities (i.e., obtaining money/property), so the imported mail/wire elements apply.

### Which subsection of § 1348 is the government proceeding under, and does the indictment need to track both prongs?
Question:
Section 1348 has two prongs: (1) a scheme to defraud any person in connection with a security, and (2) obtaining money or property by false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with a security. The opinion references both concepts. Does the government need to charge and prove both, or is alleging either prong sufficient? How does that choice affect the elements (e.g., whether ‘obtaining money or property’ must be shown as an object of the scheme)?
Answer:
Short answer
- The government does not have to plead both prongs of § 1348. It may proceed under either theory so long as the indictment pleads the elements of the theory it actually relies on.
- When the government proceeds on a fraudulent‑inducement/“obtain money or property” theory, the indictment must allege the elements that make that theory a § 1348 offense — i.e., a scheme to defraud (material misrepresentation), intent to deceive, and that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the fraud (the fraudulent inducement to part with money/property).

Why (with citations to the opinion)
- The opinion explains § 1348 contains two formulations but analyzes the case by reference to the elements courts borrow from mail/wire‑fraud doctrine: a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” (Op. § III: “securities fraud requires (1) a ‘scheme to defraud’ (2) enacted with an ‘intent to defraud.’”) The court quotes Evans and Kousisis/Ciminelli to frame those elements.  
- The opinion treats the fraudulent‑inducement theory as a way to satisfy § 1348’s protection of “money or property”: it distinguishes Ciminelli’s rejected “right‑to‑control” theory from the fraudulent‑inducement theory and says the latter “protects money and property.” (Op. § III.A: “the indictment alleges ... fraudulently induc[ing] them to buy securities … a ‘fraudulent‑inducement theory’ ... ‘protects money and property.’” (quoting Kousisis).)  
- The court also relies on Kousisis to state that a fraudster need not intend the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss — what matters is that obtaining money or property was an object of the fraud: “obtaining the victim’s money or property was ‘an object’ of his fraud.” (Op. § III, citing Kousisis and Ciminelli).

What the indictment must therefore do
- If the government relies on the “obtain money or property by false pretenses” formulation (the fraudulent‑inducement theory), the indictment must allege facts showing (1) a material misrepresentation used to induce victims to purchase securities (i.e., to part with money or property), and (2) that obtaining money/property was the object of the scheme (e.g., allegations that defendants “purchased a security, ‘pumped’ the price … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price, and ‘dump’ by secretly selling the security for a profit,” and that defendants profited $114 million). The opinion holds those allegations are sufficient. (Op. ¶¶ 13–14; III.A–B.)  
- The government need not also plead a separate “right‑to‑control” theory (the deprivation‑of‑economic‑information theory) — indeed, the opinion makes clear that a right‑to‑control theory alone would be insufficient under Ciminelli, but that is distinct from the fraudulent‑inducement theory that does allege deprivation of money/property. (Op. § III.A.)

Practical implication for drafting an indictment
- Plead the specific theory and its elements. If using fraudulent inducement, allege the misstatements, the inducement to buy (i.e., victims parted with money/property), the defendant’s intent to deceive, and that obtaining money/property was an object (e.g., profits realized). You do not have to plead both prongs of § 1348 unless the government wants to pursue both theories; an indictment that adequately alleges one lawful theory is sufficient under the standards the court applies here. (See Op. § II—indictment must allege each material element—and Op. §§ III–IV.)

Bottom line: the government need only charge and prove the elements of the particular § 1348 theory it advances (here, fraudulent inducement/obtaining money or property). It does not have to plead both § 1348 prongs in every count, but it must plead that obtaining money or property was an object of the fraud when it relies on the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

### How does the ‘in connection with the purchase or sale of any security’ element operate with social-media misstatements?
Question:
The posts were public and not directed to a specific counterparty. What makes the alleged misstatements sufficiently ‘in connection with’ the purchase or sale of a security under § 1348? Is it enough that the posts were intended to and did induce purchases generally, or is some tighter transactional nexus required?
Answer:
Short answer: Under this opinion, public social‑media posts can satisfy the “in connection with the purchase or sale of any security” element so long as the allegations show a fraudulent‑inducement scheme — i.e., that the misstatements were used to induce investors generally to buy (or sell) securities and thereby part with money or property. The court rejects a requirement of a tighter transactional nexus or a specific counterparty; it treats general inducement that causes purchases as the necessary connection.

Why — the doctrinal path in the opinion
- The court frames § 1348 with the familiar mail/wire‑fraud concepts: the offense requires a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” A scheme to defraud requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value,” and intent to defraud requires intent to deceive and to cause harm resulting from that deceit. (Op. § III, citing Greenlaw and Evans.)
- The court distinguishes Ciminelli (which rejected a right‑to‑control theory that treats deprivation of valuable economic information alone as a protected property interest). Ciminelli held that depriving a victim only of valuable information (so they make a worse decision) is not enough because that “right to valuable economic information” is not a traditional property interest. (Op. § III.A; discussion of Ciminelli.)
- Here, by contrast, the indictment pleaded a fraudulent‑inducement theory: defendants allegedly posted false and misleading information to induce followers to purchase securities (Indictment ¶ 13), then sold their holdings at inflated prices. That theory “protects money and property” because it alleges the victims were tricked into a transaction that required them to hand over money or property. The court says that distinguishes this case from Ciminelli. (Op. § III.A; quoting Kousisis.)
- The court relies on Kousisis to make clear that the fraud statutes target schemes to “obtain” money or property by false pretenses, even if the victim does not end up worse off in net economic terms. Thus it is sufficient that the defendants intended to induce purchases (i.e., to obtain victims’ money or property) and did so. (Op. § III.B; citing Kousisis.)

What that means for public social‑media posts
- You do not need a post to be addressed to one identified counterparty or to be part of a contract negotiation to be “in connection with” a purchase or sale. The connection exists when the misstatement is alleged to be part of a scheme to induce investors generally to buy (or sell) securities and thereby transfer money/property.
- The opinion treats the “nexus” as factual/functional: did the misrepresentation play the role of inducing the purchase/sale that resulted (or was intended to result) in a transfer of money/property? If yes, the “in connection with” element is satisfied under the fraudulent‑inducement theory pleaded here.

Key textual anchors from the opinion
- Indictment ¶ 13: defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.”
- Op. § III.A: explains difference from Ciminelli and endorses the fraudulent‑inducement theory as protecting money/property.
- Op. § III.B: relies on Kousisis that the government need only allege a scheme to obtain money/property by false pretenses; no requirement that the victim be left economically worse off.

Bottom line: Public social‑media misstatements satisfy “in connection with the purchase or sale of any security” when alleged as part of a fraudulent‑inducement scheme that uses those statements to induce investors to buy or sell securities (thereby parting with money/property). A tighter transactional nexus or individualized targeting is not required by this opinion.

### What role does materiality play at the indictment stage when defendants ‘concede deception’?
Question:
The opinion says a scheme to defraud requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive. If the defendants concede deception for purposes of the motion, does the indictment still need to plead facts showing materiality, or is it sufficient to allege ‘false and misleading’ statements that induced purchases without more detail? How do courts typically assess materiality on a Rule 12 motion?
Answer:
Short answer
Materiality is a required element of the “scheme to defraud” and therefore must be pleaded, but at the indictment stage it is typically satisfied by alleging that defendants made “false and misleading” statements intended to induce purchases (i.e., that investors were induced to part with money or property). On a Rule 12 motion courts accept the indictment’s factual allegations as true and will dismiss only if the pleaded facts demonstrate as a matter of law that the alleged misrepresentations were immaterial.

Why — the controlling law in the opinion
- The panel explicitly treats a “scheme to defraud” as requiring a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value” (Op. § III (quoting Evans), and the court emphasizes that an indictment must “allege each material element of the offense” (Op. § II (citing Guzman‑Ocampo, Gaytan)). So materiality is an element that must be pleaded.  
- The opinion also makes clear that the fraudulent‑inducement theory protects money/property (as opposed to the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli), and that alleging inducement to buy securities sufficiently alleges the deprivation of money/property (Op. § III.A–B; citing Kousisis). Thus allegations that defendants posted “false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” (Indictment ¶ 13; Op. § III.B) can supply the required allegation that the statements were materially related to obtaining money/property.

How courts treat materiality on a Rule 12 motion
- Standard: review is de novo, and the court must accept the indictment’s allegations as true (Op. § II). Materiality is ordinarily a fact‑intensive inquiry. As a result, courts will decline to resolve materiality at the pleading stage unless the face of the indictment shows the alleged statements were immaterial as a matter of law.  
- Application in this opinion: defendants conceded deception, but the Fifth Circuit reversed dismissal because the indictment, taken as true, alleged that false and misleading posts induced purchases (i.e., the scheme sought to obtain money/property), which satisfies the materiality/inducement element for pleading purposes (Op. §§ III.A–B; citing Kousisis). The district court erred because it treated the indictment as alleging only deprivation of “valuable economic information” (the right‑to‑control theory), which Ciminelli rejects; here the indictment alleged fraudulent inducement to buy securities, which Kousisis and this opinion treat as protecting money/property (Op. § III.A).

Practical rule for drafting/attacking indictments
- For the prosecutor: plead that statements were false or misleading, that they were material (or that they induced purchases), and that the inducement caused victims to part with money or property (e.g., “induced to purchase the security” language used in Indictment ¶ 13). That pleading typically satisfies materiality at the Rule 12 stage.  
- For the defense on a Rule 12 motion: to succeed you generally must show the indictment’s own allegations establish the statements were immaterial as a matter of law (not merely dispute materiality as a fact). If the indictment plausibly alleges inducement to buy securities, courts will usually let the charge survive to let the government prove materiality at trial.

Bottom line
Materiality cannot be ignored — it is an element that must be alleged — but an allegation that defendants made “false and misleading” statements that induced purchases (thereby causing victims to part with money/property) ordinarily suffices to plead materiality at the indictment stage. Dismissal on Rule 12 for lack of materiality is appropriate only when the indictment’s allegations, taken as true, show immateriality as a matter of law.

### Why didn’t the court address potential First Amendment issues with prosecuting social-media stock touting?
Question:
Given that the alleged pump-and-dump used public speech, is there a reason the Fifth Circuit didn’t evaluate any First Amendment defenses (e.g., opinion vs. fact, protected market commentary)? Is this because at the indictment stage the court accepts the allegations of knowing falsity and intent to defraud, placing the speech within the fraud exception, or because such defenses are fact-intensive and premature?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the panel was deciding only whether the indictment, taken as true, statutorily charged fraud under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, it treated the alleged social‑media posts as the kind of knowingly false statements that fall outside First Amendment protection and therefore did not reach First Amendment defenses — which both (1) are premature at the indictment stage where courts accept the government’s allegations and (2) usually raise fact‑intensive issues (knowledge, intent, and the character of the statements) that are properly litigated later.

Why the opinion didn’t analyze the First Amendment (with citations to the opinion)

- The governing task was indictment sufficiency, not constitutional adjudication. The court framed its review de novo and repeatedly stated that it must “take the allegations of the indictment as true” and decide whether the offense is stated. Op. § II (citing Fontenot). That standard explains why the panel confined its inquiry to whether the allegations, if true, allege a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite “intent to defraud.”

- The panel treated the alleged posts as classic fraudulent inducements rather than protected speech. In analyzing the elements, the court relied on the Supreme Court’s distinction between the rejected “right‑to‑control” theory (Ciminelli) and the valid “fraudulent‑inducement” theory (Kousisis). Op. § III.A (discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis). Because the indictment alleges defendants “induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities” and thus to part with money or property, the court concluded the allegations fall within the fraud statutes’ protection of money/property. Id. That analysis implicitly places the speech within the well‑established “fraud exception” to the First Amendment for knowingly false statements made to obtain money or property.

- The court also focused on mental state (intent) as alleged in the indictment. Op. § III.B explains that under Kousisis the government need not prove the defendant intended to leave the victim worse off; it is enough that the defendant schemed to obtain money/property. Because defendants conceded the indictment adequately alleged deception and the indictment alleged that defendants “posted false and misleading information . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase,” the panel held the pleading satisfied intent and scheme elements. That factual/mental‑state inquiry is central to First Amendment protection for speech (e.g., whether a statement is knowingly false or merely opinion), but at the pleading stage the court accepted the government’s allegations.

- The opinion never mentions or resolves any First Amendment challenges. That omission follows from the limited question before the court (whether the indictment alleged each material element of § 1348). The court’s reasoning shows it treated the alleged misrepresentations as actionable fraud if proven, leaving constitutional challenges (which would require a factual record and more detailed briefing) for later proceedings.

Practical upshot for defendants and readers
- At the indictment stage the court’s job is narrow: accept the government’s factual allegations and decide if they, if true, state an offense. Op. § II. Constitutional defenses that hinge on disputed facts (e.g., whether particular posts were knowingly false factual assertions vs. nonactionable opinion or protected market commentary) are typically premature until there is a developed record.
- The panel’s reliance on Kousisis/Ciminelli shows how prosecutors can plead around the Ciminelli problem by framing cases as fraudulent‑inducement (alleging inducement to part with money/property). If defendants want to press First Amendment objections, those will most naturally be raised later — on a motion for judgment of acquittal after trial, on summary‑judgment/PLEA‑stage motions that address factual predicates, or as facial/as‑applied constitutional challenges — because the opinion does not foreclose those arguments, it simply did not reach them at the pleading stage.

In short: the court confined itself to the indictment‑sufficiency question, accepted the government’s allegations that the social‑media posts were false misrepresentations inducing purchases, treated those allegations as nonprotected fraudulent speech for purposes of § 1348, and therefore had no occasion to resolve First Amendment defenses that depend on disputed facts.

### Does the government need to plead or prove reliance by any follower to state an offense under § 1348?
Question:
In civil securities fraud (Rule 10b-5), reliance and loss causation are elements. Here, the Fifth Circuit focuses on deception and obtaining money or property as the object. In criminal § 1348 prosecutions, is reliance by any particular investor an element, or is it enough to allege a scheme designed to induce purchases through misstatements?
Answer:
Short answer: No. Under the Fifth Circuit’s opinion (following the Supreme Court’s Kousisis guidance), an indictment under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 need not plead that any particular follower actually relied on a misstatement. It is enough to allege a scheme to defraud by using material misrepresentations to induce purchases so the defendants could obtain money or property.

Why: the opinion frames § 1348 liability in the familiar two-part way borrowed from mail/wire fraud — (1) a scheme to defraud (material misrepresentation intended to deceive) and (2) intent to defraud (an intent to deceive and to obtain money or property). The court expressly distinguishes the rejected “right-to-control” theory from the “fraudulent-inducement” theory: Ciminelli forbids treating the mere deprivation of valuable economic information as a protected property interest, but Kousisis confirms that the fraudulent-inducement theory — where a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into handing over money or property — does protect money/property and supports fraud charges. See Opinion § III.A (discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis) and the court’s conclusion that the indictment “alleges defendants induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities— that is, to part with their money or property.”

What that means for pleading/proof of reliance:
- Pleading: The indictment need only allege a scheme to induce purchases (e.g., posts misrepresenting trading positions intended to induce followers to buy), and that obtaining money/property was an object of the fraud. The Fifth Circuit held those allegations sufficient. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14 and Opinion § III (holding the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud).
- Proof at trial: The opinion makes clear that the statute does not require proof that the fraudster sought to leave victims economically worse off; Kousisis holds a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain the victim’s money/property “regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” See Opinion § III.B (discussing Kousisis). Thus the government need not prove a particular victim’s reliance or net loss to secure conviction so long as it proves the scheme, material misrepresentations, and that obtaining money/property was an object of the fraud.

Caveat: The government must still allege and prove the material misrepresentations and the requisite intent (deception and intent to obtain money/property). But it does not have to allege that any specific follower relied on a particular statement as an essential element of the offense under § 1348, consistent with the court’s reasoning here.

### Is there any Article III ‘standing’ requirement or need to identify specific victims in a federal criminal fraud indictment?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly references ‘followers’ as the deceived investors but does not name them. In criminal cases, does the United States’ sovereign interest in enforcing the law satisfy any justiciability concerns, making ‘standing’ of victims irrelevant? Are there pleading requirements to identify victims in the indictment for § 1348, or is it sufficient to describe the victim class?
Answer:
Short answer: No — Article III standing and naming individual victims are not requirements the government must meet in a criminal indictment like this one. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion shows (1) the United States’ sovereign interest in enforcing the criminal law means justiciability/standing doctrines that govern private civil suits are not in play here, and (2) an indictment need only allege the statutory elements (including that the defendant sought to obtain money or property by fraud); it may do so by describing a victim class (e.g., “followers”) rather than listing every individual victim.

Why — doctrine and the opinion’s reasoning

- Standing/justiciability is a private-law concept directed to whether a plaintiff has a judicially cognizable injury; it does not bar a criminal prosecution by the United States. The opinion treats the case as an ordinary criminal indictment review (de novo review of indictment sufficiency) and never suggests an Article III standing requirement for the government to prosecute. See Part II (standard of review) and the Court’s overall posture in reversing dismissal.

- What the indictment must allege is governed by criminal pleading standards, not civil standing. The court reiterates the settled rule: an indictment must allege each material element of the offense, give fair notice to the defendant, and preserve double-jeopardy protections. United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (quoted in Part II); United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (same). The Fifth Circuit applied those standards here and concluded the superseding indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud.

- On the substance of the fraud allegation, the opinion relies on the distinction between the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli and the fraudulent‑inducement theory upheld in Kousisis. The court holds the indictment does not rely on a theory that the followers merely were deprived of “valuable economic information” (a non‑traditional property interest barred by Ciminelli). Instead, it alleges defendants induced followers to buy securities (i.e., to part with money/property), which is squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement theory that “protects money and property.” See Part III.A (discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis).

- The court also explains that the fraud statutes do not require proof that victims were left economically worse off — only that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property by materially false pretenses. See Part III.B (citing Kousisis and noting that “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”).

Practical consequence on naming victims

- The Fifth Circuit accepted an indictment that described the victim class as “followers” and alleged the mechanics (false social‑media posts inducing purchases) and the object of the scheme (obtaining money/profiting through “pump and dump”). It found those allegations sufficient to plead the scheme and intent elements. That means an indictment can satisfy the statutory elements by describing the class of victims and the fraudulent conduct that deprived that class of money/property; it need not identify every individual victim by name in order to survive a motion to dismiss.

- That is consistent with federal pleading practice in criminal cases: an indictment must allege the elements with sufficient particularity to give notice, but prosecutors frequently allege schemes affecting many unnamed victims by describing the class, the means, and the loss or proceeds (here, the allegation that defendants “profited $114 million” and induced purchases by followers).

Bottom line

The government need not plead Article III standing or name specific victims in a criminal securities‑fraud indictment. It must allege the statutory elements — including that the defendant sought to obtain money or property by fraud — and may do so by describing a victim class and the fraudulent inducement that caused purchases, as the Fifth Circuit held in this opinion.

### How did the intervening Supreme Court decision in Kousisis affect the Fifth Circuit’s analysis, and is there any retroactivity concern?
Question:
Kousisis post-dated the district court’s dismissal. The panel applies Kousisis to reverse. Is this simply the appellate court applying current law to a case on direct review, or does using Kousisis raise any ex post facto or fair-notice issues when the conduct predated that decision?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel simply applied the Supreme Court’s intervening decision in Kousisis to the indictment on direct review — it treated Kousisis as clarifying how the fraud statutes (18 U.S.C. §§ 1348, 1349) govern a “fraudulent‑inducement” theory. That application does not create an ex post facto or fair‑notice problem in the circumstances presented: the court did not criminalize previously lawful conduct or increase punishment retroactively, and Kousisis interpreted the existing statutory elements rather than inventing a new offense.

Why Kousisis changed the Fifth Circuit’s analysis
- Timing and effect: The district court dismissed the indictment before the Supreme Court decided Kousisis. On appeal the panel relied on Kousisis (cited repeatedly: Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025)) to distinguish the case from Ciminelli and to conclude the indictment properly alleges a fraudulent‑inducement scheme. See Opinion §§ III.A–B.
- Doctrinal import: Ciminelli had closed the door on a “right‑to‑control” theory — fraud cannot be premised only on depriving someone of valuable economic information. The panel explains that Kousisis clarifies that a fraudulent‑inducement theory (using material misstatements to trick victims into handing over money or property) is distinct from and not foreclosed by Ciminelli. The opinion quotes Kousisis: the fraudulent‑inducement theory “protects money and property” and “is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory.” See Opinion § III.A (citing Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1398).
- Intent element: Kousisis also resolved a related question about intent/ harm: the Supreme Court held the fraud statutes do not require that the defendant sought to leave the victim worse off economically; it is enough that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property. The panel relies on that holding to reject defendants’ claim that intent to profit (without intending to injure victims’ bottom lines) forecloses fraud liability. See Opinion § III.B (citing Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391–92, 1394 n.5, 1397).

Is applying Kousisis on appeal an ex post facto or fair‑notice problem?
- Ex post facto: The Ex Post Facto Clause prevents retroactive criminal legislation that creates a crime or increases punishment after the fact. This appeal involved application of a Supreme Court interpretation of existing federal fraud statutes to an indictment pending on direct review. The panel did not retroactively create a new crime or increase punishment; it applied the controlling interpretation of the statute to the charged conduct. The opinion frames Kousisis as clarifying which theories fall within the statutes (fraudulent inducement protects money/property), and uses that clarification to find the indictment sufficient. See Opinion ¶¶ III–IV.
- Fair notice / due process: The Due Process fair‑notice inquiry asks whether a defendant had reasonable notice that his conduct was unlawful. The panel’s approach treats Kousisis as an interpretation of the statutory elements (scheme to obtain money/property; intent to defraud) rather than a novel rule that criminalized previously innocent conduct. The indictment alleged concrete acts — inducing followers to purchase securities by false statements so defendants could sell at a profit — conduct that any fair reading of the fraud statutes plausibly covers once the Court clarified the fraudulent‑inducement theory. The panel therefore found no notice problem: the Government charged a scheme to obtain money/property through deceit, which Kousisis confirms is within the fraud statutes. See Opinion § III.A–B (indictment alleged induction to “purchase the security,” Indictment ¶13; Kousisis confirms such a theory “protects money and property,” 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1392).
- Collateral vs. direct review: The typical retroactivity concerns are sharper on collateral review (e.g., in § 2255), where courts apply Teague/Alexander retroactivity analysis to new constitutional rules. This case was on direct appeal; courts routinely apply intervening Supreme Court decisions on direct review. The opinion follows that ordinary practice by applying Kousisis to evaluate whether the indictment states an offense. See Opinion I–IV.

Conclusion
The Fifth Circuit applied Kousisis as an intervening clarification of the fraud statutes to reverse the district court. Under the framework the opinion uses, that application poses no ex post facto or fair‑notice problem: Kousisis clarified that fraudulent inducement (tricking a victim into parting with money/property) is a valid theory of fraud and that the fraud statutes do not require proof of net pecuniary harm to the victim; the indictment here plainly alleges that sort of scheme. The panel therefore had authority to apply Kousisis on direct review and to remand for further proceedings.

### What distinguishes a ‘scheme to defraud’ from mere market manipulation without fraud under § 1348?
Question:
The indictment alleges false and misleading statements were used to ‘pump’ prices. If a trader’s conduct moves the market but involves no material misstatement (e.g., mere coordinated trading), would that still be a ‘scheme to defraud’ under § 1348, or does § 1348 require the deceptive element grounded in false representations as emphasized here?
Answer:
Short answer: Under this opinion, § 1348 requires a deceptive element—a material misrepresentation or other deception used to induce a victim to part with money or property. Conduct that merely moves a market by coordinated trading, without any deceptive misstatement or other deception that induced purchases, would not fit the fraudulent‑inducement theory the court affirms here.

Why: the opinion rests on two propositions drawn from Supreme Court precedent and applies them to the indictment.

- The court distinguishes the “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in Ciminelli from the fraudulent‑inducement theory it accepts here. Ciminelli held you cannot convert the mere deprivation of valuable economic information into a federal fraud by treating “the right to control” as a traditional property interest (Opinion III.A, discussing Ciminelli at 310–16). The Fifth Circuit says that problem is avoided here because the indictment alleges defendants did more than withhold information — it alleges they posted false and misleading information that induced followers to buy securities (Indictment ¶¶13–14), i.e., a fraudulent inducement that causes the victim to hand over money or property (Opinion III.A).

- The court also explains that under Kousisis a fraudulent‑inducement theory does not require the fraudster to have intended to leave the victim worse off in net terms; it is enough that the defendant used deception to obtain the victim’s money or property (Opinion III.B, discussing Kousisis at 1388–1392). Thus, the court treats deception that induces purchases as the proper §1348 predicate even if the victim ends up with market‑value securities or the fraudster provided something in return.

What that means for coordinated trading without misstatements:
- If the only alleged wrongdoing is coordinated trading that moves a price, but there are no false or misleading statements or other deceptive acts that induced victims to buy or sell, the indictment here would lack the fraudulent‑inducement element the court requires. The opinion repeatedly frames the “scheme to defraud” element as requiring a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value” (Opinion III, citing Evans and Greenlaw) and accepts the indictment because it alleges such misrepresentations (Indictment ¶¶13–14; Opinion III.A–B).
- The court did not hold that any market manipulation, by itself, satisfies §1348. It reversed because this indictment alleges deception used to induce purchases—i.e., a classic fraudulent‑inducement pump‑and‑dump.

Bottom line: Under this decision, §1348 liability as pleaded here depends on deception that induces victims to part with money or property. Purely manipulative trading tactics that move prices but involve no deceptive misrepresentations are not what the court sustained in this case; they would not be charged successfully under the fraudulent‑inducement theory the opinion endorses (absent additional deceptive conduct).

### Why does the opinion say the fraudulent-inducement theory ‘protects money and property’ whereas the right-to-control theory does not?
Question:
The court quotes Kousisis that fraudulent inducement is not a repackaged right-to-control theory because it targets the transfer of money or property. Conceptually, how does the law distinguish between depriving someone of decision-influencing information and causing them to transfer money by means of that same misinformation? Is the difference tied to the object of the scheme rather than the mechanism?
Answer:
Short answer: Because federal fraud statutes protect traditional money or property interests, not a mere right to accurate information. The fraudulent‑inducement theory targets a defendant’s attempt to cause victims to part with money or property (the object of the scheme), whereas the right‑to‑control theory claims only that the defendant deprived the victim of information needed to make decisions (the mechanism of influence). The Fifth Circuit (following the Supreme Court in Ciminelli and Kousisis) treats those as legally distinct.

Explanation with the opinion’s text

- The statutory baseline. Section 1348 (and the mail/wire fraud analogues) criminalize schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses … any money or property.” The court emphasizes that the fraud statutes therefore protect “money and property” — traditional property interests — not every form of economic harm or disadvantage.

- What Ciminelli held. In Ciminelli the Supreme Court rejected the “right‑to‑control” theory because it treated the victim’s “right to valuable economic information” as a protected property interest. The Court held that “the right to valuable economic information … is not a traditional property interest,” so depriving someone only of that information cannot alone support a fraud conviction. (Opinion § III.A summarizing Ciminelli.)

- What Kousisis clarified. Kousisis recognized the fraudulent‑inducement theory as different: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” The Court explained that this is not a repackaged right‑to‑control argument because the fraudulent‑inducement theory “protects money and property.” (Opinion § III.A citing Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1398.)

- Object v. mechanism. That language gets precisely to your conceptual question. Both theories can involve the same deceptive mechanism (misinformation that influences decisions). The legal difference is the object of the scheme:
  - Right‑to‑control: the defendant’s scheme is said to withhold or falsify information so the victim makes a different discretionary choice; the alleged “loss” is the victim’s informational control over her assets. Ciminelli says that informational control is not a traditional property interest, so that alone won’t satisfy the statutes.
  - Fraudulent‑inducement: the defendant’s scheme aims to cause the victim to hand over money or property (e.g., buy securities). The end result — transfer of money or property induced by false statements — is precisely the sort of “money or property” the statute protects. As the opinion puts it, the indictment “alleges defendants induced their followers … to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property.” (Opinion § III.A.)

- Practical consequence in this case. The court reversed because the indictment alleges defendants did more than deprive followers of information: it alleges they induced purchases (transfers of money/property) by materially false statements. Under Kousisis that is a valid fraudulent‑inducement theory even if victims receive something of market value in return or do not suffer a net pecuniary loss. (See Opinion § III.B.)

Bottom line: The line isn’t about whether misinformation influenced decisions (the mechanism) but about whether the fraud sought to and did cause victims to part with money or property (the object). Fraud statutes punish the latter; they do not punish mere informational control. The opinion draws that distinction directly from Ciminelli and Kousisis.

### What additional analysis, if any, is required to sustain the § 1349 conspiracy count once the substantive § 1348 theory is deemed sufficient?
Question:
The panel reverses the dismissal of both the substantive securities fraud and the conspiracy charge. At the indictment stage, is it enough that the object of the conspiracy (a § 1348 violation) is adequately alleged, or must the indictment also include specific facts showing an agreement and overt acts among the defendants? How demanding is that pleading threshold in the Fifth Circuit?
Answer:
Short answer: At the indictment stage in this case, the government needed to plead the material elements of a § 1349 conspiracy — in particular that the defendants agreed to pursue the unlawful object (a § 1348 securities-fraud scheme) with the requisite intent — but it did not have to plead minute details of each defendant’s actions or a catalogue of overt acts beyond facts sufficient to allege an agreement and intent. The Fifth Circuit treated the conspiracy count as adequately pleaded because the indictment alleges the unlawful object (fraudulent inducement to buy securities) and factual allegations (buying securities, posting false and misleading information to induce purchases, and then “dumping” for profit) that plausibly show a coordinated scheme and intent to obtain money by fraud.

Why: legal standards and how the opinion applies them
- Governing pleading rule: the court reiterates the settled Fifth Circuit test for indictment sufficiency — an indictment must “enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense,” “fairly inform the defendant,” and “provide . . . a double jeopardy defense” (citing United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996), and Guzman‑Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000)). (Op. § II.)
- The opinion then analyzes the substantive § 1348 elements (scheme and intent to defraud) and concludes the indictment sufficiently alleges a fraudulent‑inducement scheme (see Op. § III.A–B). The court treated those allegations as establishing both the unlawful object of the conspiracy and the defendants’ intent to obtain money by fraud: e.g., purchase, “pump” by posting false information, induce others to buy, and “dump” for profit — Indictment ¶¶ 13–14 (Op. I; Op. III.A–B).
- Because the indictment alleges that object (a § 1348 fraudulent‑inducement scheme) and contains factual allegations describing coordinated conduct designed to induce purchases and realize profits, the panel reversed dismissal of the conspiracy count along with the substantive counts. The court therefore found the indictment adequate to allege an agreement and intent at the pleading stage without requiring a higher level of detail.

Practical takeaway for Fifth Circuit indictments
- You must plead the conspiracy’s unlawful object and the defendants’ agreement/intention to pursue it — those are material elements the indictment must allege. (Op. § II.)
- But the Fifth Circuit does not demand detailed proof of the mechanics of the agreement at the indictment stage; factual allegations showing a coordinated course of conduct aimed at the unlawful object (here, purchases, false social‑media postings to induce purchases, and secret sales for profit) are sufficient to plead the conspiracy. (See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14; Op. I, III.)
- In short, adequate pleading of the § 1348 object plus factual allegations that plausibly show coordinated action and fraudulent intent will sustain a § 1349 conspiracy count at this stage — no heightened particularity beyond those material elements is required by the panel’s analysis.
