Chapter 11: # Significance and Takeaways of Constantinescu

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu is a pivotal post-Ciminelli treatment of fraud prosecutions built around social-media “pump-and-dump” campaigns. The opinion confirms—and explains how to plead—what prosecutors must allege to charge fraudulent conduct under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 when defendants use publicity and social influence to induce others to buy securities. It does so by embracing the fraudulent-inducement theory recognized by the Supreme Court in Kousisis and by delineating the doctrinal boundaries separating protected speech and actionable fraud. For students and practitioners this opinion is consequential in three ways: it restates what § 1348 requires elementally; it explains how profit-seeking promoters can supply the requisite mens rea; and it offers practical guidance on how to plead and defend cases that arise in influencer-driven markets. The remainder of this chapter teases these strands apart and explains their doctrinal and practical implications.

## Key doctrinal move: embracing fraudulent inducement to satisfy property

At the heart of Constantinescu is the court’s doctrinal move to reject a right-to-control theory and instead embrace a fraudulent-inducement theory as a proper foundation for a § 1348 prosecution. Under the right-to-control approach—rejected in Ciminelli—the government attempts to frame a victim’s “right to control” assets or access to valuable economic information as the protected property. The Supreme Court in Ciminelli held that deprivation of merely valuable economic information, standing alone, does not constitute the type of traditional property interest protected by the federal fraud statutes. That decision forecloses prosecutions that rely solely on a theory that the victim was deprived of the information necessary to make discretionary decisions.

Constantinescu makes explicit that fraud prosecutions grounded on fraudulent inducement are conceptually and legally distinct. A fraudulent-inducement theory alleges that a defendant made material misrepresentations (or omissions in certain circumstances) to cause a victim to enter a transaction that required handing over money or property. The key difference from Ciminelli is that the object of the deceit is the victim’s money or property itself—the purchase price paid for securities—rather than a nebulous “right to control” decisionmaking. The Fifth Circuit therefore holds that an indictment that alleges defendants used social-media misrepresentations to induce followers to buy securities adequately alleges deprivation of a protected property interest. That approach hews to the Supreme Court’s confirmation in Kousisis that fraudulent inducement “protects money and property,” and it squarely situates promoter-driven schemes within the traditional conception of property-based fraud.

Doctrinally, then, Constantinescu reaffirms that the fraud statutes are not limited to classic embezzlement or conversion; they cover schemes that induce others to transfer money or property by means of material misstatements. The proper pleading strategy after Constantinescu is to frame the misconduct as having induced victims to transfer money or property—explicitly alleging the transactional mechanism by which the defendant’s statements caused victims to part with funds—rather than as abstractly depriving victims of information.

## Clarifying mens rea: profit-seeking as intent to obtain victims’ money

Constantinescu also clarifies how to satisfy the mens rea element of § 1348 in influencer-based schemes. The indictment in that case alleged that defendants purchased shares, made misleading posts intended to “pump” demand, and then “dumped” by secretly selling into the inflated market. Defendants argued this was only profit-seeking conduct without an intent to harm victims. The Fifth Circuit, relying on Kousisis, explains why that argument fails: the fraud statutes require an intent to deceive and an intent to obtain money or property as an object of the scheme. Criminal liability does not hinge on a goal to leave the victim economically worse off; instead, liability attaches when the defendant intends to induce the victim to hand over money or property by deceit.

Kousisis is central here. There the Supreme Court made clear that a fraudster who induces a counterparty to contract and thereby obtain the counterparty’s money or property may be criminally liable even if the fraudster provides equally valuable goods or services or does not seek to create a net negative economic outcome for the victim. The Fifth Circuit reads Kousisis to mean that a promoter’s plan to profit—if accomplished by materially deceptive inducements that lead followers to hand over purchase funds—meets the “intent to obtain” element. Thus, allegations that defendants “had money in mind” by orchestrating purchases and sales after making false or misleading statements are sufficient to plead intent.

For practitioners, the doctrinal implication is straightforward: a well-pleaded indictment should allege facts showing that the defendant intended to induce purchases and to capture the proceeds of those purchases. Temporal allegations (purchases before promotion; sales after price spikes), coordinated posting and trading activity, and concealment of trading intent or positions are all classic means of pleading intent to obtain victims’ money.

## Charging guidance: how to structure § 1348 indictments in promotion schemes

Constantinescu is instructive about the structure and contents of a charging document. An indictment under § 1348 should do more than quote statutory language; it must allege the material elements with factual particularity sufficient to give fair notice. Post-Constantinescu prosecutors should allege (1) the fraudulent scheme’s mechanism—how the promotional messages were disseminated and what they said; (2) the specific securities and transactions at issue—what was bought and sold, when, in what quantities, and at what prices; (3) the causal link between the misrepresentations and the victims’ purchases—market reaction in temporal proximity to defendant statements, spikes in volume and price following posts, and statements from victims or aggregated trading patterns; (4) the defendant’s deceptive intent—circumstantial evidence such as timing of trades (buy before, promote, sell after), concealment of holdings or sales, private communications showing coordinated plans, and unusually large profits; and (5) the results—monetary gains traced to the campaign and numbers of victims affected.

It is especially important to frame the allegations as fraudulent inducement rather than as a right-to-control case. The indictment should allege that the defendant’s misstatements caused followers to transfer money (as purchase payments) in exchange for securities. Particularized descriptive allegations—direct quotations from posts, screenshots, and platform identifiers—help establish the content and falsity of the representations. Specific transactional data (brokerage records, transaction timestamps, profit calculations) strengthen the nexus between the deception and the transfer of money. Where possible, pleading that victims were induced to buy and thereby lost the opportunity to retain the funds they used to purchase securities will assist in satisfying the property requirement without invoking the prohibited right-to-control theory.

The Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of pleading sufficiency reminds prosecutors that indictments need not prove the case, but they must adequately allege each material element. Careful pleading of the mechanism of the scheme and the object of the fraud—money or property—avoids the Ciminelli pitfall.

## Boundaries: speech vs. fraud and the role of disclaimers

One of the trickiest doctrinal questions in influencer prosecutions is the line between protected expression and criminal deception. Constantinescu does not dissolve that tension, but it clarifies boundaries. Mere puffery, opinions, and hyperbolic endorsements are generally not actionable. Statements that are non-factual, subjective expressions of belief about a security’s prospects are often protected speech. By contrast, knowingly false factual statements—about a promoter’s holdings, trading activity, the involvement of market insiders, or fabricated buying interest—can form the basis of fraud.

Disclaimers and declarations of non-reliance complicate this terrain. The presence of a boilerplate disclaimer on a post or channel—“not investment advice” or “I’m just sharing my trades”—will not automatically shield promoters if their statements are materially false and intended to deceive. Similarly, a disclosure that the promoter holds a position does not excuse affirmative misstatements about the size of holdings, the sequencing of trades, or the existence of coordinated purchasers. Courts will examine the overall context: whether disclaimers were conspicuously placed, contemporaneous with the promotional content, truthful, and adequate to cure misleading impressions created by the rest of the message.

Omissions are particularly thorny. A duty to disclose material facts typically arises from a statutory or fiduciary relationship, or when an omission renders other statements misleading. Most social-media promoters do not occupy fiduciary roles to their followers. Nonetheless, an omission can be actionable where the promoter makes statements that would be misleading without disclosure of certain facts—most commonly, a failure to disclose that the promoter is secretly selling into the market while urging purchases. Constantinescu suggests that concealment of the promoter’s sales plans can be probative of deceitful intent when paired with affirmative misrepresentations that induce purchases.

In sum, authenticity and context matter: whether speech is actionable turns on whether the communicator asserted false factual claims (as opposed to opinions), whether the audience reasonably relied on the communicator’s factual representations, and whether omissions or misleading framing rendered the overall message deceptive. Disclaimers are a relevant but not decisive factor.

## Practical implications for influencers, promoters, and trading communities

The practical impact of Constantinescu is immediate for anyone who uses social media to discuss securities. Influencers should recognize that statements about securities can cross into criminal liability if they knowingly or recklessly disseminate materially false representations to induce purchases. Best practices from a risk-management perspective include avoiding false or misleading claims about one’s own trades; disclosing conflicts of interest and trading positions prominently and contemporaneously; abstaining from touting a security while effecting private sales in the same security; and keeping clear, contemporaneous records of trade intent and trading activity to demonstrate good-faith conduct.

Promoters who are paid to advertise or who coordinate with issuers or brokers should document compensation relationships and avoid making factually false claims about a company’s business prospects or their own trading. Trading communities and platforms may face increased attention; administrators and moderators should consider policies that require disclosure of material conflicts and that prohibit coordinated pump-and-dump activity. Market participants who rely on influencer content must be aware that apparent “insider” claims or too-good-to-be-true promotional narratives may mask manipulation.

Regulators and intermediaries will likely increase monitoring of cross-platform promotion and the use of private channels (direct messages, closed groups) to coordinate market-moving messages. For sophisticated users, Constantinescu underscores that the absence of a formal contract or payment does not immunize a promoter if the underlying conduct demonstrates an intent to induce purchases and capture the proceeds.

## Open questions: materiality of trading intent, extent of disclosure duties

While Constantinescu clarifies some issues, it leaves open multiple doctrinal questions that will be litigated in the coming years. First, the materiality of a promoter’s undisclosed trading intent is not definitively resolved. Is a promoter’s failure to disclose that he intends to sell at a certain price a material omission in every circumstance, or does materiality depend on the nature of the statement and the reasonable expectations of the audience? Materiality remains a fact-sensitive inquiry: courts will likely ask whether a reasonable investor would have considered knowledge of the promoter’s trading plan important in making an investment decision. The answer will turn on context—platform norms, the prominence of the promoter’s claims, and how the omitted information interacts with the promotional message.

Second, the scope of disclosure duties for non-fiduciary promoters is unclear. Traditionally, duties to disclose arise from a special relationship or statutory mandate. Constantinescu suggests that omissions can be actionable when they make affirmative statements misleading, but it does not establish a per se duty for every influencer to disclose positions, compensation, or timing. Judges and juries will need to reconcile general principles about omissions with market realities where social-media posts often function as solicitations without formal investor relationships.

Relatedly, the line between puffery and materially misleading statements will continue to be contested. Aggregated market data, the sophistication of followers, and the format of communications (threaded tweets, livestreams with chat) are all contextual factors that bear on whether a statement is a provably false factual claim or a non-actionable opinion.

## Anticipated defenses and evidentiary battles at trial

Defense strategies in cases like Constantinescu will predictably focus on several fronts. First, defendants will assert that their posts were opinions or puffery, not materially false factual representations. To succeed, defendants will press for jury instructions emphasizing the difference between opinion and false statement and challenge the sufficiency of government proof about falsity. Second, defendants will argue lack of intent, offering contemporaneous evidence of genuine belief in their statements—screenshots showing internal analysis, expert reports, or testimony about trading strategies—to rebut circumstantial inferences from trading patterns.

Causation and reliance present further battlegrounds. Defendants will contend that price movements resulted from independent market forces and that followers’ purchases were not caused by the defendant’s posts. The government will respond with market microstructure analysis showing temporal correlations between posts and volume spikes, declarations from affected investors, and platform analytics indicating engagement and flow-through. Expect extensive battles over expert testimony: market impact, attribution of trades to particular posts, and traceability of proceeds will all require experts to explain complex trading data to juries.

Discovery disputes will be fierce. Prosecutors will seek private communications (direct messages, group chats), brokerage records, and platform metadata; defendants will litigate the scope of compelled disclosures and may assert privacy or First Amendment concerns about compelled production of speech-related records. The admissibility of social-media posts (authentication, hearsay exceptions, and context) will be litigated, as will the relevance of disclaimers and contemporaneous disclosure. Finally, the applicability of SEC regulatory findings and parallel civil enforcement actions will affect evidentiary strategies and motions in limine.

## Policy considerations: investor protection and market integrity in the social era

Constantinescu locates the criminal law at the intersection of investor protection and new communications technologies. On the one hand, the decision signals robust enforcement priorities: regulators and prosecutors may treat social-media-driven manipulation as a serious threat to market integrity and retail investors. The amplification effects of large followings, coupled with the speed of execution in modern markets, make pump-and-dump schemes particularly potent and potentially destructive. Criminal enforcement serves deterrence goals and complements regulatory tools that the Securities and Exchange Commission and self-regulatory organizations employ.

On the other hand, heavy-handed prosecution risks chilling legitimate speech. Many market commentators provide valuable analysis and share trading ideas that contribute to market efficiency. Prosecutors and courts must calibrate enforcement to avoid penalizing ordinary market commentary and to preserve robust public discourse. That calibration requires careful doctrinal work—distinguishing factual misrepresentations (or misleading omissions) made with deceptive intent from protected opinion and bona fide analysis.

Policy makers also face resource-allocation questions. Social-media surveillance is technically demanding and can result in enormous caseloads if pursued aggressively. The public policy balance may favor targeted enforcement against demonstrable, coordinated campaigns that use deception to harvest investor funds, rather than broad policing of influencer speech. The opinion therefore suggests that enforcement should be guided by evidentiary strength: clear false statements, coordinated action, demonstrable transactional links, and traceable illicit gains merit criminal attention.

Conclusion: Constantinescu is a doctrine-shaping decision. It confirms that post-Ciminelli fraud prosecutions grounded in fraudulent inducement—where defendants use deceptive messages to cause transfers of money or property—are legally viable. It clarifies the mens rea inquiry by recognizing profit-seeking, if achieved through deceitful inducement, as the requisite intent to obtain victims’ money. The decision offers concrete drafting and evidentiary lessons for prosecutors, a roadmap for defense counsel to anticipate and counter charges, and a policy template for balancing enforcement with free expression. The most significant enduring lesson for market participants is simple: when public commentary is paired with private trading and a plan to reap the benefits of induced purchases, criminal liability is a real risk.
