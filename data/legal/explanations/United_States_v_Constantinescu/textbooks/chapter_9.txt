Chapter 9: # The ‘In Connection With’ Requirement

The “in connection with” language in the federal securities-fraud provision, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, is the statutory bridge that links deceptive conduct to securities transactions. That phrase is the linchpin in deciding whether deceptive conduct—whether an insider’s lie, an issuer’s misstatement, or an influencer’s social‑media post—falls within the criminal prohibition on schemes to defraud in the context of securities. This chapter explains how courts treat that statutory nexus: how the textual parallel with Rule 10b‑5 shapes analysis, how courts determine whether deception functionally induced purchases or sales, what degree of temporal and causal linkage is required, and how those principles play out in modern fact patterns such as online promotion and pump‑and‑dump schemes. The discussion uses the Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cayce and related Supreme Court precedents (notably Ciminelli and Kousisis) to illustrate the doctrinal contours students will encounter on exam and in practice.

## Textual comparison: § 1348 and Rule 10b‑5’s ‘in connection with’ language

Section 1348 prohibits, among other things, schemes “to defraud any person in connection with any security” and schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.” Rule 10b‑5, promulgated under Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act, contains parallel language: it prohibits any device, scheme, or artifice “in connection with the purchase or sale of any security” from employing fraud or deceit. Because § 1348 tracks the language and policy concerns of Rule 10b‑5, courts frequently borrow the interpretive decisions of the 10b‑5 line when construing § 1348. The Cayce opinion follows that approach, observing that the securities-fraud statute “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and that courts often consult mail/wire and 10b‑5 decisions to define statutory terms such as “scheme to defraud” and the “in connection with” nexus.

The textual parallel matters for two reasons. First, it grounds an interpretive presumption that “in connection with” requires some relationship between the fraudulent conduct and a securities transaction—more than a mere factual coincidence. Second, it indicates that courts should evaluate whether the deception affected the market or the transaction in a way that made the purchase or sale a practical objective or consequence of the deception. But the parallel does not mean that § 1348 simply imports every nuance of 10b‑5 jurisprudence unchanged; courts read § 1348 with attention to its statutory context and to the criminal-law elements of scheme and intent.

## Functional nexus: inducing purchases or sales through deception

A central question under the “in connection with” requirement is whether the deception operated to induce—or was designed to induce—a purchase or sale of securities. The Cayce panel emphasizes a functional approach: the government may proceed under a fraudulent‑inducement theory when it alleges that the defendant used material misstatements to “trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property,” i.e., to cause the victim to buy (or sell) a security. The Supreme Court’s recent decision in Kousisis clarified and reaffirmed this path: the fraudulent‑inducement theory is a valid way to show the required nexus because it protects money or property that the fraudster obtains by inducing a transaction.

This functional test is distinct from other theories that courts have rejected or treated skeptically. Ciminelli rejected a “right‑to‑control” theory that attempted to treat the victim’s loss of valuable economic information as the protected property. That decision means the government cannot rely solely on a theory that the fraudster deprived a decisionmaker of information that would influence discretionary judgments unrelated to the surrender of money or property. But when the deceit directly causes a person to hand over money or property in the form of a securities purchase, the functional nexus is present: the deceptive communication is an instrumentality by which purchases or sales are obtained.

In practical terms, a prosecutor (or civil plaintiff) must plead and later prove that the deception either (1) was intended to induce the purchase or sale and (2) had some operative effect on the decision to trade. In pump‑and‑dump cases like Cayce, the indictment typically alleges that the defendant posted false and misleading statements to induce followers to buy, thereby inflating price, and then sold shares. That sequence—misrepresentation followed by induced purchases and then defendant sales—maps cleanly onto the fraudulent‑inducement theory and satisfies the functional nexus.

## Temporal proximity and causal linkage to trading activity

How close in time and how direct a causal link must the deception have to the trading activity? Courts have resisted mechanical rules. The required linkage is contextual and flexible: the deception need not be simultaneous with the trade, and it need not be the sole cause of the trade. Instead, courts ask whether the misrepresentation was sufficiently related in time and causation to the transactions so that it can fairly be said to have been “in connection with” them.

Temporal proximity helps but is not dispositive. A misstatement communicated well before a transaction but reasonably calculated to influence trading decisions can satisfy the nexus if the other circumstances demonstrate that the statement was intended to and did affect purchases or sales. Conversely, a close temporal coincidence without evidence of intent or causation (for example, a misstatement uttered after the execution of a trade) will not suffice.

Causation is likewise treated in practical rather than metaphysical terms. The government must show that the defendant’s deception was part of a scheme that made obtaining money or property from securities purchases or sales an object or reasonably foreseeable consequence of the scheme. The Cayce court applied this view: the indictment need only allege that defendants posted false statements “so that other investors were induced to purchase” the security. That allegation ties the misrepresentations to the act of purchasing—satisfying the “in connection with” nexus even though courts do not require proof that the fraudster intended the victims to be worse off.

Students should note that proving causation at trial often relies on circumstantial evidence: timing of posts relative to buy orders, messaging that asked followers to “buy now,” coordination among accounts, and sales that realize profits after the price rise. The flexible temporal/causal standard accommodates these evidentiary realities.

## Market‑wide vs. victim‑specific connections

The “in connection with” requirement accommodates both market‑wide manipulations (schemes that affect many market participants or prices generally) and victim‑specific inducements (deceiving a particular person or group to induce a particular purchase or sale). The distinction matters for pleading and proof but not for the existence of the statutory nexus.

Market‑wide schemes—classic market manipulation and pump‑and‑dump schemes—operate by spreading false information or trading activity designed to inflate prices so that the schemers can sell into the artificially high market. The deception is typically broadcast to a broad audience (investors generally or followers of a promoter), and the causal link is established by showing that the deceptive messaging affected market demand and price. Courts have recognized that large-scale deception that foreseeably influences market participants satisfies the “in connection with” requirement even though there is no single identifiable victim.

Victim‑specific schemes align more closely with the fraudulent‑inducement model (as in Kousisis): a defendant makes a material misstatement to a discrete decisionmaker knowing it will induce that person to enter into a transaction—such as awarding a contract or purchasing a security. The “in connection with” link is straightforward where the deception directly precedes and induces a particular transaction.

Cayce illustrates that both pathways are viable under § 1348: social‑media promoters may be indicted for inducing their followers (a discrete class of investors) to buy a security, thereby creating a market effect that lets the promoters sell at inflated prices. The indictment does not have to choose a single “market‑wide” or “victim‑specific” label so long as it pleads a plausible linkage between deception and trading.

## Online promotion and trading: establishing transactional linkage

Digital platforms and social media have complicated the question of how to show that online promotion was “in connection with” purchases or sales. Modern fraud cases involving influencers, message‑board posters, or coordinated online campaigns require concrete methods to show that deceptive content was intended to, and did, produce trading activity.

Courts look to several indicia of transactional linkage in online contexts:

- The content of the communications: explicit calls-to-action (“buy now,” “accumulate shares”), false statements about control of shares, fabricated earnings claims, or fake endorsements are strong evidence that posts were designed to induce trades. Cayce’s indictment expressly relied on posts that portrayed defendants as actively trading and touted securities in ways likely to prompt purchases.

- The timing of posts relative to trades: posts that precede spikes in buying activity and are followed by defendant sales provide compelling circumstantial evidence that the posts prompted trading.

- Audience and reach: the size of an influencer’s following, engagement metrics, and the demographics of followers (retail traders, subscribers to paid trading services) help show that the messaging was aimed at prospective purchasers.

- Coordination and pattern: repeated postings across multiple accounts, coordinated messaging, or use of multiple platforms to amplify the same falsehoods suggests a plan to generate buying pressure.

- Direct channels of communication: private messages, subscriber newsletters, or paid subscription content targeted at followers may demonstrate an intent to induce particular purchases.

Taken together, these factors allow prosecutors to plead and, later, prove that online promotion was not mere puffery or market chatter but a deliberate instrumentality of a scheme to obtain money or property by inducing purchases or sales.

## Proof at trial: trade blotters, timestamps, and content archives

Because the “in connection with” inquiry is often a question of fact, trial proof commonly relies on documentary and digital records that can track the sequence and effect of deceptive communications and trading. The typical evidentiary tools include:

- Broker statements and trade blotters: these show purchase and sale dates, sizes, and prices for both defendants and particular cohorts of investors (for example, subscribers to an influencer’s channel). Matching the timing of purchases by followers to promotional posts supports an inference of inducement.

- Timestamps and metadata: platform-generated timestamps on posts, edits, and metadata can establish that a post preceded a surge of trading. Metadata also can establish that content was posted or removed strategically to conceal a scheme.

- Content archives and communications logs: archived versions of social‑media posts, screenshots, private messages, emails, newsletters, and promotional materials establish the substance of the deceptive communications.

- Platform analytics: engagement metrics—views, likes, comments, clickthroughs—can help quantify the reach and likely impact of promotions on potential buyers.

- Financial records showing realized gains: bank records, securities-account liquidation records, and profit-and-loss schedules demonstrate whether and when the defendant converted inflated prices into cash.

- Market data: order‑book snapshots, price and volume series, and comparators (similar securities, index movements) enable experts to opine whether observed price movements were plausibly linked to the defendant’s activity rather than to independent market news.

At trial, the government stitches these materials into a chronology: post → investor purchases → price increase → defendant sales. That chronology provides both the “in connection with” nexus and proof of the defendant’s objective to obtain money or property by deception.

## Defensive arguments: intervening market forces and price volatility

Defendants typically raise a range of defenses to break the “in connection with” link, many of which focus on causation and temporal proximity. The most common defensive arguments are:

- Intervening market forces: defendants may argue that independent news, analyst reports, legitimate market interest, or macroeconomic events explain price movements and investor purchases, severing the causal link between their statements and trades. To counter this, the government must show that the deceptive messages were a material contributing cause of the transactions or that the defendants’ conduct was a reasonably foreseeable source of the trades.

- Price volatility and coincidence: especially in small‑cap or penny markets, prices move rapidly for many reasons. Defendants contend that any purchases by followers were coincidental or part of ordinary market churn. Courts assess the totality of the evidence—timing, content, reach, and coordination—rather than rely on price movements alone.

- Lack of intent to induce trades: defendants may admit making misstatements but claim they never intended to influence anyone’s buying or selling decisions, asserting the statements were opinions, jokes, or non‑actionable puffery. Whether a statement is a nonactionable opinion is a mixed legal and factual question; courts examine the specificity and factual nature of the statement, whether it was presented as a material fact, and the speaker’s relationship to the audience.

- Absence of proximate causal link to a discrete victim: in some cases, defendants argue that because the purported victims received marketable securities of at least equal value (or because the defendants did not intend to make victims worse off), there is no actionable deprivation. Kousisis resolves the legal side of that argument—actual net pecuniary loss is not required—so the defense must instead undermine the claimed inducement or prove independent causation.

A successful defense strategy often requires undermining the government’s chronology, presenting alternate explanations for trading, and demonstrating that the challenged statements were too vague, rhetorical, or speculative to have been reasonably relied upon by investors.

## Applying the doctrine: how to evaluate new fact patterns

When confronting a new fact pattern, construct the “in connection with” analysis by asking a series of practical questions:

1. What was the content of the communication? Was it a factual claim likely to affect investment decisions, or mere puffery/opinion?
2. To whom was it communicated? A broad public, a group of retail followers, or a targeted institutional buyer?
3. What is the timing relationship between communication and trades? Is there credible evidence that trades followed the communication and that defendant trades followed the induced buying?
4. Was there an intent to induce trades? Do the communications, pattern of conduct, or defendant’s trading behavior suggest that obtaining money or property by prompt selling was an objective?
5. Are there plausible alternative market explanations for the trading and price movement? If so, how strong are they relative to the evidence of inducement?
6. What documentary and digital records exist (timestamps, trade blotters, analytics) to prove the sequence and effect?

If the answers point to material false or misleading statements directed at potential buyers, followed in time by purchase activity and defendant sales that realize gains, the “in connection with” requirement is likely satisfied under § 1348. Conversely, if misstatements are remote in time, too speculative to be material, or demonstrably unrelated to trading activity, the government’s theory will be weaker.

## Conclusion

The “in connection with” requirement under § 1348 is satisfied when deception is functionally tied to the purchase or sale of a security—when misrepresentations are intended to, and reasonably calculated to, induce trading and the defendant obtains money or property as a consequence. Courts apply a flexible, fact‑sensitive standard that examines the substance and timing of communications, the audience reached, and circumstantial evidence demonstrating that trading followed the deception. Recent decisions—most notably Ciminelli and Kousisis, and as applied in Cayce—clarify two important limits: the government cannot rest a fraud charge on a theory that only deprived a decisionmaker of economic information (the right‑to‑control theory), but it can proceed under a fraudulent‑inducement theory that protects money and property obtained by causing purchases or sales. In the age of digital platforms, careful pleading and rigorous use of trade records, timestamps, and platform analytics are essential to bridge the statutory nexus and prove that online promotion was “in connection with” securities transactions.
