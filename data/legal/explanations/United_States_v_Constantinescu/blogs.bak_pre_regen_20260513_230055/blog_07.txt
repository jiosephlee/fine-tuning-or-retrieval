Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# The Next Wave: Criminal Liability for Meme-Stock and Crypto Promotion Post‑Kousisis

Why this decision matters

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the district court in United States v. Constantinescu et al. marks another clear signal that federal prosecutors can — and will — bring traditional fraud charges against social‑media “finfluencers” who allegedly engineer pump‑and‑dump campaigns. Coming on the heels of the Supreme Court’s recent clarification in Kousisis, the opinion applies the fraudulent‑inducement theory to an online, social‑media driven marketplace: when promoters use material misrepresentations to induce followers to buy securities, they can be charged with schemes “to obtain” money or property even if victims end up holding tradable securities with some market value. For commentators and market participants who hoped Ciminelli might insulate deceptive online praise or selective silence about positions, this decision underscores that the line Ciminelli drew—between a forbidden right‑to‑control theory and permissible theories that protect money and property—does not immunize staged market hype.

What the Fifth Circuit held (and how it reasons)

The defendants were accused of classic pump‑and‑dump activity: acquire shares, inflate price through false and misleading social‑media posts to induce followers to buy, then secretly sell at a profit. The district court dismissed the superseding indictment, reasoning that the allegations described deprivation of “valuable economic information” rather than a traditional property interest—an approach that echoes the right‑to‑control theory rejected by the Supreme Court in Ciminelli. On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reversed.

The court anchored its analysis in two related precedents. First, it acknowledged Ciminelli’s core holding: federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, not a nebulous “right to control” assets constituted by valuable information. But the Fifth Circuit explained that the indictment here did not rest on a right‑to‑control theory. Instead, it pleaded the fraudulent‑inducement theory: defendants allegedly used material misstatements to trick followers into contracts (market purchases) that required handing over money or property. Under Kousisis — which the panel cites and relies upon — fraudulent inducement is not a repackaged right‑to‑control claim; it targets transactions in which the victim parts with money or property as a consequence of deception.

Second, the court addressed intent. Relying on Kousisis, it rejected defendants’ contention that a fraud charge requires that the fraudster seek to make the victim economically worse off. Kousisis holds that the statutes criminalize scheming to obtain money or property through deception even if the fraudster provides something of roughly equal value or the victim is not left economically worse off. Applying that rule, the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment sufficiently alleges intent to defraud: the defendants allegedly induced purchases so they could realize gains when they sold—“they had money in mind.”

Why the Fifth Circuit’s application matters in practice

Three features of the court’s reasoning are particularly consequential.

First, doctrinal clarity around fraudulent inducement. The panel carefully separates fraudulent inducement from Ciminelli’s disfavored right‑to‑control theory. That distinction matters because social‑media influencers often argue that their followers received securities with market value (or at least with some prospect of value), and that the information at issue is not “property.” The Fifth Circuit (following Kousisis) accepts that when deception causes followers to hand over money to buy securities, that transfer suffices as the “money or property” the fraudster sought to obtain.

Second, intent need not mirror victim harm. Many pump‑and‑dump schemes see the promoter pocket gains while followers end up holding securities that may or may not have lost value. The Fifth Circuit, aligning with Kousisis, confirms prosecutors do not have to prove the promoter intended to leave followers worse off; it is enough that the promoter intended to obtain money or property via deceptive inducement.

Third, procedural posture and pleading standards. The court reversed a dismissal under Rule 12, emphasizing that an indictment must be read as true and need only allege the material elements of the offense. That is an important practical reminder: especially in the online‑market manipulation context, detailed factual allegations of misrepresentations, follower inducement, purchases by followers, and the promoter’s secret sales are likely sufficient at the charging stage to survive dismissal.

Where this sits alongside the Seventh Circuit

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis tracks the Seventh Circuit’s post‑Kousisis decision in United States v. Smith, which similarly applied Kousisis to cases involving allegedly deceptive representations leading to financial transactions. Both circuits demonstrate an emerging consensus: Kousisis authorizes the continued use of fraudulent‑inducement theories to police deceptive conduct that results in the transfer of money or property, including in modern, decentralized markets shaped by social media.

Taken together, these circuits form a doctrinal baseline that disfavors reading Ciminelli as a broad shield for online promoters. Courts are distinguishing between claims that merely allege informational deprivation (which Ciminelli rejects) and claims that allege deception inducing the transfer of money or property (which Kousisis and now the Fifth Circuit allow).

Implications for enforcement and market behavior

For prosecutors: this decision is an encouraging roadmap. It suggests that, where prosecutors can allege (and eventually prove) a pattern of false statements, coordination or secrecy about positions, demonstrable purchases by followers after promotional posts, and disclosures that plausibly conceal material conflicts, they have workable fraud theories. The emphasis on intent to obtain money rather than intent to cause net loss reduces a potentially high burden for proving mens rea.

For civil enforcers (SEC and states): the criminal pendulum complements ongoing civil enforcement. The SEC has been active in policing misleading social‑media promotion of securities and crypto offerings; criminal prosecutions like this amplify deterrence. Prosecutors and civil agencies can coordinate investigations and share evidence (subject to legal constraints), and criminal indictments will increase leverage in parallel civil settlements, follow‑on private litigation, and disgorgement actions.

For market participants and “finfluencers”: the opinion should be a wake‑up call. Risk factors that appear particularly salient from the indictment and the court’s reasoning include: repeated or coordinated posts hyping thinly traded securities; false or misleading statements about one’s own holdings or trading activity (e.g., misrepresenting purchases or positions); failure to disclose material conflicts of interest or undisclosed paid promotions; rapid sell‑offs following promotional campaigns; and efforts to conceal or time trades to maximize follower purchases. Promoters who trade in tandem with their public statements, especially when withholding that information from followers, expose themselves to both civil and criminal risk.

Potential downstream effects and unanswered questions

This decision tightens the prosecutorial toolkit, but it does not resolve all hard questions. Several issues will likely see litigation or require further doctrinal refinement:

- Causation and transactional mechanics. At trial, prosecutors will still need to prove that the alleged misrepresentations induced identifiable purchases by followers or otherwise contributed materially to the scheme’s profitability. Proving that causal chain in a noisy social‑media environment — where posts mix with broader market forces and independent trading ideas — is fact‑intensive and may be contested.

- Scope vis‑à‑vis “opinion” and puffery. Courts will have to continue distinguishing between actionable material misstatements and protected opinion, hyperbole, or non‑actionable puffery typical of investment chatter. The indictment in this case alleged false and misleading statements; the line between puffery and material falsity will be litigated aggressively in future prosecutions.

- Crypto and token markets. The opinion applies to “securities” as charged here. Crypto promoters will watch closely: many tokens fall outside securities law, but where tokens are securities, the same fraudulent‑inducement framework should apply. In non‑securities token contexts, prosecutors may need to rely on alternative statutes (e.g., wire fraud) — and will have to navigate fragmentation in how courts treat digital assets.

- Collective and algorithmic amplification. The modern ecosystem includes coordinated groups, bots, and platform mechanics that amplify messages. Prosecutors may expand theories to allege conspiracies, coordinated messaging, or manipulative practices that exploit platform features. Defendants will push back on First Amendment and platform‑liability angles.

Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal affirms that the ancient crime of fraud adapts to twenty‑first century markets. Kousisis supplied the doctrinal footing, and this case applies it to the social‑media powered pump‑and‑dump. For promoters, the lesson is plain: deliberately misleading followers to induce purchases and enrich oneself is not protected by the niceties of market value or the assertion that followers “got something” back. For enforcers, the decision encourages a sustained focus on online market manipulation, and for courts, it sets the stage for difficult factbound trials about causation, materiality, and the contours of deceptive speech in a noisy public square.
