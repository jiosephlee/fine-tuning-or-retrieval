# Why Charge § 1348 (and § 1349) Instead of Rule 10b-5? DOJ Strategy in Online Manipulation Cases

## Why this matters

The Fifth Circuit’s recent reversal of the district court in the Houston “pump-and-dump” social‑media case provides a useful window into prosecutorial choice-of-forum tactics in modern securities‑manipulation investigations. The defendants were social‑media influencers who allegedly “pumped” microcap and illiquid stocks by posting materially false and misleading statements to induce purchases by followers, then “dumped” their positions for profit. The district court had dismissed the indictment on the ground that the indictment allegedly sought to criminalize deprivation of valuable economic information rather than a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed, relying on the indictment’s fraudulent‑inducement theory and the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications in Ciminelli and Kousisis that distinguish the right‑to‑control theory from a fraudulent inducement that results in the victim handing over money or property.

That procedural posture—an indictment under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) and the conspiracy statute § 1349, with aiding‑and‑abetting claims under § 2—reflects a deliberate DOJ strategy in cases involving online market manipulation. Understanding why prosecutors are gravitating to § 1348 rather than Rule 10b‑5 (Exchange Act § 10(b) and SEC Rule 10b‑5) is essential for defense counsel, market participants, and commentators tracking the contours of criminal enforcement against social‑media driven schemes.

## How § 1348 fits these fact patterns

Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to “defraud any person in connection with” a security, and schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.” The Fifth Circuit (following prior circuit practice) treats § 1348 as borrowing key concepts from mail and wire fraud jurisprudence. That lineage matters because the mail/wire fraud framework is scheme‑based: it targets fraudulent schemes and the intent behind them, not just discrete misstatements in the context of a registered securities issuer’s continuous disclosure regime.

In social‑media pump‑and‑dump scenarios, defendants are typically independent traders/influencers, not issuers or broker‑dealers subject to the SEC’s civil enforcement architecture. Their misconduct is alleged to be purposeful dissemination of misleading statements to induce followers to buy securities so the influencer can sell into the artificially elevated market. That is the archetypal fraudulent‑inducement theory—allegations that a misrepresentation tricked victims into turning over money or property by way of a securities purchase. The Fifth Circuit here made clear that such a theory is squarely within § 1348’s reach so long as the indictment pleads the elements of a scheme and the intent to obtain money or property by deception.

## Why prosecutors might prefer § 1348 over Rule 10b‑5

There are several pragmatic and doctrinal reasons the government may favor charging under § 1348 (and § 1349) in these online manipulation cases.

First, statutory form and breadth. Section 1348 is a stand‑alone criminal securities fraud statute that frames liability in terms of schemes to defraud and obtaining money or property by fraud in connection with a security. It imports the flexible “scheme” language from mail/wire fraud that courts have used historically to reach a wide variety of deceptive conduct. Rule 10b‑5, by contrast, is a civil antifraud provision that the Supreme Court has developed through a long and sometimes technical line of private‑rights and scienter doctrines. While Rule 10b‑5 has been used as the basis for criminal prosecutions (and many criminal securities cases still rely on it), § 1348 offers prosecutors statutory clarity and the ability to couch allegations as schemes to obtain money or property rather than only as securities‑law disclosure violations.

Second, doctrinal hooks around the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory. The Supreme Court’s post‑Ciminelli handling of fraudulent‑inducement theories—most notably in Kousisis—makes clear that a scheme that causes a victim to hand over money or property via deceit is within the ambit of the federal fraud statutes even if the victim is not left economically worse off. That doctrinal accommodation fits well with pump‑and‑dump facts where followers may receive shares or “something of value” in return, yet still are induced to hand over money. Section 1348 provides a natural vehicle for that theory because its statutory language expressly identifies obtaining money or property “in connection with” a security transaction. The Fifth Circuit relied on that precise logic in reversing the dismissal.

Third, mens rea and burden framing. Criminal Rule 10b‑5 prosecutions must grapple with scienter doctrines developed in the civil context (e.g., recklessness vs. conscious intent) and with the “in connection with” element that sometimes invites fine distinctions about whether the misstatement was directly tied to a securities transaction. Section 1348’s scheme‑to‑defraud rubric allows the government to focus on specific intent to deceive and to obtain money or property—the classic mail/wire fraud mens rea—without being forced into some of the technical contours courts have developed around civil‑securities scienter. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion emphasizes that intent-to-defraud can be shown where the object of the fraud is obtaining money—“they had money in mind”—even if victims receive market‑priced securities in return.

Fourth, conspiracy and accomplice liability. By charging conspiracy under § 1349, the government can aggregate and prosecute coordinated activity among multiple influencers and facilitators as a single concerted scheme. Conspiracy liability is attractive because it allows proof of an agreement and an overt act, and conspirators can be held accountable for reasonably foreseeable acts by co‑conspirators taken in furtherance of the scheme. Complementing that, aiding‑and‑abetting liability under 18 U.S.C. § 2 lets the government pin criminal responsibility on participants who provided substantial assistance—platform operators, promoters, or others—without needing to prove direct commission of the substantive offense. Together these statutes give prosecutors flexible tools to address the typical team‑based architectures of online manipulation.

## The “in connection with” question and its practical import

One recurring issue in securities‑fraud prosecutions—civil or criminal—is the “in connection with” requirement: the deception must be tied to a securities transaction. In the influencer context, the tie is usually straightforward: the alleged misrepresentations concern a particular security and are intended to induce followers to buy that security. The Fifth Circuit, following Kousisis, was comfortable treating those allegations as adequately connecting the fraud to securities purchases. That practical clarity reduces a defense foothold that might otherwise be prominent in § 10b‑5 matters—namely, that the misstatements were hortatory, general market commentary, or insufficiently transaction‑linked.

But the “in connection with” element still has bite. If a post is simply puffery or opinion, or if the government cannot plausibly show that followers actually were induced into particular trades by the misstatements, a defense argument that there is no cognizable connection will persist. Charging under § 1348 does not eliminate that factual contest; it, however, places the emphasis on inducement and obtaining money—which aligns well with typical pump‑and‑dump narratives.

## Doctrinal and policy implications

A shift toward more frequent use of § 1348 and § 1349 in online manipulation cases carries a number of implications.

On one hand, it modernizes criminal enforcement tools to address decentralized, social‑media‑driven markets that traditional issuer‑focused securities enforcement does not neatly capture. Prosecutors can target the conduct that actually harms retail investors and distorts price discovery—coordinated misinformation campaigns—not just corporate insiders or disclosure violators.

On the other hand, broad application of scheme‑based fraud statutes raises concerns about overcriminalization and vagueness. The Supreme Court’s Ciminelli decision made clear that fraud statutes should not be stretched to criminalize every misleading tactical advantage or information asymmetry. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion rides that line by relying on the fraudulent‑inducement paradigm validated in Kousisis; but future prosecutions will test the boundary between protected speech (opinion, advocacy, and market commentary) and criminal misrepresentation intended to induce transactions. That will raise First Amendment and fair‑notice issues in some fact patterns, particularly where messaging is ambiguous or where promoters rely on hyperbole. Courts will need to police adequately pled allegations of materially false statements and specific intent, lest the fraud statutes reach too far.

There is also potential for forum shopping and circuit split. Not every circuit treats § 1348 identically, and not every court will frame the “in connection with” or scienter elements the same way. The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on Kousisis and its reading of the fraudulent‑inducement theory will be persuasive but not dispositive outside its jurisdiction. Expect contested appellate litigation over the contours of intent and the sufficiency of indictments in closely balanced cases.

Finally, the practical upshot for market participants is that their conduct on social media now carries a clearer criminal risk. That could chill some forms of commentary or spur greater self‑regulation among influencers, platforms, and broker‑dealers. At the same time, it may incentivize more sophisticated concealment techniques by bad actors aware of the prosecutorial focus—raising enforcement challenges in tracing proceeds and proving inducement.

## Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit’s decision to reinstate the indictment in the Houston pump‑and‑dump case underscores why the DOJ may prefer § 1348/§ 1349 (with § 2 aiding‑and‑abetting) as the criminal vehicle for social‑media market‑manipulation prosecutions. Those statutes align well with a fraudulent‑inducement theory: they are scheme‑oriented, squarely target obtaining money or property by deception, and pair naturally with conspiracy and accomplice doctrines to address coordinated online campaigns. The approach is doctrinally defensible post‑Kousisis so long as indictments allege more than mere deprivation of information and adequately plead intent and inducement.

But the move toward scheme‑based criminal enforcement places important checks on prosecutors and courts: allegations must be precise, the “in connection with” nexus must be real, and courts must distinguish protected commentary from criminal deception. The coming years will likely refine these boundaries as more of these cases work their way through district courts and courts of appeals.
