# Pleading After Ciminelli: Drafting Indictments That Allege Property Deprivation Without ‘Right to Control’

Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu and the other appellees is a timely reminder that Ciminelli’s bar on prosecuting schemes that merely withhold “valuable economic information” did not eviscerate federal fraud prosecutions aimed at classic fraudulent-inducement scenarios. For practitioners drafting securities- and fraud-related indictments post‑Ciminelli, the opinion offers a useful roadmap: carefully plead how the defendant’s misrepresentations caused victims to part with money or property, not merely how the defendant wrested control over decision‑making information. The Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal of a “pump-and-dump” indictment because the indictment alleged the object of the scheme was to obtain money—followers were induced to buy securities—rather than to deprive them of a right-to-control or information interest. That distinction is now central to surviving pretrial challenges in fraud cases.

How the court framed the issue

The indictment charged social‑media traders who touted stocks to their large followings, allegedly posting false or misleading statements to inflate prices, then secretly selling the shares for profit. The district court dismissed, reasoning—apparently under Ciminelli—that the indictment alleged only depriving investors of valuable economic information rather than a protected property interest. On review, the Fifth Circuit took the allegations at face value and asked whether they sufficiently pleaded the elements of securities fraud as understood through the analogies to mail and wire fraud: (1) a scheme to defraud, and (2) intent to defraud. The court concluded they did.

Key doctrinal points from the opinion

1. Fraudulent‑inducement theory versus right‑to‑control theory. The opinion squarely distinguishes a fraudulent‑inducement theory from the right‑to‑control theory Ciminelli disallowed. Under fraudulent inducement, the government alleges that a defendant used material misstatements to trick victims into a transaction that required them to hand over money or property—classic theft‑by-deception. Unlike the right‑to‑control theory, which treats “the right to valuable economic information” as the victim’s property, fraudulent inducement targets money or property actually surrendered as the object of the scheme. The Fifth Circuit relied on the Supreme Court’s follow‑up in Kousisis to stress that this is a valid basis for prosecution so long as the indictment pleads that money or property was the object.

2. No requirement of victim net loss. The court reaffirmed that prosecutors need not allege the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. Following Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit emphasized that the statutes criminalize scheming to “obtain” money or property; whether the victim ends up with market‑value consideration or recoups value elsewhere does not defeat the fraudulent‑inducement theory. A scheme is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property by materially false pretenses.

3. Pleading intent to defraud. The Fifth Circuit found that alleging the defendants posted false information to induce purchases—followed by secret sales by the promoters—adequately alleges an intent to deceive and an intent to obtain money. The prosecution’s articulation that the defendants profited (over $100 million, per the indictment) supported the inference that “money was in mind” as the object of the fraud.

What this means for indictment drafting

Practitioners should take several pragmatic lessons from Constantinescu when preparing charges aimed at dishonest inducements in securities and related contexts.

Tie misstatements to the object of obtaining money or property. The indictment must make a clear causal and purposeful link: the defendant made material misstatements or omissions, the statements induced victims to purchase (or otherwise transfer) money or property, and the defendant intended to and did obtain money or property as a result. Pleadings that characterize the objective as manipulating decision‑making or “control” over assets without alleging the transfer of money or property risk running headlong into Ciminelli. By contrast, the Fifth Circuit approved the indictment precisely because it pled inducement to buy securities (i.e., lose or transfer money/property).

Allege the mechanics of the transaction. Concrete factual allegations showing how victims parted with money strengthen the indictment. Describe: the timing of the misrepresentations relative to purchases, the platforms and followers used, the securities acquired by victims, and the defendant’s secret selling (the “dump”). Allegations that victims executed purchases in response to posts, or that volume and price moves corresponded to defendant messaging, help establish the requisite nexus between deception and transfer of value.

Characterize what was obtained as “money or property.” When the agent of the fraud obtained the proceeds of sales—or otherwise acquired cash, securities, or other property—say so plainly. Where the scheme involves inducing purchases, label the victims’ assets (cash, brokerage funds) they parted with as the object of the scheme. This prevents an interpretation that the indictment only alleges interference with decision‑making information.

Avoid reliance on a “right‑to‑control” theory in the pleadings. Do not couch the gravamen of the offense as depriving victims of the right to control their assets or to receive accurate information necessary to exercise discretion. That theory is precisely the one the Supreme Court repudiated in Ciminelli and which the Fifth Circuit distinguished here. If the facts plausibly support both theories, frame the indictment around the fraudulent‑inducement theory and use factual detail to show the monetary transfers.

Plead intent with contextual facts. The indictment should allege objective indicia of intent to obtain money or property: e.g., secret sales, coordinated timing between promotional messages and sales, profits realized, or internal communications showing the scheme. Mere assertions of negative motive or profit‑seeking alone could be contested as insufficient; factual predicates that point to deception aimed at procuring funds make intent more concrete.

Address the “mirror image” concern head‑on. Anticipate the defense argument that victims received something of value (market‑priced securities) and therefore could not have been deprived of property. Use precedent (as the Fifth Circuit did) to explain that the law does not demand a net pecuniary loss—the statute criminalizes obtaining the victim’s money or property by fraud even if the victim receives some consideration in return.

Potential downstream effects and litigation traps

Constantinescu steers prosecutors toward drafting narratives that track the transactional flow of money and property. Expect defendants to continue pushing Ciminelli‑based motions to dismiss where indictments are sparse or emphasize information control rather than transfers. Courts will scrutinize whether the indictment alleges more than an information deprivation; if the connection between misstatement and the victim’s parting with money is attenuated, dismissal remains a risk.

We should also expect evolving doctrinal skirmishes at the margins. How detailed must allegations be to show that victims “parted with” property? Will courts ever require more than the canonical “induced to purchase” language? Lower courts may develop varying thresholds for how granularly an indictment must describe the mechanics of transfers (e.g., linking particular tweets or posts to identifiable purchase transactions). Prosecutors seeking to avoid such quibbles should include, where available, specific examples of purchases, timing, and realized proceeds.

Finally, civil and regulatory dimensions will intersect with criminal pleading choices. Detailed factual allegations that survive a Rule 12 challenge can be leveraged in parallel SEC or FINRA actions—and, conversely, investigative findings from regulatory probes will often supply the concrete facts needed to meet the post‑Ciminelli pleading standard.

Conclusion: drafting for the practical realities of the law

Constantinescu is not a doctrinal revolution so much as a reminder of an old principle reframed by the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications: federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests—cash and other property—and fraudulent inducement remains a cardinal theory of prosecution when the indictment demonstrates that victims were tricked into transferring money or property. For prosecutors, that means pleading the money‑or‑property object clearly and concretely. For defense counsel, Ciminelli still offers a live avenue to attack bare allegations that focus on deceptive control of information rather than transfers of value. For both sides, Constantinescu is a helpful example of an indictment that survived because it tied misstatements directly to the object of obtaining money—exactly the posture required to plead fraud in the post‑Ciminelli landscape.
