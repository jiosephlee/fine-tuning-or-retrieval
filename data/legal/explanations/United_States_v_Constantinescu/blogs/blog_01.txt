Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# After Ciminelli and Kousisis: Fifth Circuit Revives ‘Finfluencer’ Pump-and-Dump Indictment Under § 1348

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision to reverse the district court and reinstate a superseding indictment charging a group of social‑media “finfluencers” with securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 is the latest pivot point in post‑Ciminelli criminal fraud doctrine. The opinion, issued by Judge Engelhardt, squarely holds that an indictment alleging a fraudulent‑inducement theory—posts that misrepresent trading positions and prospects to induce followers to buy securities so the defendants can “pump and dump” them—states a § 1348 offense. The court rejects the district court’s view that the indictment was limited to depriving followers of “valuable economic information,” a theory the Supreme Court disallowed in Ciminelli. Instead, the Fifth Circuit treats the scheme here as one that induced followers to part with money or property, a paradigmatic object of the fraud statutes, and relies heavily on the Supreme Court’s more recent clarification in Kousisis that fraudulent inducement is a legitimate basis for liability even where victims may not suffer a net pecuniary loss.

Why should practitioners, prosecutors, and commentators care? The decision clarifies how courts should distinguish between the prohibited right‑to‑control/rule rejected in Ciminelli and the viable fraudulent‑inducement theory recognized in Kousisis. It also provides immediate breathing room for prosecutors pursuing “pump‑and‑dump” prosecutions against high‑profile social‑media traders who allegedly use false statements to move markets and monetize the movement. At the same time, the opinion spotlights continuing doctrinal tensions about what counts as “property” and how far the fraud statutes reach when deception changes the economic calculus of private actors.

## The court’s reasoning, element by element

The Fifth Circuit reviewed the indictment de novo and applied the settled framework for § 1348: a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” For the “scheme to defraud” element, the court leaned on its prior approach treating § 1348 as borrowing key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes. That led the panel to focus on whether the indictment sufficiently alleged a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” The indictment here alleges that the defendants—each with large social‑media followings and a public persona as traders—purchased securities, posted false and misleading information to induce their followers to buy (thereby inflating the price), and then secretly sold their positions. The government alleges aggregate profits of $114 million.

The district court dismissed, concluding the indictment pleaded only that defendants deprived followers of potentially valuable economic information—not money or property—thereby invoking the discredited right‑to‑control theory of Ciminelli. The Fifth Circuit reversed. Two Supreme Court decisions guided the panel’s analysis.

First, Ciminelli stands for the proposition that a fraud prosecution cannot rest solely on the assertion that the defendant deprived the victim of valuable economic information (the right to control). The Fifth Circuit acknowledged that limitation but emphasized that the indictment did not proceed under a right‑to‑control theory: it affirmatively alleges that the defendants “induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property.” In short, the indictment alleges a fraudulent‑inducement scheme, not a pure right‑to‑control deprivation.

Second, the panel relied on Kousisis, where the Supreme Court confirmed that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is not a repackaging of Ciminelli’s disallowed theory and that the fraud statutes protect “money and property.” Kousisis also settles a critical intent question: a defendant may violate the fraud statutes even if the scheme does not ultimately leave the victim worse off economically. The Fifth Circuit seized on Kousisis’s holding that a fraud is complete once a defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim sustains a net pecuniary loss. That doctrine forecloses the defendants’ argument here that their intent to profit, rather than to harm followers, defeats the “intent to defraud” element.

Taken together, these points led the court to conclude that the indictment adequately alleged both a scheme to defraud—through materially false statements intended to induce purchases—and the requisite intent to defraud—an intent to deceive and to cause the deprivation (i.e., induce the transfer of money/property). The dismissal was reversed and the case remanded.

## Analytical perspective: doctrine and limits

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is careful to cabin itself within the Supreme Court’s distinctions: it does not overturn Ciminelli but rather applies Kousisis to articulate the difference between the forbidden right‑to‑control theory and a legitimate fraudulent‑inducement theory. That is the right doctrinal posture. Ciminelli’s core worry was criminalizing ordinary political and commercial influence by treating lost information as property; Kousisis clarified that inducing transfers of money or property through lies remains classic fraud.

Still, some doctrinal friction remains. The line between “depriving valuable economic information” and “inducing the transfer of money or property” can be thin in securities markets, where information often drives buying and selling decisions. The Fifth Circuit leans on the indictment’s explicit allegation that the defendants induced purchases—i.e., the transfer of wealth out of followers’ pockets—which reasonably distinguishes the case from Ciminelli. But questions will persist about how granular that pleading must be. For example, might an indictment be vulnerable if it alleges only that misinformation affected market price without identifying specific induced purchases or funds transfers tied to the defendants’ deception? The Fifth Circuit sidesteps that hypothetical by emphasizing what this indictment specifically alleges.

Kousisis’s validation that liability does not require a showing of net pecuniary loss to the victim reduces a doctrinal foothold defendants might have used: courts can no longer argue that so‑called “harmless” reciprocal transactions (where victims received something of market value in return) defeat fraud prosecutions. That doctrine is consequential for pump‑and‑dump prosecutions, because typical victims often end up holding the stock they bought at inflated prices rather than suffering a straightforward theft of money. Under Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit’s reading, the misconduct is completed when the induced purchase occurs; downstream market movements that leave victims worse off are unnecessary to sustain the offense.

## Practical and downstream implications

For prosecutors, the decision is a practical green light in a high‑visibility enforcement front. Social‑media traders who leverage their followings to promote thinly traded securities—and then sell their positions—now face a clearer path to indictment under § 1348 so long as the government can plausibly plead and later prove that the promoters made material misrepresentations to induce purchases. The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on an allegation of specific inducement suggests prosecutors should plead (and develop at trial) clear causal links between the deceptive statements and the targeted purchases or timing of sales.

For defense counsel, the opinion identifies the contours of the viable defense arguments moving forward: challenge the sufficiency of factual allegations tying specific victims’ purchase decisions to defendants’ misrepresentations; press for particularity in identifying transactions and timing; and emphasize First Amendment and opinion‑speech defenses where the statements alleged are puffery or non‑actionable opinion. The Fifth Circuit did not resolve those issues; it only held the indictment adequate under Rule 7’s pleading standard. Those defenses will still have bite at later stages—motions for a bill of particulars, summary judgment (if appropriate), and trial.

For courts, the decision underscores the heavy lifting remaining in distinguishing fraudulent inducement from noncriminal market commentary. Lower courts will need to navigate gray areas: what constitutes a material misrepresentation in the context of market predictions? When do enthusiastic endorsements cross the line into intentional deception designed to monetize followers’ trades? Those are evidentiary questions that often depend on nuance—timing of trades, deceptive concealment of positions, coordination among promoters, and evidence of intent.

Finally, there is an institutional consideration: prosecutions against high‑profile “finfluencers” carry public attention and First Amendment optics. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is narrow enough to avoid sweeping overbroad holdings, but prosecutors should be mindful of constitutional and policy arguments about chilling lawful market commentary. Careful charging decisions and robust proof will be critical to avoid encroaching on protected speech.

## Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal is a predictable, jurisprudentially coherent application of the post‑Ciminelli landscape as clarified by Kousisis. It reaffirms a simple principle: inducing someone to hand over money or property by means of materially false statements remains classic fraud, and criminal liability does not require that the victim end up with a net, demonstrable economic loss. For anyone tracking the regulation and criminal enforcement of marketplace speech—particularly the fast‑moving world of online securities promotion—the opinion is an important marker. It preserves a path for prosecuting pump‑and‑dump schemes that operate through social media while leaving for another day the hard evidentiary work of proving who was induced, by what message, and when.
