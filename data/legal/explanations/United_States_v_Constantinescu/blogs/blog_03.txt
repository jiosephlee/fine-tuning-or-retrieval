Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# Mail/Wire Fraud as a Template: What This Decision Means for § 1348 Jury Instructions

Why this matters: The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the district court in the “pump-and-dump” prosecutions is more than a routine sufficiency ruling. In rejecting the district court’s narrow reading of 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the panel explicitly embraces the practice—common among lower courts—of importing mail- and wire-fraud concepts (material misrepresentation, intent to deceive, and the requirement that the scheme have an object of obtaining money or property) into securities-fraud prosecutions. That embrace, reinforced by the panel’s engagement with Ciminelli and Kousisis, will have immediate and practical consequences for how prosecutors frame § 1348 counts and how judges instruct juries about the elements of securities fraud in social-media driven trading schemes.

What the court said (and why it matters doctrinally)
The Fifth Circuit anchors its analysis in two basic propositions. First, § 1348 incorporates core notions familiar from the mail- and wire-fraud cases: a scheme to defraud (involving a material misrepresentation intended to deceive) and a culpable intent to defraud (both to deceive and to cause harm or obtain an object, typically money or property). Second, the panel distinguishes between the right-to-control theory the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli and the fraudulent-inducement theory approved in Kousisis. Put simply, Ciminelli says you cannot convict for stealing only “valuable economic information” (a nontraditional property interest). By contrast, Kousisis confirms that a defendant can be prosecuted for using a false statement to induce a victim to hand over money or property—even if the victim does not suffer net economic loss. Applying those precedents, the Fifth Circuit concluded that an indictment alleging that influencers posted false trading information to induce followers to buy securities adequately pleads a § 1348 offense: the object of the scheme was to obtain money (proceeds from sales), and the deception pleaded was aimed at inducing purchases.

That doctrinal package—(1) material misrepresentation, (2) intent to deceive, and (3) an object of obtaining money/property—now serves as the court’s roadmap for § 1348 indictments and, critically, for jury instructions in securities-fraud trials. The panel’s reasoning thus signals continued reliance on mail/wire fraud doctrine as a doctrinal template for § 1348 cases.

How that will shape charging language and jury instructions
Prosecutors will likely follow the Fifth Circuit’s cues in three concrete ways when they draft indictments and request jury instructions under § 1348.

First, charging instruments will continue to frame the “scheme” element in familiar mail/wire-fraud terms: alleging material misrepresentations or omissions by the defendant, deceptive conduct to induce a transaction, and the defendant’s intent to obtain money or property. Expect explicit language about the defendant’s use of false or misleading social-media posts to induce purchases, and parallel recitals that the object of the scheme was to realize proceeds from secret sales. Those allegations track Kousisis’ “fraudulent-inducement” theory and head off the Ciminelli problem by emphasizing that the victim surrendered money or property (even if, as Kousisis permits, the victim receives something of market value in return).

Second, jury instructions will be cast in the traditional mail/wire-fraud mold: (a) the government must prove a scheme to defraud involving material misrepresentations or omissions; (b) the defendant acted with intent to deceive; and (c) the object of the scheme was to obtain money or property. Judges will likely incorporate language from mail/wire precedent (and the Fifth Circuit’s opinion) explaining that the “object” can be the defendants’ own receipts from concealed sales and that actual net economic loss to a victim is not an element. That instruction will be important for prosecutors seeking convictions in cases where victims ultimately hold securities worth something, but the defendants’ deception nonetheless induced the purchase.

Third, prosecutors will press for a concise statement to juries of the fraudulent-inducement theory: that a defendant obtains money or property by intentionally making material false statements that cause the victim to hand over money or property (for example, by purchasing shares). Kousisis provides cover for presenting to jurors the notion that the fraud is complete once the victim parts with money or property under materially false pretenses—even if the defendant provides consideration of equal value or the victim doesn’t end up economically worse off.

Open questions and instruction drafting headaches
Although the Fifth Circuit’s opinion is clear about the broad contours, it leaves several instruction drafting issues unresolved—practical disputes that will surface when judges try to distill the opinion’s reasoning for juries.

Materiality. The opinion assumes materiality as part of the scheme-to-defraud element (consistent with the mail/wire model) but does not elaborate on what juries should understand by “material” in the social-media context. In classic mail/wire fraud cases, courts instruct juries that a fact is material if it would have a natural tendency to influence, or be capable of influencing, the decision of the decisionmaker. That standard fits poorly with influencer-promoted securities: what matters is not only whether the misstatement would influence a reasonable investor, but whether it was of the sort likely to induce the particular followers to buy. Expect litigation over whether to give a “reasonable investor” formulation, a “capable of influencing” formulation, or some hybrid. Prosecutors will want to emphasize the capacious “capable of influencing” test (to capture diverse follower reactions), while defense counsel will press for a narrower standard that requires a showing the misstatement actually influenced purchasing decisions.

The “object” of the scheme. Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit permit prosecutors to identify the object as obtaining money or property even where the defendant provided something of value in return. But jury instructions must be careful in describing that “object.” If the instruction is too terse—merely saying “the object was to obtain money or property”—defense jurors may misunderstand whether the government must prove that victims were left worse off. The safer path (from the government’s perspective) is an instruction that explains: obtaining money or property need not mean the victim suffered net pecuniary loss; it is enough that the defendant intended to and did induce the victim to part with money or property through materially false statements.

Proof of intent to defraud. The Fifth Circuit follows the classic two-pronged intent test from Evans: intent to deceive and intent to cause some harm to result from the deceit (or at least to obtain money/property). How to frame that in jury instructions will be disputed. Should judges permit the government to rely on circumstantial inferences—e.g., coordinated timing of purchases and posts, secret sales, and profit-taking—to show intent? Likely yes, but clearer guidance will be necessary about what inferences are permissible and which would improperly allow jury speculation about motive or mere greed.

Fraudulent inducement vs. right-to-control. The opinion stresses the distinction between fraudulent inducement and the right-to-control theory disallowed by Ciminelli. Trial judges will therefore need to craft instructions that explain that the case is not about depriving victims of “valuable economic information”—which Ciminelli says is not a property interest—but about inducing victims to hand over money or property. That distinction can be subtle in a social-media context: the same post that misstates trading positions both withholds information and makes a concrete claim that induces a transaction. Jury instructions must therefore make clear (1) the government’s theory is fraudulent inducement (i.e., the inducement caused a transfer of money/property), and (2) that merely withholding or controlling information (without inducing surrender of money/property) cannot sustain a conviction.

Practical downstream effects
The Fifth Circuit’s approach has both deterrent and prosecutorial implications. For prosecutors, it endorses a broad toolkit for pursuing modern securities frauds that rely on influencers and online micro-targeting: plead traditional elements (material misrepresentation, intent, and object), emphasize inducement and proceeds, and use mail/wire-fraud precedent to craft jury narratives. That will make § 1348 prosecutions more tractable in contexts where defendants traded on social-media credibility to move markets.

For defense counsel, the decision tightens the battleground: defenses will focus less on abstract appeals to “information control” and more on concretely disputing materiality and intent. Expect pretrial fights over proposed instructions on materiality, intent, and the description of the “object” of the scheme. Defenses might also emphasize that (i) followers received securities (not nothing), and (ii) social-media posts are often opinionated or hyperbolic rather than precise misstatements, seeking instructions that require the government to prove specific, actionable false statements rather than puffery.

Finally, appellate courts and the Supreme Court may not be done shaping the perimeter. The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on mail/wire doctrine will encourage other circuits to adopt similar templates, but differences in how courts instruct on materiality, intent, and the “object” of the scheme could produce circuit splits that prompt higher-court clarification. The interplay among Kousisis, Ciminelli, and preexisting mail/wire-fraud doctrine will be the doctrinal axis along which those disputes turn.

Conclusion
The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is an explicit invitation to treat § 1348 as a securities-specific incarnation of mail/wire fraud doctrine, especially when allegations involve fraudulent inducement on social media. Practically, that means prosecutors will continue to borrow standard mail/wire-fraud language for indictments and will press judges for jury instructions that: (1) treat material misrepresentations as central, (2) allow circumstantial proof of intent to deceive and to obtain money/property, and (3) make clear that net pecuniary loss to victims is not a required element. But the opinion leaves important drafting and instructional questions open—materiality’s precise formulation, how to explain the “object” of the scheme, and how to cabin permissible inferences of intent—so expect sustained litigation over jury charge language in this fast-growing class of cases.
