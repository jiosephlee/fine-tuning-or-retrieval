# No Net-Loss Needed: Profit Motive and the Intent Element in Securities Fraud

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision reversing the dismissal of a multi-defendant “pump-and-dump” indictment is a clean, contemporary application of two recent Supreme Court decisions — Ciminelli and Kousisis — and it answers a question that has troubled defense counsel and lower courts since those cases: can a securities-fraud prosecution survive when the government’s theory is that the defendants induced people to buy securities (parting with their money), even though the investors received market-value shares and may not have suffered a net pecuniary loss? The Fifth Circuit says yes. That holding breathes life into fraudulent-inducement theories in market-manipulation prosecutions and forecloses a common “no-harm, no-foul” defense that defendants in social-media-driven pump-and-dump schemes have increasingly advanced.

At stake are classic questions about the contours of “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (the securities-fraud statute), and their overlap with the mail- and wire-fraud jurisprudence that the Supreme Court has recently refined. The decision is important not only for the defendants in this particular indictment — alleged influencers who used social media to inflate securities prices and then sell out — but for any cases in which defendants claim their misrepresentations merely deprived victims of “valuable economic information” rather than of money or property.

## What the panel did

The indictment accuses multiple social-media traders of a classic pump-and-dump: buy a security, tout it falsely to followers, induce purchases that inflate the price, and then sell for profit. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the allegations only showed deprivation of valuable economic information — a theory the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli as an insufficient basis for fraud convictions under the mail/wire/statutory fraud framework. The district court thus concluded the indictment failed to allege defendants sought to deprive victims of a traditional property interest.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. It treated the indictment as alleging a fraudulent-inducement theory — i.e., that the defendants used material misstatements to trick followers into buying securities and thereby parting with money or property. Relying principally on the Supreme Court’s decision in Kousisis, the panel held that:

- Ciminelli bars prosecutions premised only on deprivation of valuable economic information (a right-to-control theory), but that is not this case.
- The fraudulent-inducement theory implicates money or property directly because the victims were induced to hand over money (to buy securities).
- Kousisis establishes that the fraud statutes are violated when a defendant schemes to obtain money or property, even if the victim does not ultimately suffer a net pecuniary loss (for instance, because the fraudster provided something of equal value or the market price later changed).
- The indictment alleged both deception and an intent to obtain money (the profit motive and alleged proceeds), satisfying the “intent to defraud” requirement.

Because the indictment alleged that defendants induced investors to purchase securities through material misstatements and then profited from selling, the panel concluded the government had pleaded the necessary elements and remanded.

## Why the court’s reasoning is persuasive

Two features make the panel’s reasoning strong and doctrinally faithful. First, it carefully separates the right-to-control theory invalidated in Ciminelli from the fraudulent-inducement theory validated in Kousisis. Ciminelli said courts cannot treat the denial of “valuable economic information” as a traditional property interest for purposes of mail/wire fraud. But Kousisis recognized that where the deceit causes a target to part with money or property — say, by entering a contract or purchasing an asset — the fraud statutes protect that money or property. The Fifth Circuit correctly read those precedents as complementary rather than contradictory.

Second, the panel relies on Kousisis’s clarification of the intent element: the government does not have to prove the fraudster sought to leave the victim economically worse off, only that the fraudster intended to “obtain” the victim’s money or property through deceit. That doctrinal point is central to many modern fraud prosecutions where the wrongdoer pockets proceeds after inducing transactions — even when the victim receives some economic value. The panel appropriately applied that principle to the indictment’s allegations that the defendants pumped and dumped for profit.

## Practical and doctrinal implications

The decision has several practical consequences for prosecutors, defense lawyers, market participants, and lower courts.

First, it validates pump-and-dump prosecutions that rest on fraudulent inducement rather than classic right-to-control theories. When an influencer touts a thinly traded stock and induces followers to buy, the government can allege that those buys are what the fraudster sought to obtain — the transfer of money — and thus plead a scheme to defraud. This gives prosecutors buoyancy in cases driven by social-media hype, where victims did indeed part with funds to buy securities.

Second, the ruling clarifies a limiting principle from Ciminelli without letting Ciminelli become a shield for market manipulators. Ciminelli drew a line against criminalizing the transfer of “information” per se. The Fifth Circuit’s application preserves that protection while rejecting a defense that mischaracterizes a classic manipulation as merely informational misconduct.

Third, the decision highlights what prosecutors must still do to make the theory stick: plead and later prove that misstatements were material, were intended to induce purchases, and that the defendants obtained money or property as a result. That is still a nontrivial evidentiary burden. Allegations of “pumping” must be tied to concrete transactions and profits. Mere boasts or hyperbole, absent evidence linking posts to induced purchases and defendant proceeds, will be vulnerable at motions to dismiss or for acquittal.

Finally, the ruling raises potential issues courts and litigants will need to grapple with going forward. For example, in some pump-and-dump instances victims may indeed purchase securities, but the defendants later buy back shares, or market dynamics produce no net loss to investors. Kousisis tells us that those outcomes do not foreclose liability, but they will affect proof of causation and damages at trial. The government must still demonstrate that the fraudulent misrepresentations were what induced the purchases — a fact question often requiring granular transactional and communications evidence.

## Potential downstream effects and open questions

Several downstream effects and doctrinal tensions deserve attention.

First, Case Selection and Pleading Strategy. Prosecutors will be encouraged to frame future market-manipulation schemes as fraudulent-inducement cases and to plead facts showing induced purchases and realized profits. Defence attorneys will focus on poking holes in the causal chain: did the tweet actually move the market? Were follower purchases the proximate result of the misrepresentations, or were other factors at play? Early discovery battles over communications, timing, and trading records will be consequential.

Second, Causation and Market Dynamics. Markets are fluid; multiple actors and public information influence prices. Proving that followers purchased because of a specific influencer’s misstatement — particularly at the aggregate level — can be challenging. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion reflects pleading-stage deference, but causation will be a live, contested issue at trial and on appeal. Expect vigorous disputes over econometric and forensic evidence, and possibly divergent approaches across circuits on what suffices to connect puffery or promotional posts to induced purchases.

Third, Interaction with Securities Law and Private Remedies. This criminal path sits alongside civil securities-enforcement tools (SEC actions, private fraud suits, Rule 10b-5 jurisprudence). The criminal bar’s expansion into social-media pump-and-dump prosecutions may influence civil settlements and enforcement priorities, but prosecutors must still observe the heightened proof and constitutional protections that attend criminal cases.

Fourth, Free Speech and Overbreadth Concerns. While the Fifth Circuit’s opinion rests squarely on fraudulent inducement, defenses will continue to raise First Amendment concerns where hyperbolic commentary or opinion is punished as fraud. The decision does not expand criminal liability to protected speech; it reinforces that when speech is knowingly false and used to obtain money, it can support fraud charges. But litigants may press broader challenges about mens rea, materiality, and the line between puffery and actionable deception.

Finally, Forum Shopping and Circuit Divergence. The Fifth Circuit’s approach follows Kousisis closely, but as lower courts continue to apply the post-Ciminelli/Kousisis framework, differences may emerge over fine points — for instance, how granularly to assess alleged inducement, or whether certain evidence suffices to show the defendant “had money in mind.” We may see interlocutory appeals and split-lines that the Supreme Court or en banc courts will eventually need to resolve.

## Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal reaffirms a practical and doctrinal point: fraud statutes criminalize schemes that use deception to obtain money or property, and courts need not wait for victims to show a net pecuniary loss before permitting prosecutions to proceed. Ciminelli’s narrow repudiation of right-to-control theories remains intact, but it does not immunize commercial fraudsters who induce people to hand over money through materially false statements. For pump-and-dump prosecutions centered on social-media influencers, that is a significant — and, for prosecutors, welcome — clarification. Defense teams will respond by sharpening challenges to causation, materiality, and mens rea; the next wave of motion practice and trials will test how robust that prosecutorial theory is in the messy reality of modern markets.
