Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# Injury Without Loss? Sentencing and Restitution After Fraudulent Inducement

## Why this matters

The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision reversing the dismissal of a “pump-and-dump” indictment crystallizes something the Supreme Court made clear in Kousisis v. United States: a fraudster can be guilty under the federal fraud statutes for inducing victims to part with money or property even if the victims ultimately received something of equivalent market value. That holding is all but inevitable in many social‑media‑driven market-manipulation prosecutions: a promoter who lies to induce purchases has “obtained” money or property from followers whether or not the ultimate transaction left those followers with a net pecuniary loss.

If that doctrinal position survives trial, it raises pressing downstream questions that matter far more for defendants and victims than the threshold question of guilt: how should courts quantify “loss” for Sentencing Guidelines purposes, how should restitution and forfeiture be calculated, and what defense and prosecutorial strategies will be most consequential when inducement — not a classic theft — is the theory of liability?

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion frames those issues by adopting the “fraudulent‑inducement” framing (as distinct from the “right‑to‑control” theory that Ciminelli rejected). But it leaves open several practical measurement problems. Below I walk through the legal contours and likely flash points that will determine outcomes at sentencing and during asset recovery.

## The doctrinal baseline: guilt without net loss, but rules still require quantification

Two key doctrinal points from the opinion (and from Kousisis) set the landscape:

1. Fraudulent inducement is a legitimate theory: a defendant who uses material misstatements to trick victims into contracts that require handing over money or property can be charged with fraud even where the victims receive something of market value in return.

2. The fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off — it is enough that the defendant intended to obtain money or property by deceit.

But conviction is only the beginning. Sentencing enhancements, restitution obligations, and forfeiture remedies all hinge on measures of loss or gain, proximate causation, and traceability. Those measures were not addressed in detail by the Fifth Circuit and can produce starkly different consequences from those in cases involving clear theft or embezzlement.

## Sentencing: the Guidelines “loss” calculation and its tensions

Most fraud cases use U.S. Sentencing Guidelines §2B1.1, which turns heavily on an offender’s “intended” or “actual” loss for purposes of calculating offense level increases. Several practical issues arise in inducement cases:

- Actual loss vs. intended loss. If victims ultimately received shares that had market value, the government may rely on intended loss (the amount the scheme aimed to obtain) rather than actual net loss. The Guidelines permit using intended loss when actual loss is hard to measure or when the defendant’s objective was to cause a loss. But Kousisis suggests that a defendant can be culpable even without a net loss, which invites divergence: prosecutors may attempt to use the full amount of investors’ purchases as “loss,” while defense counsel will emphasize market value received, subsequent sales, and whether investors were net harmed.

- Market value offset. A natural defense is to argue that victims received something of value (shareholdings) and thus any “loss” should be limited to the gap, if any, between what victims paid and the market value of what they received, taking into account subsequent market performance. This can sharply reduce or eliminate “actual” loss figures. Courts will wrestle with whether and how to credit the market value of securities at the time of purchase, at the time the misrepresentation was discovered, or at some other point; each choice yields different loss numbers.

- Gain as a fallback. Where measuring victim loss is thorny, prosecutors often rely on the defendant’s gain as a proxy — e.g., proceeds the defendants actually realized from sales. The Guidelines allow (and courts frequently use) readily calculable gains when victim loss is speculative. For pump-and-dump cases, tracing the defendants’ proceeds can produce large numbers (brokerage sales, transfers to beneficiary accounts), but gains-based calculations raise proximate-cause and traceability questions (were those funds the product of the fraud or of legitimate trading?).

- Number of victims and multiplicative effects. Pump-and-dump schemes can involve numerous victims whose purchases may be credited as individual losses. That multiplicity can balloon Guidelines calculations, but it also raises accuracy problems: double-counting of transactions, identifying which followers actually relied on which posts, and apportioning loss where investors transact in volatile markets.

- Temporal choice points. Whether courts measure value at the time of inducement, the time of sale, or when the fraud was discovered matters greatly. Defense counsel will press for a valuation point that minimizes loss; prosecutors will seek the point that maximizes it.

Given these frictions, sentencing judges will often exercise substantial discretion in resolving factual disputes around loss — and district courts’ factual findings (so long as they are not clearly erroneous) typically control Guidelines calculations on appeal.

## Restitution (MVRA) and the challenge of “value returned”

Restitution under the Mandatory Victims Restitution Act (MVRA) is another major arena of contention. The MVRA requires a court to order restitution to victims for the “full amount of each victim’s losses.” But what is a “loss” in fraudulent‑inducement settings?

- Victims who received market‑value securities. A central question is whether victims who paid for securities at an inflated price but retained shareholdings at market value have suffered a compensable loss. Kousisis tells us the fraud is actionable even if victims received value; it does not, however, say whether restitution must be awarded where the victim ultimately possessed securities worth the purchase price. Courts will likely require a showing of actual pecuniary loss — for MVRA purposes — that takes account of the market value of the consideration received. Thus, a victim who paid $X and received stock worth $X at the relevant valuation point may have a restitution claim of $0, while losses will be recognized where the security’s value declined (for example, if the investor was induced to buy and suffered post-purchase devaluation).

- Proximate cause and foreseeability. The government must, in practice, link a victim’s loss causally to the defendant’s misrepresentations. If a follower purchased because of a myriad of factors or engaged in later trading decisions that caused loss, courts may limit restitution to the portion of loss proximately caused by the fraud. That inquiry can be fact-intensive and invites expert testimony and tracing.

- Victim mitigation and offset. Courts will consider whether victims received any mitigation (e.g., partial recovery through subsequent profitable trades, insurance, or disgorged funds). Defense counsel will press for offsets that reduce restitution obligations.

- Administrative feasibility and victim identification. Identifying victims who relied on particular posts — and the amounts they paid because of that reliance — is administratively burdensome. Where proof is inadequate, judges may order restitution only to victims with clear loss models, leaving others uncompensated.

In short, restitution is likely to be ordered where victims can show a real pecuniary shortfall caused by reliance, not merely the fact of having been induced to purchase.

## Forfeiture and tracing proceeds

Forfeiture operates on the defendant’s proceeds or property traceable to the offense. It is often cleaner for prosecutors than loss-based sentencing enhancements because it focuses on what the defendant actually gained.

- Traceability is key. Prosecutors must trace particular bank accounts or transfers to ill-gotten gains; where funds are mingled among legitimate trading proceeds, tracing becomes complicated and contested. Courts will apply established tracing doctrines, but mixed sources and high-frequency trading can erode certainty.

- Disgorgement vs. criminal forfeiture. Criminal forfeiture aims at the defendant’s proceeds; it does not compensate victims directly. Victim restitution and criminal forfeiture can coexist, but the amount available for restitution may be affected by prior civil recoveries or administrative fees. Defense counsel will favor defenses that peel away proceeds as legitimately earned, reducing the scope of forfeitable assets.

## Litigation and defense strategies to contest quantification

Several practical defense approaches will be front and center in inducement cases:

- Attack causation and reliance. Show that investors’ purchases were not caused by the defendant’s posts (e.g., purchases preceded posts, or investors relied on other sources). Absent clear reliance, victim loss figures may shrink.

- Emphasize receipt of value. Argue that victims received market‑value consideration and therefore suffered no compensable loss at relevant times; press for valuation at points advantageous to the defense.

- Narrow victim lists and contest multipliers. Push back on broad victim counts and on the aggregation methods the government uses to multiply small misstatements into large, guideline‑raising loss figures.

- Disaggregate gains from legitimate trading. Meticulously trace transactions to separate legitimate profits from illicit proceeds; use expert accounting and market analyses to challenge traceability.

- Challenge intended‑loss attributions. Where the government uses intended loss to support sentencing, attack the factual sufficiency of any alleged “plan” to extract certain sums, showing absence of a calculable intended loss.

- Invoking acceptance of responsibility and mitigation evidence. Present post-offense cooperation, restitution arrangements, or remedial steps to reduce Guidelines exposure and support downward departures.

Effective defense strategy will often require parallel factual development at trial and at the sentencing phase, including forensic accounting and market-and-reliance experts.

## Prosecutorial approaches and practical tips

Prosecutors, for their part, will want to maximize recoveries and sentence enhancements while avoiding court skepticism:

- Build airtight tracing. Preserve records showing direct transfers of proceeds from sales to defendants’ accounts, and document transfers to personal expenses or purchases to support forfeiture.

- Aggregate with caution. When presenting loss figures, be prepared to link specific victims to specific misstatements and purchases; avoid overreliance on blunt aggregation that courts may find speculative.

- Use gain as a fallback. Where victim loss is contestable, show defendants’ profits (sales proceeds) as an alternative metric for punishment and forfeiture.

- Prepare market‑value analyses. Be ready to rebut defenses that claim victims suffered no loss because they received equivalent value; that will likely require analysis of market conditions, timing, and whether the value was illusory (e.g., stock price inflated shortly after sales and collapsed).

- Consider civil remedies in parallel. Recognize that some victims may be better served by civil claims or SEC actions that can more flexibly address disgorgement and investor recovery.

## Broader implications and final thoughts

The Fifth Circuit’s decision — consistent with Kousisis — resolves an important threshold question: fraudulent inducement, even without an ultimate net pecuniary harm to victims, can be prosecuted under the federal fraud statutes. But that doctrinal clarity creates, rather than dispels, practical complexity. Sentencing courts and restitution proceedings will increasingly have to grapple with valuation, causation, and traceability issues that are technically involved and factually contestable.

Practitioners should expect vigorous litigation at the sentencing and asset-recovery stages. Defendants will focus on proving receipt of value and breaking causal chains; prosecutors will concentrate on tracing proceeds and adducing intended-loss evidence or demonstrable gains. District courts will be charged with making fine-grained factual findings — and those findings will determine whether a fraud prosecution that survived dismissal results in severe punishment and meaningful victim compensation or something much less.

In the era of influencer-driven market movements, these unresolved measurement problems are not theoretical. They will determine whether fraud prosecutions produce deterrence and restitution or become exercises in punitive overreach. Careful, case-specific valuation and rigor in tracing will be the levers that determine which of those outcomes follows.
