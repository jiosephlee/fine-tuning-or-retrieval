Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 3: # Pump-and-Dump, Scalping, and Social‑Media Touting

This chapter uses United States v. Constantinescu, 5th Cir. (Oct. 2, 2025), as the vehicle for explaining how classic “pump‑and‑dump” schemes and modern social‑media touting are treated under the federal criminal securities‑fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, and how those schemes overlap with parallel civil securities doctrines. Constantinescu is useful because it applies recent Supreme Court decisions—Ciminelli (rejecting a pure “right‑to‑control” theory) and Kousisis (endorsing the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory)—to a social‑media pump‑and‑dump indictment. The opinion clarifies what an indictment must allege and illustrates the doctrinal line between protected “economic information” and the fraud that targets money or property.

The chapter proceeds in seven parts corresponding to common issues that arise in pump‑and‑dump prosecutions and related civil enforcement: the mechanics of a pump‑and‑dump; how affirmative misstatements, half‑truths, and omissions operate as fraud; materiality in market‑touting cases; manipulation versus misrepresentation theories; evidentiary and causation issues unique to social media; the statutory landscape (including Securities Act § 17(b) and the enforcement roles of the DOJ and SEC); and the “in connection with” requirement and its relation to Rule 10b‑5 jurisprudence.

## Mechanics of a pump‑and‑dump: accumulation, promotion, price inflation, secret liquidation

A pump‑and‑dump follows a predictable pattern. The fraudster first accumulates a position in a thinly traded security (accumulation). Then the fraudster broadcasts promotional statements designed to induce others to buy the security (promotion/pumping), creating buying pressure that inflates the market price (price inflation). Finally, the fraudster sells into that inflated market (dumping), locking in profits. Criminal liability under § 1348 and civil liability under Rule 10b‑5 turn on whether the promotional conduct involved deception (false statements, half‑truths, or misleading omissions) and whether the deception was used to obtain money or property.

Constantinescu’s indictment alleged precisely this sequence: defendants purchased securities, used their social‑media platforms to disseminate false and misleading information to induce purchases, and sold into the resulting price run, allegedly realizing $114 million in profits. The Fifth Circuit treated those allegations as prototypical fraudulent‑inducement conduct—i.e., a misrepresentation made to trick investors into transactions that transferred money or property to the scheme operators.

For prosecutors and civil plaintiffs, proving the accumulation/promotion/dump pattern usually involves combining trading records (to show accumulation and time of sales), contemporaneous communications (posts, messages, promotional materials), and market data (volume/price spikes temporally linked to the promotion). The stronger the temporal and circumstantial connections among these elements, the better the inference that the promotional activity was calculated to create liquidity and to provide an opportunity to dump into an inflated market.

## Misstatements and half‑truths: false trading‑position claims, misleading price predictions, and omissions about intent to sell

Fraud in pump‑and‑dump cases commonly takes three forms: affirmative falsehoods, half‑truths, and omissions.

- Affirmative falsehoods. These are straightforward lies—e.g., a promoter claims “I’m long and holding; this will go to $10,” when in fact she has already sold or plans to sell. Under the standard elements of securities fraud, an objectively false statement that is material and made with scienter can support liability.

- Half‑truths. A statement that is true in isolation but so framed as to create a materially misleading impression is actionable. Courts have long treated half‑truths as equivalent to affirmative misstatements because they “convey a meaning that is false.” Thus, a tout that posts selected facts about a company’s product or contract winsomely omitting that the poster was compensated to promote the company, or omits that the poster had already sold out, can be treated as deceptive.

- Omissions. Whether a nondisclosure is actionable depends on context. There are three classic scenarios in which an omission can be fraudulent: (1) where the defendant has a duty to disclose (e.g., fiduciary duty, or other relationship creating an affirmative disclosure obligation); (2) where silence renders affirmative statements misleading because those statements half‑truthfully describe the facts; and (3) where a statute or rule requires disclosure (e.g., Securities Act § 17(b) requires disclosure of paid promotion). In pump‑and‑dump cases, prosecutors often rely on the second and third routes: a promoter’s glowing statements about a security can be misleading if the promoter fails to disclose material facts about compensation, trading intent, or existing short‑ or long‑positions.

Constantinescu illustrates these points. The indictment did not rely on subtle duty‑to‑disclose theories; it charged defendants with making misrepresentations and omissions that induced purchases—classic fraudulent inducement. The court emphasized that alleging that promoters induced investors to hand over money in exchange for securities is sufficient to allege deprivation of a protected property interest under the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

## Materiality in market‑touting cases: what a reasonable investor would consider important in trading decisions

Materiality is an objective standard: a fact is material if a reasonable investor would view it as having significantly altered the "total mix" of information available. TSC Industries v. Northway (the classic formulation) remains the guidepost in both civil and criminal securities litigation. In the pump‑and‑dump context, facts commonly found material include the promoter’s ownership position in the security, whether the promoter has plans to sell, whether the promoter is being paid to tout the stock, and the basis (if any) for predictions about imminent price appreciation.

When the medium is social media, courts consider the practical significance of the information to a typical follower. A promoter’s failure to disclose that posts are sponsored or that the promoter will sell at the shown price point will likely be material because followers reasonably rely on the promoter’s expressed trading activity and expertise in deciding whether to buy. Constantinescu’s holding implicitly recognizes that such omissions and misrepresentations are material: alleged misrepresentations that induced purchases are at the core of the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

Materiality questions are often fact‑intensive and may survive indictment or summary judgment stages. At indictment, allegations that promoters induced purchases by materially false or misleading statements will, if pleaded with sufficient detail, satisfy the pleading requirement—even if the ultimate factfinder might disagree about materiality.

## Manipulation versus misrepresentation theories: where conduct fits within § 1348 and parallel securities doctrines

“Securities fraud” can describe several different legal species. Two important species in pump‑and‑dump contexts are (1) manipulation and (2) fraudulent inducement by misrepresentation/omission.

- Manipulation. Exchange Act provisions and Rule 10b‑5 prohibit manipulative acts that artificially affect market prices—spoofing, wash trades, matched orders, or other conduct intended to mislead the market about supply and demand. Manipulation does not necessarily depend on express misstatements; it targets conduct designed to create false or misleading impressions of price or volume. A pump‑and‑dump can be prosecuted as manipulation when the focus is on conduct that creates artificial market activity to move the price.

- Misrepresentation/fraudulent inducement. This theory focuses on deceptive statements or omissions that induce investors into securities transactions, thereby transferring money or property. The fraudulent‑inducement theory requires a material misrepresentation or omission made with intent to deceive and with an objective of obtaining money or property. Kousisis and Constantinescu reaffirm that fraudulent inducement is independent of manipulation: inducing someone to buy through false statements suffices to constitute fraud because the victim parts with money or property.

Under § 1348, courts have treated both manipulation and fraudulent‑inducement theories as viable. The line between them matters for proof and for the kinds of evidence courts will require. Manipulation prosecutions may emphasize market microstructure evidence and trading patterns; misrepresentation prosecutions emphasize the content of communications and the promotor’s intent and connections to the issuer or trading. In practice, pump‑and‑dump schemes often support both theories, and prosecutors will plead alternative theories.

Constantinescu exemplifies a misrepresentation theory: the indictment alleged that defendants posted false and misleading information to induce purchases and thereafter dumped their positions. The Fifth Circuit declined to treat the indictment as a mere “right‑to‑control” case and accepted the fraudulent‑inducement allegations as prototypical § 1348 conduct.

## Social‑media as the medium: evidentiary and causation considerations when fraud is disseminated online

Social media changes the way promotional messages are disseminated and complicates evidence and causation issues, but it does not change the legal principles.

- Reach and amplification. Social platforms enable rapid, wide dissemination. The size of a promoter’s following, the engagement rates of particular posts, and reposting/resharing patterns are all relevant to whether communications were likely to induce trading.

- Attribution and anonymity. A defender may contend that social posts cannot be attributed to them or that multiple accounts obscured who made statements. For prosecutors, demonstrating authorship requires platform metadata, account management evidence, login records, drafts, or corroborating statements (screenshots, communications with followers, or with co‑conspirators).

- Timing and causation. Social media often allows detailed time‑series analysis: linking the timestamp of a post to contemporaneous spikes in volume and price strengthens an inference that the post caused buying. Courts accept circumstantial evidence of causation—temporal proximity, concentration of buy orders after promotion, and matching of follower demographics with trading accounts. Constantinescu’s indictment relied on the natural inference arising from posts by influencers with large followings followed by price moves and by the defendants’ secret sales into the spikes.

- Platform features and content forms. Short, punchy claims, livestream statements, emojis, or screenshots of positions can all convey misleading impressions. Courts look to the substance rather than the form: an Instagram story or TikTok clip that claims a promoter is “loading up” when they are actually unwinding can be a materially misleading statement.

For prosecutors and civil plaintiffs, social media leaves a digital trail—posts, comments, direct messages, drafts, and payment records—that can be mined for evidence of intent and causation. For defendants, the medium also raises potential defenses—lack of attribution, lack of reliance by followers, or that posts were puffery—but such defenses are fact questions for trial.

## Contextual statutes and rules: the role of Securities Act § 17(b) (touting) and how DOJ and SEC divide enforcement

Two statutory instruments are especially relevant to paid promotion and touting:

- Securities Act § 17(b). This provision makes it unlawful to publish, give, or circulate any communication offering to buy or sell a security if such communication has been prepared or disseminated by a person who has been compensated for that activity unless the person discloses the receipt, the amount, and the fact of such compensation. § 17(b) thus targets paid touting and requires disclosure. A § 17(b) violation can be the basis of a civil enforcement action by the SEC and can supply proof that a promotion was paid and undisclosed.

- Rule 10b‑5 and Exchange Act provisions. Rule 10b‑5 broadly prohibits making untrue statements of material fact or omitting material facts necessary to make statements not misleading, “in connection with” the purchase or sale of any security. It is the principal civil mechanism for policing fraudulent touting and scalping.

Division of enforcement: the SEC enforces civilly and can seek injunctions, disgorgement, penalties, and industry bars. The Department of Justice prosecutes criminally under § 1348 and related statutes when the conduct is sufficiently culpable and the government elects criminal enforcement. Conduct can give rise to parallel SEC civil and DOJ criminal actions; the standards differ (criminal prosecutions require proof beyond a reasonable doubt and usually higher scienter), but the factual predicates overlap. In Constantinescu, the government brought criminal charges under § 1348; similar fact patterns are common subjects of SEC enforcement under Rule 10b‑5 and § 17(b).

The presence of paid promotion without required disclosure under § 17(b) is a strong factual hook for both civil and criminal enforcement. It may be probative of intent to deceive and of a design to induce purchases for the promoter’s benefit.

## The “in connection with” requirement under § 1348 and parallels to Rule 10b‑5

Both § 1348 and Rule 10b‑5 require a nexus between the deceptive conduct and a securities transaction—commonly expressed as the “in connection with” requirement. The requirement is designed to tie the fraud to securities markets and to avoid criminalizing all deceptive commercial conduct.

What the government must show depends on the theory:

- Fraudulent inducement. The government must allege that defendants used material misstatements or omissions to induce victims to enter transactions that involved handing over money or property (purchases of securities). Constantinescu relied on the Supreme Court’s Kousisis decision holding that fraudulent inducement is a valid way to ground securities fraud, because it targets the deprivation of money or property. Under this theory, it suffices that the false statements were made with the intent to induce purchases, and that purchases followed or were intended to follow.

- Right‑to‑control theory (rejected as a sole basis). Ciminelli held that a prosecution cannot rest solely on the claim that the defendant deprived the victim of the right to make discretionary economic decisions (i.e., “valuable economic information”) when no money or property was obtained. The decision protects purely informational expectations. Constantinescu avoids Ciminelli because the indictment alleged inducement to purchase securities—i.e., deprivation of money/property.

- Manipulation. To satisfy “in connection with” in manipulation cases, the government must show that the manipulative acts had a natural tendency to influence market activity in securities and were directed at the securities markets. Proving manipulative intent and market impact is key.

Parallel to Rule 10b‑5. The “in connection with” inquiry under § 1348 mirrors that under Rule 10b‑5, but criminal cases sometimes allow different evidence burdens about causation because the government does not need to prove reliance in the way a private Rule 10b‑5 plaintiff must. In criminal prosecutions, circumstantial proof that the deceptive conduct targeted purchasers is sufficient.

Constantinescu’s principal doctrinal point on this element is that conduct that induces buyers to part with money to buy securities squarely satisfies the “in connection with” requirement because the scheme’s object is to obtain money or property through the purchase or sale of securities. The opinion underscores that a fraud is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—even if the victim is left with market‑value securities or the defendant provided something of equal value.

## Proof of scienter and intent to defraud: what the indictment must allege

Scienter—generally an intent to deceive, manipulate, or defraud—is a required element of § 1348. Constantinescu shows how an indictment may properly allege scienter:

- Intent to deceive. The indictment alleged that defendants posted false and misleading information knowing or recklessly disregarding its falsity. Social‑media posts that contradict contemporaneous trading behavior (for example, claiming to be “loading up” while selling) are classic indicia of intent to deceive.

- Intent to obtain money or property. Kousisis and Constantinescu emphasize that alleging the defendant sought to obtain money or property is sufficient. Proof often comes from the economic motive (profits realized by dumping), the timing of sales, evidence of secret sales at or near peaks created by promotions, and the coordination of messages and trades.

- Intent to cause harm. Earlier Fifth Circuit formulations require an intent to cause harm as part of the “intent to defraud” element (e.g., Evans). But Kousisis clarifies that criminal and civil fraud statutes do not require proof that the fraudster intended to leave the victim worse off economically; it suffices that the scheme was aimed at obtaining money or property. Thus, alleging that defendants intended to profit by inducing purchases satisfies the intent requirement even when victims ended up with something of value.

At indictment, the government need only allege facts that, taken as true, make scienter plausible and that a scheme had money or property as an object. Constantinescu reversed a dismissal precisely because the indictment pleaded a classic scheme (pump, promote, dump) and alleged the necessary intent.

## Practical lessons and common defenses

From Constantinescu and related authorities, the following practical lessons emerge for students and practitioners:

- Charging. When seeking an indictment in a pump‑and‑dump case, plead the elements of fraudulent inducement: specific misstatements or omissions, the promoters’ intent to induce purchases, the resulting transfers of money or property, and the connection between the misstatements and the purchases. Detail social‑media posts, dates, follower sizes, trading records, and realized profits.

- Causation and market impact. Temporal linkage between posts and trading spikes is powerful circumstantial proof. Platform analytics, brokerage statements, and co‑conspirator communications are commonly used to show causation.

- Defenses. Defendants may raise several defenses: statements were puffery or opinion; attribution of posts is uncertain; disclosures were not material to reasonable investors; no intent to deceive (posts were mistakes or hyperbole); the conduct was legitimate trading or commentary rather than a scheme; or the conduct falls within Ciminelli’s protection of pure informational control when no money or property was obtained. Constantinescu shows that many of these defenses are fact questions that survive indictment but may be disputed successfully at trial or on summary judgment depending on the evidence.

- Civil/criminal overlap. Expect parallel SEC investigations and possible civil actions under Rule 10b‑5 and § 17(b). Disclosure violations under § 17(b) can reinforce criminal scienter allegations.

Conclusion

United States v. Constantinescu restates that classical pump‑and‑dump schemes disseminated through modern media remain within the reach of criminal securities fraud law so long as the government alleges a scheme to obtain money or property through material misrepresentations or omissions. The decision synthesizes Ciminelli’s limit on “right‑to‑control” claims and Kousisis’s validation of the fraudulent‑inducement theory: depriving someone of mere economic information is not crime, but inducing someone to part with money or property by deception is. For students, Constantinescu is a useful model for understanding how the elements of scheme, materiality, scienter, and the “in connection with” nexus operate in a social‑media amplifier era.
