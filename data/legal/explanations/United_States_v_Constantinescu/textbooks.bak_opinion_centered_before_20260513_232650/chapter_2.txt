Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 2: # Property, Deception, and the Fraud Statutes After Ciminelli and Kousisis

This chapter explains how modern Supreme Court decisions—most notably Ciminelli and Kousisis—shape what counts as “property” and what sorts of deception qualify as federal fraud under the mail, wire, and securities-fraud statutes. United States v. Constantinescu, a Fifth Circuit decision decided in 2025, serves as the primary vehicle for applying those doctrines in the securities (pump-and-dump) context. The goal is practical: to give you the doctrinal tools to identify when an alleged misrepresentation is a federal fraud, when it is only an unenforceable wrong or regulatory violation, and how prosecutors must plead a fraud offense to survive a motion to dismiss.

## Protected property interests under the federal fraud statutes

The wire-mail fraud statutes and § 1348 (securities fraud) are concerned with schemes to obtain money or property by deception. Courts therefore begin by asking whether the interest the defendant allegedly deprived the victim of is a “traditional” form of property protected by the fraud statutes. Traditional property includes cash, bank accounts, negotiable instruments, ownership interests, and other well-recognized proprietary rights. What it does not encompass, consistently with recent Supreme Court decisions, are generalized or intangible control interests that amount to a loss only of information, discretion, or “the right to control” assets.

This distinction matters because the government may not use the fraud statutes to criminalize all deceptive influence over another’s choices. The statutes reach deceptive schemes that secure money or property; they do not reach deceit that merely secures access to information or influence over a decision-maker if the only alleged “property” is the decision-maker’s control over his own assets.

## Ciminelli: limits on the right‑to‑control theory

Ciminelli clarified the outer boundary. There the government prosecuted a public-works contractor under wire fraud, alleging the contractor paid a lobbyist to obtain favorable action from a public official. The government relied on a “right‑to‑control” theory: the official’s right to control state funds was, the government said, the victim’s property. The Supreme Court rejected that theory. It held that the “right to valuable economic information” and the right to control one’s own assets are not among the traditional property interests the fraud statutes protect; accordingly, depriving someone solely of valuable information necessary to make a discretionary business decision cannot support a fraud conviction.

Ciminelli thus draws a bright line: a prosecution that depends solely on showing that the defendant deprived the victim of the ability to exercise discretion (by withholding or corrupting information) will not survive. The decision does not, however, immunize all deceptive conduct that affects choices. It only rejects using the fraud statutes to police the purely informational or control-related harms that do not involve the obtaining of money or property.

## Kousisis: validating the fraudulent‑inducement theory and dispelling a net‑loss requirement

Kousisis supplied the complementary principle that breathes life back into many ordinary fraud prosecutions. The Court recognized and approved the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory: when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that requires handing over money or property, the defendant has “obtained” that money or property within the meaning of the fraud statutes—even if the defendant provided something of comparable value in return and even if, in net economic terms, the victim is not worse off.

Two points from Kousisis are especially important for doctrine and pleading:

1. Object-of-the-fraud focus. The critical inquiry is whether obtaining money or property was an object of the scheme. If deception was used to secure a transfer of money or property, that transfer is the object of the fraud, and the statute can be satisfied.

2. No net-loss prerequisite. The statutes do not require proof that the defendant intended to, or actually did, cause a net pecuniary loss to the victim. A defendant may be guilty under the fraudulent‑inducement theory even when the victim receives fair-market value or some benefit in return. What matters is that the victim was induced—by material misstatements—to part with money or property.

Kousisis thereby preserves classic fraud liability for inducements to buy, to contract, or to transfer property when the inducement depends on a fraudulent misrepresentation—even in contexts (like government contracting or securities trading) where the recipient later obtains some value.

## Intent to defraud after Kousisis and Ciminelli

The criminal mental state in fraud prosecutions has two dimensions in the Fifth Circuit formulations that Constantinescu applies: (1) an intent to deceive, and (2) an intent that some harm result from the deceit. After Kousisis, the second prong does not require an intent to inflict a net pecuniary injury; rather, it is satisfied when the defendant intended to obtain money or property as the object of the deception. Thus “harm” in this context is not limited to making the victim poorer overall but includes causing the victim to part with money or property that the defendant sought to acquire.

This distinction answers a frequent defense argument: many fraud defendants concede they sought to profit but contend they never intended to harm anyone. Under current Supreme Court precedent, that concession does not defeat fraud liability. If the profit was pursued by knowingly inducing victims to transfer money or property through material lies, the intent-to-defraud element is satisfied.

## Why market‑value exchanges do not negate fraud liability

A recurring factual scenario raises the question whether a victim’s receipt of market-value consideration negates a fraud charge: for example, an investor buys a stock at a price the seller or promoter misrepresented as justified by undisclosed information, or a government agency receives services under a contract procured by lies. Kousisis directly addresses this: providing something of “equal value” in return does not immunize the defendant if the victim was induced to make the transfer by material misrepresentation. The focus is the integrity of the transaction—not whether the victim walked away poorer on net.

The policy rationale is straightforward. The fraud statutes protect the integrity of a party’s decision to transfer property; when lies manipulate that decision, the victim’s consent is no more legitimate than consent procured by force or theft. Proof that a victim later benefited or received market value goes to the defendant’s theory of the case and to issues of causation and damages at trial, but it does not negate the statutory elements where the indictment or proof establishes inducement-by-deception and an object to obtain property.

## Fifth Circuit precedents on loss, gain, and injury: Baker and related authority

United States v. Baker (5th Cir. 2019) is emblematic of the Fifth Circuit’s approach to loss and gain in fraud prosecutions. Baker stressed that the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” correspondence between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain. The government may prove a scheme that obtains property without showing that the defendant’s gain precisely equals the victim’s loss. Baker and cases like Evans and Greenlaw provide the Fifth Circuit’s working formulation: a scheme to defraud requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive to obtain value; intent to defraud requires intent to deceive and to cause harm, understood as causing the transfer of money or property.

Constantinescu fits comfortably within this line. The Fifth Circuit applied those principles to reverse dismissal of an indictment alleging a social-media pump-and-dump: the indictments alleged material misrepresentations by influencers that induced their followers to purchase securities, and alleged the object of the scheme was to obtain money through later secret sales. Under Baker and the Supreme Court precedents, those allegations pleaded a scheme to obtain money or property by deception.

## Applying the doctrines in securities contexts: inducement to purchase as deprivation of money

Securities prosecutions commonly present tricky questions about property and deception. Two recurring fact patterns illustrate the doctrinal work you must do:

1. Insider trading/omission cases in which the only alleged wrongdoing is withholding material nonpublic information. If the government’s theory is that the defendant deprived the market or counterparties of information (a “right‑to‑control” claim), Ciminelli warns that this theory alone cannot support a fraud conviction. There must be an allegation that the defendant obtained money or property by deception, not merely that the defendant left others ignorant.

2. Pump‑and‑dump and other market‑manipulation schemes in which the defendant posts false information to induce others to buy or sell. Here the fraudulent‑inducement theory is directly applicable. When a promoter makes material misrepresentations to induce followers to buy securities—and those induced purchases cause followers to part with cash to acquire securities—the indictment alleges the deprivation of money or property. The promoter’s subsequent sale of shares at an inflated price shows the object of the scheme was to obtain money.

Constantinescu illustrates the second pattern. The indictment alleged that social-media influencers bought securities, made false and misleading public statements to induce followers to buy, and then sold their holdings. The Fifth Circuit held those allegations properly pleaded a scheme to obtain money or property: the followers were tricked into transferring money in exchange for securities; that transfer was the object of the fraud. The district court’s contrary view—that the indictment alleged only a wrongful deprivation of “valuable economic information” and not property—was erroneous because the indictment explicitly alleged induced purchases and the defendants’ intent to profit from those induced transfers.

## Practical consequences for pleading and proof

What must prosecutors allege to survive a motion to dismiss after Ciminelli and Kousisis? At minimum, an indictment should do three things (the Fifth Circuit’s indictment sufficiency criteria):

1. Plead the elements. Allege a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, describing the material misrepresentations or omissions and the defendant’s fraudulent objective.

2. Plead the object of the deception. If the government relies on fraudulent inducement, allege that the victim was induced to transfer money or property (for example, to purchase securities), and that obtaining that money or property was an object of the scheme.

3. Plead causation facts that go beyond bare conclusions. Identify how the misrepresentations were communicated, by whom, to whom, and how they caused transfers (e.g., followers purchased shares after the posts).

At the proof stage, defendants’ best arguments will be targeted to causation, reliance, materiality, and proximate cause. They will emphasize Kiminelli‑type themes—this was only information control, or the victim got something equal in value, or the alleged misstatements did not actually cause the purchases. Those are trial issues; they do not ordinarily defeat a facially sufficient indictment that alleges inducement-to-pay and an object to obtain money.

## Limits and closing observations

Ciminelli and Kousisis together create a balanced framework. Ciminelli prevents the fraud statutes from becoming a catchall for regulatory or political corruption theories that rest solely on manipulating discretionary decision-making or access to information. Kousisis ensures that the statutes still reach classic commercial deception—fraudulent inducement to buy, contract, or transfer property—even when the victim later received some tangible benefit. The Fifth Circuit’s Constantinescu applies those principles in a securities context: inducements to purchase securities by material misstatements properly allege the deprivation of money or property, and an indictment doing so survives dismissal even if the victims later obtained market-value securities or otherwise were not left worse off on net.

As you study fraud doctrine, keep these practical rules in mind. Identify clearly whether the alleged victim’s “property” is traditional money or property—or merely control or information. If the latter, Ciminelli bars a fraud theory that rests on deprivation of control alone. If the former, Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit authorize a fraudulent‑inducement theory that focuses on whether the defendant used deception to obtain money or property as the object of the scheme.
