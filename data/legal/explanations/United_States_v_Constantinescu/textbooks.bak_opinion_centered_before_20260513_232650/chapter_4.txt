Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 4: # United States v. Constantinescu: Indictment Sufficiency and the Scheme/Intent Elements

This chapter uses the Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu to teach how courts evaluate whether an indictment sufficiently alleges the two central elements of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348: (1) a scheme to defraud, and (2) an intent to defraud. The opinion is a useful vehicle because it applies recent Supreme Court limits and clarifications (Ciminelli and Kousisis) to a modern factual setting: alleged “pump-and-dump” activity by social‑media traders. The opinion illustrates how pleading rules and substantive doctrine intersect at the indictment stage, and it clarifies what an indictment must and need not allege to survive dismissal.

## Factual setting: social-media traders and alleged pump-and-dump conduct

The indictment alleges that a group of social‑media personalities—each with large followings and self‑described expertise in stock trading—purchased securities, posted false and misleading statements about their positions and the prospects of the securities to induce followers to buy, and then sold their holdings at elevated prices. The complaint quantifies the alleged proceeds at roughly $114 million. That factual posture—traders broadcasting trading activity and price prospects to followers who then buy—frames a classic fraudulent‑inducement theory: victims were allegedly tricked into handing over money in exchange for securities.

Understanding the charged conduct matters because the legal question was not whether the defendants actually did it (that is for trial), but whether the allegations, taken as true, described conduct that falls within the reach of § 1348.

## Procedural posture: motion to dismiss and de novo review

Defendants moved to dismiss the superseding indictment. The district court granted the motion, reasoning that the indictment alleged only deprivation of valuable economic information (a right to control) and not a protected property interest, so it failed to state an offense. The Fifth Circuit reviewed the sufficiency of the indictment de novo. At this pleading stage the court accepts the indictment’s allegations as true and decides whether those allegations, if proven, would constitute the charged crime. The relevant pleading standard is drawn from Rule 7(c) principles as developed in Fifth Circuit cases: an indictment must allege each material element of the offense, provide sufficient notice to the defendant of what is charged, and preserve a double jeopardy bar to future prosecutions.

## What an indictment must do: elements, fair notice, double jeopardy

The opinion reiterates long‑standing pleading requirements. An indictment must enumerate prima facie elements of the charged offense, fairly inform defendants of the charges, and support double jeopardy protections against later reprosecution (Gaytan; Guzman‑Ocampo; Fontenot). That standard is deliberately modest: it does not require detailed proof or evidence at the pleading stage. Instead, the question is whether the words of the indictment, accepted as true, charge each material legal element.

Applied here, the court asked whether the indictment’s allegations—false and misleading public statements intended to induce purchases, purchases by followers, and defendants’ secret sales—sufficiently pleaded a scheme to defraud and the requisite intent under § 1348.

## The “scheme to defraud” element: targeting money/property, not just information

§ 1348 criminalizes schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” and schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” The Fifth Circuit, following its own precedents (Greenlaw; Evans), treats these concepts in line with mail and wire fraud doctrine: a “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value (for example, money).

The critical distinction is between (a) a scheme that seeks to deprive a person of a traditional property interest, and (b) a scheme that merely seeks to deprive a person of valuable economic information or the right to control an asset. A fraudulent inducement to buy a security is the archetypal example of (a): the defendant’s misrepresentations caused victims to part with money in exchange for securities. The Constantinescu indictment expressly alleged that defendants’ misstatements induced followers to purchase securities—i.e., to transfer money or property. That allegation directly targets money/property rather than only informational control.

## Right‑to‑control versus fraudulent‑inducement: Ciminelli and Kousisis applied

Two recent Supreme Court decisions structured the trial court’s and the Fifth Circuit’s analysis. In Ciminelli, the Court rejected the “right‑to‑control” theory of fraud: federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, and the “right to valuable economic information” is not such an interest, so depriving someone solely of that informational control cannot sustain a fraud conviction. Thus, an indictment that pleads only that a defendant sought to manipulate a decisionmaker by withholding or corrupting information about how that decisionmaker would use assets, without alleging the defendant sought the victims’ money or property, is insufficient.

But Kousisis clarified and limited Ciminelli’s reach by approving the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a defendant who uses material misstatements to trick a victim into a transaction that requires handing over money or property can be charged with fraud even if the defendant’s scheme does not aim to leave the victim economically worse off. Kousisis emphasized that fraudulent inducement is not a repackaged right‑to‑control claim; instead it protects money and property that victims transfer as a result of fraudulent misrepresentations. Kousisis thus made plain that the presence or absence of net economic loss to the victim is not the determining factor; the relevant inquiry is whether the defendant sought to obtain the victim’s money or property by deception.

Applying those two decisions, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the district court erred in treating the indictment as a disguised right‑to‑control case. The Constantinescu indictment did not allege only that the defendants deprived followers of information; it alleged that defendants induced purchases—i.e., that they induced followers to hand over money or property by materially false statements. Under Kousisis, those allegations state a fraudulent‑inducement claim within § 1348 despite any argument that the followers ultimately received securities of market value.

## Intent to defraud and the absence of required net loss

The “intent to defraud” element requires an intent to deceive and an intent that harm will result from the deception. The Fifth Circuit relied on Kousisis to reject the notion that the government must allege the defendants intended to leave victims economically worse off. Kousisis held that a fraudster violates the statute when she schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property, regardless of whether the fraudster sought to cause a net pecuniary loss. Thus, a profit motive—evidence that the defendant had “money in mind”—is sufficient to allege an intent to defraud when tied to allegations that the defendant used deception to cause victims to transfer money or property.

In Constantinescu, the indictment alleged the object of the scheme was to “pump” prices by false statements and then “dump” by selling for profit. Those allegations directly support an inference that defendants intended to obtain their followers’ money through deceptive inducements. The indictment therefore pleaded the necessary intent even though victims may have received securities of value rather than “nothing” in return.

The court also noted that precedent in the Fifth Circuit (Baker) and elsewhere has recognized that the fraud statutes do not impose a “mirror‑image” requirement—victim loss need not precisely equal defendant gain. What matters for pleading purposes is that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the scheme.

## Integration of Greenlaw, Evans, and Baker

The Fifth Circuit framed its analysis by relying on Greenlaw for the basic two‑element formulation of securities fraud (scheme to defraud; intent). It drew on Evans to articulate that a “scheme to defraud” involves a material misrepresentation intended to deceive to obtain something of value. Baker provided doctrinal support for the conclusion that the absence of a precise net loss to the victim does not defeat an allegation that the defendant sought to obtain money/property. Taken together, these precedents, as applied in Constantinescu, show how the panel navigated the boundary established by Ciminelli and the clarifying holding in Kousisis.

## Outcome and what remains for trial

The Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal and remanded for further proceedings. That outcome is important doctrinally but limited in practical effect: the court decided only that the indictment, as pleaded, satisfies Rule 7(c) and the statutory elements when the allegations are accepted as true. It did not resolve any contested factual issues—materiality, whether statements were actually false, whether defendants had the necessary scienter, reliance by followers, causation between posts and purchases, or the precise measure of victims’ losses. Those factual questions and potential defenses remain for trial or summary disposition under appropriate standards.

## Applying Constantinescu to new fact patterns: guidance for pleadings and defenses

A few practical lessons follow from the opinion. First, at the pleading stage, an indictment survives challenge if it alleges deception that induced victims to transfer money or property—even where victims received something of market value in return—because fraudulent inducement targets the transfer of money/property as the object of the fraud. Second, prosecutors should avoid characterizing schemes solely as deprivation of economic information if they intend to charge fraud; framing must show the defendant sought to obtain money or property through deception. Third, defendants retain several factual and legal defenses for trial: they can dispute falsity, materiality, causation (that the posts caused purchases), intent/scienter, whether followers were reasonable investors, and First Amendment issues in speech cases. Fourth, dismissal at the indictment stage is disfavored when the indictment tracks statutory language and alleges the core acts and intents: the court’s de novo review emphasizes legal sufficiency, not the strength of the government’s evidence.

## Key takeaways

United States v. Constantinescu teaches that (1) dredging recent Supreme Court authority—Ciminelli and Kousisis—into wire/mail/securities fraud doctrine requires careful discrimination between right‑to‑control claims and fraudulent‑inducement claims; (2) an indictment that alleges deception designed to induce victims to hand over money or property satisfies the “scheme to defraud” element under § 1348; and (3) the government need not allege an intent to cause a net pecuniary loss, only an intent to obtain victims’ money or property by deception. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal preserves the government’s ability to prove, at trial, whether the alleged pump‑and‑dump scheme actually occurred and whether the statutory elements are proved beyond a reasonable doubt.
