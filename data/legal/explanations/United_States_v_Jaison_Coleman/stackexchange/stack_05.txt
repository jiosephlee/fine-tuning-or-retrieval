### How did the court determine the scope of consent during a welfare check, and why did that allow a sweep of multiple rooms?
Question:
The panel applies Florida v. Jimeno’s objective reasonableness test and says the officers could "scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions" to check on the children’s safety. What in Lisa’s words (e.g., telling them where the kids were, warning about the dogs, and indicating where Coleman might be) objectively authorized movement through multiple rooms? Where is the line between a welfare check within consent and an impermissible broader search absent exigency or a warrant?
Answer:
Short answer: The court read Lisa’s words and conduct through the objective-reasonableness lens of Florida v. Jimeno: a reasonable person in the officers’ position would have understood her consent as authorizing entry and movement through the house to accomplish the stated welfare-check purpose—i.e., to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask basic questions about the children’s safety. The panel relied on specific statements Lisa made (where the kids were, that the dogs wouldn’t bite, and that Coleman was likely in a back bedroom) plus her conduct (opening the door, brushing the dogs aside, and leading officers to a bedroom) to conclude that movement through multiple rooms was within the scope of consent. 

Why those particular words mattered
- The opinion emphasizes that Lisa not only said “Okay” when the officer said “we almost need to come inside to make sure of that,” but immediately told the officer where the children were and that the dogs were not going to bite (Op. II.B). The court treated those statements as confirming she understood and acquiesced to an entry focused on locating and checking on occupants: “After responding ‘Okay,’ Lisa told Officer Kizer where the children were inside the home while also assuring him…and the other officers present that her dogs…would not bite.” 
- She also indicated Coleman’s likely location (“I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom”), which would naturally authorize the officers to check that area in the course of determining whether he posed a danger to the children.
- Her conduct reinforced the verbal consent: she opened the door, brushed the dogs away, led the officers to a back bedroom, and pointed out the children (Op. I.A). Those actions are the kind of outward conduct the Jimeno test asks us to interpret objectively.

The governing standard and how it was applied
- The court applied Jimeno’s “objective reasonableness” scope inquiry (what would a typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange) and reviewed the district court’s factual findings for clear error (Op. II.A). 
- Under that test, because the officers’ stated purpose was to “check the welfare of her children” and the interaction made clear where people and hazards (dogs, a possible location for Coleman) were, it was reasonable to “scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions” (Op. II.B). The sweep of multiple rooms was not an unrelated rummage; it was the minimal movement necessary to accomplish the welfare-check purpose.

Where the line is (as drawn by the opinion)
- The opinion frames the permissible intrusions as those reasonably necessary to accomplish the welfare-check objective: locating occupants and confirming safety. Movement to other rooms that a reasonable person would expect would be needed to accomplish that mission is within consent (Op. II.B: “Doing so necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions.”).
- By contrast (implicit in the opinion’s reasoning), consent does not authorize an objectively unreasonable expansion into searches unrelated to that purpose—e.g., rummaging through closed containers, searching areas clearly unrelated to locating people or ensuring safety, or a warrant-style exploratory search of personal effects—unless separate consent, exigent circumstances, or a warrant exists. The court expressly declined to rely on exigency or inevitable discovery here because it found the initial entry and sweep lawful on consent grounds (Op. II.B).

Practical rule for the line between permissible sweep and broader search
- Permissible: movement into rooms where a reasonable person would expect occupants or weapons to be located given the stated purpose (here, the children and the husband), and limited questioning to assess safety—precisely what Lisa’s statements and actions authorized.
- Impermissible without more: additional intrusive searches beyond what a reasonable person would understand as necessary to check welfare—for example, opening closed containers or searching private areas unrelated to locating people—would exceed the scope unless the resident’s words/conduct clearly authorize that broader intrusion or exigency/warrant exists.

Bottom line: The court relied on the totality of Lisa’s words and actions—her explicit locations for the kids and Coleman, assurances about the dogs, opening the door and guiding officers—to conclude a reasonable person would understand she consented to a sweep of rooms necessary to locate people and ensure their safety, not to a blanket, exploratory search of personal effects.
