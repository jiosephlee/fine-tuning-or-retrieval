### What exactly must a Rule 11(a)(2) conditional plea specify to preserve a suppression issue on appeal, and what issues are then waived?
Question:
The court says Coleman pled guilty while reserving the right to challenge the suppression ruling. Under Rule 11(a)(2), does the written reservation need to identify the specific suppression grounds (e.g., voluntariness vs. scope), or is a general "denial of motion to suppress" sufficient? Which other issues (like challenges to elements of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)) are waived by such a plea?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under Rule 11(a)(2) a defendant may plead guilty while reserving the right to appeal a pretrial motion, but the reservation is limited to the ruling(s) actually identified and accepted by the district court. In this opinion Coleman “plead guilty while reserving his right on appeal to challenge the suppression ruling,” and the Court of Appeals treated that reservation as preserving the district court’s denial of his motion to suppress generally — i.e., the Fourth Amendment ruling about consent and the scope of consent. See Opinion pp. 2–3 (plea with reservation) and II.A–B (appellate review of consent and scope).
- A general written reservation of “the denial of the motion to suppress” was sufficient here to preserve the suppression ruling on appeal; the Seventh Circuit reviewed the district court’s factual findings about voluntariness and the scope of consent (clear‑error for facts, de novo for legal principles) and addressed both issues. See II.A–B.
- Everything not encompassed by the preserved suppression ruling was waived by the plea — in particular, challenges going to guilt that do not implicate the suppression ruling (e.g., factual or legal challenges to the elements of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)) are forfeited by pleading guilty. The opinion treats the case as presenting only the suppression question and notes that the proved facts and the plea leave no need to reach alternative government theories (exigent circumstances, inevitable discovery). See pp. 3, 10 (district court adopted magistrate’s findings re consent and scope; Court affirms and says that conclusion “eliminate[s] any need” to consider alternatives).

What the opinion shows about what must be specified
- The opinion demonstrates that a reservation need not parse every conceivable sub‑theory of the suppression motion (voluntariness vs. scope) in technical detail so long as the written reservation and the district court’s acceptance make plain that the appeal will be limited to the motion to suppress. Coleman’s written reservation was treated as preserving review of the district court’s denial of his suppression motion as a whole; the Seventh Circuit then reviewed both voluntariness (totality of circumstances) and scope issues (objective‑reasonableness) as part of that preserved suppression ruling. See II.A (law governing consent and scope) and II.B (appellate treatment of voluntariness and scope).
- Practical point visible from the opinion: a broader, but clear, reservation of “the denial of the motion to suppress” will preserve appellate review of the district court’s suppression ruling in its entirety (including different legal/factual components the district court decided). But if a defendant intends to preserve only a narrow legal question, or wants to be sure particular sub‑issues survive, it is safer to specify them in the written plea and obtain explicit district‑court approval of that limited reservation on the record.

What is waived
- By pleading guilty (even conditionally under Rule 11(a)(2)) the defendant forfeits non‑preserved claims and most challenges going to guilt that are not encompassed by the preserved pretrial ruling. In this case the Court confined review to the Fourth Amendment issues the district court decided — consent and scope — and therefore Coleman’s plea waived other challenges to his conviction (for example, independent challenges to elements of § 922(g)(1), or factual disputes bearing on guilt that do not turn on suppression). The opinion’s structure — resolving consent and scope and saying those conclusions “eliminate any need” to reach alternative doctrines or other defenses — illustrates that the preserved issue was the suppression ruling and other claims were foreclosed by the plea. See pp. 3, 10.

Takeaway for practice
- To preserve a suppression issue on appeal under Rule 11(a)(2), get a clear written reservation that identifies the motion to be appealed and secure the district court’s explicit acceptance on the record. A general reservation of “the denial of the motion to suppress” was sufficient in Coleman to preserve both voluntariness and scope issues decided by the district court, but specificity avoids any dispute about the scope of the reservation. Everything beyond the preserved suppression ruling (including challenges to elements of the charged offense) will generally be waived by the guilty plea.
