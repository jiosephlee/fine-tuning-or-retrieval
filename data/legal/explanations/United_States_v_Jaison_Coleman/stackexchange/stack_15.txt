Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Jaison Coleman

### Does the objective reasonableness test for scope allow officers to infer consent to speak with the children and conduct a pat-down of Coleman, or was separate consent needed?
Question:
Inside the home, officers talked to the children about what happened and conducted a protective pat-down of Coleman (which he reportedly permitted). Under Jimeno’s objective test, does consent to "come in to make sure the kids are okay" include interviewing the children and briefly patting down the allegedly threatening spouse, or would those steps require separate consent or a different doctrine (like officer safety) to be valid?
Answer:
Short answer: Under the Jimeno objective-reasonableness standard, a reasonable person could understand Lisa’s consent to “come in to make sure the kids are okay” to include entering the house, briefly canvassing/locating persons, and asking basic questions of occupants. A protective pat‑down of the allegedly threatening spouse, however, is not automatically included in third‑party consent to entry; it requires either the suspect’s own consent or another legal justification (e.g., officer‑safety doctrine). In this case the court sustained the entry and scope because (1) a reasonable person would have understood the entry to include scanning and checking on the children, and (2) Coleman himself expressly permitted the pat‑down.

Explanation and authority from the opinion

- Legal standard. The court applied the Jimeno objective‑reasonableness test for scope: courts ask “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent?” (Opinion § II.A; citing Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991)). Consent must still be voluntary under the totality of the circumstances.

- What the consent covered. The Seventh Circuit agreed with the district court that Lisa’s consent to entry was voluntary and that checking the welfare of the children “necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions.” (Opinion § II.B). The court therefore held there was “no infirmity” in the district court’s finding that the officers respected the scope of Lisa’s consent once inside.

- The pat‑down. The opinion separately notes that, after the officers located Coleman, “Coleman permit[ted] Officer Kizer to conduct a protective pat down.” (Factual summary, § I.A). The court did not rest the validity of the pat‑down on Lisa’s consent to enter; instead, the record shows Coleman personally consented to the pat‑down. The court did not rely on exigent‑circumstances or officer‑safety doctrines to validate the entry or initial sweep because it found consent sufficient. (Opinion § II.B: “These conclusions eliminate any need to consider the government’s alternative contention that the doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery also supported the police’s entry.”)

Practical takeaway for the Jimeno scope question
- Third‑party consent to enter to “make sure the kids are okay” will, under Jimeno, reasonably include moving through the home to locate the children and asking them (and other occupants) brief welfare questions. Those actions are within the ordinary scope of such consent.
- A frisk/pat‑down of an adult occupant is a distinct intrusion. It is not automatically authorized by another occupant’s consent to entry; officers either need the suspect’s own consent (as happened here) or independent justification (e.g., a Terry‑style officer‑safety frisk) to lawfully conduct it.
- The Seventh Circuit affirmed on the factual record here because (a) Lisa’s consent was voluntary and reasonably understood to permit welfare checking, and (b) the pat‑down was in fact permitted by Coleman.
