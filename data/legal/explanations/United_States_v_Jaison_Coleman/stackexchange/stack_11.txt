### Are statements from children during a consent-based welfare check admissible to establish probable cause for a warrant, and what if the initial entry were unlawful?
Question:
The children reported a gun and threats, which were used to obtain a search warrant. Assuming the consent entry was valid, those statements support probable cause. But if the entry were unlawful, would the children’s statements be suppressed as fruits, or could doctrines like attenuation or independent source apply, given that the children were identifiable residents? How do courts analyze causation and taint in this scenario?
Answer:
Short answer
- If the entry was lawful (as the Seventh Circuit found here), the children’s in‑home statements can be used to establish probable cause for a warrant. That is what happened in this opinion.
- If the entry were unlawful, the children’s statements would presumptively be “fruits” of the illegal entry and therefore subject to suppression unless the government can break the causal chain of the Fourth Amendment violation by showing (a) attenuation of the taint, (b) an independent source for the information, (c) inevitable discovery, or (d) a subsequent valid consent (or another recognized exception). Which doctrine wins turns on the specific causation and voluntariness facts.

Legal doctrine and how courts analyze causation/taint
1) Fruit of the poisonous tree and causation
- The basic rule is that evidence obtained by exploitation of an unlawful search or entry is excluded as a “fruit” of the constitutional violation. Courts ask whether the challenged evidence was obtained by means sufficiently close to the illegality that it should be excluded (i.e., whether there is a causal chain from the illegal conduct to the evidence).
- The seminal attenuation inquiry (see Wong Sun v. United States and Brown v. Illinois) examines whether intervening circumstances have dissipated the taint. Factors include temporal proximity, the presence of intervening circumstances, and the purpose/ flagrancy of the official misconduct.

2) Attenuation
- If the children’s statements were elicited only after officers had entered unlawfully and the statements resulted from the presence of police in the home, the government must show intervening events that render the statements sufficiently an act of free will (or otherwise break the causal chain). Examples courts look for: a significant time lapse, Miranda warnings and a voluntary break in causal connection, or independent acts by the suspect or witnesses that produce the evidence.
- By contrast, where the statements are immediate and clearly produced because officers were in the house (no intervening change of circumstance), attenuation is unlikely to be found.

3) Independent source and inevitable discovery
- Independent source: the government may persist in using evidence discovered during an illegal entry if it can show that the evidence later was acquired by means wholly independent of the illegal entry (i.e., by a lawful search or sources untainted by the entry). If the warrant (or other lawful investigation) was supported by information not derived from the children’s statements, that can sustain admissibility.
- Inevitable discovery: the government can sometimes show that the evidence would inevitably have been discovered by lawful means. That doctrine requires proof that lawful means would have uncovered the same evidence regardless of the unlawful conduct.

4) Third‑party consent and later voluntary consent
- The Court in this opinion relies on Matlock/Cellitti principles that a third party with common authority can consent. If the initial entry were unlawful but the resident later and validly consented to a police return/search (or otherwise provided the information voluntarily and independently of the initial illegality), that later consent can purge the taint. Critical here is timing and voluntariness: a consent given while the taint remains (or coerced by the illegality) will not cure the taint; a clear, voluntary, and independent consent might.

Applying these doctrines to the facts in the opinion
- What the opinion actually holds: the Seventh Circuit found Lisa’s on‑porch “Okay” and her other responses furnished valid consent to enter; the officers’ in‑home actions stayed within that consent; the children’s statements then supplied probable cause for a warrant. The court therefore did not need to reach attenuation/independent source/inevitable discovery arguments (the opinion explicitly says those alternative doctrines were unnecessary).
- If one assumes the converse hypothetically (that Lisa’s “consent” was invalid and the entry unlawful), the children’s statements would have been collected only after the officers were inside because of that entry. On the record the court describes, the statements were immediate in‑house declarations to officers who had just entered and then asked the children questions. That sequence tends to show a close causal link between entry and statements, which weighs against attenuation.
- The government could try to argue independent source or inevitable discovery, but the opinion’s chronology indicates the warrant was sought “based on information gained following their entry into the house” — i.e., the children’s statements were the trigger for the warrant. In that factual posture, independent source is weak unless the government can point to some separate pre‑entry source (not present in this opinion). Likewise, inevitable discovery is a difficult showing when the only disclosed pre‑entry facts were the 911 call and Lisa’s on‑porch equivocation; the officers’ in‑home observations and children’s disclosures were the decisive new facts leading to the warrant.
- A potentially stronger government route (if the record supported it) would be to show a subsequent valid consent or other voluntary statement by a resident untainted by the prior illegality. The opinion emphasizes that the children were identifiable residents and that Lisa’s consent (as decided) would be sufficient. If, hypothetically, Lisa later (after a meaningful break and voluntary choice) gave a fresh, uncoerced consent or the children voluntarily repeated their observations in a context sufficiently detached from the illegal entry, courts may find the taint attenuated.

Bottom line for practitioners/students
- Under the actual opinion, the entry was lawful and the children’s statements properly supported probable cause.
- Hypothetically treating the entry as unlawful, suppression of the children’s statements is the likely outcome unless the government can demonstrate one of the recognized doctrines that severs the causal link (attenuation, independent source, inevitable discovery) or show a later, voluntary, and independent consent/statement by an authorized resident. The analysis will center on temporal proximity, the degree to which the statements were a direct product of the illegal presence, and whether any genuine intervening act or independent source broke the chain of causation.
