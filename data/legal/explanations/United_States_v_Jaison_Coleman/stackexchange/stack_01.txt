### How did the Seventh Circuit have appellate jurisdiction after a guilty plea—does 28 U.S.C. § 1291 treat a conditional plea as a final decision?
Question:
The defendant entered a guilty plea but reserved the right to appeal the denial of his suppression motion. The panel proceeds to the merits. Can someone explain the jurisdictional basis here? Is the "final decision" under § 1291 satisfied by a judgment following a Rule 11(a)(2) conditional plea, and are there any limits on the issues the court can hear when the appeal stems from a conditional plea focused on suppression?
Answer:
Short answer: The Seventh Circuit had jurisdiction because the district court entered a final judgment after Coleman pleaded guilty while explicitly reserving his right to appeal the denial of his suppression motion under the Rule 11(a)(2) procedure. That judgment is a “final decision” for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 1291 as to appellate review of the preserved pretrial issue, and the court’s review is limited to the issues the defendant properly reserved (here, the denial of the suppression motion), applying the usual standards of review for fact (clear error) and law (de novo).

Why that follows from the opinion
- The opinion itself describes the procedural posture: “Following the district court’s denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman chose to plead guilty while reserving his right on appeal to challenge the suppression ruling. That appeal is now before us.” (Opinion, II.A opening paragraphs.) The court therefore treated the district-court judgment entered after that conditional plea as the final judgment that triggered appellate jurisdiction.
- The panel proceeds to the merits and frames its review accordingly: it reviews the district court’s “finding of voluntary consent and any challenge to its scope for clear error.” (Opinion, II.A.) That statement shows the court understood its jurisdiction to review the specific, preserved suppression ruling and applied the standard appropriate to the district court’s factual findings.

Legal doctrine and limits
- Rule 11(a)(2) conditional pleas: When a defendant pleads guilty but expressly reserves the right to appeal a pretrial ruling (commonly a suppression ruling), courts treat the resulting judgment as appealable on that preserved issue. The Seventh Circuit’s opinion embodies that approach: it accepted the appeal and confined review to the suppression question Coleman reserved.
- Scope of appellate review: The appellate court is limited to the issues actually preserved by the conditional plea. Here, the opinion confines itself to whether Lisa’s consent was voluntary and whether officers stayed within its scope (and it expressly declines to reach alternative doctrines “in the end, then, we AFFIRM” because consent sufficed). The court does not re-litigate the plea, does not expand the defendant’s challenges to other aspects of the conviction, and does not allow collateral issues beyond the preserved suppression ruling.
- Standards of review applied in the opinion: factual determinations (e.g., that Lisa consented, scope of consent) are reviewed for clear error; legal questions (e.g., whether consent is an exception to the warrant requirement, scope-of-consent doctrine) are treated under settled legal standards cited in the opinion (Bumper; Matlock; Jimeno). (See Opinion, II.A–B.)

Practical takeaway for future appeals
- A Rule 11(a)(2) conditional plea produces a final judgment sufficient to invoke § 1291 appellate jurisdiction as to the specifically reserved pretrial ruling. The appellate court’s authority is, however, cabined to the preserved issue(s) and the usual standards of review. In this case, the Seventh Circuit limited itself to reviewing the voluntariness and scope findings (clear-error review of the district court’s factual findings) and affirmed.
