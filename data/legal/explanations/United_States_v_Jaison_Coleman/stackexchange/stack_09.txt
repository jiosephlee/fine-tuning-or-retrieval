Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Jaison Coleman

### How does Scott v. Harris affect appellate treatment of video evidence in suppression appeals—can the court rely on the video to override deference?
Question:
The panel quotes Scott that video can "evaporate any factual dispute." In suppression appeals, does the presence of clear bodycam footage license the appellate court to independently assess what happened (functionally de novo on facts) or does it still credit the district court’s view unless clearly erroneous? What are the Seventh Circuit’s limits on using video to displace credibility determinations or inferences?
Answer:
Short answer
Scott v. Harris supplies a tool — not a license — for an appellate court to treat body‑cam/video evidence as dispositive of disputed facts, but it does not abolish the ordinary standard of review. Where the video objectively resolves the factual dispute (i.e., the footage “evaporate[s]” the disagreement), an appellate court may rely on its own viewing to reject a contrary factual finding. Where the recording is ambiguous, the Seventh Circuit still defers to the district court’s factfinding and credibility calls unless they are clearly erroneous.

How the opinion applies (with the court’s own language)
- The panel begins by citing Norville, which in turn quotes Scott: a “video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist.” (Opinion I.A (quoting Norville, 43 F.4th at 682) (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007)).)
- But the panel also states the correct standard of review: district‑court findings on voluntariness and scope of consent are reviewed for clear error. (Opinion II.A: “As a court of review, our role is more limited. We review a district court’s finding of voluntary consent and any challenge to its scope for clear error.”)
- The court then shows how it applied both principles: “Having viewed the complete interaction between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we see no clear error in the district court’s finding that she consented ….” (Opinion II.B.) The panel explains specific, observable features from the video (tone, pause, initial denial, subsequent “Okay,” where she led officers) that make the court comfortable both to rely on the recording and to conclude there was no clear error.

Put another way: when Scott allows an appellate court to resolve facts itself
- If the video is truly clear and contradicts the district court’s factual finding (for example, it shows the complained‑of coercion never occurred or that the alleged sequence of events is impossible), Scott supports independently resolving that factual dispute on appeal. The Seventh Circuit invoked that idea but did not treat it as the only path: it used its own viewing to confirm the district court’s factual findings rather than to overturn them.
- The Seventh Circuit’s practical limit is that the video must objectively resolve the dispute. If reasonable minds could differ about what the video shows, the court will credit the district court’s view unless clear error.

Where deference remains
- For issues that turn on credibility, subtle inferences, or equivocal nonverbal cues that could reasonably be read more than one way, the Seventh Circuit adheres to clear‑error review. The opinion exemplifies this: after watching the footage, the panel still framed its ruling as finding “no clear error” rather than substituting its own credibility call outright. (Opinion II.B.)
- The court also uses the totality‑of‑the‑circumstances framework for voluntariness and scope of consent (citing Bumper, Schneckloth, Jimeno, and line items such as length of interaction, custody, and coercion), which means it will weigh multiple indicia that may not be fully captured by a clip. (Opinion II.A–B.)

Bottom line for suppression appeals
- If the bodycam footage is unambiguous and contradicts a district‑court factfinding, the appellate court may rely on the video and treat the factual dispute as resolved (the Scott principle).
- If the footage is ambiguous or the determination depends on credibility or inferences open to reasonable interpretation, the Seventh Circuit will defer to the district court unless its findings are clearly erroneous — and it will express its conclusion using that standard (as this opinion does).

Practical tip for briefing
- If you want an appellate court to adopt your factual account based on video, emphasize objective, unmistakable features on the footage and show that no reasonable factfinder could interpret them differently. If the dispute depends on subtle conduct or mixed evidence, prepare to meet clear‑error deference.
