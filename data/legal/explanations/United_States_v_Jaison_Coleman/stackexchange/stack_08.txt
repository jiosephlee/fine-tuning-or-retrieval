### Why did the panel decline to reach exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery, and when will an appellate court affirm on alternative grounds?
Question:
The government offered exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery as alternative justifications for the entry and sweep, but the panel said its consent holding "eliminate[s] any need" to consider them. As a matter of appellate practice, when will the Seventh Circuit reach alternative grounds to provide guidance or cement the judgment, and is there a doctrinal preference to resolve cases on the narrowest ground (e.g., avoiding separate exigency analysis post-Caniglia)?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel declined to discuss exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery because its dispositive ruling—that Lisa voluntarily consented to entry and the officers stayed within the scope of that consent—resolved the appeal. As the opinion puts it, the consent holding “eliminate[s] any need” to consider the government’s alternative theories, so the court affirmed on that ground alone.

Why the panel stopped at consent (doctrinal and practical reasons)
- Functionally dispositive ruling. The Seventh Circuit treated consent as the controlling legal basis for the legality of the entry and sweep. Once the district court’s consent finding was reviewed and found not clearly erroneous, there was nothing left to decide on suppression: the evidence was properly admitted because the entry was lawful. The opinion explicitly ties those points together: after analyzing consent and scope, the court says those “conclusions eliminate any need to consider the government’s alternative contention that the doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery also supported the police’s entry.”  
- Ordinary appellate restraint. The panel applied the narrow, case-dispositive principle: decide only what is necessary to resolve the case. By resolving voluntariness and scope under the Fourth Amendment, the court avoided unnecessary analysis of separate doctrines. That restraint is reflected in the opinion’s structure—careful fact-review of the consent exchange, application of the “totality of the circumstances” test, and then the explicit statement that alternative theories need not be reached.  
- Standard-of-review considerations. The opinion emphasizes that voluntariness and scope findings are reviewed for clear error. Because the court concluded the district court’s factual finding was not clearly erroneous, there was no practical need to reach other, potentially more novel, legal questions.

When an appellate court will nonetheless address alternative grounds
- If the primary ground fails. If the appellate court could not sustain the judgment on the first ground (for example, if it found the district court clearly erred about consent), the court would then decide whether any alternate theory (exigency, inevitable discovery, plain view, etc.) independently supports the result—assuming the alternative argument was properly preserved and presented.  
- If the alternative ground is necessary to resolve the case or to prevent a retrial. If the appellate court believes that affirming on an alternative ground is required to avoid remand or further litigation on a contested issue, it will analyze that ground. The opinion’s choice implies the inverse: because the consent ruling disposed of the suppression issue, the court did not need to—and did not—reach the alternatives.  
- If the parties fully brief and argue the alternative or the appellate court thinks guidance is important. Courts are more likely to address alternative legal doctrines when those doctrines were meaningfully litigated below and argued on appeal, or when the court concludes that guidance is needed for future cases. The opinion’s phrasing shows the court was not compelled to provide such additional guidance here.

About doctrinal preference for the narrowest ground (e.g., avoiding a separate exigency inquiry)
- The opinion demonstrates a clear preference for deciding the narrow question that disposes of the appeal. By resolving consent and scope first—and expressly declining to reach exigency or inevitable discovery—the panel followed the ordinary judicial practice of avoiding broader or unnecessary constitutional analysis when a narrower, dispositive ground is available. The court’s statement that its consent conclusions “eliminate[s] any need” to consider alternatives is the textual basis for that preference in this case.

Bottom line
The Seventh Circuit affirmed on consent because that ruling was dispositive and reviewed under the usual clear-error standard. The court therefore declined to reach exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. An appellate court will consider alternative grounds only when the dispositive ground fails, when the alternatives are necessary to avoid further litigation, or when the parties have properly preserved and developed those alternatives such that the court believes it should provide guidance. The opinion’s explicit language shows the panel exercised ordinary judicial restraint in adopting the narrowest resolution that disposed of the appeal.
