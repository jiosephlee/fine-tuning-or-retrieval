Chapter 10: # Practice Problems and Hypotheticals

This chapter uses variants of the facts in United States v. Coleman to sharpen your understanding of four interlocking Fourth Amendment concepts: (1) voluntariness of consent, (2) third‑party authority to consent, (3) the scope of consent, and (4) exigent‑circumstance and emergency‑aid doctrines. Each subtopic presents a short factual variation and then explains the governing law, how courts will typically analyze the problem (with attention to the Coleman decision), and practical lessons for litigators and officers. Because Coleman is a close fact‑driven decision about a welfare check, it is an especially useful vehicle to see how small changes in timing, words, tone, or the presence of third parties can change the constitutional outcome.

## Officer says, “We have to come in to check the kids,” and the occupant steps aside silently

Facts: As in Coleman, officers arrive at 4:00 a.m. for a domestic disturbance report. The occupant opens the door but does not speak when an officer says, in a firm tone, “We have to come in to check the kids.” She simply steps aside and lets the officers enter.

Law and analysis: Consent to enter may be given verbally or by conduct. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte announces the voluntariness inquiry; courts apply a totality‑of‑the‑circumstances test considering the person’s age, education, custodial status, length of detention, presence of physical coercion, and whether the consent was immediate or followed repeated requests. Matlock permits third‑party consent where the consenting person has mutual use, joint access, or other common authority over the premises. Jimeno governs the scope of consent by asking what a reasonable person would have understood.

A silent step aside is equivocal: it may be an implied grant of access, but it may equally be an acquiescence to police command or a coerced response to an authoritative statement. Whether court treats it as consent depends on context. Coleman is instructive: the Seventh Circuit emphasized the officer’s demeanor, the fact that he asked for permission, and that the occupant initially refused and later assented after a calm, short exchange. By contrast, an explicit command—“We have to come in”—carries a different tone than the Coleman officer’s “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside.” If officers state a command and the occupant makes no affirmative statement of consent, a court may be reluctant to treat silence and stepping aside as voluntary consent because the voluntariness calculus is influenced by whether the occupant perceived a real choice.

How courts usually resolve such a scenario: If the officer’s language and demeanour are compelling and the occupant reasonably perceives no choice, courts will likely find no voluntary consent and suppress evidence obtained from the entry. If, however, the officer’s tone is conversational, the occupant’s conduct is unmistakably inviting (for example, stepping aside while saying “go ahead” or opening the door wider), and there are other indicia of voluntariness (no custody, short duration, no repeated demands), courts will treat the conduct as consent. In close cases, the recorded footage (as in Coleman) will often control the outcome.

Practical takeaways: Officers should avoid framing requests as commands. If an occupant remains silent, officers should ask for clear communicative assent or, if safety legitimately requires swift entry, articulate exigent facts (e.g., audible cries from inside, sight of imminent injury) and document them. Defense counsel should emphasize coercive language and silence; prosecutors should emphasize conduct unmistakably inviting entry and the totality of circumstances.

## Spouse consents at the door; the other spouse appears at the threshold and objects

Facts: Officer Kizer asks for permission and Mrs. Coleman says, “Okay, come in.” As the officers step forward, Mr. Coleman opens the bedroom door and stands at the threshold, shouting, “No—get out—don’t come in!” The officers nonetheless enter and find contraband.

Law and analysis: The key Supreme Court cases are Matlock and Georgia v. Randolph. Matlock permits warrantless entry and search when a third party with common authority consents. Georgia v. Randolph holds, however, that when one co‑occupant is physically present and objects, the objecting occupant’s refusal prevails over another occupant’s consent; police cannot rely on the consenting occupant’s permission to enter against the physically present objector. Fernandez v. California clarifies an exception: if the objecting occupant is removed from the premises legitimately (for example, by lawful arrest), a later consent by the remaining occupant can validate entry.

Applying these holdings to the hypothetical: If Mr. Coleman is physically present at the threshold and openly objects at the time of entry, a court following Randolph will treat the entry as unconstitutional if the officers proceed over that specific, contemporaneous objection. Coleman is distinguishable: Lisa was the only person expressing consent at the door and Jaison was not present at the doorway until officers had already entered and conducted a sweep that discovered him. That sequence matters. In the hypothetical above, the objection is contemporaneous and physically present; Randolph therefore would likely suppress subsequent evidence derived from the unlawful entry.

Variations that change the outcome: If the objecting spouse appears after officers lawfully entered (for instance, he comes to the threshold only after the welfare check has commenced), the objection is not a contemporaneous refusal that precludes entry. If the objecting spouse is lawfully removed (e.g., arrested for assault) and then the other spouse consents, under Fernandez the subsequent consent may validate continued presence and search. Finally, if the objecting person is merely visible through a window or a doorway but not present at the door when the consent was actually given and relied upon, courts will examine who was “present” in the Randolph sense.

Practical takeaways: Officers should be vigilant to identify and respect an on‑the‑spot, present objection. If a present occupant objects, the safest route is to retreat and seek a warrant or otherwise justify entry by exigency. Defense counsel should emphasize precise timing—was the objection contemporaneous?—and stress Randolph when the objector was at the threshold.

## Consent to “look in on the kids” followed by officers opening drawers in the kitchen

Facts: Lisa says “okay” to a request to “look in on the kids.” Inside, officers open bedroom doors, briefly scan rooms, then begin opening kitchen drawers and a bedside dresser. They find drugs and a gun.

Law and analysis: This is a classic scope‑of‑consent problem under Jimeno’s objective‑reasonableness test. Consent to “look in on the kids” is appropriately limited; a reasonable person would understand that such consent permits officers to enter, locate the children, and check their safety. It does not, in ordinary circumstances, authorize a search of containers or drawers for evidence of crimes. Jimeno instructs that the scope of consent depends on what a typical reasonable person would have understood from the exchange.

Applying to this fact pattern: Opening bedroom doors to ascertain whether the children are present or whether someone is threatening them is within a welfare check’s scope. But opening kitchen drawers and personal dressers to search for contraband exceeds what a reasonable person would expect when consenting to a welfare check—unless the officers articulate additional facts making such a search reasonable. For example, if an officer reasonably believes a weapon is hidden in a drawer and there is a particularized and immediate danger (e.g., children report seeing a gun and the officer reasonably believes it is inside a locked drawer that could be accessed imminently), that belief could justify a limited search for the gun under exigent circumstances. Absent such facts, the contents of drawers would generally be the product of an overbroad search and subject to suppression.

Practical litigation points: When challenging a search, defense counsel should argue narrow scope and point to the absence of facts supplying exigency or probable cause for drawer searches. Prosecutors should document contemporaneous articulable facts that might transform the welfare check into a justified search (specific child statements locating a weapon, sounds, or a visible weapon in plain view). Officers should be trained to stop at a cursory welfare sweep and, if they suspect hidden weapons or contraband, to secure the premises and obtain a warrant unless exigent conditions are clearly present.

## Teen child opens the door at 2 a.m. and invites officers in while parents sleep

Facts: A 15‑year‑old answers at 2 a.m., tells officers his parents are asleep, and says, “Come in, check on my mom—she’s been fighting.” The teen steps aside and leads officers to his mother’s bedroom, where officers see signs of an altercation and then conduct a broader search.

Law and analysis: This hypo raises two questions: (1) can the minor consent to allow officers into the family home, and (2) what is the scope of any consent the minor can validly give?

Authority to consent: The controlling principle from Matlock is not age but “common authority”—the consenting person must possess mutual use of, and joint access to, the area searched. That access can be established by relationship and control over a space. Whether a minor has common authority depends on the facts: is the teen a caregiver or the primary person controlling the household space at that hour? Courts are cautious when minors purport to consent to a search of premises owned or principally used by adults. Many courts find a minor’s invitation authoritative to the extent it is reasonably necessary to protect the minor (i.e., officers may accept a teen’s request to check on the teen), but they do not treat it as authorizing a broad search of the parents’ belongings.

Emergency‑aid exception: Independently of consent, if officers have an objectively reasonable basis to believe a person is injured or imminently threatened, they may enter without a warrant under Brigham City v. Stuart to provide emergency assistance. A credible 15‑year‑old’s report that a parent has been assaulted can supply reasonable grounds for a welfare entry to check the parent’s safety, without needing the minor’s formal authority to consent.

Scope: If the teen invites officers in solely to check on his mother, a reasonable scope permits the officers to enter and look for injured persons or obvious threats. It does not, without more, permit rummaging through private compartments or searching for contraband unrelated to the immediate welfare concern.

Practical guidance: Prosecutors can rely on the emergency‑aid doctrine when a minor reports a domestic assault, documenting the teen’s age, clarity, and specificity. Defense counsel should attack reliance on a minor’s consent for anything beyond a discrete welfare check and point to the limited common authority minors typically have over adult spaces.

## Aggressive dogs; officer commands entry for safety—consent or exigency?

Facts: Mrs. Coleman tells officers her dogs may jump but will not bite. In a variant, however, the dogs are barking viciously; one snaps at an officer’s boot. An officer announces, “We need to come in for safety reasons,” pushes past the bereft occupant and enters, who neither objects nor consents.

Law and analysis: Two pathways may justify the entry: (1) valid consent (rare where the entry is initiated by a command rather than an invitation), and (2) exigent circumstances—here, officer safety and the safety of occupants. Courts analyze exigency by asking whether officers had an objectively reasonable basis to believe immediate action was necessary to prevent physical harm or destruction of evidence. Brigham City recognizes that officers may enter without a warrant to render aid or to protect those in peril. A dog that bites or imminently threatens a person can create an exigency supporting compelled entry.

Consent concerns: If the officer’s “we need to come in” is phrased as an order and the occupant does not acquiesce but simply does not resist, an argument exists that there was no voluntary consent—particularly if the officer used physical force to enter. In Coleman the court accepted that “need” was not coercive under the totality of the circumstances; but where physical force or overt threats accompany the statement, consent will likely be found involuntary.

Exigency concerns: If the dogs pose an immediate hazard to the officers or to children inside, a court may treat the entry as justified under the emergency‑aid or officer‑safety rationales. Exigency is evaluated objectively; officers should document the dogs’ behavior (barking, snapping, bites), any prior information about the dogs, and any statements indicating immediate danger.

Practical consequences: Officers confronted with aggressive dogs should weigh three options: request consent clearly, secure the scene and call animal control, or, if immediate danger is present, state the exigency and enter. When relying on exigency, contemporaneous documentation of why delay was unreasonable is critical to survive suppression challenges. Defense counsel should probe whether less intrusive alternatives existed and whether officers used unjustified force when entering.

## Language barrier and implied assent; reliance on gestures and conduct

Facts: The occupant speaks little English. When an officer asks in English for permission, the occupant nods, steps aside, and makes hand gestures toward the entryway. The officers enter, relying on the gesture as consent.

Law and analysis: Consent can be manifested through words or conduct; however, voluntariness requires that consent be truly a product of free choice. Language barriers present special problems: if the officers know or reasonably should know the occupant does not understand English and do not seek an interpreter or otherwise ensure comprehension, courts will scrutinize the voluntariness of any implied assent. The totality of circumstances includes whether the person appeared to understand the exchange and whether police took steps to ensure understanding.

Objective reasonableness: Courts also ask what a reasonable officer would have understood. If the gestures clearly and unambiguously communicate consent (for example, the occupant points inside and says a word the officer understands to mean “yes,” or the occupant opens the door and steps inside voluntarily), many courts will accept implied consent. Yet if the gesture could reasonably be interpreted as merely making room for officers to pass (coerced acquiescence) or if the officer was aware of the language barrier, the defense has strong grounds to argue consent was not voluntary.

Best practices: When encountering a language barrier, officers should make reasonable efforts to secure an interpreter (phone or human), use simple yes/no questions, and obtain an affirmative verbal “yes” in a language the occupant understands if at all possible. For litigators, the defense should emphasize the known communication gap and the officer’s failure to verify comprehension; the prosecution should stress any contextual markers showing understanding (e.g., the occupant’s prior interaction in English, the use of a bilingual family member, or evident nonverbal clarity).

## Occupant initially refuses; after five minutes and multiple requests, says “Okay, fine”

Facts: Lisa tells the officer “I’d prefer you not,” when initially asked. The officer waits outside for five minutes, asks a second time, then a third, and finally says “Okay, fine” with an exhausted tone.

Law and analysis: Repeated requests for entry bear on voluntariness. Figueroa‑Espana and other Seventh Circuit authorities list repeated requests as a relevant factor. The mere passage of five minutes is not determinative; context matters: Was the occupant free to leave? Were officers using a commanding tone? Was there any sign of physical coercion or of the occupant being detained? How immediate was the threat to safety? Coleman weighed similar facts and concluded that a second request after a brief pause, absent coercion, supported a finding of voluntary consent. But the cumulative effect of multiple insistent requests, especially with signs of intimidation—lights shone into the home, numerous officers, or an implied threat of force—could render a later “okay” involuntary.

Analogous case law: Courts have found consent involuntary where police persisted unrelentingly or used coercive tactics that undermined free will. Short, respectful re‑requests for permission in a genuine welfare check, however, typically do not. The key is whether a reasonable person would feel free to refuse.

Practical advice: Officers should avoid iterated pressure. If an occupant initially refuses, officers should either articulate a lawful basis for entry (exigency) or seek a warrant if circumstances allow. Defense counsel will highlight repeated entreaties and officer conduct to argue involuntariness; prosecutors will emphasize brevity, lack of coercion, and the exigencies driving repeated questions.

## Officers observe contraband in plain view during a welfare check: next steps and warrant strategy

Facts: During a lawful welfare entry to check on children, officers see a bag of methamphetamine sitting on the kitchen counter. They then search adjacent rooms and find a handgun under a mattress.

Law and analysis: The plain‑view doctrine permits seizure of evidence without a warrant when (1) the officers are lawfully in the viewing position, (2) the incriminating nature of the item is immediately apparent, and (3) officers have lawful access to the item. Horton v. California is instructive: once officers are legitimately present and they see contraband, they may seize it.

Plain view and further search: Plain‑view discovery of contraband supplies probable cause to obtain a warrant to search the rest of the premises. Once officers lawfully seize the plainly visible contraband, they may secure the scene and seek a magistrate’s warrant to expand the search. The handgun under the mattress presents two separate issues: if the mattress was opened during a contemporaneous welfare sweep to check for people or weapons that could immediately endanger children, the seizure may be justified within the sweep’s scope; if officers rummaged through the mattress solely to find evidence, that would exceed the scope absent exigent facts.

Warrant strategy: When officers lawfully see contraband, the safest legal path is to seize what is in plain view (if immediately apparent), secure the premises to prevent removal or destruction, and promptly obtain a search warrant predicated on the plain‑view observations plus any corroborating facts (statements of witnesses, signs of forced entry, admissions, etc.). If the exigent circumstance that justified the original entry dissipates, securing the scene and getting a warrant helps avoid suppression claims.

Practical tips: Officers should contemporaneously document the location, visibility, and incriminating nature of any plain‑view items. Prosecutors should build warrants on plain‑view facts plus corroboration (e.g., child statements, visible injuries, or other indicators). Defense counsel will examine whether the initial entry that put officers in position to see the contraband was lawful and whether subsequent searches exceeded the permissible scope.

Conclusion: Applying Coleman to these hypotheticals underscores a recurring lesson: small variations in factual detail—tone, the occupant’s response, the presence of an objectioning co‑occupant, an officer’s wording, the presence of children, animals, or language barriers—can change the constitutional analysis. Courts apply a totality‑of‑the‑circumstances test to voluntariness and an objective‑reasonableness test to scope. Exigent‑circumstance rules permit some warrantless entries for health and safety reasons, but those entries must be justified with contemporaneous, articulable facts. For practitioners, the advisable habits are these: (1) if feasible, get a warrant; (2) if relying on consent, request it clearly and avoid coercive language; (3) if relying on an emergency, document the exigent facts in the report; and (4) when in doubt about a third party’s authority—especially minors or sleeping parents—proceed narrowly to the welfare function and secure the scene for a warrant if additional intrusions are necessary.
