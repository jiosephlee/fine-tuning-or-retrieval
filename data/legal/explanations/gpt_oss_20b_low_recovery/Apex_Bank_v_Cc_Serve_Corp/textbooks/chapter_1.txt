Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 1: # Parties, Facts, and Procedural Posture

In **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.**, the dispute centers on whether the Bank’s proposed “ASPIRE BANK” marks are likely to cause consumer confusion with CC Serve’s pre‑existing “ASPIRE” credit‑card mark. The case arose through the ordinary channels of the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO): an intent‑to‑use application, a formal opposition by the prior owner, and a subsequent refusal by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB). The litigation culminated in an appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.

## Profile of Apex Bank and its banking and financing services

Apex Bank is a Tennessee‑chartered retail bank headquartered in Nashville. It operates 18 branch locations and offers a standard suite of banking products, including personal checking and savings accounts, business bank accounts, mortgages, and personal and commercial loans. The Bank expressly does not provide credit‑card services. In pursuit of expanding into online banking, Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications on August 2019 for the word mark “ASPIRE BANK” and a complementary design mark, both intended for “banking and financing services.” These applications were published for opposition on December 17, 2019, after the prior owner, CC Serve, protested that the proposed marks were confusingly similar to its own “ASPIRE” mark.

## Profile of CC Serve Corp. and the ASPIRE credit‑card mark

CC Serve Corp. operates in the credit‑card services industry, acting as a provider of credit‑card account administration and processing services. The company holds U.S. registration No. 2,126,948 for the word mark “ASPIRE” in connection with credit‑card services. The mark was registered in 1998 with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. CC Serve’s business model involves partnering with banks that issue branded credit cards (including those bearing the “ASPIRE” name) and then servicing those accounts—handling payments, authorizations, and account management. While CC Serve does not issue credit cards directly, its services are integral to the credit‑card ecosystem.

The TTAB’s refusal was based on a § 2(d) likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis, concluding that consumers would likely confuse Apex’s proposed marks with CC Serve’s earlier “ASPIRE” mark because the services were related and the marks were similar.

## Key factual distinctions between the parties’ services

While both companies operate in the broader realm of financial services, the factual record distinguishes them in the following ways:

1. **Scope of Services** – CC Serve’s services are limited to credit‑card account administration and processing. Apex offers a comprehensive banking product line that currently excludes credit‑card issuance, though it intends to launch an online bank under the “ASPIRE BANK” name.
2. **Business Model** – CC Serve acts as a back‑end service provider; its partners (banks) issue the cards. Apex is an issuer in its own right, intending to offer direct banking products to consumers.
3. **Target Audience** – CC Serve’s “ASPIRE” mark targets consumers of credit‑card services, while Apex’s “ASPIRE BANK” mark targets the broader banking‑and‑financing services market.

These distinctions are germane to the Board’s analysis of the second DuPont factor (similarity of services) and underpin the appellate court’s focus on whether the Board incorrectly narrowed the scope of similarity.

## Jurisdictional basis: Lanham Act § 2(d) & 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)

The central legal question is whether a prior user’s existing registration should be refused or denied registration to a later applicant when the marks are likely to cause confusion. Under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act, the TTAB may refuse registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods or services.” The Board applied this provision in refusing Apex’s marks.

The Federal Circuit admitted the appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which allows for the appeal of denial or refusal orders issued by the TTAB. The statute directs that the appellate court review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo while resituating its findings of fact under the substantial‑evidence standard, a framework the court applied to the specific DuPont factors that the Board invoked.

## Board’s final ruling and the questions for appeal

The TTAB’s decision rested on its application of the thirteen DuPont factors, concentrating on the following key points:

1. **Second factor (similarity of goods/services)** – The Board concluded that Apex’s banking and financing services were “highly similar” to CC Serve’s credit‑card services because they both involve the financing of purchases, albeit through different mechanisms.
2. **Sixth factor (number and nature of similar marks)** – The Board limited its analysis to marks used in the credit‑card services field, concluding there were not “considerably” many such marks and that CC Serve’s mark was not weak.
3. **First factor (overall similarity of marks)** – The Board found that the marks were dissimilar in appearance, sound, and commercial impression.

Apex’s appeal challenges the Board’s conclusions on the second, sixth, and first factors. The court held that the Board erred only in its sixth‑factor analysis, vacated that conclusion, and remanded for reconsideration; it affirmed the second‐factor finding and vacated the first factor, leaving the disposition of those contentious factors to the Board upon remand.
