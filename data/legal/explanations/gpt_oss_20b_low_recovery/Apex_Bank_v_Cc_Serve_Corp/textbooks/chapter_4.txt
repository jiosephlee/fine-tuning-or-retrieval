Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 4: # 4. Analysis of the Sixth DuPont Factor – Similar Marks in Use  

The Sixth DuPont factor is the fulcrum on which a trademark opposition often tilts. It asks whether the *number and nature* of similar marks in use on similar goods or services signals that a mark is weak and therefore entitled to a narrow scope of protection. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit vacated the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (TTAB) finding on this factor and remanded the case for a new determination. This chapter explains the Board’s methodology, the court’s critique, and the implications for the parties and future opposition practice.

## Board’s Methodology for Selecting Third‑Party Marks  

During its Sixth‑factor analysis, the TTAB relies on *third‑party use* as evidence. The Board first defines the “relevant public”—the persons actually encountering the marks in question. In this case, the Board identified consumers of *credit‑card services* as the relevant audience because the contested marks—Apex’s proposed "ASPIRE BANK" and CC Serve’s registered "ASPIRE"—both appeared in the credit‑card arena. After defining the public, the Board gathered evidence of marks that included the word “Aspire” and were registered or used in connection with *credit‑card services*. Five examples from AJ‑45 were cited, and the Board narrowed its scope to those existing “Aspire‑formative marks” that matched the consumer demographic it had just defined.

The Board also assessed whether these third‑party marks were “considerable” or “ubiquitous.” Drawing on precedent such as *Jack Wolfskin v. New Millennium* and *Juice Generation v. GS Enters*, the Board concluded that the nine identified marks did not reach the required threshold of prevalence. Thus, the Board held that CC Serve’s mark possessed commercial strength and that the Sixth factor weighed *against* a finding of likelihood of confusion.

## Limiting the Relevant Public to ‘Credit‑Card Services’ Consumers  

A key procedural choice in the Board’s analysis was its restriction of the relevant public to consumers of credit‑card services. The Board reasoned that although the affiliated “banking and financing services” also carried the "ASPIRE" mark, the primary consumer base for both parties’ offers was the same credit‑card audience. Consequently, third‑party marks that appeared in broader banking or financing contexts were deemed “essentially irrelevant” and excluded from the evidence pool.

This approach rests on the principle that the likelihood of confusion hinges on the specific consumer group who would encounter both marks. Nevertheless, the restriction can be problematic if other markets overlap significantly with the contested services, as it may omit pertinent evidence of mark usage and strength.

## Legal Requirement that Only ‘Similar’ Goods/Services are Necessary  

Under the DuPont framework, the Sixth factor only demands *similar* goods or services—not identical ones. The Supreme Court, in *E.I. DuPont v. E. P. Brewster*, emphasized that the factor is concerned with “the breadth of the common characteristics” shared between the competing marks’ usages. In *Olde Tyme Foods v. Roundy’s*, the court reinforced that mere similarity suffices, even when the goods are not identical.

In *Apex Bank*, the Court recognized that the Parties’ services were “highly similar” and in fact “partially legally identical,” a finding established in the analysis of the Second DuPont factor. The Sixth factor, therefore, should expand its scope to encompass *all* similar marks—regardless of whether those marks are applied to credit‑card services or to the broader banking and financing services the Parties also offer. By limiting the universe of marks to only those that apply to credit‑card services, the Board applied an unnecessarily strict definition of “similarity,” counter to established precedent.

## Court’s Reasoning for Expanding the Universe of Comparable Marks  

The appellate court concluded that the Board’s narrow definition violated the duty to consider the *full range* of similar marks that could inform the mark’s commercial strength. Because the Court had earlier decided that the Parties’ services were “highly similar” (Second factor), it followed that the relevant goods for the Sixth factor are exactly those services. The Court applied the reasoning in *Olde Tyme* and *Juice Generation*: similarity in *social context* and *marketing environment* is enough.

Accordingly, the court held the Board should evaluate all third‑party marks that use “Aspire” in connection with *any* banking or financing service that is reasonably similar to the contested services—regardless of whether those marks are tied to credit‑card services specifically. By expanding the universe, the Board would capture a broader picture of market saturation and potential consumer confusion. The court also noted that the Ninth factor’s conclusion about commercial strength could be altered by considering these additional marks, thereby influencing the overall likelihood of confusion assessment.

## Implications of Vacating and Remanding for Reconsideration  

Vacating the Sixth‑factor finding remands the case to the TTAB for a new assessment that incorporates:

1. **A broader set of third‑party marks**—including those used in banking, financing, and credit‑card services that contain “Aspire.”
2. **A re‑evaluation of market saturation**—determining whether the extended set of marks is “considerable” or “ubiquitous” under the standards of *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*.
3. **A potential shift in commercial strength**—if more marks are found to be widespread, the Court may deem the contested mark weaker, narrowing its protection.

For Apex, a remitted Sixth‑factor analysis could tilt the balance *against* them, reinforcing the Board’s refusal. For CC Serve, a broader market view could affirm the strength of its registered mark and confirm the rejection of the challenger’s mark.

On a policy level, the vacatur serves as a reminder that trademark opposition analysts must *avoid* arbitrarily restricting the scope of third‑party marks based on their own discretion. The TTAB must ensure that all evidence pertaining to *similar* goods or services is considered, lest the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis be defective.

In sum, the Sixth DuPont factor in *Apex* illustrates the delicate interplay between factual findings (similarity of services) and procedural rigor (appropriate scope of evidence). The appellate court’s intervention underscores that the question is one of *public perception*—to what extent can consumers distinguish between marks that operate in overlapping markets—rather than a rigid, technical exercise of enumerating complementary services.
