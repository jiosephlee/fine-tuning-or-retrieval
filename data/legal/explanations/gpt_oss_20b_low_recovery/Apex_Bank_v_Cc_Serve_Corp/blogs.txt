# When the Second DuPont Factor Wins: Why Apex Bank’s Services Are “Legally Identical” to Credit‑Card Services  

## The Significance of the Decision

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* may seem, at first glance, to be a perfunctory affirmation of a trademark board’s ruling. But the court’s focus on the second DuPont factor—“the similarity of goods and/or services”—has far‑reaching consequences for any institution that risks a future conflict over a name that is glued together with seemingly disparate financial offerings. The Court’s reasoning clarifies how the statutory definition of “credit‑card services” dovetails with the broader legal notions of banking and financing, thereby tightening the net for banks, fintech firms, and the nascent market of digital‑only banks that rely on cross‑platform brand equity.

## The Board’s Logic and the Court’s De‑novo Review

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) had already weighed the “legal identity” of the services provided by CC Serve’s credit‑card program and Apex’s intended “banking and financing services.” The board’s conclusion was that, because the dictionary definition of “credit‑card services” includes the issuance of credit facilities, the handling of credit‑card accounts, and the provision of credit‑score access, the two service lines were “legally identical, in part.”  
The Federal Circuit agreed de novo: it found the board’s use of the statutory definition (and its supplementation with the Trademark ID Manual entries) to be correct, and it concluded that any “substantial evidence”—here, the facts presented by the board—support the finding that the services are highly similar. The court’s affirmation of the second DuPont factor thus validates the board’s legal framework for comparing seemingly disparate service lines.

Why does this matter? Because the DuPont factors were devised to surface complex consumer psychology under a streamlined analytical lens. The second factor has traditionally been the first point of contact; if the underlying services are dissimilar, the rest of the duPont matrix rarely needs to be consulted. When the court concedes that “credit‑card services” and “banking and financing services” overlap sufficiently for the purposes of a trademark proceeding, it is essentially confirming that the regulatory sphere of “finance” is an umbrella that encompasses credit‑card activity—a fact that has been obvious to bankers but only formally stitched together by the court in a case that could be framed as “just another opposition.”

## Unpacking the Statutory Definition

The Lanham Act’s statutory definition of “credit‑card services” is not simply a 1960s ark of old banking practices; it is an active, evolving concept that has been expanded by later statutes, court decisions, and, crucially, the Trademark ID Manual. By enumerating the various facets of card usage—authorization, monitoring, account management, and even credit‑score reporting—the definition ensures that anyone offering a credit‑card program, whether a traditional bank or a fintech startup, falls within the same gate. That the court relied on the Handbook’s expanded definition (and was correct in its application of it) carries two important implications:

1. **Quantitative Marking:** The necessity of plugging the new definition into the trademark analysis signals that the board—and by extension the court—view the “credit‑card‐related” services as a canonical basis for comparison with other financial service lines. In practical terms, any “banking” service that includes a credit‑card management arm risks being rolled into the same doctrinal bucket.
2. **Breadth of “Banking and Financing Services”:** The court noted that the broader category encompasses extending credit or providing funds via “the issuance of credit cards.” What sits at the intersection of the two categories—the issuance of a card that functions as a credit instrument—does not qualify as a purely “different” service.

In the *Apex* case, the court basically decided: if a credit‑card service is exercised by a bank or a corporation, it is categorized the same as a bank’s core financing function. The result is a statement that the very boundary between “credit‑card services” and “banking and financing services” is porous.

## Implications for Related Businesses

### Fintechs and Bancorp Platforms

Many fintech firms partner with banks to host their card programs. When a fintech applies for a brand under the umbrella of “banking and financing,” it is safe from inference that a concurrent credit‑card program—whether opened via a bank or a third‑party processor—will fall under the same DuPont assessment. In other words, the court is saying that the triad of brand, “financial services,” and “credit‑card services” cannot be legally teased apart.

Consequently, fintechs need to be proactive in keeping their brand architecture distinct. A “pure‑play” card brand might be safe if its registration explicitly specifies a service class that excludes “banking and financing.” However, once the brand’s descriptive scope is broadened, even post‑digital‑only banks (think *Chime*, *Varo*, or *N26*) may find their card‑based brands caught in the same hoop.

### Digital‑Only Banks

Digital‑only banks have been riding a wave of optimism that they can carve out a “banking” niche that sidesteps traditional brick‑and‑mortar concerns. *Apex* signals that this optimism should not be misplaced when the bank in question offers credit‑card products, whether directly or through agency arrangement. For instance, *SoFi*, *Simple* (now part of BBVA), and *Ally* were all scrutinized for overlapping brand coverage where credit‑card services were packaged into a “banking and financing” promotion. The *Apex* decision reinforces that, legally, a bank’s credit‑card engagement is inseparable from its broader financial identity when it comes to trademark opposition.

### Legacy Banks

Standing firm from the old guard, legacy banks that already own trademarks for “ASPIRE” and its variants can interpret the *Apex* ruling as a green light: they are protected under the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks” as the court reiterates for the Sixth DuPont factor. But their brand’s reach—because of the legal identity of the services—covers a more expansive swath of the market. The takeaway is that legacy banks should be vigilant not only to protect but also to define the precise scope (including any future sub‑brands or IT-enabled services) of their trademarks to avoid inadvertently weakening their IP position when the law hones in on legal identity.

## Potential Downstream Effects

### Narrowing of the “Separate” Service Distinction

The *Apex* decision signals a push toward narrower distinctions between “separate” services. In past cases, fear of redundancy has been a shield: if a bank’s brand was used for loans but not credit cards, it might not be seen as overlapping. The court’s outright saying that services with "credit‑card issuance" cover, in part, banking suggests a trend of broader classification that could restrict a brand’s ability to hold multiple service names without conflict.

### Reassessing Trademark Oppositions

Opponents like CC Serve now have a stronger argument that if a future registration includes a compelling “banking and financing” category, the overlap will be too close for opposition. The court's explicit reliance on the "legal identity" of services lowers the bar for establishing similarity. This re‑balance could make it more onerous for banks or fintechs to secure trademark registrations that would otherwise be seen as incremental or additional features of an existing brand.

### The Tech‑Bank Interface

As banks and fintechs increasingly operate similar services through the same backend (shared payment processors, artificial intelligence‑driven risk models), the lines between “credit‑card services” and “banking” will blur even further. The *Apex* decision can be seen as a clarion call that double‑counting is not "allowed." In effect, the DuPont factors may become a new way to gauge not just trademark distinctiveness, but the *legal* integration of diversified financial services.

## My Own Analysis: Was the Court Right?

It is tempting to think that the court's affirmation of the second DuPont factor is merely a copy‑and‑paste from the board’s analysis. However, the court took a measured stance: it recognized that the board’s second factor evaluation was sound, but it also highlighted the legal error in the board’s sixth factor assessment—a misstep that shows the court’s willingness to balance de novo legal interpretations with de facto factual findings. So, while the court may appear humble, it is actually sharpening the line between “things that are truly different services” and those that are legally uniform.

From a policy perspective, the decision reinforces the **consumer‑centric** angle of the Lanham Act. If a consumer of “credit‑card services” might be led to believe that an “ASPIRE BANK” offering those services was, in fact, the same source as a “ASPIRE” credit‑card brand, the risk of confusion is unavoidable. As digital‑only banks become more ubiquitous, ensuring clear consumer identity markers becomes essential and the *Apex* decision is an explicit nod toward that clarity.

## Takeaway for Businesses and Attorneys

1. **Define Service Classes Carefully**: When filing a trademark application, make sure the service class is as narrowly scoped as possible, and be aware that “banking” & “financing” and “credit‑card services” may be mutually inclusive under the Lanham Act.
2. **Expect Overlap**: Even if a brand’s core service is “delivery apps,” adding a “credit‑card service line” will automatically draw the brand into insurance territory, and vice‑versa.  
3. **Prepare for Opportune Opposition**: Opponents may use the *Apex* framework to argue that your expansion into new services is a legal identity‑based overlap.
4. **Think Forward**: As fintech integrates more deep‑tech, consider how your brand can be protected against involuntary overlap with partner institutions.

By toting the second DuPont factor into sharper focus, *Apex* underscores one simple truth: the legal identity of your service line is the real battleground for trademark protection. In a marketplace where banks, fintechs, and digital‑only institutions blur the lines of service lines, ensuring your IP stays robust will require a more disciplined approach to service classification and brand strategy than ever before.



# The Sixth DuPont Factor Re‑opened: A Window of Opportunity for Trademark Registrants in a Crowded Field  

## Why This Matters  

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* restarts the debate over how courts define “similar goods” for the Sixth DuPont factor. The court’s vacatur forces the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) to broaden its universe of third‑party marks when a prior user’s service and the applicant’s service have already been found “highly similar” under the Second DuPont factor. The practical upshot is that a crowded field of marks—otherwise deemed too weak to allow a narrow defense—may now shift to a setting where a mark’s inherent distinctiveness no longer guarantees the broad protection it once enjoyed.

## The Court’s Core Holding  

The court affirmed the TTAB’s finding under the Second DuPont factor that Apex’s “banking and financing services” were legally “identical, in part” with CC Serve’s “credit‑card services.” Because the services were so closely aligned, the TTAB’s narrow sweep of third‑party marks in its Sixth DuPont analysis was out of step with the earlier factual finding. The court held that the Sixth DuPont factor “does not require identical goods—only similar ones,” and therefore the TTAB must evaluate all marks used on *similar* goods and services, not just those limited to credit‑card–specific services. In other words, *a prior user’s “highly similar” service finding mandates a broader consideration of the competitive landscape.*  

The court left the First DuPont factor on hold, noting that it too may change once the Sixth factor is properly applied.  
In short: **if your service is similar enough to the prior user’s service to trigger a strong “as‑if” identity, you cannot point to a sparse field of competing marks that are narrowly restricted by the specific service description.**

## What the Sixth DuPont Factor Really Is  

The Ninth and Eleventh Amendment repealed “the shopkeepers” doctrine; since then, the Court has repeatedly reminded litigants that the DuPont factors are a *flood of guidance* rather than a rigid formula. Yet the Sixth factor—“the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services”—has been one of the more contested elements.  

Two critical questions frequently arise:

1. **Scope of “similar goods.”** Does the factor require *identical* goods, or merely goods that sit in the same economic ecosystem?  
2. **Relevance of third‑party marks.** Must the marks in the competitive field bear the same *service* classifications as the applicant, or can they be more broadly linked via the broader concept of “finance” or “financial services”?

*Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation* provide the benchmarks for a broader view. In both cases, the TTAB considered marks that, while not exactly the same product, operated in overlapping commercial contexts (e.g., dairy products vs. generic food items, or beverage beverages). The court recognized that consumers may perceive a single source when sounds, language, or branding signals cross over common consumer expectations, even if the exact goods differ.  

The rule established in *Apex* is that a prior recognition of “high similarity” among services automatically carries over to the Sixth factor’s scope. The court is essentially saying: *When you pick the rightful court, you must treat the searching aperture accordingly.*

## How Broader Competitor Analysis Can Disrupt Inherently Distinctive Marks

For a mark like CC Serve’s *ASPIRE*—a standard‑character mark that has achieved *inherent distinctiveness* by virtue of early registration—the commercial‑strength analysis is critical. The Sixth factor is the place where the board determines whether *“the mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.”*  

Under the heightened analysis mandated by *Apex*, the Board would have to include more than nine credit‑card‑specific marks. They would need to consider the 42 additional competing marks discovered by Apex that use *Aspire* in *broader banking and financing services.* This expands the perceived competitive field, exposing the *ASPIRE* mark to potential arguments that it is weak or easily confused because a consumer might differentiate among many *Aspire‑branded* offering; perhaps the common marketing language (e.g., “aspire for better credit”) undercuts the mark’s distinctiveness.  

In practical terms, an inherently distinctive mark could see its scope reduced from “almost all forms of finance” to a narrower segment that courts now must treat as the *real* competitive environment. When third‑party marks saturate the broader field, the inherent distinctiveness may be outweighed by the commercial‑strength calculus.  

## Implications for Registrants and Opposers

1. **Opposers Must Expand Their Witness Bases.** If you’re seeking to interfere with a mark’s registration because it conflicts with your own, you must now gather evidence of competitor usage across all *similar* financial services, not just your precise niche.  

2. **Applicants Must Anticipate Broader Analysis.** Registrants relying on an inherently distinctive mark no longer can safely assume that a narrow competitor universe will protect them. They should anticipate a more rigorous peer‑mark assessment across the entire financial services sector.  

3. **Strategic Use of Services Descriptions.** When filing for a new mark, precision in the service description can be a double edge. Narrowing the description might shield the mark from other *similar* services, but it also risks a tighter *similar goods* universe for the Sixth factor if the underlying services are found to be *highly similar* to existing marks.  

4. **Potential for New Remedies.** The more expansive Sixth factor could impact the *scope* of prior user rights. Where the Board would previously allow an inherently distinctive mark to enjoy continental protection, the new standard may open the door to *partial cancellation* or *partial infringement* claims.

## Downstream Effects on Trademark Law

The court’s decision reinforces the *fact‑oriented* nature of the DuPont factors, opening the door to a more holistic competition study in the trademark realm.  

- **TTAB Procedure Adjustments:** Boards will likely modify serval procedure lists to insert *“consider similar goods and services as defined under the second factor”* as a pre‑condition for Sixth factor analysis.  
- **Litigation Strategies:** Litigation may now hinge on a broader array of third‑party evidence. Opposers could compel applicants to testify about the *entire* competitive field, not just a narrow subset.  
- **Precedent for Inherently Distinctive Marks:** The implications for inherently distinctive marks are considerable. Courts may start awarding *less protection* to inherently distinctive marks that once benefited from a narrower analysis.  

Ultimately, *Apex* simulates a market‑centric approach to trademark claims: the more marks that surround the same economic space, the less shield a single mark enjoys, even if it is legally distinctive.  

## A Final Thought

For trademark practitioners, *Apex* is a wake‑up call: the old “one‑step‑forward‑two‑steps‑back” approach to the DuPont factors is now untenable. The Sixth factor’s standard is no longer a mere flicker of the *similar goods* doctrine but a full‑blown lens that must incorporate all relevant third‑party marks if the meriting services are already recognized as highly similar. The result is a more burdensome, more equitable, and, arguably, more realistic calculation of trademark protection in crowded fields. As we chart this new terrain, the balance tilts toward a more competitive evaluation of commercial strength—an outcome that both applicants and opposers will feel keenly.



# Commercial Impression vs. Commercial Strength: Why the First DuPont Factor Matters Even When the Second Factor Is Decisive

## Why This Case Matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* nudges the trademark law canonists a few centimeters in the right direction: when a court vacates a Sixth‑Factor analysis for misapplying the scope of “similar goods,” the ripple effect may be so large that the Board must re‑evaluate the First‑Factor “commercial impression” as well. That may feel a little counter‑intuitive because the First Factor is usually considered the cornerstone of the likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry—“the marks look, sound, or feel alike.” But *Apex* reminds us that a mark’s “commercial strength” and its “commercial impression” are tightly interwoven. If the sixth factor’s view of the market changes, the First Factor may no longer be a paint‑brush applied to a static canvas but an opinion that must be reassessed in a new light.

For practitioners, the decision has a practical upside: plaintiffs no longer need to throw away a first­‑factor dismissal on the technicality that the cemetery of “similar‑to‑similar” marks was too narrow. Rather, they can argue that the Sixth Factor’s flawed market scope distorted the Board’s perception of the mark’s distinctiveness, thereby necessitating a fresh look at the First Factor.

## The Five‑Step Playbook Revisited

The DuPont framework is a stovepipe of thirteen “factors” but the heart of many post‑*DuPont* bench opinions is how the first, second, and sixth factors interact. Courts nest the logic as follows:

1. **Similar services (Second Factor)**  
   The foundation of any likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis: are the goods/services in such close relation that a consumer might reasonably think they come from a single source?

2. **Commercial strength (Sixth Factor)**  
   Once you’ve established similarity, you assess how “strong” each mark is in the market. A strong mark receives broad protection; a weak mark can be “bumped” to the periphery.

3. **Commercial impression (First Factor)**  
   The “looks‑like” test sits atop and is sensitive to the knowledge of the mark’s strength. A mark that is deemed weak will have a weaker “commercial impression,” even if the appearance is superficially similar.

In *Apex*, the Board had boxed the competitors’ goods into the very narrow category of “credit‑card services.” That restriction was a mistake explained thoroughly in the Sixth‑Factor opinion. Consequently, the Board’s view that CC Serve’s *ASPIRE* was neither weak nor highly distinctive was partially based on a curtailed universe of third‑party users. If, instead, the Board had seen a broader field—including other banking and financing services—CC Serve’s *ASPIRE* might have appeared weak, thereby eroding the “commercial impression” held against it.

## How the Court Untangled the First Factor

The Fifth paragraph of the appellate reasoning is pure textbook theory: the First Factor’s “commercial impression” is not an isolated fact‑finding but a cumulative effect of the other DuPont factors. Because the Sixth Factor was vacated, the appellate court held that *the Board must reconsider the First Factor in light of an altered assessment of the mark’s distinctiveness.* In simpler terms, a weaker mark may look less similar, or certainly may be perceived less strongly, altering the overall source‑confusion calculus.

The court explicitly noted that *“the commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis here (…) because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness.”* The logical chain—similar services → commercial strength → commercial impression—breaks down the moment we touch any link.

## Practical Takeaways for Word‑and‑Design Marks

1. **Broader Market Grounds Matters**  
   Even if you can prove a meritorious similarity of services, a narrow scope of “similar goods” in a Sixth‑Factor analysis might still lead to an overall “commercial impression” misreading. Courts expect the sixth factor to use a “similar” goods standard consistent with the second factor’s findings. A disconnected scope invites remand.

2. **Word‑and‑Design Marks Are Not a Veto**  
   For marks that rely heavily on design, the “appearance” component of the First Factor can be deceptive. If the design element is generic but the word is distinctive, an erroneous Sixth Factor may cause the Board to over‑credit the design, misrepresenting overall distinctiveness. Conversely, a design that mitigates alleged similarity (e.g., adding bold font, unique color) might not shield a brand if the Sixth Factor shrinks the market scope.

3. **Commercial Strength Remains a Central Player**  
   In *Apex*, the court’s reference to *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation* is instructive: if a crowded field of third‑party marks dilutes a mark’s commercial strength, the First Factor’s perceived similarity lowers. Courts will re‑evaluate appearance, sound, and connotation in that diluted context.

4. **Remands Should Not Be a Pitfall**  
   A remand imposed by the appellate court for a Board to take another look at the First Factor is not a nuisance. Rather, it signals that the Board’s original “source‑confusion” story was built upon an unstable foundation. A diligent reclamation of the Sixth Factor can tip the scales that influence the First Factor.

## What Could Be the Bottom Line for “ASPIRE BANK”?

Apex’s *ASPIRE BANK* marks are a classic “word + design” case. The word *ASPIRE* is already inherently distinctive because of CC Serve’s earlier registration. However, the design element—a stylized “bank” text and an aspirational graphic—does not add legally defensible distinctiveness on its own. The question becomes whether that combination, considered in light of the broader market of *banking and financing services*, would be deemed likely to cause confusion with *ASPIRE*.

Under a more expansive Sixth‑Factor view:

- **Higher volume of third‑party marks with the word “Aspire”** across banking and finance would likely weaken *ASPIRE*’s commercial strength.
- **A weaker *ASPIRE*** would diminish the “commercial impression” of the *ASPIRE BANK* design, making it appear less like the prior mark from a consumer’s point of view.
- **On balance**, the Board might lean toward refusal of the *ASPIRE BANK* marks. Even if the appearance is distinct enough in terms of typography, the underlying word, now deemed weak, could enter the “normal scope” of protection for a mark that is not exceptionally distinctive.

That said, the Board is still bound by the Second Factor’s finding that the services are highly similar. If Apex can demonstrate that its “internet bank” is marketed to a niche demographic distinct from CC Serve’s credit‑card‑oriented clientele, the combined analysis may still work in Apex’s favor. The key is that any success hinges on the Board’s willingness to re‑appraise *ASPIRE*’s commercial strength with a broader lens, then re‑translate that into the “commercial impression” of the *ASPIRE BANK* marks.

## A Quick Verdict for the Community

*The *Apex* decision is a small but meaningful reminder that the DuPont framework is more interdependent than most people realize. A shaky Sixth Factor can knock the entire edifice down, forcing the Board to revisit the First Factor and, by extension, the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion outcome.* Practitioners should therefore:

- Scrutinize the *market scope* of the Sixth Factor—can it justify a narrower field than the Second Factor demanded?
- Remember that *commercial strength* is not a static label; it is a dynamic evaluation that can swell or shrink depending on third‑party evidence.
- Treat the First Factor’s “commercial impression” as fluid, not an immutable fact; a vacated Sixth Factor can change that impression as much as it changes digital footprint.

In short, *Apex* tells us that “the mark is not just how it looks” but how the market perceives it, and how that perception shifts when the market’s scope is expanded. The interplay between the First and Sixth factors is now a living doctrine—and a crucial battleground for any trademark plaintiff or defendant.



## The Future of Internet‑Only Banking Trademarks: Lessons from **Apex Bank v. CC Serve**

When a fledgling “online‑only” bank chooses a name that echoes a well‑known traditional lender, it is **not merely marketing a new business model**—it is walking into a complex maze of trademark law. The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* (2025) clarifies exactly where that maze turns crooked. The case centers on a dispute over an **ASPIRE** word and a newly drafted **ASPIRE BANK** design applied to internet‑banking services. The Board’s refusal of registration was challenged, and the court affirm‑ed the Board’s *second* DuPont factor while vacating its analyses of the *first* and *sixth* factors. For FinTechs that wish to piggyback on legacy monikers, the holding sends a clear, codified signal: **the scope of “similar goods and services” set out in the second factor governs who counts as a competitor in the sixth factor**. Moreover, the court underscores that the overall conjunctive analysis of similarity and commercial impression (the first factor) cannot be taken separately from the breadth of third‑party competition.

To illustrate the broader implications, let’s dissect the court’s reasoning, its doctrinal spill‑over, and the practical playbook it offers to future online banks.

### 1. Why the *Apex* Decision Matters to New FinTechs

Historically, the USPTO has treated **banking** and **credit‑card** services as separate, neighborhood markets. That distinction lasted until increasingly hybridized offerings—wholesale credit cards issued by banks and “neo‑banking” accounts that provide credit, debit, and even lending—blurred the lines. Under the Lanham Act, the “likelihood of confusion” test asks whether the public “mistakenly believes” services come from a single source. The court’s emphasis on the **second DuPont factor** and its **legal remedy for the sixth factor** abolish the old two‑tier “separate‑market” thinking:

1. **If the court finds the services to be sufficiently similar (as a factual finding), the same similarity bounds the field of competing marks that must be considered under the sixth factor.**  
2. **If the court had mistakenly narrowed that field, it must also re‑examine the first factor’s overall commercial impression**, because the market context is altered.

This structural import is far from purely academic. A new online bank that registers a mark that sounds, looks, or spells like a widely used legacy brand will plausibly face a finer scrutiny of third‑party marks—even those that do not involve credit cards—if their services overlap substantially.

### 2. The Court’s Analysis of the Second DuPont Factor

The Board’s detailed review was unsurprising and grounded: it parsed the **Trademark ID Manual** to translate *“credit‑card services”* into broad consumer‑facing functions (issuing cards, processing payments, credit counseling, etc.), and then linked that dictionary definition to *“banking”* and *“finance”* under the assumption that a bank that issues credit cards is “legally identical in part” to a credit‑card issuer. The court found the Board’s step **sound and supported by substantial evidence**. For a Quick‑start FinTech:

- **Be explicit about services** in your USPTO filing—don’t merely rely on a generic service class code.  
- Set up a compelling **legal argument** that the services you provide are not only *related* but *comparable* to the legacy brand’s services.  
- Once the court determines your services are similar, **you can self‑serve a more robust list of third‑party marks** for conduct under the sixth factor (see below).

### 3. The Sixth DuPont Factor – “Third‑Party Marks on Similar Goods”

The most doctrinally intriguing portion of the opinion concerns the court’s vacating of the Board’s **sixth factor** analysis. The Board basically did a minimal‑scope audit: it considered only marks that used the word “Aspire” in the *credit‑card* context, ignoring the more than 40 Omni‑financial marks that used only *“Aspire”* or derivations. The court deemed that *unlawful circumscription*, because the earlier factual finding (high similarity of services) warranted a wider universe of comparable marks.

In other words: **When the lock‑step unique nature of “credit‑card services” is the yardstick in the second factor, it must also be the yardstick in the sixth factor**. The court denied “multiple layers of reasonableness” between the factors: instead, they need to rest on the same factual bedrock.

#### Doctrinal Implications

- **Uniformity Across DuPont Factors**: The opinion supports a policy that facts that enlarge the “similar goods” sphere in any one DuPont factor automatically expand that sphere for the others. This reduces the potential for fine‑tuned, factor‑by‑factor arbitrage.  
- **Mercury‑like Drift of "Similar Goods"**: If a new entrant registers a mark for “online‑banking” services that includes “expanding financial services,” courts will have a broader set to probe—including “fintech advisory,” “investment brokerage,” or even “wealth‑management.”  
- **Back‑door “Competitive Self‑Interest”**: An old brand can’t restrict “similar good” scope to a narrower field to avoid dilution or pricing power discrimination—this is a constitutional jab, too.

### 4. The First DuPont Factor – “Total Commercial Impression”

Because the sixth factor’s scope was vacated, the court also vacated the first factor’s determination—“appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression”—**only after remanding**. What this signals is that a mark’s appearance **cannot be assessed in isolation** from who it is competing against. If the court expands the set of “similar goods” for consideration, the perceived “similarity” of the marks may also change. Collision risk increases because the context of **broader competition** reshapes the “source” confusion metric.

What FinTechs need to do now:

- **Brighten the Design**: A simple scribble or a clever layout can detach the brand image from the legacy one, shifting the commercial impression.  
- **Capitalise on the “Design” Itself**: Because the design element on the marks for *ASPIRE BANK* is substantive, build a recognisable logo that visually differentiates from legacy brands.  
- **Rely on the “Sound” Factor**: If your chosen name reads less like *“Aspire”* from a phonetic perspective, you reduce the risk of confusion.

### 5. Practical Takeaways for FinTechs

#### a. Define the Service Envelope Precisely

A respectable approach is to **over‑specify** the services in your application. For instance, if you want to carve out *“online‑banking credit‑line only”* services, manifest that as a separate service treaty (`class 36`, `class 35`, etc.) to avoid a blanket “banking” claim that invites parallel market competition.

#### b. Conduct a Targeted Third‑Party Mark Survey

Once you establish the “similar services” scope as a fact, you should **systematically gather third‑party marks** that appear in any overlapping service classes. The court’s opinion implicitly demands that *ASPIRE* be considered against *“all* Aspire‑related marks used in any “banking or financial services” category, not only credit‑card. This increases the number of “useless” marks considered, but it also adds a **wealth of evidence** that can argue either for weakness or for breadth.

#### c. Consider the “Design” Element Early

Because the court vacated the first factor—based on the sixth factor’s real‑world re‑analysis—**design can tip the scales**. A distinctive geometric shape or a colorful shield can diminish “commercial impression similarity.” Designers should weigh the brand‑identity importance against possible confusion burdens.

#### d. Map the “Relevant Public”

The court’s decision implicitly equates the relevant public with those who “purchase or consume” the overlapping services. For a pioneer that offers “low‑fee online banking”, “deferred‑payment tools” and “investment jumping‑start accounts,” the “relevant public” is **the e‑banking mass**—who concurrently attends both sets of forums, such as digital‑money blogs, FinTech podcasts and broadband‑only credit‑application sites. The perceived probability of confusion escalates if the public is cross‑exposed to both brands within the same channels.

#### e. Plan for “Remand” in the Future

Odds are that you’ll see a remand on the sixth factor—just as the court did. For that, the USPTO will need:

- A **long‑listed set of competitor marks** (spending time on web‑scraping and legal research).  
- A **fine‑grained discussion of how the competitor marks overlap** with each service class you claim.  
- Documentation on whether those brands are **“widely used”** (registered and in active commerce) or merely **“registered and dormant”**.

Being prepared for a *remand review* is as important as passing the initial Board stage.

### 6. The Upside: How New Marks Can Carve a Space

The principal lesson from *Apex* is that **extraordinary care** in filing can carve a moat, even when the brand name recycles a familiar word. A “non‑traditional” approach to trademarks—elevating the **design**, including an *IP class that encompasses “online‑banking” or “high‑throughput digital lending”*—can effectively reduce confusion while not violating the “career” competitor’s existing marks.

1. **Innovative Service Class**: If a bank offers “non‑banked digital wallet services” (public class 45, covered by “financial services”), the brand can claim it does **not** square with “traditional banking” per se.  
2. **Securing Unique Design**: A distinctive, fresh physical or digital logo image plus a unique tagline helps sidestep the "commercial impression" filter.  
3. **Substantially Better Disclosure**: Use the content section of the application to detail that “ASPIRE BANK” is strictly limited to *“high‑speed, low‑cost digital banking services”*—narrowing the market context and likely reducing similar goods.

### 7. Bottom Line

The *Apex Bank* decision will reverberate through every new FinTech’s trademark strategy. While the second DuPont factor protects the futility of claims that services are dissimilar, the vacated sixth factor elevates the risk that a new entrant’s mark will be weighed against **a broader swathe of third‑party marks**—not just the ones in the same narrow niche. The overarching moral: **A brand name is only as safe as the breadth of competition you successfully exclude via precise service definitions, redesign, and evidence of distinctness**.

For the fast‑moving FinTech ecosystem where “banking” is increasingly synonymous with “digital‑capital services,” this case signals that each new entrant must shape its trademarks around an airtight definition of services, a well‑designed visual identity, and a rigorous competitor‑mark database. Any deviation—and especially the temptation to cherry‑pick only the “credit‑card competition”—will likely lead to a Board refusal and, if left unchallenged, to a costly footnote in the prochain “little‑Python‌ల ల” ts.  The promise of internet‑only banking remains, but its trademarks must equal the ambition of that future.
