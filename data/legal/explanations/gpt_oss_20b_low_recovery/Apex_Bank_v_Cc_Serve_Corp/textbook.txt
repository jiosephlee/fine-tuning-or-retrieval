Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Parties, Facts, and Procedural Posture

In **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.**, the dispute centers on whether the Bank’s proposed “ASPIRE BANK” marks are likely to cause consumer confusion with CC Serve’s pre‑existing “ASPIRE” credit‑card mark. The case arose through the ordinary channels of the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO): an intent‑to‑use application, a formal opposition by the prior owner, and a subsequent refusal by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB). The litigation culminated in an appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.

## Profile of Apex Bank and its banking and financing services

Apex Bank is a Tennessee‑chartered retail bank headquartered in Nashville. It operates 18 branch locations and offers a standard suite of banking products, including personal checking and savings accounts, business bank accounts, mortgages, and personal and commercial loans. The Bank expressly does not provide credit‑card services. In pursuit of expanding into online banking, Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications on August 2019 for the word mark “ASPIRE BANK” and a complementary design mark, both intended for “banking and financing services.” These applications were published for opposition on December 17, 2019, after the prior owner, CC Serve, protested that the proposed marks were confusingly similar to its own “ASPIRE” mark.

## Profile of CC Serve Corp. and the ASPIRE credit‑card mark

CC Serve Corp. operates in the credit‑card services industry, acting as a provider of credit‑card account administration and processing services. The company holds U.S. registration No. 2,126,948 for the word mark “ASPIRE” in connection with credit‑card services. The mark was registered in 1998 with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. CC Serve’s business model involves partnering with banks that issue branded credit cards (including those bearing the “ASPIRE” name) and then servicing those accounts—handling payments, authorizations, and account management. While CC Serve does not issue credit cards directly, its services are integral to the credit‑card ecosystem.

The TTAB’s refusal was based on a § 2(d) likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis, concluding that consumers would likely confuse Apex’s proposed marks with CC Serve’s earlier “ASPIRE” mark because the services were related and the marks were similar.

## Key factual distinctions between the parties’ services

While both companies operate in the broader realm of financial services, the factual record distinguishes them in the following ways:

1. **Scope of Services** – CC Serve’s services are limited to credit‑card account administration and processing. Apex offers a comprehensive banking product line that currently excludes credit‑card issuance, though it intends to launch an online bank under the “ASPIRE BANK” name.
2. **Business Model** – CC Serve acts as a back‑end service provider; its partners (banks) issue the cards. Apex is an issuer in its own right, intending to offer direct banking products to consumers.
3. **Target Audience** – CC Serve’s “ASPIRE” mark targets consumers of credit‑card services, while Apex’s “ASPIRE BANK” mark targets the broader banking‑and‑financing services market.

These distinctions are germane to the Board’s analysis of the second DuPont factor (similarity of services) and underpin the appellate court’s focus on whether the Board incorrectly narrowed the scope of similarity.

## Jurisdictional basis: Lanham Act § 2(d) & 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)

The central legal question is whether a prior user’s existing registration should be refused or denied registration to a later applicant when the marks are likely to cause confusion. Under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act, the TTAB may refuse registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods or services.” The Board applied this provision in refusing Apex’s marks.

The Federal Circuit admitted the appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which allows for the appeal of denial or refusal orders issued by the TTAB. The statute directs that the appellate court review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo while resituating its findings of fact under the substantial‑evidence standard, a framework the court applied to the specific DuPont factors that the Board invoked.

## Board’s final ruling and the questions for appeal

The TTAB’s decision rested on its application of the thirteen DuPont factors, concentrating on the following key points:

1. **Second factor (similarity of goods/services)** – The Board concluded that Apex’s banking and financing services were “highly similar” to CC Serve’s credit‑card services because they both involve the financing of purchases, albeit through different mechanisms.
2. **Sixth factor (number and nature of similar marks)** – The Board limited its analysis to marks used in the credit‑card services field, concluding there were not “considerably” many such marks and that CC Serve’s mark was not weak.
3. **First factor (overall similarity of marks)** – The Board found that the marks were dissimilar in appearance, sound, and commercial impression.

Apex’s appeal challenges the Board’s conclusions on the second, sixth, and first factors. The court held that the Board erred only in its sixth‑factor analysis, vacated that conclusion, and remanded for reconsideration; it affirmed the second‐factor finding and vacated the first factor, leaving the disposition of those contentious factors to the Board upon remand.

# 2. Governing Law and Standards of Review  

In a standard trademark opposition under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act, the Department of Justice’s Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) must decide whether a new applicant’s proposed mark is likely to be confused with an earlier mark. The legal framework is set out in statutes, case law, and the TTAB’s own procedural rules. This chapter explains the statutory background, the 13 DuPont factors used to assess likelihood of confusion, and the appellate standard applied to the Board’s findings in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*.

## Lanham Act § 2(d) and the Concept of Registration Refusal  

Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act provides that a mark registration will be refused if the applicant’s mark “is destructive or a damage to the buyer’s point of purchase or that they it will create the prospect confusion…” [15 U.S.C. § 1052(d)]. The statutory language is couched in “confusion” and the TTAB interprets it through the established “likelihood‑of‑confusion” test, which is a blend of legal principles and factual inquiries. The Board must conclude that a substantial number of consumers, between the parties’ markets, would be likely to mistake the applicant’s mark for the earlier mark. The result of that analysis is a *registration refusal*, not merely a denial of the application but an explicit pronouncement that the mark cannot be registered.

The core test has been articulated in *DuPont v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The Supreme Court later affirmed the Board’s approach in *Wiredye, Inc. v. Walk, Inc.*, 179 F.3d 185 (Fed. Cir. 1999), emphasizing that the Board’s determination is a question of law based on findings of fact.

In *Apex Bank*, the Board’s refusal married the factual findings of similarity of goods, services, and marks to the legal question of likelihood of confusion. The appellant’s appeal required the Court to determine whether the Board’s legal conclusions were correct and whether its factual findings were supported by substantial evidence.

## The DuPont Factors: Overview and Purpose  

The “DuPont factors” are the TTAB’s 13 statutory‑based inquiries used to determine likelihood of confusion. They are:

1. **Similarity of the marks** – in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression.  
2. **Similarity of the goods/services** – whether they are related or could produce a mistaken belief that they come from the same source.  
3. **Offer and advertising** – whether the marks are put on or advertised in a manner that suggests association.  
4. **Quality of the earlier mark** – the mark’s inherent distinctiveness and its actual market strength.  
5. **Proposed mark’s usage** – the applicant’s intended manner of use and marketing.  
6. **Number and nature of similar marks on similar goods** – the crowdedness of the field and the presence of competing marks.  
7. **Repeated attempts to trade under the proposed mark** – attempts to circumvent the earlier mark.  
8. **Design features** – distinctive visual elements.  
9. **Distinctiveness of the applicant’s mark** – primary vs secondary distinctiveness.  
10. **Consumer confusion on purchase** – actual consumer responses.  
11. **Geographic market** – common territories of sale.  
12. **Evidence of source confluence** – other signs of source attribution.  
13. **Other circumstances** – any other facts that influence confusion.  

The factors are not ordered by weight; their relevance varies case‑by‑case. The Board is free to consider only those factors that are relevant and on record (M2 *Software*). The factors collectively transform a legal question—“Is there a likelihood of confusion?”—into a structured factual analysis.

## Differentiating Factual Findings (Substantial Evidence) from Legal Conclusions (de novo)  

The Board’s work is a two‑step process. First, it makes **factual findings**: determinations of the state of the facts that underlie the legal question. Second, it reaches **legal conclusions**: whether, based on those facts, the law requires a refusal.  

*Substantial evidence* applies only to factual findings. Per *Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197 (1938), it requires that the evidence be “sufficient to a reasonable mind to accept as a basis for the conclusion.” The Board must satisfy this standard for each factual element it alleges.

Legal conclusions are reviewed **de novo** by the appellate court. That means the court can re‑examine the law and the legal conclusions without regard to the Board’s reasoning, but it must still rely on the Board’s factual findings if those are supported by substantial evidence (*M2 Software*, 450 F.3d 1382). Thus, an appellate court can disagree with a legal conclusion even if the underlying facts are accepted.

In *Apex*, the Court affirmed the Board’s second DuPont factor (“similarity of services”) because the Board’s findings met the substantial evidence standard. It vacated the sixth factor (“number and nature of similar marks”) because the Board had applied the factor with an improper, too‑narrow scope, thus undermining the factual foundation required for a de novo review.

## The Court’s Role in Reviewing Board Findings: M2 Software Precedent  

*M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Communications, Inc.*, 450 F.3d 1378 (Fed. Cir. 2006) set the tone for all TTAB appeal review. The Court held that:

1. **Legal conclusions** – reviewed de novo; the appellate court is free to re‑apply legal tests.  
2. **Factual findings** – reviewed for *substantial evidence*; the appellate court must defer to the Board’s findings unless the evidence is insufficient.  

The Court stressed that appellate review is not “abuse of discretion” but a “superior court review” that also protects the procedural rights of the parties. In *Apex*, the Court applied this framework directly. When the Board’s factual finding regarding the similarity of services was robust, the Court affirmed the legal conclusion drawn from that finding. When the Board’s factual analysis of the sixth factor was suspect—because it excluded a legitimate scope of third‑party marks—the Court vacated the legal conclusion and remanded for reconsideration.

## Criteria for Evaluating Likelihood of Confusion in Trademark Opposition  

Because the ultimate question is whether confusion is likely, the Board’s inquiry must be comprehensive. The criteria include:

1. **Proximity of the goods or services** – even parallel services in different industries can be relevant if consumers might ‘misattribute’ the source.  
2. **Consumer sophistication** – in some industries (e.g., high‑end fashion) consumers are more discerning; in others (e.g., generic credit cards) they are less so.  
3. **Distinctiveness of the earlier mark** – inherently distinctive marks receive strong protection, while descriptive marks may be allowed only to the extent they have acquired distinctiveness.  
4. **Strength of the later mark** – a highly distinctive later mark can dampen confusion, especially if the earlier mark is weaker.  
5. **Field congestion** – many similar marks on similar goods will dilute distinctiveness.  
6. **Marketing practices** – whether the parties’ advertising clearly signals a single source.  
7. **Actual consumer confusion** – evidence of confusion is rare, but when present, it weighs heavily.  

In *Apex*, these criteria guided the Board’s evaluation of the second (similar services) and sixth (crowdedness of the field) DuPont factors. The Board’s error in the sixth factor was that it narrowed the market to "credit card services" alone, ignoring the broader "banking and financing services" that Apex would serve under the *ASPIRE BANK* mark. Because the APPLE’s services included internet banking, a wider market of consumers would be confronted with the marks, altering both the “crowdedness” assessment and the potential for source attribution.

By grounding the analysis in these legal ingredients, a practicing or studying attorney can anticipate how appellate courts scrutinize TTAB decisions and can better prepare evidence and arguments that survive de novo review.

# 3. Analysis of the Second DuPont Factor – Similarity of Goods and Services  

In a trademark opposition, the Board’s central inquiry is whether the prospective applicant’s services or goods are “likely to cause confusion” with the prior user’s. The second DuPont factor—**the similarity of the goods or services**—provides a factual basis for that inquiry. The appellate court in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* scrutinized the Board’s finding that Apex’s intended “banking and financing services” were highly similar to CC Serve’s “credit‑card services.” The court ultimately affirmed the Board’s conclusion, finding that the evidence supported the Board’s legal reasoning and that no error occurred in the factual determination.

## Definition of “credit‑card services” per the Trademark Identification Manual  

The Trademark ID Manual (TID Manual) is the USPTO’s go‑to source for parsing the specific meanings of service marks. When a term appears in the manual’s “Deleted Entries” list—as “credit‑card services” does following CC Serve’s registration—the Board must *first* determine the service’s precise scope. The manual’s full entry for “credit‑card services” reads:  

> *Issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit‑card debt, processing credit‑card payments and transactions, credit‑card authorization, credit‑card monitoring and alerts, managing credit‑card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.*

The Board applied this definition wholesale. By narrowing the market to this set of activities, the Board effectively bound the comparison to the *exact* service that CC Serve provides. The TID Manual’s guidance is binding on the USPTO examining and opposing officers, so the Board’s reliance on the manual was procedurally sound.

## Legal Logic Linking Credit‑Card Services to Banking/Financing Services  

The question is whether **banking and financing services**—the category Apex seeks to register—encompass or overlap with “credit‑card services.” The Board used two strands of legal reasoning:

1. **Dictionary Definitions**  
   The board consulted standard dictionaries (e.g., Merriam‑Webster, Oxford English) to interpret the common meaning of *banking*, *bank*, and *finance*. These sources define banking broadly as “the business of protecting, sending, and managing funds” and finance as “the management of large sums of money, especially by the issuing of securities.” The board noted that these definitions include “extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards.” The court agreed that such linguistic analysis is permissible and defensible under DuPont’s “case‑by‑case” approach: the Board was allowed to consider whether the services in question are *legally identical, in part,* because the dictionary frames the services as overlapping.

2. **Industry Practice and Service Structure**  
   The board stressed that credit‑card services are a subset of broader banking and financing activities: banks grant credit and issue cards, both fitting under the umbrella of “banking and financing services.” The appellate court favored this logic, noting that the board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar, so it was reasonable to extend that similarity to encompass the broader category.

## Board’s Synthesis of Dictionary Definitions and Third‑Party Registrations  

Beyond dictionary support, the Board examined *third‑party registrations* that fit the same or similar service categories. In particular, the board reviewed other marks registered for **credit‑card services** as well as those for **banking and financing services**. The Board’s approach:

- Identified marks that use the word “Aspire” or a derivative in connection with credit‑card services (e.g., “Aspire‑Finance”).
- Noted that these marks were *termed* under the same services listed in the “credit‑card services” definition from the TID Manual.
- Determined that the presence of these marks demonstrated that the market for credit‑card services is one in which multiple operators seek distinct marks for essentially identical offerings.

The court acknowledged that the Board carried out a *reasonable exercise* of fact‑finding, considering both the textual definition from the TID Manual and actual third‑party uses. Although Apex argued that CC Serve actually partners *with* banks rather than directly offering credit‑card services, the board’s analysis did not require an exclusive provision of credit‑card services; instead, it considered the overall *functional* overlap: managing credit‑card programs, handling payments, and offering related financial services—all of which fall under “banking and financing services.”

## Court’s Evaluation of the Evidence Supporting Similarity  

To determine whether the Board’s conclusion was supported by substantial evidence, the Supreme Court applied the standard set in *M2 Software* and the subsequent doctrine: “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.” The court reviewed the evidence on the second factor and found:

1. **Taxonomy and Scope** – The TID Manual provided a clear, authoritative definition that bound the comparison to a specific, well‑understood set of services.
2. **Dictionary Corroboration** – The dictionaries filled the semantics gap between “credit‑card services” and “banking and financing services,” showing a normative overlap in how industry and consumers understand these terms.
3. **Third‑Party Usage** – The presence of other “Aspire” marks in the credit‑card space signaled a *crowded* field, confirming that the services are traded among numerous competitors, and thereby reinforcing the similarity argument.
4. **Logical Consistency** – The Board’s synthesis was internally consistent; it applied a single definition for the services, tied that definition to broader banking/financing activities, and then compared the two parties within that unified framework.

Because the appellate court found that the Board’s factual determinations were sufficiently supported, it held “substantial evidence” existed to support the conclusion that the services are highly similar. The court recognized that *confusion* depends not on *direct competition* but on *relatedness* of offerings and *consumer perception* of common source. The court rejected Apex’s assertion that CC Serve’s involvement *with* banks rather than *direct* issuance of credit cards mitigated similarity, noting that the legal analysis did not require exclusive provision of credit‑card services to determine similarity.

## Rationale for Affording the Board the Second‑Factor Finding  

Having upheld the Board’s factual findings, the court affirms the Board’s legal conclusion on the second DuPont factor because:

- The Board’s analysis followed the *legal framework* established by DuPont and *Citing* sources, applying the test “whether the services are related in some manner or share circumstances that could give rise to mistaken belief.”
- The court found no *misapplication* of the TID Manual, dictionary definitions, or industry practice. The Board’s reliance on these resources was appropriate and granted the court deference under the *de novo* review of legal conclusions but *substantial‑evidence* review of factual determinations.
- The second factor is a factual question; the court is confined to the evidence the Board found and the reasonable interpretations thereof. The board’s conclusion that the services are “legally identical, in part,” is consistent with *Olde Tyme Foods* and other precedents emphasizing *similarity* rather than exact duplication.

The appellate court therefore concluded that the Board’s finding supporting likelihood of confusion under the second DuPont factor is properly affirmed. The court noted that the second factor weighs heavily in the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis but, combined with other factors, ultimately led the Board to a conclusion that Apex’s Marks should be refused registration, a finding that the court also affirms for this particular factor.

# 4. Analysis of the Sixth DuPont Factor – Similar Marks in Use  

The Sixth DuPont factor is the fulcrum on which a trademark opposition often tilts. It asks whether the *number and nature* of similar marks in use on similar goods or services signals that a mark is weak and therefore entitled to a narrow scope of protection. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit vacated the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (TTAB) finding on this factor and remanded the case for a new determination. This chapter explains the Board’s methodology, the court’s critique, and the implications for the parties and future opposition practice.

## Board’s Methodology for Selecting Third‑Party Marks  

During its Sixth‑factor analysis, the TTAB relies on *third‑party use* as evidence. The Board first defines the “relevant public”—the persons actually encountering the marks in question. In this case, the Board identified consumers of *credit‑card services* as the relevant audience because the contested marks—Apex’s proposed "ASPIRE BANK" and CC Serve’s registered "ASPIRE"—both appeared in the credit‑card arena. After defining the public, the Board gathered evidence of marks that included the word “Aspire” and were registered or used in connection with *credit‑card services*. Five examples from AJ‑45 were cited, and the Board narrowed its scope to those existing “Aspire‑formative marks” that matched the consumer demographic it had just defined.

The Board also assessed whether these third‑party marks were “considerable” or “ubiquitous.” Drawing on precedent such as *Jack Wolfskin v. New Millennium* and *Juice Generation v. GS Enters*, the Board concluded that the nine identified marks did not reach the required threshold of prevalence. Thus, the Board held that CC Serve’s mark possessed commercial strength and that the Sixth factor weighed *against* a finding of likelihood of confusion.

## Limiting the Relevant Public to ‘Credit‑Card Services’ Consumers  

A key procedural choice in the Board’s analysis was its restriction of the relevant public to consumers of credit‑card services. The Board reasoned that although the affiliated “banking and financing services” also carried the "ASPIRE" mark, the primary consumer base for both parties’ offers was the same credit‑card audience. Consequently, third‑party marks that appeared in broader banking or financing contexts were deemed “essentially irrelevant” and excluded from the evidence pool.

This approach rests on the principle that the likelihood of confusion hinges on the specific consumer group who would encounter both marks. Nevertheless, the restriction can be problematic if other markets overlap significantly with the contested services, as it may omit pertinent evidence of mark usage and strength.

## Legal Requirement that Only ‘Similar’ Goods/Services are Necessary  

Under the DuPont framework, the Sixth factor only demands *similar* goods or services—not identical ones. The Supreme Court, in *E.I. DuPont v. E. P. Brewster*, emphasized that the factor is concerned with “the breadth of the common characteristics” shared between the competing marks’ usages. In *Olde Tyme Foods v. Roundy’s*, the court reinforced that mere similarity suffices, even when the goods are not identical.

In *Apex Bank*, the Court recognized that the Parties’ services were “highly similar” and in fact “partially legally identical,” a finding established in the analysis of the Second DuPont factor. The Sixth factor, therefore, should expand its scope to encompass *all* similar marks—regardless of whether those marks are applied to credit‑card services or to the broader banking and financing services the Parties also offer. By limiting the universe of marks to only those that apply to credit‑card services, the Board applied an unnecessarily strict definition of “similarity,” counter to established precedent.

## Court’s Reasoning for Expanding the Universe of Comparable Marks  

The appellate court concluded that the Board’s narrow definition violated the duty to consider the *full range* of similar marks that could inform the mark’s commercial strength. Because the Court had earlier decided that the Parties’ services were “highly similar” (Second factor), it followed that the relevant goods for the Sixth factor are exactly those services. The Court applied the reasoning in *Olde Tyme* and *Juice Generation*: similarity in *social context* and *marketing environment* is enough.

Accordingly, the court held the Board should evaluate all third‑party marks that use “Aspire” in connection with *any* banking or financing service that is reasonably similar to the contested services—regardless of whether those marks are tied to credit‑card services specifically. By expanding the universe, the Board would capture a broader picture of market saturation and potential consumer confusion. The court also noted that the Ninth factor’s conclusion about commercial strength could be altered by considering these additional marks, thereby influencing the overall likelihood of confusion assessment.

## Implications of Vacating and Remanding for Reconsideration  

Vacating the Sixth‑factor finding remands the case to the TTAB for a new assessment that incorporates:

1. **A broader set of third‑party marks**—including those used in banking, financing, and credit‑card services that contain “Aspire.”
2. **A re‑evaluation of market saturation**—determining whether the extended set of marks is “considerable” or “ubiquitous” under the standards of *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*.
3. **A potential shift in commercial strength**—if more marks are found to be widespread, the Court may deem the contested mark weaker, narrowing its protection.

For Apex, a remitted Sixth‑factor analysis could tilt the balance *against* them, reinforcing the Board’s refusal. For CC Serve, a broader market view could affirm the strength of its registered mark and confirm the rejection of the challenger’s mark.

On a policy level, the vacatur serves as a reminder that trademark opposition analysts must *avoid* arbitrarily restricting the scope of third‑party marks based on their own discretion. The TTAB must ensure that all evidence pertaining to *similar* goods or services is considered, lest the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis be defective.

In sum, the Sixth DuPont factor in *Apex* illustrates the delicate interplay between factual findings (similarity of services) and procedural rigor (appropriate scope of evidence). The appellate court’s intervention underscores that the question is one of *public perception*—to what extent can consumers distinguish between marks that operate in overlapping markets—rather than a rigid, technical exercise of enumerating complementary services.

# 5. Analysis of the First DuPont Factor – Overall Commercial Impression

The first DuPont factor is the linchpin that ties together all of the subsequent considerations in a likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry. It focuses on how the mark presents itself to the intended audience—its appearance, sound, connotation, and the overall commercial impression it generates. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Court underscored that the evaluation of this factor cannot be made in isolation from the decisions made under the other DuPont factors, especially the sixth factor, which examines the comparative strength of the marks within a field of similar use. The Court’s remand ordering requires the Board to revisit the first factor after vacating its conclusion on the sixth factor, illustrating the intertwined nature of these analyses.

## Components of Commercial Impression: Appearance, Sound, Connotation

1. **Appearance** – The visual design of a mark, including any stylized lettering, graphic elements, or color schemes. In the Apex case, the Board had reviewed the word “ASPIRE” used in a standard‑character form, noting its lack of distinctive stylization or design elements that might differentiate it from other “Aspire” marks.

2. **Sound** – The phonetic quality of a mark when spoken. Although the Apex dispute involved primarily written marks, the Board considered the spoken form of “Aspire” in assessing whether consumers would recognize it as a single source when heard on the telephone or in an advertising context.

3. **Connotation** – The set of associations or meanings that the mark evokes beyond its literal definition. For “Aspire,” connotations of ambition, upward mobility, or personal improvement were relevant, especially given its use in both credit‑card and broader banking contexts.

The Court emphasized that the first factor is an "overall commercial impression" assessment. It is not merely an aggregate of the separate subcomponents but a holistic view of how consumers will perceive the mark in the marketplace.

## Relationship Between Commercial Impression and the Scope of Similarity

The Court noted that the first factor’s weight in a likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis depends heavily on the “scope of similarity” established in the second, third, and sixth factors. In other words, if the Board determines that the services are legally identical or highly similar (as it did in the second factor) and that the field is saturated with multiple “Aspire” marks (as the sixth factor should have examined), the overall commercial impression can shift dramatically. A mark that may seem benign when considered in isolation could become confusing when placed among a crowded market of similarly sounding or looking marks.

Because the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis was found deficient—it narrowly considered only credit‑card‑service marks and excluded other banking‑related “Aspire” uses—the Court held that the Board’s conclusion regarding the overall commercial impression was premised on an incomplete view of mark similarity within the broader industry. Removing or widening that analysis could alter the Board’s perception of how distinctive or ordinary the mark is within its market field.

## Why the Court Found the Board’s Analysis Contingent on the Sixth Factor

The Court’s reasoning hinged on the legal doctrine that the first factor is relev­ent and determinative **only** when the context of the other factors is fully understood. Since the Board found the services to be “highly similar” (second factor) but limited the universe of comparable marks to those in the credit‑card sector (sixth factor), the Court determined that the “overall commercial impression” was evaluated against a reduced sample. A more expansive sample could reveal either a broader field of “Aspire” marks, support a perception of a “weak” or “non‑distinctive” mark, or demonstrate that the mark’s appearance is sufficiently distinct to avoid confusion.

Moreover, the Court clarified that the first factor is not an isolated test; its outcome can be modified by findings in the sixth factor, which gauges the logical strength of a mark in a competitive environment. By vacating the sixth‑factor conclusion, the Court created an uncertainty that invalidated the Board’s prior determination on the first factor.

## Ordering of Remand: Reconsideration of the First Factor Post‑Vacatur

The Court’s remand order is precise: it vacates the Board’s findings on the sixth factor and directs the Board to reconsider the first factor **after** addressing the sixth factor’s scope. The sequence is intentional for two reasons:

1. **Logical Flow** – A comprehensive review of how many and what kind of similar marks exist informs the final judgment about the “strength” of Apex’s mark. Without this foundational assessment, any subsequent consideration of appearance, sound, or connotation remains incomplete.

2. **Avoiding Redundancy** – By requiring the Board to first broaden (or revise) its analysis of the sixth factor, the Court prevents the Board from engaging in a new first‑factor evaluation that would implicitly rely on data it has not yet confirmed. The remand ensures the Board does not re‑estimate commercial impression based on an incorrect assumption about the competitive landscape.

Hence, the remand emphasises that the Board must first quantify the marketplace’s density of similar marks and only then can it accurately assess the overall commercial impression.

## Potential Avenues for the Board to Alter Its Conclusion

In light of the vacatur, the Board has multiple pathways to potentially reverse its earlier finding that the “overall commercial impression” is likely to cause confusion:

1. **Expanding the Third-Party Mark Base** – The Board may now consider the full 42 third‑party “Aspire” marks identified by Apex, not just the nine credit‑card‑specific ones. If a large number of these marks are in the broader banking and financing sector, the field becomes more crowded, potentially diluting Apex’s mark and weakening the “overall commercial impression.”

2. **Reassessing Commercial Strength** – A broader mark universe may reveal that “Aspire” is not a distinctive mark but rather merely descriptive or generic within the financial context. The Board could then find that Apex’s mark suffers from weakness and that the appearance, sound, and connotation are insufficiently distinctive to prevent confusion.

3. **Revisiting Consumer Perception Evidence** – The Board might gather new evidence on how consumers actually perceive “Aspire” when exposed to Apex’s “Aspire Bank” versus CC Serve’s “Aspire.” If surveys or expert testimony show that consumers categorize both as coming from a single source due to the similarity in appearance and sound, the Board could affirm its original finding.

4. **Focusing on Connotative Salience** – If the Board determines that the signals of ambition and upward mobility associated with “Aspire” are strong and distinct from those associated with CC Serve’s credit‑card system, it could argue that the overall commercial impression remains distinct, thus refuting the likelihood of confusion.

5. **Mitigating Overlap Through Narrowed Service Scope** – The Board might argue that Apex’s “Aspire Bank” targets a different consumer base (internet bank users versus credit‑card users) and that the overlap is minimal. By demonstrating distinct marketing channels, the Board could demonstrate that the commercial impression differs sufficiently between the two marks.

Whichever avenue the Board pursues, it must do so within the framework that the Court has outlined: begin with a thorough, unbiased assessment of third‑party similarity and then proceed to a reassessment of the mark’s overall commercial impression in light of that broader context.

---

By understanding the interplay between the first and sixth DuPont factors, law students can appreciate why appellate courts demand a logical, incremental approach to revising district‑court findings. The *Apex* decision illustrates that a partial vacatur coupled with a directed remand is a powerful tool to correct analytical errors without overhauling an entire appellate decision.

# 6. Holding, Significance, and Practical Take‑aways

## Aff’d‑in‑part, Vac’d‑in‑part, Remanded: What the Judgment Means  

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* is a partial affirmation of the Board’s refusal of the “ASPIRE BANK” marks. The court specifically affirmed the Board’s analysis of the second DuPont factor—providing that the services offered under the two marks are highly similar, thereby supporting a finding of likelihood of confusion. In contrast, the court vacated the Board’s findings on the first and sixth DuPont factors. Because the Sixth factor concerns the number and nature of similar marks on similar goods, and the First factor concerns the overall commercial impression of the marks, the court remanded the case to the Board for reconsideration of those factors in light of the Board’s earlier factual finding that the services are legally identical in part.

In practical terms, the decision leaves the Board’s refusal of registration in place but with the caveat that the Board must revisit its conclusions about how third‑party marks dilute CC Serve’s distinctive identity and how that dilation affects the overall commercial impression of the marks. Thus, the appellate ruling does not overturn the refusal, but it injects a level of uncertainty that a remand places back on the Board’s discretion to lift or maintain the refusal.

## Effect on the Status of the ASPIRE‑BANK Application  

Because the court affirmed the Board’s refusal under the second factor, the ASPIRE BANK application remains denied as of the instant decision. The vacatur of the Sixth and First factors does not retroactively revive the registration; instead, it requires the Board to submit a revised refusal (or a possible approval) that accounts for the broader universe of third‑party “Aspire”‑based marks and the potential impact on commercial impression. The applicant will be given an opportunity, upon remand, to amend its petition with additional evidence—such as marketing statistics or consumer surveys—to counter the Board’s revised analysis. Until the Board issues a new order, the marks remain unregistered and the applicant’s claim to federal protection in those classes of goods and services is denied.

## Influence on the Application of the DuPont Factors in Opposition Cases  

The court’s reasoning clarifies that the Board must apply a consistent standard of similarity across all DuPont factors that involve analysis of goods or services. In particular, the court emphasized that when the Board has already found the goods/services to be *highly similar* under the Second factor, it cannot arbitrarily narrow the scope of similarity in the Sixth factor to a narrower subset (e.g., “credit‑card services” only). Instead, the Board must assess *all* third‑party marks that operate on *similar* goods or services, consistent with the earlier finding.

For the First factor, the decision reiterates that a third‑party’s market presence and the resulting commercial impression can alter the perceived distinctiveness of a mark. Because the commercial impression is shaped by consumer awareness, a broad sweep of third‑party uses can weaken a supposedly inherently distinctive mark.

These doctrinal points will likely prompt Boards, now wary of appellate scrutiny, to broaden their analysis of third‑party competition in all relevant DuPont factors. Opposers should feel empowered to submit third‑party evidence that casts doubt on the uniqueness of the applicant’s mark, while applicants may need to provide stronger counter‑evidence to demonstrate that the mark retains distinctive power despite a crowded field.

## Strategic Implications for Applicants and Opposers Regarding Third‑Party Evidence  

1. **Comprehensiveness of Third‑Party Evidence**  
   – *Opposers*: The decision signals that limiting third‑party evidence to a narrow product or service class (e.g., credit‑card services only) may not satisfy the Board. A broader set of evidence—covering all similar banking and financing services—can create a more robust argument that the mark is weak or already diluted.  
   – *Applicants*: When facing opposition, applicants must anticipate that the Board may enlarge the scope of third‑party evidence. They should prepare a comprehensive dossier that includes not just primary competitors but also ancillary or adjacent services that can reinforce the distinctiveness of the mark.

2. **Testing Commercial Strength**  
   – *Opposers*: Introduce data that demonstrates widespread use of the common word or its derivatives across the financial industry, thereby undermining the Applicant’s claim to a “normal scope of protection.”  
   – *Applicants*: Prepare to prove that the mark has achieved a distinctive status—whether descriptively or conceptually—through evidence such as secondary meaning or comparative sales data that can counter the dilution argument.

3. **Time Management and Opportunity for Counter‑Adjustments**  
   – *Opposers*: Already filing early in the opposition can allow them to lock in a broader field of third‑party evidence that the Board will be required to consider.  
   – *Applicants*: If the Board remands, applicants should use the remand period to adjust their petition, perhaps adding usage data, marketing materials, or consumer survey results that demonstrate the mark’s distinctiveness or counter the dilution claim.

4. **Potential for Remand‑Induced Litigation Cost**  
   – *Both parties*: A remand can extend the litigation timeline and increase costs. Applicants should budget for this possibility, while opposers must weigh the value of a remand against the additional expense of preparing a more robust evidence package.

## Cost Order and Procedural Next Steps  

The court awarded costs to the appellant, Apex Bank, reflecting that the Board’s refusal was upheld. In practice, this means that Apex Bank will bear the financial burden of its own opposition proceedings, and the Board will not be required to reimburse the applicant for costs incurred. This cost award may influence the applicant’s decision about whether to pursue a remand or to abandon the marks entirely.

On remand, the Board is instructed to reconsider the Sixth and First DuPont factors. The procedural next steps are:

1. **Court‑Mandated Reconsideration** – The Board must file a motion for reconsideration of those two factors, necessarily including a revised analysis that accounts for the broader set of third‑party marks and the potential impact on the commercial impression.
2. **Opportunity for Respondents** – The applicant and the opposer are entitled to respond to the Board’s remand position, potentially through written submissions or oral arguments.
3. **Examination and Decision** – After reviewing the Board’s amended findings, the Board will issue either a new refusal (or a limited approval) or a resolution that addresses the earlier vacatur.
4. **Appellate Review** – Should either party disagree with the Board’s new decision, they may again seek appellate review under § 1295, but the appellate standards remain the same: legal conclusions are reviewed de novo; factual findings for substantial evidence.

In sum, the appellate decision maintains the refusal of the ASPIRE BANK marks but introduces a procedural reset for the Sixth and First DuPont factors. Applicants and opposers must now anticipate a broader, more rigorous examination of third‑party competition and commercial impression, and they must be prepared with comprehensive evidence to navigate the potential remand and any resulting Board decision.
