Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What constitutes a 'protected property interest' under federal fraud statutes?
Question:
In United States v. Constantinescu the court rejects the district court’s dismissal by citing that the indictment alleged a scheme to defraud followers of *money* or *property*. The defendants argue the plaintiffs only deprived them of valuable economic information, not a traditional property interest. How does the Supreme Court’s view in Ciminelli and Kousisis clarify what is considered a protected property interest, and why does the court conclude the indictment does not fall under the right‑to‑control theory?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit is faced with a district‑court dismissal that rests on the claim that the indictment failed to allege that the defendants stole a *“protected property interest”*. The court turns to the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), and *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), to decide whether the alleged fraudulent scheme falls within the ambit of the federal fraud statutes.

---

### 1.  What is a “protected property interest”?

Both *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* recognize that the five fraud statutes (mail, wire, securities, fraud‑in‐the‑money, and fraud‑in‑the‑contract) protect *traditional* property interests—money, narrowly defined property, or the right to control property. *Ciminelli* cast a clear line: a *right to valuable economic information* is **not** a protected property interest. The Court explained that the “right‑to‑control theory”—which seeks to convict when a defendant “deprives a victim of the right to control his assets” by withholding information—does not satisfy this requirement because the victim’s economic distinctiveness is not a property right under the statutes.

---

### 2.  Applying the *Ciminelli* distinction to Constantinescu

The defendants in *Constantinescu* argue that the indictment only says they *“intended to deprive followers of potentially valuable economic information”*. That language, by itself, would mirror the *Ciminelli* scenario and would be insufficient: depriving a victim of information alone is not a protected interest, so the indictment would be defective.

The Court rejects that interpretation because the indictment does **not** rely on a rights‑to‑control argument. The indictment alleges that the defendants, through false statements on social media, *induced followers to buy securities*. That is a *fraudulent‑inducement* scheme, the Court notes, which is *explicitly* distinct from the right‑to‑control theory:

> “Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1388.

Because the defendants actually sought to *obtain money or property*—the followers’ cash used to purchase the securities—the statute’s protected property interest is satisfied. The indictment therefore states a scheme to defraud that involves a protected property interest.

---

### 3.  Why the indictment survives the “intent to defraud” objection

The defendants also claim that the indictment fails to allege intent to defraud, arguing that the defendants “intended to profit, not to hurt the victims.” *Kousisis* clarified that the fraud statutes do **not** require that the victim suffer an economic loss or that the defendant’s motive be to “hurt” the victim. What matters is that the defendant *“schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property.”* The Court cites *Kousisis*:

> “A defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392.

*Constantinescu* therefore contains the necessary intent element because it alleges that the defendants posted materially false information to induce purchases, and that the scheme was aimed at extracting the followers’ money to enable the defendants to “pump” the stock and later “dump” for profit. The fact that the followers end up holding the securities (even if at market value) does **not** defeat the fraud claim, per *Kousisis* and *United States v. Baker*, 923 F.3d 390.

---

### 4.  Conclusion

The Supreme Court cases establish that:

* **Protected property interest**: Only *money, narrowly defined property, or the right to control property* is protected. A right to valuable economic information is not.  
* **Right‑to‑control theory**: Offenses cannot be based solely on depriving a victim of the right to control their assets by withholding information.  
* **Fraudulent‑inducement theory**: Offenses can be based on deception that induces a victim to part with money or property, even if the victim ultimately does not suffer a net loss.

The *Constantinescu* indictment, by alleging that the defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities and thereby caused those followers to part with money, satisfies both the protected‑interest requirement and the intent‑to‑defraud requirement under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Therefore, the court concludes that the indictment *is* within scope of the federal fraud statutes and reverses the dismissal.

---

### Why does the fraudulent‑inducement theory not require victim harm?
Question:
The defendants claim that the indictment fails to allege an intent to defraud because it only says they intended to profit, not to hurt victims. The court cites Kousisis to say a fraud conviction does not need evidence of net economic loss. How does the fraudulent‑inducement theory differ from the right‑to‑control theory, and what constitutional concern does it address that allows a prosecution even when the victim may not suffer a financial loss?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
The fraud statutes protect *property* interests, not the mere “right to know” that might afford a better economic decision. Under the **right‑to‑control theory** (the theory Kousisis rejected) the government must show that the defendant deprived the victim of that *right* to control her own money or property. Because “valuable economic information” is not a protected property interest, a conviction premised on that theory fails.  

The **fraudulent‑inducement theory**, which the indictment alleges, is different: it requires that the defendant use a material misrepresentation to **force** a victim into a contract that requires the victim to give up her money or property. The victim’s *actual loss*—whether net, temporary, or even zero—does not matter. The Supreme Court upheld that *intent to obtain money or property* is the essential element, not an intentional harm to the victim’s bottom line. The constitutional issue that drives this distinction is the federal government’s power under the *fraud statutes* to proscribe any deceptive scheme that obtains money or property, *irrespective of the victim’s net loss*. The statutes are designed to prevent the federal government from being a “cruel and mischievous tool” used to defraud any person, even if that person ultimately ends up with an equal‑valued good.  The court in *Kousisis* made it clear that the statute is “not a re‑packaging of the right‑to‑control theory” and that “a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property, regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off” (145 S. Ct. at 1392).

Thus, the indictment in *Constantinescu* is sufficient under the fraudulent‑inducement theory because it alleges a material misrepresentation that induced victims to part with money or property, independent of whether those victims suffered a net pecuniary loss. The right‑to‑control theory is inapplicable, because the statute protects property interests, not the right to receive information.

### Which elements must an indictment state for securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §1348?
Question:
The opinion refers to the need to allege a scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and that the victim’s money or property was obtained. How do judges determine whether an indictment "enumerates each prima facie element" in the context of §1348, and what factual allegations would be considered sufficient to satisfy that requirement?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 the offense is “defrauding any person in connection with any security … by means of false or fraudulent pretenses … to obtain any money or property … in connection with the purchase or sale of a security.”  
Because the statute is a “petty” fraud statute, its elements follow the same doctrinal pattern that courts have applied to mail and wire fraud:  

| Element | What must be alleged | Source in *Constantinescu* |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| **Scheme to defraud** | A set‑up a course of conduct that deliberately misleads the victim; it must be *material* and *intended* to deceive. | ¶ 13–14: “purchase a security, ‘pump’ the price by posting false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially inflate its price.” |
| **Intent to defraud** | The defendant must have an *intent to deceive* and an *intent to obtain money or property* by means of that deception. | ¶ 13: “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” The indictment therefore alleges the two required intents: to deceive and to obtain money. |
| **Victim’s money or property acquired** | The victim must part with money or property, and that money/property is the *object* of the fraud. | ¶ 13–14: The scheme “allowed defendants to profit … $114 million.” This shows that the victim’s money was actually acquired. |

---

### How judges determine “enumeration of each prima‑facie element”

1. **Treat the indictment as true.** A judge first accepts every allegation in the indictment as fact, a principle underscored in *United States v. Rafoi* (5th Cir. 2023).

2. **Apply the standard from *United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo*** and its progeny: the indictment must *enumerate* each “material” element. The court does **not** look for the *exact statutory language* but at the factual detail required to satisfy the elements.  
   * *Sex* the indictment present a *scheme* (a set of conduct) that would, if factual, commit fraud.  
   * *Sex* the indictment show *intent* (the state of mind) necessary for fraud.  
   * *Sex* the indictment identify the *victim’s loss* (money or property taken).

3. **Sufficient specificity.** A single “intent to profit” is not enough; the indictment must connect that intent to *deceiving the victim* to obtain money. As the Supreme Court clarified in *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*, fraud requires *“deception”* that *obtains* money or property, not merely that the offender wants to hurt the victim. The indictment in *Constantinescu* makes that connection explicit: the false statements *are* what induce followers to buy, and the purchase is what gives defendants the money.

4. **No “mirror‑image” requirement for loss.** The court, following *Kousisis*, may allow an indictment to claim fraud even if the victim’s net economic loss is ambiguous, as long as the victim actually gave up money or property.

---

### Factual allegations that satisfy the requirement

The indictment must state facts that, when taken together, satisfy every element:

- **Scheme to defraud:**  
  *“Defendants would purchase a security, ‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on social media so that other investors were **induced** to purchase the security and artificially increase its price.”*  
  (¶ 13–14)

- **Intent to defraud (deceive + obtain money/property):**  
  *“Defendants… post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were **induced** to purchase the security.”*  
  (¶ 13)

- **Victim’s money/property acquired:**  
  *“Defendants profited $114 million from their scheme.”*  
  (¶ 13–14)

These allegations are more than surface‑level; they specify *who* was acting (the defendants), *what* they did (purchased, pumped, dumped), *how* they misled (false posts), *who* was affected (followers who bought), and *what* was taken (money, reflected in the $114 million profit). The indictment therefore “enumerates each prima‑facie element” of § 1348.

---

### Bottom line for the student

When reviewing an indictment for § 1348, judges look to see whether the allegations pull together a **complete picture** that satisfies the three building blocks—scheme, intent, and victim’s loss—using the *factual detail* required, **not** the exact statutory phrasing. The *Constantinescu* indictment does so; it explicitly states that the defendants *misrepresented* securities to *induce* purchases, that they *intended* to obtain money through deception, and that the victims *did* part with money. That is why the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal.

### How do courts apply mail and wire fraud doctrines to securities fraud?
Question:
The opinion states that §1348 borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes and often relies on those cases. What specific elements from the mail and wire fraud cases are used to analyze §1348 violations, and why is this approach appropriate for securities fraud?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* squarely addresses how the court treats the mail‑ and wire‑fraud doctrines when analyzing §1348 (the securities‑fraud statute). The court explicitly adopts the same elements the federal fraud statutes use for mail and wire fraud and then explains why that approach fits the “pump‑and‑dump” scheme at issue.

---

### 1.  The key elements borrowed from mail and wire fraud

| Element | Mail / Wire Fraud (as adopted by the court) | How it is applied to §1348 in this case |
|---------|--------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **“Scheme to defraud”** | A material falsehood or misrepresentation intended to deceive and to obtain money or property. | The indictment alleges that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security … so that other investors were *induced* to purchase the security” (Id. at ¶ 13). The court treats this as a material misrepresentation *intended to deceive* and *to obtain money or property* (the followers’ investment dollars). |
| **“Intent to defraud”** | The defendant must intend **to deceive** and **to cause harm**—or, under the Supreme Court’s recent reasoning, merely to “obtain” money or property, even if no net loss results. | The indictment claims that defendants “depdfreted … to profit” (Id. at ¶ 13–14). The court treats that as intent: the defendants’ objective was to “obtain” followers’ money through deceptive posts. The court points to *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* to note that a victim’s loss of money or property, not a loss of information, creates a **protection** under the fraud statutes. |
| **Fraudulent‑inducement theory** | The proven concept that a defendant uses a false statement to induce a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property, regardless of whether actual loss ensues. | The court describes that the indictment does not rely on a “right‑to‑control” theory (which would be invalid under *Ciminelli*). Instead, it relies on the fraudulent‑inducement theory: defendants “tricked” their followers into buying securities, thereby “obtain[ing] the victim’s money or property.” This theory is expressly permissible under §1348. |

---

### 2.  Why this approach is appropriate for securities fraud

1. **Same protective purpose** – the fraud statutes protect *property interests* (money or securities). The accidental “loss” of economically valuable information alone is insufficient (*Ciminelli*). By applying the fraudulent‑inducement framework, the court ensures the statute only applies when a *property interest* is harmed.

2. **Preserves the “deception” element** – the fraudulent‑inducement theory requires a **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive. Securities fraud is inherently about *market‑efficiency distortion* through deception, so the mail‑ and wire‑fraud framework naturally captures that element.

3. **Ensures a single‑step analysis** – the fraud statutes are “all‑or‑nothing” (no mens rea or motor‑vehicle‑style “intent to hurt”). Using the mail‑/wire‑fraud elements means the court can quickly decide whether the indictment has “each prima facie element” (*U.S. v. Rafoi*). That satisfies the Sixth‑District‑Court requirement that indictments enumerate every material element of the charged offense.

4. **Consistent with Supreme Court precedent** – *Kousisis* confirmed that “fraudulent‐inducement” governs recovery under §1348, and *Ciminelli* clarified the boundary between a mere loss of information and a loss of property. By aligning §1348 with the same doctrines the Court has applied to §1341 and §1343, the court maintains doctrinal consistency across the federal fraud suite.

---

### Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* holds that §1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and relies on those same doctrines—the “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” elements under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Applying this framework is proper because it preserves the statute’s focus on protected property interests, ensures the requisite deception is demonstrated, and aligns the securities‑fraud analysis with the long‑standing, well‑established Supreme Court line in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*.

### What is the 'right‑to‑control' theory and why was it rejected in Ciminelli?
Question:
I find the discussion of the right‑to‑control theory confusing. The court says Ciminelli attempted to prove wire fraud by showing the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions. Why is that theory considered a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory, and why was it struck down as lacking a traditional property interest?
Answer:
**Answer**

The *right‑to‑control* theory is a way to interpret the federal fraud statutes that was advanced in *Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306 (2023)*.  Under that theory the “property” that the statutes protect is the victim’s **right to control** his or her own economic affairs—specifically, the right to use valuable economic information to make discretionary decisions about purchases or investments.  The government would prove fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive the victim of that information, thereby leaving the victim vulnerable to befuddlement or misallocation of resources.

In *Ciminelli* the Supreme Court rejected that theory because the statutes protect only **traditional property interests** (money or physical property).  The Court explained that “the right to valuable economic information … is not a traditional property interest” and therefore cannot be the basis for a fraud conviction:

> “Because the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, … the right‑to‑control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.”  
> *Ciminelli*, 598 U.S. at 316.

The *Ciminelli* indictment did not attempt to allege fraud over the mere deprivation of potentially useful information; it instead alleged a classic *fraudulent‑inducement* scheme in which the defendant induced the victim to part with money or property.  In that case the victim’s loss was concrete—money or securities—rather than a loss of informational advantage.

In the *Constantinescu* opinion the court made it clear why *Ciminelli* is not sub‑sidiary to the present case:

1. **Different theory** – The indictment relies on the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory, not the *right‑to‑control* theory.  The latter is expressly rejected in *Ciminelli*; the former, which involves actually taking money or property from a victim through deception, is upheld.

2. **Protected property interest** – The indictment alleges that defendants caused followers to “purchase securities … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price.”  The victims therefore lost money or property, a bona‑fide protection under the statutes.

3. **No requirement of harm** – Even if the victim’s net monetary loss is not proven, *Kousisis* (the companion case to *Ciminelli*) held that the fraud statutes do not require an economic loss for a conviction: “a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property’… regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.”  *(Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392).  Thus the alleged intent to profit does not defeat the indictment.

Consequently, *Ciminelli* was struck down because it dealt with a loss of information—an *unprotected* interest—while *Constantinescu* built its claim on the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory that secures a traditional property interest.  The Court therefore rejected the right‑to‑control argument and affirmed that the indictment properly alleges both a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud.

### On what basis did the Fifth Circuit reverse the district court’s dismissal of the indictment?
Question:
The district court dismissed the indictment for failing to allege a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed on de novo sufficiency review. What standard did the court apply to decide that the indictment was sufficient, and which factual allegations did it find compelling enough to support a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit applied a *de novo* sufficiency review under the standard set out in *United States v. Rafoi* (60 F. 4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023)).  In that review the court “takes the allegations of the indictment as true and determines whether an offense has been stated.”  The court then looks to whether the indictment **(1)** enumerates each prima‑facie element of the charged offense, **(2)** fairly informs the defendant of the charges, and **(3)** provides the defendant with the double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.  See *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F. 3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996).

In *Constantinescu* the government alleged securities‑fraud conspiracy and multiple counts of securities fraud under § 1348.  The statute requires a **scheme to defraud** *and* an **intent to defraud** (*Id.* at 339).  The court therefore examined the indictment’s factual allegations to see whether these two elements were sufficiently pleaded.

---

### 1.  Scheme to Defraud

The indictment alleged that each defendant:

* purchased a security;  
* “pumped” the price by posting *false and misleading* information on social media so that “other investors were induced to purchase the security” (indictment ¶ 13–14);  
* secretly “dumped” the security for a profit, resulting in a $114 million gain (indictment ¶ 13).

These facts establish a **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive others for the purpose of obtaining money or property from the victims – the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory.  The court rejected the defendants’ claim that the indictment only alleged depriving followers of “valuable economic information” because that theory, derived from the *right‑to‑control* doctrine, was rejected by the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli v. United States* (598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)).  The indictment instead alleges that followers were *induced* to part with **money** (to purchase securities), a protected property interest.  The court concluded that the indictment “did not run afoul of *Ciminelli* because it alleged defendants deprived their followers of a protected property interest by fraudulently inducing them to buy securities” (*Id.*, A).

### 2.  Intent to Defraud

The defendants argued that the indictment’s claim of intent to profit did not satisfy the *intent to defraud* element.  The court rejected that argument, citing *Kousisis v. United States* (145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025)).  The Supreme Court held that the fraud statutes do **not** require the victim to suffer an economic loss; it is sufficient that the defendant “schemes to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property’” regardless of whether the victim is left economically worse off.  The indictment expressly stated that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” (indictment ¶ 13), thereby showing an intent to deprive victims of their money through deception.  The court noted that the defendants’ objective was “to obtain money,” and that the “pump and dump” scheme was clearly aimed at “getting a profit” (see *Id.* at 1397).  Consequently the indictment sufficiently alleged an intent to defraud.

---

### Bottom line

Because the indictment, when viewed in the light most favorable to the government, **enumerated the material misrepresentation, the intent to obtain money or property, and the alleged financial result** (the $114 million gain), the Fifth Circuit held that it **sufficiently alleged a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud**.  Applying the *de novo* sufficiency standard, the court reversed the district court’s dismissal.

### Does the Supreme Court’s decision in Kousisis change how we interpret 18 U.S.C. §1348 for future cases?
Question:
The opinion hinges on the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis. If a defendant’s misrepresentation results in the victim receiving something of equal value, does §1348 still apply? How might this affect future prosecutions where the victim is a more sophisticated investor or an institutional client?
Answer:
**Answer**

No; the *Kousisis* ruling does **not** change the basic test for §1348, but it does clarify that a defendant need not cause a *net* economic loss to the victim for a fraud conviction. In *Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit applied both Supreme Court cases to hold that the indictment was sufficient because it alleged a fraudulent‑inducement scheme and an intent to obtain the victim’s money. The analysis can be summarized as follows:

| Issue | Ciminelli (2023) | Kousisis (2025) | Constantinescu | Practical Take‑away |
|-------|-----------------|-----------------|----------------|---------------------|
| _What purchase defrauds?_| Right‑to‑control theory – deprivation of potentially valuable economic information | Fraudulent‑inducement theory – deception that causes the victim to part with money or property | Same as Kousisis; the defendants induced followers to buy securities | A §1348 violation requires a *material misrepresentation that induces the victim to give up money or property*, not merely the handing over of securities of equal value. |
| _Need for actual loss?_| Not necessary under fraudulent‑inducement; Ciminelli rejected the right‑to‑control theory on the basis that information is not a protected property interest | Explicitly rejected the “mirror‑image” requirement: one can be liable even when the victim receives something of equal value | The Court stressed that “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”, regardless of net loss | In future cases, prosecutors can focus on the act of inducement; a sophisticated investor receiving a stock at inflated price still leaves the victim’s money “deprived” if that money was paid to the defendant. |
| _Application to sophisticated/institutional victims?| Not addressed | Recognizes that “the advantage of fraud is relatively independent of the victim’s sophistication” | The Court treats the followers as ordinary victims; its reasoning extends to sophisticated investors or institutions because the statutory language is broad. | Prosecutors can target institutional clients, but must show that the misrepresentation was *material* to the contract and that it caused the client to part with money or property. |

### How it affects future prosecutions

1. **Sufficient to allege a scheme** – The indictment must state that the defendant made a material misrepresentation *with the goal of inducing the victim to give up money or property*. Whether the victim ends up holding the same securities is irrelevant, as long as the victim paid money.

2. **No net loss requirement** – The victim’s receiving market‑value securities does **not** defeat liability. The important factor is that the victim’s **money** (or other property) was transferred to the defendant. This widens the scope of §1348 to include cases where the victim is a sophisticated investor or an institution, provided the misrepresentation was material and the victim’s money was obtained fraudulently.

3. **Defendant’s intent** – The Supreme Court reinforced that the defendant must intend to obtain the victim’s money or property, not necessarily intend to cause any loss. Thus, the prosecution can rely on an allegation that the defendant “intended to profit” from the scheme.

4. **Sophisticated vs. ordinary victims** – The *Constantinescu* Court explicitly treated the followers (who may be ordinary or sophisticated) alike. The same logic applies to large institutional clients; §1348 protects them if the misrepresentation is material to the contract and they part with money or property.

### Bottom line for a law student

- **Do not argue** that the victim’s receipt of securities of equal value defeats §1348.  
- **Focus** on establishing (1) a material misrepresentation, (2) intent to defraud, and (3) the victim’s deprivation of money or property.  
- **Apply** the *Kousisis* “no mirror‑image” rule when arguing that the victim’s economic interest was not harmed net‑wise; what matters is that the victim’s money was obtained fraudulently.

Thus, *Kousisis* did not alter the statutory definition of “money or property” but clarified that inducing a victim to give up money— even if they later end up with securities of equal value—can still constitute §1348 fraud. This broadening will make future prosecutions against sophisticated or institutional investors more straightforward, provided the prosecution can meet the Material Misrepresentation + Intent + Deprivation elements.
