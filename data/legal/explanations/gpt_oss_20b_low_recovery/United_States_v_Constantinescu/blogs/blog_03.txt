Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Indictment Sufficiency on Trial: What United States v. Constantinescu Means for Prosecutorial Drafting  

## Why This Case Matters  

In a 2025 reversal by the Fifth Circuit, Judge Engelhardt tossed a district‑court dismissal of a multi‑million‑dollar securities‑fraud indictment that was built around a “pump‑and‑dump” scheme carried out via social media. The court didn’t touch the underlying facts or merits of the scheme; it held that the indictment itself contained enough detail to survive the procedural safeguard that protects defendants from vague or malformed charges. For prosecutors—especially those wading into the murky waters of online influence, H‑KUY, and cryptocurrency—this ruling is a siren call: indictments must be written with an iron‑clad eye on the *de‑novo* sufficiency test and the elemental train of the crime they seek to charge.

The decision clarifies three contentious points: the de‑novo standard’s focus on the indictment’s content, the strict requirement that each element of the offense be *enumerated* in the indictment, and the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of the securities‑fraud statute’s “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” components in light of the Supreme Court’s recent split on the fraudulent‑inducement versus right‑to‑control theories.

Knowing how the court carried the law will give prosecutors a better chance of having the indictments survive the first critical test—dismissal by the court of law for lack of specificity—before the case ever reaches a grand jury or the courtroom.

---

## The De‑novo Sufficiency Standard in the Fifth Circuit  

The court’s opening doctrine is almost a textbook refresher on the de‑novo requirement. In the Fifth Circuit, “review of an indictment is a *de‑novo* inquiry” (see *Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982). The judge must take the allegations on face value and then decide whether an offense has been *statutory*ly “stated.” This means the indictment must satisfy three pocket‑book conditions:  

1. **Enumerate each prima facie element** of the offense;  
2. **Fairly inform the defendant** of the charges; and  
3. Preserve the **double‑jeopardy** barship from future prosecutions.

When Judge Engelhardt examined the Constantinescu indictment, he found that the alleged “misrepresentations” and “induction” of followers into purchasing securities satisfied the enumerative requirement: each alleged act mapped neatly onto the *scheme to defraud* and *intent to defraud* elements of 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Any prosecutor can read that map into practice—make sure every statutory element has a named paragraph in the indictment.

---

## The “Scheme to Defraud” and “Intent to Defraud”: A Blue‑print for Satirical, Twitter‑Driven Schemes  

The Fifth Circuit again turned to the law on the front lines of securities law. In *Constantinescu*, the judge recited the Ninth- and Ninth‑circuit precedent:

* **Scheme to defraud** – a *material misrepresentation* intended to deceive, to obtain something of value.  
* **Intent to defraud** – an intention to *deceive* and to *cause harm*, even if that harm is not monetarily obvious.

What is new in the paper is the court’s insistence that the indictment x‑mounted the fraudulent‑inducement theory in sharp contrast with the supremely rejected right‑to‑control theory used in *Ciminelli*. In practice:

* **Fraudulent‑inducement**: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.”  
* **Right‑to‑control**: a weaker, now‑banned theory, where a victim’s *right* to money is the property interest at stake.

By invoking the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the indictment sidestepped potential mis‑statements about “potential economic information” and pinned the crime on the *actual* act of selling *money* in the form of securities. Prosecutors who draft indictments on similar grounds need to make sure the language spells out that the defendant induced victims to part with *money or property*—not merely to “control” a piece of information. This distinction matters because, as Judge Engelhardt cited, the federal statutes protect *traditional* property interests, not the abstract value of information.

---

## “Intent to Defraud” Is Not a Causation Clause  

A striking element of the decision is the disavowal of a “mirror‑image” injury requirement. The Supreme Court has repeatedly clarified that, under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, a defendant does not need to *cause* an actual financial loss. The *Kousisis* holding that “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses” (even when the victim ends up with equal‑value goods or services) firmly tilts the pendulum toward indictments that merely need to show that the target *was induced* to part with money.

In practice, this means prosecutors can—and should—neatly word the intent to defraud portion of an indictment as: “the defendant attempted to obtain money from individuals by means of a material misrepresentation.” The jury need not prove that the victim went “net‑worse off” to convict. It also means that a defendant who may, after the fact, “buy back” the securities or *reimburse* the victim soon after a scheme may still fall under the statute, because the initial inducement was fraudulently obtained.

---

## Bottom‑Line Affect on Drafting  

1. **Detail Explicitly**: Include a paragraph that names each element: deception, material misrepresentation, inducement, and the resultant *money or property* transfer.  
2. **Avoid Ambiguity**—Does the indictment avoid talking in terms of “informational loss” or “potential value” alone? Yes. Do the allegations specifically say “the defendant induced followers to purchase securities, thereby making them part with money”? Yes.  
3. **Underpin with Corporate Standards**: The Fifth Circuit’s emphasis on *enumeration* and *intention* should shake the slide of rhetoric. A vague infringement on “social‑media influence” or “misleading language” will not survive.  
4. **Test your language against the Supreme Court’s framework**: The *Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory* is your lexicon; the *Right‑to‑Control* is your ban.  
5. **Leave room for the ‘intent to defraud’**: Place a clear, explicit statement that the defendant *intended to gain money from the victims* through false statements—this extinguishes that *conclusion of detriment* obstacle.

---

## Downstream Consequences  

For the Seventh and other circuits, *Constantinescu* will likely echo as a clarion call that indictments in complex, mixed‑media, online schemes must be “sufficiently honest about the lightbulb moment after which the victim part‑wits money into the defendant’s hands.” If lesser courts hold to the *strict dictum* used here, prosecutors will stay on the right side of the law by drafting indictments that map the incidents as “deception + money acquisition.”  

From the defense perspective, attorneys can sharpen their argument against indictments that inflate the “deception” portion but do not confront the money‑transfer step.  

And for law students and policy analysts? This decision, in effect, wedges a decisive *testing point* between the Supreme Court’s jurisprudence and the Fiveth circuit’s roster of white‑collar prosecutions. Prosecutors will have plenty of reason to brush up their skills before the next indictment lands on a bench that is primed to test every line against the statutory rubric.

In short, *United States v. Constantinescu* is not a case about a specific “pump‑and‑dump” scheme—it is a litmus test for every prosecutor drafting a modern securities‑fraud indictment: if you want your indictment to survive the de‑novo spotlight, make sure you lay out the fraudulent‑inducement theory in bold, unmistakable language, and remember that the federal statutes demand *money* only, not *information*.
