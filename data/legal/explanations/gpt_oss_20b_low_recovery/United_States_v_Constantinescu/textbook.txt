Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Chapter I – Background and Procedural History  

## Facts of the alleged scheme  

Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight were alleged to run a coordinated “pump‑and‑dump” operation targeting publicly traded securities. Each defendant maintained large followings on platforms such as YouTube, TikTok, and Twitter and publicly promoted themselves as experienced traders. The scheme involved a three‑step routine: first, the defendants purchased a relatively low‑price security; second, they posted exaggerated or false claims about the company’s prospects and the potential upside of the stock; and third, they persuaded their audience to buy the security, thereby increasing its price (“pump”). Once the artificially inflated price was achieved, the defendants would covertly sell their holdings at the high, profiting from the difference (“dump”). The indictment claims that the collective profits from the scheme amounted to $114 million.  

## Indictment allegations and counts  

The superseding indictment was filed under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1349 (conspiracy to commit securities fraud) and 1348 & 2 (multiple counts of securities fraud). For each defendant, the indictment alleges:

1. A “scheme to defraud any person in connection with any security” by misrepresenting their trading positions and the potential price of securities.
2. The use of false or misleading information posted on social‑media platforms to induce other investors to purchase the securities.
3. The execution of the “pump” by artificially inflating the market price through the induced purchases.
4. The “dump” through secret sales at the inflated price.
5. The resulting profit of $114 million from the coordinated scheme.

These allegations are intended to satisfy the elements of the securities‑fraud statute: a scheme to defraud, a fraudulent misrepresentation, an intent to defraud, and the deprivation of victims’ money or property.  

## Defendants’ motions and plea  

Daniel Knight pleaded guilty to the charges, effectively accepting the indictment’s allegations for himself. The remaining defendants, however, filed an orderly motion to dismiss the indictment pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1442, arguing that the indictment fails to state a charged offense. Their central claim was that the indictment merely accused them of depriving followers of “potentially valuable economic information,” not of any tangible property interest, and thus violated the statutory definition of fraud.  

The motion was granted by the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Texas. The court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the alleged conduct did not “deprive any person of a traditional property interest” and that the scheme focused on information rather than money or property.  

## District court’s dismissal rationale  

In its dismissal, the district court applied its own internal test for sufficiency: the indictment must contain factual allegations that, taken as true, would constitute a crime under the statute. The court concluded that because the alleged misrepresentations were aimed solely at the followers’ knowledge, the indictment did not satisfy:

1. The “unlawful scheme to defraud” element characterizing the sought deprivation of a protected property interest.
2. The “intent to defraud” element, as it alleged only a desire to profit without any deprivation of money or property by the victims.

With these deficiencies identified, the court concluded that the indictment was defective and dismissed it under § 1442.  

## Federal appellate review process  

The government promptly filed an appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. Under 28 U.S.C. §§ 1291‑1298, the appellate court reviews a district court’s decision de novo for sufficiency of indictment. The Fifth Circuit applied the precedent of *United States v. Rafoi*, which requires the court to:

1. Assume the truth of the indictment’s allegations.
2. Assess whether the allegations, considered together, articulate a statutory offense.
3. Apply the established elements of the securities‑fraud statute, noting that the Supreme Court’s decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* clarify that fraud can be based on a fraudulent‑inducement theory, not merely a right‑to‑control theory.

The appellate court determined that the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, invoking the Supreme Court’s guidance that the adverse party need not suffer an actual economic loss for a fraud conviction. Consequently, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its findings.

# Chapter II – Legal Framework: Indictment Sufficiency and Securities Fraud Statutes

In the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the court reviewed a district court dismissal of a securities‑fraud indictment on the ground that it allegedly failed to state an offense. The appellate court reaffirmed the de‑novo standard for reviewing indictments and explained how the elements of the 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348, 1349, and 2 apply to pump‑and‑dump schemes that rely on social‑media influence. Below we unpack the procedural doctrines that govern indictment sufficiency and the substantive requirements that transform a mere misstatement of price into a federal violation of securities fraud.

## Doctrine of Indictment Sufficiency (de novo Review)

The Fifth Circuit applies a de‑novo review of indictments, a standard entrenched in *United States v. Rafoi* (60 F.4th 982, 993) and *United States v. Fontenot* (665 F.3d 640, 644). Under this approach the appellate court must **take the allegations of the indictment as true** and then decide whether an offense has been stated. The court looks only at the text of the indictment and does not consider later evidence or prosecutorial intent. This standard is designed to protect defendants’ constitutional right to be informed of the charges against them and to safeguard the separation of powers.

## Elements of a Sufficient Indictment

A sufficient indictment must satisfy three linked requirements, as summarized in *United States v. Gaytan* (74 F.3d 545, 551), *United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo* (236 F.3d 233, 236), and the Fifth Circuit’s own rulebook:

1. **Enumerating Each Prima Facie Element** – The indictment must state every material component of the charged offense. If a statutory element is omitted, the indictment is defective, regardless of how convincing the remaining allegations are.

2. **Fair Notice to the Defendant** – The indictment must provide reasons for the alleged crime and present the alleged conduct in a way that a reasonable defendant could understand the specific allegations. This is the “notice” element.

3. **Double‑Jeopardy Prevention** – The indictment must avoid duplicating the same factual allegations across multiple charges when a single indictment could give rise to a subsequent criminal proceeding for the same conduct.

An indictment that meets these requirements enables the prosecution to move forward; one that does not forces dismissal or a fresh indictment.

## Statutory Definition of Securities Fraud

Under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348, 1349, and 2, the federal fraud statutes prohibit schemes that **"defraud any person in connection with"** a security (§ 1348) or *“...to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of any security”* (§ 2). The Fifth Circuit’s analysis in *Constantinescu* drew heavily on earlier cases that described the two key elements of a securities‑fraud scheme:

1. **Scheme to Defraud** – A **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive others in order to obtain money or property. The misrepresentation must be purposeful (not merely inaccurate) and must deceive a substantial number of investors about a security’s value or prospects.

2. **Intent to Defraud** – The offender must intend **both** to deceive and to cause some adverse effect from that deception. In *United States v. Evans* (892 F.3d 692) the Fifth Circuit defined this intent as a desire to deceive and to make the victim bear a burden of loss.

Importantly, the statutes do **not** require that the victim suffer a pecuniary loss. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) held that the “fraudulent‑inducement theory”—in which a material misstatement induces a victim to part with money or property—does not require that the victim be economically worse off after the fact. Thus a scheme that merely induces investors to purchase a security at an inflated price can satisfy the voluntary element of intent to defraud.

## Relationship to Mail and Wire Fraud Cases

The statutes for securities fraud borrow several conceptual tools from the mail and wire‑fraud statutes, which is why courts often look to *United States v. Greenlaw* (84 F.4th 325) and *United States v. Evans* for interpretive guidance. In particular, the idea that a scheme must involve a **device to facilitate the fraudulent conduct**—often an email or text message—is common to all three statutes. Therefore a pump‑and‑dump scheme that relies on social‑media posts medial the same “device” understood in wire fraud: a communications medium that transmits the misrepresentation to a crowd of potential investors.

While the construction of § 1348 is admittedly scant in the Fifth Circuit, the borrowing principle provides a useful lens: **mail‑fraud bans oral or written misrepresentations delivered by mail; wire‑fraud bans oral or written misrepresentations transmitted by interstate wire; securities‑fraud bans misrepresentations that influence the purchase or sale of securities.** When social media post effectively acts as a “wire” that delivers the misstatement to thousands of potential investors, the petition can be viewed as satisfying the “mail” and “wire” prerequisites when combined with the securities‑fraud basis.

## Application to the *Constantinescu* Indictment

In *Constantinescu*, the court determined that the indictment met the sufficiency standard for the following reasons:

- **Enumeration**: The indictment laid out each element—defendants’ position as social‑media influencers, the false statements about securities, the induced purchases, and the subsequent “dumping” of the securities for profit.

- **Notice**: By detailing how social‑media followers were misled into buying a security at a price that was not justified by fundamentals, the indictment gave defendants a clear sense of the alleged conduct.

- **Double‑jeopardy**: The indictment was structured as a single, coherent charge, with no overlapping claims that would create procedural redundancy.

The court also reinforced that the *Kousisis* framework of fraudulent inducement—which the indictment embraces—permits a conviction even when the victims' economic loss is not guaranteed or is offset by a gift of equal value. Thus, the defendants’ intent to profit from the sale of securities, while simultaneously inducing followers to purchase those securities, satisfies the statutory “intent to defraud” element, obviating the district court’s dismissal on the basis of a lack of a “traditional property interest.”

By satisfying both procedural sufficiency and substantive elements of securities fraud, the indictment survived the appellate review and the court remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.

# Chapter III – The Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory versus the Right‑to‑Control Theory

Federal fraud statutes, notably 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) and § 1349 (conspiracy to commit securities fraud), have been interpreted through two distinct doctrinal lenses: the **right‑to‑control theory** and the **fraudulent‑inducement theory**. Although both aim to carve out the phenomena that constitute “defrauding a person in connection with any security,” the theories differ in the nature of the protected interest and the alleged fraudulent conduct. The Supreme Court’s recent opinions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States* sharpened that distinction. This chapter unpacks both theories, explains their historical employment, and applies them to the indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu*.

## Right‑to‑Control Theory and *Ciminelli*’s Exclusion

The right‑to‑control theory derives from the early case law surrounding federal wire fraud. Under this theory, a defendant may be convicted if he **schemes to deprive a victim of the right to control his own assets or the right to make discretionary economic decisions**, even when no money or property is actually transferred. The interdicted right is essentially a *latent* property interest: a right to act or to decide. A classic illustration is the *U.S. v. Asper* scenario, where a computer system hack prevented an investor from making timely trades, thereby depriving the investor of the right to manage his holdings.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli* made it clear that this view lies outside the permissible ambit of federal fraud statutes. In *Ciminelli*, the government prosecuted a construction‑company executive under § 1349 for allegedly defrauding a lobbyist by promising state‑funded contracts in exchange for a bribe. The alleged fraud hinged solely on the threatened deprivation of the lobbyist’s ability to control or benefit from the state’s procurement funds. *Ciminelli* held that such a deprivation is **not a “traditional property interest”** protected by the statutes. Consequently, the right‑to‑control theory was rejected: a defendant may not be convicted for merely depriving a victim of potentially “valuable economic information” that could affect discretionary decisions. The Court emphasized that defrauding an individual of *information* alone is insufficient; the victim must be deprived of *property* (money or tangible assets) for the statute to apply.

Key take‑aways from *Ciminelli*:

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Protected Interest** | *Traditional property interest* (money, tangible or intangible assets). |
| **Scope** | Denial of the right to control *property*; not merely the right to *information*. |
| **Outcome** | Supreme Court rejected right‑to‑control theory in the context of federal fraud statutes. |

Thus, for a felony conviction under § 1348 or § 1349, the prosecutor must show deception that causes a victim to *hand over money or property*, not just to *lose informational advantage*.

## Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory and *Kousisis* Confirmation

In contrast, the **fraudulent‑inducement theory** recognizes fraud when a defendant **uses material misrepresentations to induce another party into a contract that requires the victim to surrender money or property**. This theory does not demand that the victim ultimately suffer a net pecuniary loss; the critical element is the *obtain* of money or property under false pretenses. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis* reaffirmed this view, clarifying that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is distinct from the right‑to‑control theory and is perfectly squarely within the scope of § 1348 and § 1349.

In *Kousisis*, the defendant obtained government contracts by lying about fulfilling a disadvantaged‑business subcontract requirement. The alleged fraud rested on a material misrepresentation that induced the state to award contracts, after which the defendant sub‑contracted to a “pass‑through” entity. The state argued it was a right‑to‑control fraud—depriving it of the opportunity to enforce its procurement policy. The Court rejected that argument and held that fraudulent‑inducement sufficed: the state’s purchase of contracts (i.e., its payment) constituted the prohibited “obtainment of money or property” under the statute.

Key points from *Kousisis*:

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Protected Interest** | Money or property transferred under a contract. |
| **Doctrine** | Fraudulent‑inducement: material misrepresentation leading to a victim’s surrender of money/property. |
| **Requirement** | Victim’s actual economic loss is *not* a prerequisite. |
| **Outcome** | Supreme Court upheld the application of § 1348/§ 1349 under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. |

This precedent directly informs cases involving misleading securities marketing, such as the indictment against the social‑media “pump‑and‑dump” operators in *United States v. Constantinescu*.

## Application of Each Theory to the Indictment Allegations

### Right‑to‑Control Theory

The indictment alleges that defendants:

1. Purchased securities and then **"pumped"** their prices by disseminating false posts;
2. Induced followers to buy those securities through misleading information;
3. Subsequently “dumped” the securities for profit.

Under a right‑to‑control lens, one might argue that the prosecution is claiming a deprivation of the followers’ **intention to control their financial decisions**—that is, the followers were misled about the securities’ potential, leading them to make suboptimal choices. However, the indictment does *not* assert that the followers were deprived of the right to **buy or hold these securities**; rather, it alleges that they were compelled to purchase them under false pretenses. The right‑to‑control theory requires that the victim be deprived, at least at the point of potential decision, of the *right* to control *property*. In this case, the followers were never denied the right to trade; they voluntarily chose to “control” their holdings by buying the securities. Consequently, the right‑to‑control theory is misplaced here. The prosecution’s emphasis is on *deception that leads to the transfer of money or property*, not on a deprivation of informational control alone.

### Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory

The fraudulent‑inducement theory aligns neatly with the indictment’s allegations:

- The defendants made **material misrepresentations** (“post[ed] false and misleading information about the security”) that induced investors to purchase the securities.
- The followers’ purchase involved the *transfer of money* (investment capital) and the *handing over of property* (securities) to the defendants’ corrupt scheme.
- The defendants’ objective was to acquire money and profit from the artificially inflated price, as the indictment explicitly states that the scheme generated a $114 million profit.

Because the statute’s fraudulent‑inducement requirement is satisfied by a *deceiving conduct that results in the victim handing over money or property*, the indictment does not need to prove that the followers incurred a net financial loss. Indeed, the Supreme Court in *Kousisis* clarified that a victim’s loss is not a mirror image requirement; the mere fact that the defendant obtained money or property under false pretenses suffices.

## Why the Right‑to‑Control Theory Does Not Fit This Case

The core of the adjudicative analysis in *United States v. Constantinescu* hinges on the distinction highlighted in *Ciminelli* versus *Kousisis*. The indictment’s claims *do not* fall under the right‑to‑control umbrella for several reasons:

1. **No Deprivation of Control**: The followers retained the ability to choose whether or not to buy the securities. The issue is not that they were denied the right to make a trading decision but that they were led *to make a particular decision* based on false information.
2. **No Traditional Property Interest Allegation**: The indictment focuses on the *transfer of money and securities*, i.e., actual property, which is precisely the protected interest under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. 
3. **Legal Precedent**: *Ciminelli* explicitly excludes cases that rely solely on deprivation of valuable economic information. The indictment, meanwhile, centers on *deception that results in the transfer of money and property*, the very ground that *Kousisis* affirms as within the reach of § 1348/§ 1349.

Because the indictment’s allegations center on inducing followers to hand over money through false statements, it remains squarely within the scope of the fraudulent‑inducement theory. The court’s reversal of the district court’s dismissal confirms this interpretation, underscoring that the prosecution need not prove a victim’s net loss to satisfy the fraud element. The defendants’ intent to profit, their misrepresentations, and the resulting transfer of money and securities together satisfy the fraudulent‑inducement standard.

By distinguishing right‑to‑control and fraudulent‑inducement theories and applying them to the factual matrix of the *Constantinescu* indictment, students can appreciate how subtle differences in statutory construction and Supreme Court guidance shape the outcome of federal fraud prosecutions.

# Chapter IV – Evaluating the Indictment’s Allegations of Scheme and Intent

In this chapter we dissect how the Fifth Circuit applied the statutory framework for securities fraud to the facts alleged in the indictment of *United States v. Constantinescu*. The court’s decision hinges on two pivotal elements: (1) a *scheme to defraud* and (2) an *intent to defraud*. The judge’s analysis references two recent Supreme Court decisions—*Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*—to clarify the bounds of each element and to show how the indictment satisfies those bounds. We follow the court’s logic step by step, beginning with the material misrepresentations that lay the groundwork for fraud and proceeding through how those misrepresentations, coupled with the defendants’ conduct, constitute a scheme to defraud. We then turn to the requirement of intent, illustrating how the indictment’s allegations of a profit motive and the fraudulent inducement of followers meet the statutory threshold even in the absence of demonstrable financial loss to the victims.

## Alleged Material Misrepresentations by Defendants

The indictment’s first act is to locate the defendants’ material lies. Paragraphs 13–14 allege that each defendant “purchased a security, “pumped” its price by posting false or misleading information on social media, and “dumped” the security for a profit.” The description of the false statements is not merely generic; it tells a narrative in which the defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” The Supreme Court’s definition of a *material misrepresentation* in *Ciminelli*—that is, a factual statement that would influence a reasonably prudent person—remains the functional test. The indictment identifies the defendants as “skilled stock traders” with large followings, establishing that their statements were given weight by the public. By labeling the posts “false” and “misleading,” the indictment alleges that the defendants intended to deceive and that the deception was sufficient to prompt purchases. These statements satisfy the first requirement of the *scheme to defraud* element: the presence of a materially false statement intended to mislead.

## Induction of Followers to Purchase Securities as Fraud

Once the misrepresentations are laid out, the indictment moves to the consequential conduct: the *induction* of followers to buy securities. The court interprets the indictment’s language as fitting the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory, which the Supreme Court affirmed in *Kousisis*. Under this theory, the core is a material misstatement that induces a victim to enter a contract that transfers money or property to the defendant. The indictment details that after “pumping” a stock’s price, the defendants “dump” the security, meaning they sold it at a high price while their followers still held it. The followers, lured by the alleged misrepresentations, purchased the stock without the benefit of honest information, thereby giving money or property to the defendants. The indictment does not require the shareholders to lose money; it merely claims that they were “induced to purchase” the securities. In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court rejected the notion that the victim must suffer an economic loss to constitute fraud. Instead, the Court emphasized that fraud requires that the defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property," regardless of whether the victim eventually profits or loses. The indictment’s allegation that the defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” satisfies this structural requirement: a misstatement, an inducement, and a transfer of money or property.

## Intent to Defraud: Obtaining Money or Property Regardless of Victim Harm

The *intent to defraud* component is arguably the most intricate. The statutory schema is that the defendant must “schematically intend” to deceive and thereby obtain money or property from the victim. The defendants’ motive—“to profit from their scheme” and to “obtain money”—is directly alleged in the indictment. The Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* decision clarified that the statute does not demand that the victim suffer an actual loss. Instead, the defendant’s intent to “obtain” money or property is sufficient, no matter if the victim looks better off or worse off after the transaction. The indictment’s language that the defendants “pumped and dumped to gain a profit” suffices as proof of the requisite intent. The Court in *Kousisis* stressed that the fraud statutes are designed to convict those who deceptive tactics to acquire money or property—“regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The indictment’s claims of a profit motive, coupled with the fraudulent inducement of followers, thereby satisfy the statutory “intent to defraud” threshold.

## Supreme Court Guidance from Kousisis on Loss Requirement

The *Kousisis* ruling fundamentally reshaped the loss requirement for fraud. In *Kousisis*, the Court held that the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory could support a liability even when the defendant’s false representation did not result in “a net pecuniary loss” for the victim. This meant that a defendant could be found in violation of the federal fraud statutes simply by having a deceptive intent that caused the victim to transfer money or property. The *Constantinescu* indictment mirrors the *Kousisis* facts in several respects: both involve a defendant who lures a firm or individual into a contract by false statements and then receives money or property under false pretenses. The Fifth Circuit explicitly references *Kousisis* to validate the defendants’ alleged intent. By asserting that “the indictment’s allegation that defendants fraudulently induce their followers to purchase securities is sufficient to allege an injury” and that “obtaining the victim’s money or property … is the ‘object’ of the fraud,” the court applies the *Kousisis* doctrine directly to the indictment’s claims.

In sum, the court concludes that the indictment says enough to satisfy the statutory requirement for both the scheme and intent. The misrepresentations, the fraudulently induced purchases, and the defendants’ profit motive together fulfil the *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* tests, enabling the indictment to proceed. The reversal of the district court’s dismissal affirms that the indictment’s allegations are duly sufficient under federal fraud statutes.

# Chapter V – Holding, Reverse, and Significance

## Court’s Holding and Remand Instructions

The Fifth Circuit held that the indictment satisfies the requirements for sufficiency under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and § 1349. The court concluded that the indictment appropriately alleges every material element of a securities‑fraud scheme: a scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and the specific misrepresentations used to induce victims to purchase securities. Because the indictment therefore states an offense, the court reversed the district court’s dismissal. The remand order instructs the district court to continue the case as if the indictment had never been dismissed, providing the defendants with the right to proceed to trial, plea negotiations, or other substantive filings consistent with the court’s holding. No procedural alterations to sentencing or appellate procedure are mandated; the primary effect is the restoration of the prosecution’s ability to proceed under the original indictment.

## Impact on Future Securities Fraud Indictments

The court’s decision reinforces the viability of fraudulent‑inducement theories in securities‑fraud prosecutions. Prosecutors may no longer rely on the notion that a plaintiff’s lawful “right to control” is the only protected property interest. Instead, any misrepresentational inducement that results in the transfer of money or property—whether or not the victim suffers a net economic loss—obeys the fraud statutes.

* **Broadening the “fraudulent‑inducement” test:** The opinion interprets § 1348 as resembling the traditional mail‑ and wire‑fraud standards, emphasizing deception intent and the plaintiff’s deprivation of money or property. The court argues that a “material misrepresentation” coupled with intent to obtain money or property suffices, even when the plaintiff merely consumes market‑value securities. This clarifies that prosecutors can seek conviction for schemes where the victim ultimately receives securities or goods of equal or higher value, so long as the fraud was the mechanism of acquisition.

* **Practical guidance for indictment drafting:** The decision underscores the importance of stating in the indictment that the defendant’s deceptive conduct “caused another to part with money or property” and that the defendant’s intent “was to obtain that money or property." Fine‑tuned language is less critical than the presence of the core elements of the fraud statutes. This encourages prosecutors to attack the alleged “pump and dump” conduct in the same language used here: procurement of securities through misrepresentation, followed by a plan to liquidate them at inflated prices.

* **Wake‑up call for defense counsel:** Defendants will need to challenge the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory where it is not inherently clear that victims were induced to part with money or property. Thus, pleading patterns that merely allege indexing of information or “depriving” of knowledge will likely face dismissal under the new parsing, forcing defense counsel to seek more robust factual allegations that a material purchase or sale of securities resulted from the defendant’s misrepresentations.

## Relationship to Ongoing Investigations and Potential Plea Deals

The reversal has immediate, practical implications for in‑process investigations and plea negotiations:

1. **Continuity of Current Investigations:** Agencies that were investigating the defendants’ social‑media activity now have a valid legal foundation to prosecute the fraud charges. The reversed dismissal removes a procedural barrier that could have been used to halt or delay prosecutions.

2. **Plea Dynamics:** With the indictment reinstated, prosecutors can advance plea offers with a stronger evidentiary base. Defense counsel may account for the likely success of the case when negotiating plea terms, potentially reducing the defendants’ plea range or tightening plea agreements to mitigate the severity of the fraud allegations that now carry the full force of § 1348/§ 1349.

3. **Prosecutorial Strategy Adjustments:** The court’s scope clarifies that defendants may face concurrent conspiracy and securities‑fraud counts if their conduct acts in concert with other conspirators. Prosecutors will be encouraged to pursue those conspiracy charges vigorously because the fraud indictment's sufficiency removes a practical hurdle.

## Implications for the Scope of Protected Property Interests

The court’s application of the fraudulent‑inducement theory carries a substantive effect on the definition of protected property interests within the fraud statutes. By distinguishing this theory from the rejected right‑to‑control concept, the court organizes the legal landscape as follows:

* **Traditional Property Interests:** In fraud statutes, the plaintiff’s protected interest is not abstract or theoretical but is a concrete asset—money or tangible property—transferred after deceit. Thus, the court affirms that “information” alone fails to constitute a protected property interest for the purposes of § 1348/§ 1349. A demonstrable payment or transfer remains indispensable.

* **Fraudulent‑Inducement vs. Right‑to‑Control:** The court’s ruling clarifies that the fraudulent‑inducement theory subsumes the right‑to‑control theory. A plaintiff need not prove that the defendant deprived them of a right to control their assets; it suffices that the defendant’s misrepresentation induced the plaintiff to give up money or property. This consolidation narrows defenses based on the alleged “absence” of a monetary loss, focusing instead on the mechanics of the deceit.

* **Broader Policy Considerations:** By permitting fraud charges against schemes that may not result in net loss but do still involve deceptive inducement, the court promotes a deterrent effect within the securities market. Investors are assured that misleading promotional tactics are actionable, even if the market mechanism ultimately rewards the “victim” with securities of comparable market value. The decision thus expands the reach of securities fraud enforcement, discouraging “pump and dump” operations premised on advertising‐driven price inflation.

In sum, the Fifth Circuit’s reversal validates the prosecutorial use of fraudulent‑inducement arguments in securities fraud cases and clarifies the indispensable role of protected monetary interests. The decision sets a clear precedent that the mere presentation of attractive securities, if induced by deception, suffices for indictment sufficiency under federal fraud statutes, thereby tightening the net over securities‑fraud schemes that might otherwise escape conviction by claiming only a deprivation of potentially valuable economic information.
