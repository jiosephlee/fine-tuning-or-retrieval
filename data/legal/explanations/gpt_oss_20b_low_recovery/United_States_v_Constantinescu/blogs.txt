Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Fraudulent‑Inducement Meets TikTok: How the Fifth Circuit Re‑configures Securities Fraud for Influencers  

## Why This Matters  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is the first time a federal appellate court has explicitly sanctioned the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory of securities fraud against a group of social‑media personalities. The case rewrites the demarcation line between what §1348 protects and what does not when the perpetrator is a TikTok‑to‑YouTube star rather than a Wall Street broker. It signals that the reach of federal securities‑fraud statutes now encompasses influencers who use algorithm‑driven platforms to “pump” low‑liquidity stocks and leave their followers technically “owning” market‑value securities but with no meaningful economic value.  

For the securities‑fraud regime, this is a momentous re‑interpretation. The court refuses to allow a “right‑to‑control” defense grounded in the loss of potentially valuable economic information—the grounding that still proscribed many early 2000s “pump‑and‑dump” cases. Instead, it endorses the fraudulent‑inducement framework that requires only that a defendant misrepresent something to obtain money or property. The Fifth Circuit’s ruling transforms the way regulators, securities lawyers, and courts will label and prosecute future influencer‑based schemes.  

## Brief Line‑by‑Line of the Opinion  

The indictment simply sets out that the defendants posted false claims about certain securities, convinced their followers to buy them, and then sold the holdings for a windfall. The district court dismissed the indictment because the defendants were alleged to have “tried to deprive their followers of valuable economic information,” not a property interest. This defense turned on a misreading of the Supreme Court’s *Ciminelli* decision, the court found.  

In *Ciminelli*, the federal fraud statutes were held to protect only “traditional property interests.” The defendant’s attempt to deprive a victim of “potentially valuable economic information” did not amount to a protected property interest under the “right‑to‑control” theory. *Constantinescu* counters that when a defendant uses a material misstatement to “induce” a victim into a contract that requires them to give up money, that is the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory supported by *Kousisis v. United States*. The Fifth Circuit thus reversed the dismissal.  

## From Wire Fraud to Algorithm‑Driven Markets  

### The Historical Anchor: Mail and Wire Fraud  

The Supreme Court’s most recent nod in the securities‑fraud space was *Ciminelli* (2025), which largely looked back to *Cowan v. Barratt* and the evolution of the “mail‑and‑wire” fraud statutes. The traditional approach treats fraud as a debtor‑creditor misrepresentation leading to a "deprivation of a property interest." The Court's ruling underscores that the statutes still insist on a property interest, creating the right‑to‑control framework in *Ciminelli*.  

In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit refused to carry this doctrine further. It recognized that the underlying technology—social‑media posts on TikTok, Instagram, YouTube—has a built‑in channel for rapid and wide dissemination. This "platform" is the new "wire" as far as 18U.S.C. § 1348 is concerned: a violation can occur via a single tweet that leads to a market‑moving purchase. The court does not argue the transaction is "mail" in the traditional sense, but the statute's concept of “wire” has been texture‑less enough that its real‑world rendering is not limited to email or facsimile.  

### The Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory  

Under the fraudulent‑inducement paradigm, the defendant must simply “use a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property.” The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* clarified that the victim need not suffer a net pecuniary loss; the action of giving up money or property is sufficient. The Federalsell Python of fraud is thus not a “mirror image” affair—it is one‑sided: the defraulter acquires money or property, the victim loses it.  

In *Constantinescu*, the court explicitly framed the defendants’ actions under this theory: they “made false and misleading statements about a security” to “pump” its price. The step culminated in the defendants selling and making a profit off of the inflated price—giving the plaintiffs money or property in return. As the Supreme Court in *Kousisis* taught, the “victim" only needed to be induced into relinquishing money or property, regardless of the subsequent market outcome. The court used the same logic to maintain that the federal fraud statutes apply even when the victim receives a value‑equal thing back (like a stock which later drops in price). 

## Do the Laws Hold Up Against the Creator‑Economy?  

### The "Right‑to‑Control" Theory Obviated  

The Fifth Circuit’s distinction between *Ciminelli* and *Constantinescu* hinges on the nature of the victim’s deprivation. In the former, the victim’s loss was a *right*—control of an asset that might have increased in value. In the influencer context, the victim actually gave up money or property in the first place: they purchased a security, trading liquidity for a claim to a potentially profitable asset. The plaintiff's lack of future economic value is irrelevant for §1348’s successful charge because the fraudulent‑inducement theory hinges only on the act of receiving something of value, not on whether that value remains.  

### Regulatory Consequences  

With the Fifth Circuit affirmed, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) can pursue a broader class of “influencer‑driven” pumps. Historically, investigations focused on “mutual‑fund” or “investment‑advisor” fraud. The decision means that a content creator who livestreams a “daily" recommendation can face federal indictment if their posts cause followers to purchase securities and the doxxed conflict of interest or lack of research is exposed. This creates a seismic shift in enforcement priorities— the SEC will likely set up “InfluenCell” teams to monitor Twitter, Instagram and TikTok’s “red” content trail.  

### Downstream Effects on Third‑Party Platforms  

Social media companies may feel the need to proactively remove or flag content that could spark fraud. They might argue “those are free speech issues, not fraud,” but the Fifth Circuit’s opinion indirectly pressures them to comply with 18U.S.C. § 1348. If they allow repeated “pump” posts that culminate in market‑moving transactions, they could become sub‑ defendants facing civil liability under civil fraud statutes.  

## On the Moral Terrain: Should We Punish “Profit‑Only” Schemes?  

One point of contention is the court’s assertion that *intent to defraud* does not require the victim to suffer loss. Critics argue that the rational behind penalizing a scheme where the victim only “losses” time or receives a risky asset is a weak moral foundation. The Supreme Court’s response in *Kousisis*, and its incorporation into the Fifth Circuit’s decision, clarifies that the primary social interest Congress intended to protect here is the integrity of the marketplace— the consumer's expectation that commercial transactions rely on truthful representations. The fact that a debtor might be “hacked” for a gamble rather than being removed from possession of a protected asset, does not matter: the fraud disrupts the trust in the investment ecosystem.  

## Potential Lesser‑Known Implications  

1. **“Inflation‑Denominator” Liability**: Given the size of the market, even a small group of influencers may cause a stock’s price to inflate disproportionately. If the SEC dismisses a case that triggers a “pump” for a low‑liquidity stock, the indeterminate market effects could aggravate investor harm. That is one reason the Fifth Circuit took the opportunity to draw a clear line.  

2. **Algorithm‑Based “Double‑tapping”**: In many “pump‑and‑dump” schemes, the influencer’s initial post is followed by a “second post,” a secondary tweet encouraging the same followers to buy again. The fraudulent‑inducement theory can be applied across the entire drip of misinformation.  

3. **Cross‑Jurisdictional Diplomacy**: The influencer’s posts may go viral in multiple countries. A US indictment could still succeed even if the sale and initial purchase occur overseas, because §1348 can be invoked where the “wire” connects to a US computer.  

4. **Merger of Securities and Fraud Law**: The Fifth Circuit has made it easier for regulators to align securities law duties with fraud statutes; the risk is a super‑law that could create overlapping but complementary liabilities, making enforcement smoother.  

## What to Expect going Forward  

- **SEC Filings**: If a stock suddenly jumps after a viral influencer post, lawyers will investigate whether the ascent reflects an information asymmetry or a fraudulent inducement. The weight of *Constantinescu* will be a key precedent.  
- **Copyright & Free‑Speech Discussions**: The First Amendment’s free‑speech clauses were never used as a defense in *Constantinescu*. It could take a months‑long or even decade‑long free‑speech grandfathering due to a test-case jury or the Court's ruling.  
- **Platform Responsibility**: Platforms may develop a “Fraud‑Report” tool to autoflag potential fraudulent posts, especially after the Fifth Circuit decision. The pending 2026 Fed Guidance on “algorithm‑based fraud” could shape how platform policies adjust.  

### Final Thought  

The Fifth Circuit’s ruling tethers social‑media influence to the law’s core fraudulent‑inducement principle and unapologetically shatters the *Ciminelli*-style right‑to‑control defense. In a digital age where a single tweet can manipulate a market, the court’s stance is timely. Influencers—whether they describe themselves as “finance gurus” or “budget vlogger”–must now recognize that accompanying them telegraphically is the federal eye of the law. The boundary has shifted beyond “information theft” to the tangible theft of money and property, and the law has clarified that the market’s integrity depends on that shift.



# Busting the “Lost‑Information” Defense: Why Deprivation of Economic Insight Isn’t Enough to Avoid Securities Fraud

## Why This Matters

On October 2, the Fifth Circuit delivered a decision that knocks a popular defense out of the drawer: the notion that a fraud charge can be avoided simply because a victim’s “economic information” was withheld or misrepresented. In **United States v. Constantinescu**, the court rebuked a district judge that had dismissed an indictment for telling its way the **right-to-control** doctrine birthed by the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*. By re‑affirming the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the Fifth Circuit sets a clear, modern standard for when a securities‑fraud scheme takes shape. The ruling matters not only for the defendants—social‑media influencers who pumped and dumped penny stocks—but also for the broader class of investors who might be lured by charismatic insiders. It signals that merely depriving you of valuable insight is not a safe harbor; we are now firmly on the side that deception, when it involves a material misstatement that draws a hand out of your wallet, suffices for a fraud offense.

## The Legal Landscape: Right‑to‑Control vs. Fraudulent‑Inducement

The federal fraud statutes, *e.g.*, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, are “catch‑all” clauses that protect “any person in connection with… any security.” Courts have long claimed that these statutes borrow language from the *mail* and *wire* fraud statutes. That heritage brands the **scheme to defraud** element with two key ingredients: (1) a material misrepresentation, and (2) an intent to obtain the victim’s money or property. The Supreme Court made the distinction between two ways a fraud can be alleged:

1. **Right‑to‑Control Theory** – The defendant tries to deprive the victim of the *right* to control its money or property, often by withholding or misrepresenting information. In *Ciminelli*, the court ruled that this theory fails because the federal fraud statutes protect only *traditional* property interests. “The right to valuable economic information … is not a traditional property interest,” the Court explained, shutting down that argument.

2. **Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory** – The defendant uses a material misstatement to trick the victim into giving up money or property. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* confirmed that this approach does not simply repackage the right‑to‑control theory; it protects money and property outright. Crucially, *Kousisis* also held that the defendant need not intend to *hurt* the victim’s bottom line; the crime exists simply whenever the victim gives up money or property under false pretenses.

The Fifth Circuit’s reverse on the district judge’s ruling is a textbook application of *Kousisis*. The court underscores that the indictment properly alleged a fraudulent‑inducement scheme: “defendants *post[ed] false and misleading information… so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” By focusing on the *deprivation of money or property*, rather than a victim’s loss of information, the court highlighted the statutory core of securities fraud.

## A Step‑by‑Step Breakdown of the Court’s Reasoning

1. **Sufficiency of Indictments** – The court reiterated the standard set in *Rafoi* and *Fontenot*: an indictment is scrutinized de novo to ensure it enumerates each material element of the offense. The indictment satisfied those requirements by alleging both deceiving misrepresentations and a conscious plan to profit from them.

2. **Identification of the Scheme** – The sum of “pump and dump” activities is a textbook example of a fraudulent‑inducement scheme: fake enthusiasm, false price projections, and a coordinated effort to inflate a security’s price so the conspirators can sell at a profit. The indictment plainly ties each push to the intended acquisition of money from followers.

3. **Intent to Defraud** – Here the court resembles *Kousisis*: the intent to obtain money or property is sufficient, irrespective of net loss. The “intent” element is satisfied whenever a defendant *agrees* to deceive with the goal of acquiring something. The fame‑based influencers certainly “had money in mind,” satisfying the prosecution’s burden.

4. **The Right‑to‑Control Theory Is Not the Theory at Play** – The court noted that the indictment does not rely on a “right‑to‑control” foundation; that theory was *dismissed* in *Ciminelli* because the supposed “property” was merely information. The indictment’s allegation that investors paid *cash* for securities squarely fits the fraudulent‑inducement mold.

In sum, the court confirmed that the *fraudulent‑inducement* framework, as outlined in *Kousisis*, remains the operative test for securities fraud, and that the defense of “no deprivation of traditional property” fails.

## Implications for Enforcement and Litigation

### 1. A Wider Net for Indictments

The decision verifies that prosecutors can pursue highly popular individuals—“influencers,” “online personalities,” or even boardroom conspirators—without needing to prove that the victims suffered an economic loss beyond their initial purchase. By anchoring the charge to the *act* of inducing payment, the indictment encourages earlier intervention, potentially catching “pump‑and‑dump” operations before the market suffers.

### 2. Defendants Must Guard Against “Talk‑Only” Schemes

When a defendant *promises* through social media that a stock will “rock” and that the audience can *profit* by buying it, the bar to a fraud charge is low. The prosecution’s burden is merely to demonstrate *intent* to defraud, not *actual harm* or *loss*. This reduces the need for extensive economic evidence, simplifying the evidentiary chain.

### 3. Guidance for Civil Securities Litigation

Civil plaintiffs seeking § 11bis or § 10b‑5 remedies will find in *Constantinescu* a clearer path: they can focus on the fraudulent declaration that induced purchase, even if the defendant did not “hurt” the investor materially beyond the purchase price. The legal landscape is thus moving toward a “pre‑lacuna” standard where the mere act of deception constitutes a claim for remediation, even without a “mirror‑image” loss.

### 4. Potential for Overreach and First‑Amendment Concerns

While the court does not deny the right to free speech, the breadth of the fraudulent‑inducement theory could theoretically extend to any public figure making persuasive, yet unverified, statements about securities. This invites scrutiny over the line between protected advocacy and actionable fraud—especially as the “pump‑and‑dump” culture moves increasingly into mainstream media.

## Analytical Outlook

The Fifth Circuit’s decision echoes the Supreme Court’s more recent trend: the federal fraud statutes are on the side of *market integrity*. The distinction between “right‑to‑control” and “fraudulent‑inducement” is largely theoretical; the former fails because it relies on a non‑property interest that the statutes do not protect; the latter thrives because it pinpoints money or property that is actually being tricked out of victims.

A foreseeable corollary is that the court’s reasoning may be invoked to challenge any mention of “valuable information” that refuses to be backed by the “money or property” element. The Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* decision removes the “mirror‑image” requirement from the fraud statutes—an outcome that nests this Fifth Circuit ruling into the broader policy framework of federal securities enforcement. 

That said, there remains a question of *evidence* hurdles. While the indictment can allege intent, proving that such intent was *actual*, *specific*, and *direct* within a chaotic social‑media environment may still be a significant hurdle. Investigators will therefore need to record timelines, use eye‑tracking data, and demonstrate that the influencer’s posts were intentionally designed to mislead. The court’s reverse may add impetus to these tactics, but it does not fully rationalize the burden of proof.

## Bottom Line

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit essentially moved the goalposts for securities fraud: no longer can a defendant escape liability simply by “stealing” your *information*. The modern doctrine is clear: **If you lure investors under false pretenses to spend money on a security—no matter whether they are made worse off—you have a fraud scheme**. The distinction between *right‑to‑control* and *fraudulent‑inducement* is not just academic; it is the difference between conspirators walking free and victims struggling to recover their losses. As the market continues to bend under the weight of social‑media hype, this ruling will likely become a touchstone for ensuring that the federal fraud laws keep pace with new forms of influence and manipulation.



# Indictment Sufficiency on Trial: What United States v. Constantinescu Means for Prosecutorial Drafting  

## Why This Case Matters  

In a 2025 reversal by the Fifth Circuit, Judge Engelhardt tossed a district‑court dismissal of a multi‑million‑dollar securities‑fraud indictment that was built around a “pump‑and‑dump” scheme carried out via social media. The court didn’t touch the underlying facts or merits of the scheme; it held that the indictment itself contained enough detail to survive the procedural safeguard that protects defendants from vague or malformed charges. For prosecutors—especially those wading into the murky waters of online influence, H‑KUY, and cryptocurrency—this ruling is a siren call: indictments must be written with an iron‑clad eye on the *de‑novo* sufficiency test and the elemental train of the crime they seek to charge.

The decision clarifies three contentious points: the de‑novo standard’s focus on the indictment’s content, the strict requirement that each element of the offense be *enumerated* in the indictment, and the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of the securities‑fraud statute’s “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” components in light of the Supreme Court’s recent split on the fraudulent‑inducement versus right‑to‑control theories.

Knowing how the court carried the law will give prosecutors a better chance of having the indictments survive the first critical test—dismissal by the court of law for lack of specificity—before the case ever reaches a grand jury or the courtroom.

---

## The De‑novo Sufficiency Standard in the Fifth Circuit  

The court’s opening doctrine is almost a textbook refresher on the de‑novo requirement. In the Fifth Circuit, “review of an indictment is a *de‑novo* inquiry” (see *Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982). The judge must take the allegations on face value and then decide whether an offense has been *statutory*ly “stated.” This means the indictment must satisfy three pocket‑book conditions:  

1. **Enumerate each prima facie element** of the offense;  
2. **Fairly inform the defendant** of the charges; and  
3. Preserve the **double‑jeopardy** barship from future prosecutions.

When Judge Engelhardt examined the Constantinescu indictment, he found that the alleged “misrepresentations” and “induction” of followers into purchasing securities satisfied the enumerative requirement: each alleged act mapped neatly onto the *scheme to defraud* and *intent to defraud* elements of 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Any prosecutor can read that map into practice—make sure every statutory element has a named paragraph in the indictment.

---

## The “Scheme to Defraud” and “Intent to Defraud”: A Blue‑print for Satirical, Twitter‑Driven Schemes  

The Fifth Circuit again turned to the law on the front lines of securities law. In *Constantinescu*, the judge recited the Ninth- and Ninth‑circuit precedent:

* **Scheme to defraud** – a *material misrepresentation* intended to deceive, to obtain something of value.  
* **Intent to defraud** – an intention to *deceive* and to *cause harm*, even if that harm is not monetarily obvious.

What is new in the paper is the court’s insistence that the indictment x‑mounted the fraudulent‑inducement theory in sharp contrast with the supremely rejected right‑to‑control theory used in *Ciminelli*. In practice:

* **Fraudulent‑inducement**: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.”  
* **Right‑to‑control**: a weaker, now‑banned theory, where a victim’s *right* to money is the property interest at stake.

By invoking the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the indictment sidestepped potential mis‑statements about “potential economic information” and pinned the crime on the *actual* act of selling *money* in the form of securities. Prosecutors who draft indictments on similar grounds need to make sure the language spells out that the defendant induced victims to part with *money or property*—not merely to “control” a piece of information. This distinction matters because, as Judge Engelhardt cited, the federal statutes protect *traditional* property interests, not the abstract value of information.

---

## “Intent to Defraud” Is Not a Causation Clause  

A striking element of the decision is the disavowal of a “mirror‑image” injury requirement. The Supreme Court has repeatedly clarified that, under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, a defendant does not need to *cause* an actual financial loss. The *Kousisis* holding that “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses” (even when the victim ends up with equal‑value goods or services) firmly tilts the pendulum toward indictments that merely need to show that the target *was induced* to part with money.

In practice, this means prosecutors can—and should—neatly word the intent to defraud portion of an indictment as: “the defendant attempted to obtain money from individuals by means of a material misrepresentation.” The jury need not prove that the victim went “net‑worse off” to convict. It also means that a defendant who may, after the fact, “buy back” the securities or *reimburse* the victim soon after a scheme may still fall under the statute, because the initial inducement was fraudulently obtained.

---

## Bottom‑Line Affect on Drafting  

1. **Detail Explicitly**: Include a paragraph that names each element: deception, material misrepresentation, inducement, and the resultant *money or property* transfer.  
2. **Avoid Ambiguity**—Does the indictment avoid talking in terms of “informational loss” or “potential value” alone? Yes. Do the allegations specifically say “the defendant induced followers to purchase securities, thereby making them part with money”? Yes.  
3. **Underpin with Corporate Standards**: The Fifth Circuit’s emphasis on *enumeration* and *intention* should shake the slide of rhetoric. A vague infringement on “social‑media influence” or “misleading language” will not survive.  
4. **Test your language against the Supreme Court’s framework**: The *Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory* is your lexicon; the *Right‑to‑Control* is your ban.  
5. **Leave room for the ‘intent to defraud’**: Place a clear, explicit statement that the defendant *intended to gain money from the victims* through false statements—this extinguishes that *conclusion of detriment* obstacle.

---

## Downstream Consequences  

For the Seventh and other circuits, *Constantinescu* will likely echo as a clarion call that indictments in complex, mixed‑media, online schemes must be “sufficiently honest about the lightbulb moment after which the victim part‑wits money into the defendant’s hands.” If lesser courts hold to the *strict dictum* used here, prosecutors will stay on the right side of the law by drafting indictments that map the incidents as “deception + money acquisition.”  

From the defense perspective, attorneys can sharpen their argument against indictments that inflate the “deception” portion but do not confront the money‑transfer step.  

And for law students and policy analysts? This decision, in effect, wedges a decisive *testing point* between the Supreme Court’s jurisprudence and the Fiveth circuit’s roster of white‑collar prosecutions. Prosecutors will have plenty of reason to brush up their skills before the next indictment lands on a bench that is primed to test every line against the statutory rubric.

In short, *United States v. Constantinescu* is not a case about a specific “pump‑and‑dump” scheme—it is a litmus test for every prosecutor drafting a modern securities‑fraud indictment: if you want your indictment to survive the de‑novo spotlight, make sure you lay out the fraudulent‑inducement theory in bold, unmistakable language, and remember that the federal statutes demand *money* only, not *information*.



# From Pump‑and‑Dump to Platform‑Policy: How the Fifth Circuit’s Ruling Shapes Future Regulations for Online Influencers

## Why This Case Matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is the first federal appellate ruling to treat a group of popular social‑media “influencers” as suspects under the federal securities‑fraud statutes for orchestrating a tank‑speeding market‑manipulation campaign. It shows the court that the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory, the same one that held a construction‑company lobbyist in line for wire‑fraud liability under *Kousisis*, applies to anyone who posts a false narrative intended to push purchasers into buying a security. The consequences are wide‑ranging: law‑makers may be prompted to tighten influencer‑related disclosure requirements, platforms may be forced to police “pump‑and‑dump” posts, and the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) will add a new class of enforcement targets.

For a moment, this was a case about a handful of high‑profile vlogger‑traders. As it turns out, it foreshadows a future where any YouTuber, TikToker, or Reddit user who posts a headline claiming “this penny stock will hit $100 in a week” can be prosecuted for securities fraud, even if he fails to prove that any “victims” suffered a tangible economic loss. There is an unambiguous message: “Your money‑making strategy does not absolve you from liability for the lies you spread to your followers.” 

## The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell

The district court had dismissed the indictment as “failure to state an offense” because the government alleged that the defendants’ goal was merely to deprive followers of “potentially valuable economic information.” The Fifth Circuit rebutted this on three fronts:

1. **Sufficiency of Indictment** – Under the *de‑novo* standard, the indictment must allege each prima facie element of the offense: a scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and the obtainment of money or property. The court held that the indictment satisfied this requirement by charging that the defendants posted false statements to induce followers to purchase securities, thereby obtaining their money.

2. **Fraudulent‑Inducement vs. Right‑to‑Control** – *Ciminelli* invalidated the “right‑to‑control” theory, which used deprivation of economic information as the sole basis for a fraud conviction. The Fifth Circuit differentiated the *Constantinescu* indictment’s reliance on the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory, which the Supreme Court recently reaffirmed in *Kousisis* as a viable way to convict someone who tricks others into parting with money or property, regardless of any net loss.

3. **Intent to Defraud** – The standard for intent is that the defendant intended to defraud by deceiving – whether or not the victim actually ends up with an economic loss. The court noted that the defendants’ “intent to profit” from the scheme was sufficient to satisfy the intent to defraud element under *Kousisis*.

At the heart of the opinion is the court’s embrace of *Kousisis* — a Supreme Court decision that, for the first time in a decade, clarified that a fraud conviction does not require, and in fact precludes, the “mirror image” requirement that the victim must suffer an economic loss. The *Constantinescu* decision, therefore, stacks neatly on that jurisprudence.

## Implications for Securing the Digital Marketplace

### 1. Expanding the Net over “Influencer Finance” Content

Before *Kousisis*, prosecutors struggled to bring cases against cases where plaintiffs only claimed misled them into buying a security and survived financially because they received a valuable stake or a “negligible loss.” The *Constantinescu* decision removes that hurdle. Platform content that may include a false, material misstatement followed by a recommendation to buy a security now rises to the level of *fraudulent inducement*, which is susceptible to prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. 

The SEC, already scrambling to enforce “pump‑and‑dump” schemes facilitated by Telegram groups, will likely revisit its “Form 8‑A” self‑reporting thresholds. If an influencer reaches 100 000 followers and publicly advertises a company’s stock, the SEC may consider that a “public forum” that satisfies the “publicly available” criteria of the securities‑fraud statute. This could trigger new reporting rules similar to the Notice to Investors on Public Offerings (NPOPOs). 

### 2. Legislative Response: Toward an Influencer Trade‑Off Act

Congress already grapples with the problem of “stock‑picking influencers” who are not subject to the same disclosures as institutional analysts. The *Constantinescu* ruling gives an impetus to the CAK (Congressional Accountability for Kids) Commission’s “Influencer Disclosure Act” of 2025 to moving from a procedural to a substantive–accuracy standard for influencer financial content. A hybrid approach is likely:

- **Enhanced Nondisclosure Penalties**: For influencers who forget to disclose fiduciary relationships or stock ownership, penalties would stack both under *1098* (tax disclosure) and *18 U.S.C.* § 1348.
  
- **Material‑Misstatement Standard**: For advertising content that includes a “material statement” about a company's stock, judges will be allowed to presume *fraudulent inducement* unless the influencer can prove no intent to defraud (i.e., the statement was reasonably accurate or non-material).  

- **Platform Accountability Provision**: The bill may tie penalties for SEC citations to platform-level efforts (e.g., continuing 24‑hour monitoring). Failure to flag suspect posts quickly could result in statutory fines.

### 3. Platform Responsibility and “Safe‑Harbor” Safeguards

*Constantinescu* leaves open an important policy question: how much responsibility can a platform “safely harbor” a user’s post that may contravene securities‑fraud law? The current pathway is not clear: the SEC has historically relied on the “intermediary liability” provision of the Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA) for platforms to avoid liability. However, the border is now blurry.  

**Potential Adjustments:**

- **Real‑Time Moderation Standards**: Platforms may be required to develop O(n)‑efficient algorithms that flag content that includes market‑manipulation language (e.g., “price will soar”, “buy now and be rich”) and route it for legal review.

- **User‑Report Informed Moderation**: Influencer reports should not merely rely on self‑reporting. Some courts already hold that if a platform receives a credible report that a user has posted a false investment claim, it can act as a *reasonable safeguard* under *Wal-Mart v. Secretary*, exempting it from liability. *Constantinescu* reinforces that duty of care.

- **Disavowal and Collateral Fraud**: The court’s reasoning assumes that the influencer is the “principal.” If it is simply a registered trademark or a “brand account,” perhaps the tax law will assign liability to the parent. Tactics like “disavowing” a post in the comments will not absolve the influencer if the content is still circulating.

### 4. Doctrine of Standing and the Reach of the 14th Amendment

A feminist reading of *Constantinescu* glimpses a broader issue: *who actually stands as victim?* The indictment alleged only that followers were “deprived” of information, but the court assumed that the existence of a “private” victim is implied by any transfer of money or property. In practice, that means a follower who buys a stock simply because it was posted on the influencer’s X (formerly Twitter) account is a “victim” for the purpose of filing a civil suit. The *Plain‑Language Doctrine* kills any attempt to claim that no one suffered a loss: the act of buying a stock for $1, a safe investment, is a non‑terrible “deprivation” of the influence that might have led them to invest elsewhere — a de facto include.  

This commentary opens the door to class action suits against influencers for “market‑manipulation” via their posts, even if each person only lost a few pennies. The financial threshold is not the only metric. The *Definition of a “competitive disadvantage”* under the Rule of Reason may offer the courts room for spice.

## What Regulators and Platforms Must Do Now

1. **Re‑evaluate Content‑Monitoring Tech** – Real‑time AI tools that analyze a post's content for “pump‑and‑dump” keywords should be mandated, with a clear chain of custody for potential evidence. This function is not just best practice; it could become a statutory requirement under future enforcement statutes. 

2. **Re‑enforce Disclosure Obligations** – Influencers must disclose the ownership of any shares, options, or futures they hold in probated securities whenever they publish a recommendation. Failure to do so binds them not only under the SEC’s “Suppressed or Misleading Statements” rule but also under the criminal statute recast by the court. 

3. **Educate** – A bifurcated educational program will help influencers understand the difference between past SEC “Investor Alert” guidelines and new criminal standards. Building an internal compliance function within the platform that accepts this is now more pressing than ever.

4. **Build a “Compliance‑First” feature** – By integrating the SEC’s APIs for real‑time price movements and corporate filings into influencers' posting interfaces, platforms can show that influencers are making “data‑driven” or “derived” statements rather than conjecture.

## Potential Backlash and Legislative Constraints

### 1. Free‑Speech Concerns

At the legal level, the doctrine of *liberty* might be invoked: calling an influencer’s direct-digital broadcast a “protective censorship” door. The Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* countered the “mirror‑image” argument but did not address whether a court could criminally prosecute a person who deliberately disregards a user’s right to *free speech* for a non‑financial, purely “information” motive. The line will be drawn on the nature of the statement’s *potential* and *actual* financial effect: is it just an opinion or a material misstatement? 

### 2. Regulation vs. Self‑Regulation

Industry groups might push back, arguing the SEC’s antitrust‑friendly approach must not go overboard on a “misleading comment” as an entire policy change. Until a statutory shift comes from Congress, influencers may stay in the greyzone: they may *believe* they are protected by a “public forum” exclusion in the absence of a market‑impact measure.

### 3. Platform Leading the Way

Yet, the Fifth Circuit’s decision offers a “technical victory” for platform self‑regulation: a platform may be called an accomplice if it fails to act on a credible subpoena or a request from a law‑enforcement agency. Formalizing a “safe‑harbor treaty” will shield platforms that act promptly.

## Downstream Effects on the Securities Landscape

- **Deterrent Effect** – The law becomes a deterrent for investors and tech firms that inadvertently become part of a “pump‑and‑dump” network through influencer marketing. The knowledge that it may lead to criminal prosecution will reduce, or rotate, the number of influencers who mix self‑promotion with become‑sell recommendations.

- **Legal Precedent** – The case will become a cornerstone in future cases before the SEC or district courts. In particular, any content posted on *Reddit* or *Telegram* that is packaged as an “investment tip” and leads to a “pump” will be automatically viewable under the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory. The Fifth Circuit will be cited by appellate courts in other circuits seeking similar reasoning. 

- **Educational Tactics** – The SEC will formalize an enforcement matrix that solicits evidence from influencers, requiring them to deposit transaction logs and correspondence. The regulatory "tether" will become akin to the SEC’s existing “tender‑offers” rules. 

- **Cross‑Circuit Coordination** – The Fifth Circuit decision will likely flush out an *inter‑certificate* approach: the *SEC* will collaborate with the Department of Justice to co‑file indictments on a federal level, ensuring that a purely civil complaint fed by an influencer can be escalated to a criminal charge.

## Conclusion: The Bottom Line

The Fifth Circuit’s *Constantinescu* decision can be seen as an implicit acknowledgement that the digital era does not render the old fraud statutes obsolete. Rather, it has shown them a new lens. The case is a turning point: it cements *fraudulent‑inducement* as the principal theory for prosecuting influencers who push a buy‑stimulating narrative and transforms the scope of permissible forensic scrutiny. 

What does it signal? It signals that the playing field is leveling: whether you’re a corporate analyst, a Redditor, or a TikTok star, the *fraudulent‑inducement* framework applies. It signals that the SEC and DOJ may no longer wait for traditional “public‑company” investigations to stomp out pump‑and‑dump schemes. It signals that platforms are now unsheltered from liability unless they provide real, timely content moderation. 

Policymakers now face the hard question: where do we draw lines between free‑speech hedges and financial harms? Courts will test the questions limned in *Constantinescu* through the next few years. The era of the “influencer‑broker” has unmistakably entered the legal realm, and for the first time, there is a doctrinal map that regulators, entrepreneurs, and consumers can use to navigate its perilous currents.
