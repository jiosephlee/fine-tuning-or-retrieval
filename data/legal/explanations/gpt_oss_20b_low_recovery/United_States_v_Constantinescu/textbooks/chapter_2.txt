Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Chapter II – Legal Framework: Indictment Sufficiency and Securities Fraud Statutes

In the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the court reviewed a district court dismissal of a securities‑fraud indictment on the ground that it allegedly failed to state an offense. The appellate court reaffirmed the de‑novo standard for reviewing indictments and explained how the elements of the 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348, 1349, and 2 apply to pump‑and‑dump schemes that rely on social‑media influence. Below we unpack the procedural doctrines that govern indictment sufficiency and the substantive requirements that transform a mere misstatement of price into a federal violation of securities fraud.

## Doctrine of Indictment Sufficiency (de novo Review)

The Fifth Circuit applies a de‑novo review of indictments, a standard entrenched in *United States v. Rafoi* (60 F.4th 982, 993) and *United States v. Fontenot* (665 F.3d 640, 644). Under this approach the appellate court must **take the allegations of the indictment as true** and then decide whether an offense has been stated. The court looks only at the text of the indictment and does not consider later evidence or prosecutorial intent. This standard is designed to protect defendants’ constitutional right to be informed of the charges against them and to safeguard the separation of powers.

## Elements of a Sufficient Indictment

A sufficient indictment must satisfy three linked requirements, as summarized in *United States v. Gaytan* (74 F.3d 545, 551), *United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo* (236 F.3d 233, 236), and the Fifth Circuit’s own rulebook:

1. **Enumerating Each Prima Facie Element** – The indictment must state every material component of the charged offense. If a statutory element is omitted, the indictment is defective, regardless of how convincing the remaining allegations are.

2. **Fair Notice to the Defendant** – The indictment must provide reasons for the alleged crime and present the alleged conduct in a way that a reasonable defendant could understand the specific allegations. This is the “notice” element.

3. **Double‑Jeopardy Prevention** – The indictment must avoid duplicating the same factual allegations across multiple charges when a single indictment could give rise to a subsequent criminal proceeding for the same conduct.

An indictment that meets these requirements enables the prosecution to move forward; one that does not forces dismissal or a fresh indictment.

## Statutory Definition of Securities Fraud

Under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348, 1349, and 2, the federal fraud statutes prohibit schemes that **"defraud any person in connection with"** a security (§ 1348) or *“...to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of any security”* (§ 2). The Fifth Circuit’s analysis in *Constantinescu* drew heavily on earlier cases that described the two key elements of a securities‑fraud scheme:

1. **Scheme to Defraud** – A **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive others in order to obtain money or property. The misrepresentation must be purposeful (not merely inaccurate) and must deceive a substantial number of investors about a security’s value or prospects.

2. **Intent to Defraud** – The offender must intend **both** to deceive and to cause some adverse effect from that deception. In *United States v. Evans* (892 F.3d 692) the Fifth Circuit defined this intent as a desire to deceive and to make the victim bear a burden of loss.

Importantly, the statutes do **not** require that the victim suffer a pecuniary loss. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) held that the “fraudulent‑inducement theory”—in which a material misstatement induces a victim to part with money or property—does not require that the victim be economically worse off after the fact. Thus a scheme that merely induces investors to purchase a security at an inflated price can satisfy the voluntary element of intent to defraud.

## Relationship to Mail and Wire Fraud Cases

The statutes for securities fraud borrow several conceptual tools from the mail and wire‑fraud statutes, which is why courts often look to *United States v. Greenlaw* (84 F.4th 325) and *United States v. Evans* for interpretive guidance. In particular, the idea that a scheme must involve a **device to facilitate the fraudulent conduct**—often an email or text message—is common to all three statutes. Therefore a pump‑and‑dump scheme that relies on social‑media posts medial the same “device” understood in wire fraud: a communications medium that transmits the misrepresentation to a crowd of potential investors.

While the construction of § 1348 is admittedly scant in the Fifth Circuit, the borrowing principle provides a useful lens: **mail‑fraud bans oral or written misrepresentations delivered by mail; wire‑fraud bans oral or written misrepresentations transmitted by interstate wire; securities‑fraud bans misrepresentations that influence the purchase or sale of securities.** When social media post effectively acts as a “wire” that delivers the misstatement to thousands of potential investors, the petition can be viewed as satisfying the “mail” and “wire” prerequisites when combined with the securities‑fraud basis.

## Application to the *Constantinescu* Indictment

In *Constantinescu*, the court determined that the indictment met the sufficiency standard for the following reasons:

- **Enumeration**: The indictment laid out each element—defendants’ position as social‑media influencers, the false statements about securities, the induced purchases, and the subsequent “dumping” of the securities for profit.

- **Notice**: By detailing how social‑media followers were misled into buying a security at a price that was not justified by fundamentals, the indictment gave defendants a clear sense of the alleged conduct.

- **Double‑jeopardy**: The indictment was structured as a single, coherent charge, with no overlapping claims that would create procedural redundancy.

The court also reinforced that the *Kousisis* framework of fraudulent inducement—which the indictment embraces—permits a conviction even when the victims' economic loss is not guaranteed or is offset by a gift of equal value. Thus, the defendants’ intent to profit from the sale of securities, while simultaneously inducing followers to purchase those securities, satisfies the statutory “intent to defraud” element, obviating the district court’s dismissal on the basis of a lack of a “traditional property interest.”

By satisfying both procedural sufficiency and substantive elements of securities fraud, the indictment survived the appellate review and the court remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.
