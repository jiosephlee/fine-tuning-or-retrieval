Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 3: # Chapter III – The Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory versus the Right‑to‑Control Theory

Federal fraud statutes, notably 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) and § 1349 (conspiracy to commit securities fraud), have been interpreted through two distinct doctrinal lenses: the **right‑to‑control theory** and the **fraudulent‑inducement theory**. Although both aim to carve out the phenomena that constitute “defrauding a person in connection with any security,” the theories differ in the nature of the protected interest and the alleged fraudulent conduct. The Supreme Court’s recent opinions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States* sharpened that distinction. This chapter unpacks both theories, explains their historical employment, and applies them to the indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu*.

## Right‑to‑Control Theory and *Ciminelli*’s Exclusion

The right‑to‑control theory derives from the early case law surrounding federal wire fraud. Under this theory, a defendant may be convicted if he **schemes to deprive a victim of the right to control his own assets or the right to make discretionary economic decisions**, even when no money or property is actually transferred. The interdicted right is essentially a *latent* property interest: a right to act or to decide. A classic illustration is the *U.S. v. Asper* scenario, where a computer system hack prevented an investor from making timely trades, thereby depriving the investor of the right to manage his holdings.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli* made it clear that this view lies outside the permissible ambit of federal fraud statutes. In *Ciminelli*, the government prosecuted a construction‑company executive under § 1349 for allegedly defrauding a lobbyist by promising state‑funded contracts in exchange for a bribe. The alleged fraud hinged solely on the threatened deprivation of the lobbyist’s ability to control or benefit from the state’s procurement funds. *Ciminelli* held that such a deprivation is **not a “traditional property interest”** protected by the statutes. Consequently, the right‑to‑control theory was rejected: a defendant may not be convicted for merely depriving a victim of potentially “valuable economic information” that could affect discretionary decisions. The Court emphasized that defrauding an individual of *information* alone is insufficient; the victim must be deprived of *property* (money or tangible assets) for the statute to apply.

Key take‑aways from *Ciminelli*:

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Protected Interest** | *Traditional property interest* (money, tangible or intangible assets). |
| **Scope** | Denial of the right to control *property*; not merely the right to *information*. |
| **Outcome** | Supreme Court rejected right‑to‑control theory in the context of federal fraud statutes. |

Thus, for a felony conviction under § 1348 or § 1349, the prosecutor must show deception that causes a victim to *hand over money or property*, not just to *lose informational advantage*.

## Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory and *Kousisis* Confirmation

In contrast, the **fraudulent‑inducement theory** recognizes fraud when a defendant **uses material misrepresentations to induce another party into a contract that requires the victim to surrender money or property**. This theory does not demand that the victim ultimately suffer a net pecuniary loss; the critical element is the *obtain* of money or property under false pretenses. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis* reaffirmed this view, clarifying that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is distinct from the right‑to‑control theory and is perfectly squarely within the scope of § 1348 and § 1349.

In *Kousisis*, the defendant obtained government contracts by lying about fulfilling a disadvantaged‑business subcontract requirement. The alleged fraud rested on a material misrepresentation that induced the state to award contracts, after which the defendant sub‑contracted to a “pass‑through” entity. The state argued it was a right‑to‑control fraud—depriving it of the opportunity to enforce its procurement policy. The Court rejected that argument and held that fraudulent‑inducement sufficed: the state’s purchase of contracts (i.e., its payment) constituted the prohibited “obtainment of money or property” under the statute.

Key points from *Kousisis*:

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Protected Interest** | Money or property transferred under a contract. |
| **Doctrine** | Fraudulent‑inducement: material misrepresentation leading to a victim’s surrender of money/property. |
| **Requirement** | Victim’s actual economic loss is *not* a prerequisite. |
| **Outcome** | Supreme Court upheld the application of § 1348/§ 1349 under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. |

This precedent directly informs cases involving misleading securities marketing, such as the indictment against the social‑media “pump‑and‑dump” operators in *United States v. Constantinescu*.

## Application of Each Theory to the Indictment Allegations

### Right‑to‑Control Theory

The indictment alleges that defendants:

1. Purchased securities and then **"pumped"** their prices by disseminating false posts;
2. Induced followers to buy those securities through misleading information;
3. Subsequently “dumped” the securities for profit.

Under a right‑to‑control lens, one might argue that the prosecution is claiming a deprivation of the followers’ **intention to control their financial decisions**—that is, the followers were misled about the securities’ potential, leading them to make suboptimal choices. However, the indictment does *not* assert that the followers were deprived of the right to **buy or hold these securities**; rather, it alleges that they were compelled to purchase them under false pretenses. The right‑to‑control theory requires that the victim be deprived, at least at the point of potential decision, of the *right* to control *property*. In this case, the followers were never denied the right to trade; they voluntarily chose to “control” their holdings by buying the securities. Consequently, the right‑to‑control theory is misplaced here. The prosecution’s emphasis is on *deception that leads to the transfer of money or property*, not on a deprivation of informational control alone.

### Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory

The fraudulent‑inducement theory aligns neatly with the indictment’s allegations:

- The defendants made **material misrepresentations** (“post[ed] false and misleading information about the security”) that induced investors to purchase the securities.
- The followers’ purchase involved the *transfer of money* (investment capital) and the *handing over of property* (securities) to the defendants’ corrupt scheme.
- The defendants’ objective was to acquire money and profit from the artificially inflated price, as the indictment explicitly states that the scheme generated a $114 million profit.

Because the statute’s fraudulent‑inducement requirement is satisfied by a *deceiving conduct that results in the victim handing over money or property*, the indictment does not need to prove that the followers incurred a net financial loss. Indeed, the Supreme Court in *Kousisis* clarified that a victim’s loss is not a mirror image requirement; the mere fact that the defendant obtained money or property under false pretenses suffices.

## Why the Right‑to‑Control Theory Does Not Fit This Case

The core of the adjudicative analysis in *United States v. Constantinescu* hinges on the distinction highlighted in *Ciminelli* versus *Kousisis*. The indictment’s claims *do not* fall under the right‑to‑control umbrella for several reasons:

1. **No Deprivation of Control**: The followers retained the ability to choose whether or not to buy the securities. The issue is not that they were denied the right to make a trading decision but that they were led *to make a particular decision* based on false information.
2. **No Traditional Property Interest Allegation**: The indictment focuses on the *transfer of money and securities*, i.e., actual property, which is precisely the protected interest under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. 
3. **Legal Precedent**: *Ciminelli* explicitly excludes cases that rely solely on deprivation of valuable economic information. The indictment, meanwhile, centers on *deception that results in the transfer of money and property*, the very ground that *Kousisis* affirms as within the reach of § 1348/§ 1349.

Because the indictment’s allegations center on inducing followers to hand over money through false statements, it remains squarely within the scope of the fraudulent‑inducement theory. The court’s reversal of the district court’s dismissal confirms this interpretation, underscoring that the prosecution need not prove a victim’s net loss to satisfy the fraud element. The defendants’ intent to profit, their misrepresentations, and the resulting transfer of money and securities together satisfy the fraudulent‑inducement standard.

By distinguishing right‑to‑control and fraudulent‑inducement theories and applying them to the factual matrix of the *Constantinescu* indictment, students can appreciate how subtle differences in statutory construction and Supreme Court guidance shape the outcome of federal fraud prosecutions.
