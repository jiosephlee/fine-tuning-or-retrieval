Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How do courts apply mail and wire fraud doctrines to securities fraud?
Question:
The opinion states that §1348 borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes and often relies on those cases. What specific elements from the mail and wire fraud cases are used to analyze §1348 violations, and why is this approach appropriate for securities fraud?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* squarely addresses how the court treats the mail‑ and wire‑fraud doctrines when analyzing §1348 (the securities‑fraud statute). The court explicitly adopts the same elements the federal fraud statutes use for mail and wire fraud and then explains why that approach fits the “pump‑and‑dump” scheme at issue.

---

### 1.  The key elements borrowed from mail and wire fraud

| Element | Mail / Wire Fraud (as adopted by the court) | How it is applied to §1348 in this case |
|---------|--------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **“Scheme to defraud”** | A material falsehood or misrepresentation intended to deceive and to obtain money or property. | The indictment alleges that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security … so that other investors were *induced* to purchase the security” (Id. at ¶ 13). The court treats this as a material misrepresentation *intended to deceive* and *to obtain money or property* (the followers’ investment dollars). |
| **“Intent to defraud”** | The defendant must intend **to deceive** and **to cause harm**—or, under the Supreme Court’s recent reasoning, merely to “obtain” money or property, even if no net loss results. | The indictment claims that defendants “depdfreted … to profit” (Id. at ¶ 13–14). The court treats that as intent: the defendants’ objective was to “obtain” followers’ money through deceptive posts. The court points to *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* to note that a victim’s loss of money or property, not a loss of information, creates a **protection** under the fraud statutes. |
| **Fraudulent‑inducement theory** | The proven concept that a defendant uses a false statement to induce a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property, regardless of whether actual loss ensues. | The court describes that the indictment does not rely on a “right‑to‑control” theory (which would be invalid under *Ciminelli*). Instead, it relies on the fraudulent‑inducement theory: defendants “tricked” their followers into buying securities, thereby “obtain[ing] the victim’s money or property.” This theory is expressly permissible under §1348. |

---

### 2.  Why this approach is appropriate for securities fraud

1. **Same protective purpose** – the fraud statutes protect *property interests* (money or securities). The accidental “loss” of economically valuable information alone is insufficient (*Ciminelli*). By applying the fraudulent‑inducement framework, the court ensures the statute only applies when a *property interest* is harmed.

2. **Preserves the “deception” element** – the fraudulent‑inducement theory requires a **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive. Securities fraud is inherently about *market‑efficiency distortion* through deception, so the mail‑ and wire‑fraud framework naturally captures that element.

3. **Ensures a single‑step analysis** – the fraud statutes are “all‑or‑nothing” (no mens rea or motor‑vehicle‑style “intent to hurt”). Using the mail‑/wire‑fraud elements means the court can quickly decide whether the indictment has “each prima facie element” (*U.S. v. Rafoi*). That satisfies the Sixth‑District‑Court requirement that indictments enumerate every material element of the charged offense.

4. **Consistent with Supreme Court precedent** – *Kousisis* confirmed that “fraudulent‐inducement” governs recovery under §1348, and *Ciminelli* clarified the boundary between a mere loss of information and a loss of property. By aligning §1348 with the same doctrines the Court has applied to §1341 and §1343, the court maintains doctrinal consistency across the federal fraud suite.

---

### Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* holds that §1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and relies on those same doctrines—the “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” elements under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Applying this framework is proper because it preserves the statute’s focus on protected property interests, ensures the requisite deception is demonstrated, and aligns the securities‑fraud analysis with the long‑standing, well‑established Supreme Court line in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*.
