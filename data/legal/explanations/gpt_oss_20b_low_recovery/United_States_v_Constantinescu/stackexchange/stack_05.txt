Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the 'right‑to‑control' theory and why was it rejected in Ciminelli?
Question:
I find the discussion of the right‑to‑control theory confusing. The court says Ciminelli attempted to prove wire fraud by showing the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions. Why is that theory considered a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory, and why was it struck down as lacking a traditional property interest?
Answer:
**Answer**

The *right‑to‑control* theory is a way to interpret the federal fraud statutes that was advanced in *Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306 (2023)*.  Under that theory the “property” that the statutes protect is the victim’s **right to control** his or her own economic affairs—specifically, the right to use valuable economic information to make discretionary decisions about purchases or investments.  The government would prove fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive the victim of that information, thereby leaving the victim vulnerable to befuddlement or misallocation of resources.

In *Ciminelli* the Supreme Court rejected that theory because the statutes protect only **traditional property interests** (money or physical property).  The Court explained that “the right to valuable economic information … is not a traditional property interest” and therefore cannot be the basis for a fraud conviction:

> “Because the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, … the right‑to‑control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.”  
> *Ciminelli*, 598 U.S. at 316.

The *Ciminelli* indictment did not attempt to allege fraud over the mere deprivation of potentially useful information; it instead alleged a classic *fraudulent‑inducement* scheme in which the defendant induced the victim to part with money or property.  In that case the victim’s loss was concrete—money or securities—rather than a loss of informational advantage.

In the *Constantinescu* opinion the court made it clear why *Ciminelli* is not sub‑sidiary to the present case:

1. **Different theory** – The indictment relies on the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory, not the *right‑to‑control* theory.  The latter is expressly rejected in *Ciminelli*; the former, which involves actually taking money or property from a victim through deception, is upheld.

2. **Protected property interest** – The indictment alleges that defendants caused followers to “purchase securities … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price.”  The victims therefore lost money or property, a bona‑fide protection under the statutes.

3. **No requirement of harm** – Even if the victim’s net monetary loss is not proven, *Kousisis* (the companion case to *Ciminelli*) held that the fraud statutes do not require an economic loss for a conviction: “a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property’… regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.”  *(Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392).  Thus the alleged intent to profit does not defeat the indictment.

Consequently, *Ciminelli* was struck down because it dealt with a loss of information—an *unprotected* interest—while *Constantinescu* built its claim on the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory that secures a traditional property interest.  The Court therefore rejected the right‑to‑control argument and affirmed that the indictment properly alleges both a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud.
