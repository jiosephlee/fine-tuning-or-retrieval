Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Does the Supreme Court’s decision in Kousisis change how we interpret 18 U.S.C. §1348 for future cases?
Question:
The opinion hinges on the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis. If a defendant’s misrepresentation results in the victim receiving something of equal value, does §1348 still apply? How might this affect future prosecutions where the victim is a more sophisticated investor or an institutional client?
Answer:
**Answer**

No; the *Kousisis* ruling does **not** change the basic test for §1348, but it does clarify that a defendant need not cause a *net* economic loss to the victim for a fraud conviction. In *Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit applied both Supreme Court cases to hold that the indictment was sufficient because it alleged a fraudulent‑inducement scheme and an intent to obtain the victim’s money. The analysis can be summarized as follows:

| Issue | Ciminelli (2023) | Kousisis (2025) | Constantinescu | Practical Take‑away |
|-------|-----------------|-----------------|----------------|---------------------|
| _What purchase defrauds?_| Right‑to‑control theory – deprivation of potentially valuable economic information | Fraudulent‑inducement theory – deception that causes the victim to part with money or property | Same as Kousisis; the defendants induced followers to buy securities | A §1348 violation requires a *material misrepresentation that induces the victim to give up money or property*, not merely the handing over of securities of equal value. |
| _Need for actual loss?_| Not necessary under fraudulent‑inducement; Ciminelli rejected the right‑to‑control theory on the basis that information is not a protected property interest | Explicitly rejected the “mirror‑image” requirement: one can be liable even when the victim receives something of equal value | The Court stressed that “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”, regardless of net loss | In future cases, prosecutors can focus on the act of inducement; a sophisticated investor receiving a stock at inflated price still leaves the victim’s money “deprived” if that money was paid to the defendant. |
| _Application to sophisticated/institutional victims?| Not addressed | Recognizes that “the advantage of fraud is relatively independent of the victim’s sophistication” | The Court treats the followers as ordinary victims; its reasoning extends to sophisticated investors or institutions because the statutory language is broad. | Prosecutors can target institutional clients, but must show that the misrepresentation was *material* to the contract and that it caused the client to part with money or property. |

### How it affects future prosecutions

1. **Sufficient to allege a scheme** – The indictment must state that the defendant made a material misrepresentation *with the goal of inducing the victim to give up money or property*. Whether the victim ends up holding the same securities is irrelevant, as long as the victim paid money.

2. **No net loss requirement** – The victim’s receiving market‑value securities does **not** defeat liability. The important factor is that the victim’s **money** (or other property) was transferred to the defendant. This widens the scope of §1348 to include cases where the victim is a sophisticated investor or an institution, provided the misrepresentation was material and the victim’s money was obtained fraudulently.

3. **Defendant’s intent** – The Supreme Court reinforced that the defendant must intend to obtain the victim’s money or property, not necessarily intend to cause any loss. Thus, the prosecution can rely on an allegation that the defendant “intended to profit” from the scheme.

4. **Sophisticated vs. ordinary victims** – The *Constantinescu* Court explicitly treated the followers (who may be ordinary or sophisticated) alike. The same logic applies to large institutional clients; §1348 protects them if the misrepresentation is material to the contract and they part with money or property.

### Bottom line for a law student

- **Do not argue** that the victim’s receipt of securities of equal value defeats §1348.  
- **Focus** on establishing (1) a material misrepresentation, (2) intent to defraud, and (3) the victim’s deprivation of money or property.  
- **Apply** the *Kousisis* “no mirror‑image” rule when arguing that the victim’s economic interest was not harmed net‑wise; what matters is that the victim’s money was obtained fraudulently.

Thus, *Kousisis* did not alter the statutory definition of “money or property” but clarified that inducing a victim to give up money— even if they later end up with securities of equal value—can still constitute §1348 fraud. This broadening will make future prosecutions against sophisticated or institutional investors more straightforward, provided the prosecution can meet the Material Misrepresentation + Intent + Deprivation elements.
