Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### Is the doctrine of 'involuntary discovery' applicable when a warrant is obtained after a voluntary entry?
Question:
The court noted that the Must also dismissed the government's argument that inevitable discovery would justify the initial entry. If the officers had entered without consent but found the weapon and then got a warrant, would that be a classic 'involuntary discovery' scenario? How does that differ from the scenario in this case?
Answer:
**Answer**

No.  The “involuntary‑discovery” doctrine would not apply in this case because the police entered the home *with* the wife’s consent and because the evidence discovered thereafter was obtained after an *obtained* warrant, not after an entry made solely on the basis of discovery.  

---

### The doctrine in the Ninth‑and‑Thirteenth‑Circles

An *involuntary discovery* exception arises only when officers *enter without consent* but *discover evidence* that would have been found through a warrant that is later secured.  The Supreme Court made clear that the exception is limited to “narrative” discovery that is *inevitable* (see *Riley v. California*, *United States v. Johnson*).  The doctrine is applied only when the police had no lawful authority to enter the premises and the evidence discovered would have been found “by way of an inevitability” had the officers obtained a warrant first.  

In *United States v. Coleman*, the officers **did** have the statutory and common‑law authority to enter the home in the first place:  

> “The district court found that this exchange supplied Officer Kizer with the consent necessary to enter the home and that he and his colleagues did not exceed the scope of Lisa’s consent once inside.” (para. 2)

Because the entry was **already** lawful, the police had no need to rely on the “involuntary discovery” exception.  The “inevitable‑discovery” exception serves no purpose when a valid consent was obtained.  It applies only when the police had no authority to enter at all.

---

### What would have mattered if entry had been unlawful?

If the officers had forcibly entered without consent and had found the weapon, the court would then examine whether they could later justify that entry under the “involuntary discovery” exception.  That analysis would hinge on whether the evidence found (the firearm) would have been discovered inevitably, had the police obtained a warrant first (i.e., without having to enter).  The court in *Coleman* does not touch on that hypothetical because it was irrelevant: the entry was lawful to begin with.

---

### How the *Coleman* facts differ

1. **Consent was granted.**  
   The court rejected any claim that Lisa denied consent.  The discussion (para. 10‑12) shows that she “did not in any sense acquiesce under duress” and “her refusal was withdrawn after a brief request.”  The isolated use of the word “need” was dismissed as non‑coercive (par. 13‑15).

2. **Warrant was obtained *after* entry.**  
   The warrant was secured following the opening of the home (“Based on information gained following their entry … the officers sought and secured a warrant”).  It was not an *inevitable* discovery that would have gotten them the firearms *without* entering.

3. **No implied “absence of consent” clause.**  
   §4.5(4) of the Ifus model law states that an exception to the warrant requirement may be invoked *only* when “no consent is given or when any consent is withdrawn by the occupant.”  Here, consent was both *given* and *withdrawn* after it was offered, but the withdrawal occurred *after* the officers had entered.  The court specifically rejected the government’s suggestion that “involuntary discovery” would justify the initial entry (para. 21).

Thus the legal inference is that the “involuntary discovery” doctrine was inapplicable because the officers’ initial entry was not made “without consent.”  Had the officers entered without consent, found the weapon, and then obtained a warrant to search the home, *Coleman* would have turned to the involuntary‑discovery exception.  That scenario is not presented in the record, so the court rightly dismissed the government’s argument that the doctrine applied.

---

### Bottom line for a law student

* **Entry with consent → no need for involuntary‑discovery.**  
* **Entry without consent → can invoke the doctrine only if the evidence would otherwise be inevitably discovered.**  
* In *United States v. Coleman*, the entry was lawful, so the doctrine was not relevant and the court concluded that the firearms were lawfully obtained once the warrant was issued.
