Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### What constitutes voluntary consent when the subject initially refuses and later allows police entry?
Question:
I’m confused about how a police officer can obtain valid consent after the suspect says ‘no’ the first time. The opinion says the second request was uncoerced because the subject could deny again. In my understanding, once a person consents, the police can’t just ask again. Did the court treat the second assent as a new, independent consent? What distinguishes a new offer of consent from a coerced one here?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The court treated the second “yes” as a *new, independent* consent.  Because Lisa was free to deny the second request, and because the officer’s phrasing did not convey coercion, the consent was deemed voluntary and distinct from the first refusal.  A new offer is never coerced when the person still has the option to say “no” and when the officer does not use force, threats, or subtle pressure beyond the normal urgency of a domestic‑violence call.

---

### 1.  What the opinion says about the two exchanges

* **First request** – Officer Kizer asked “Are we going to get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like …?” – Lisa said, “I’d prefer you not.”  
  The court notes that Lisa “had the ability to deny the request,” and that she *did* deny it.

* **Second request** – “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure the kids are okay.” – Lisa replied, “Okay.”  
  The court stresses that the second request was “unaccompanied by any coercion” and was made after Lisa had already had her chance to refuse again.

The court therefore concludes that the consent “was voluntary” and that the officer’s use of the word “need” did not make the request coercive.

### 2.  Legal standard applied

The Seventh Circuit, following **Bumper v. North Carolina** and reiterated in **United States v. Han**, holds that *voluntary consent* is judged by a *totality‑of‑circumstances* inquiry.  Factors include:

1. the person’s age, intelligence, and education;  
2. whether he was advised of his constitutional rights;  
3. how long he was detained before consenting;  
4. whether the consent was immediate or prompted by repeated requests;  
5. whether any physical coercion was used; and  
6. whether the individual was in police custody.

Only *coercion*—physical, verbal, or psychological—throws off the voluntariness bar.

### 3.  Why the second consent is considered *new* and *voluntary*

| Issue | Requirement | Opinion’s finding |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Right to deny again** | The officer must give the subject an opportunity to refuse each time. | The opinion states Lisa “had the ability to deny consent to entry, as she did just that in response to Officer Kizer’s initial request.” (para. B) |
| **Absence of coercion** | No force, threat, or undue pressure. | “At no point did Officer Kizer exert coercion.” (para. B) |
| **Timing & context** | The request must be made after any prior refusal, and the officer cannot press the subject to agree. | “The second request being unaccompanied by any coercion” (para. B). |
| **Voluntariness assessment** | Totality of circumstances. | The court found no clear error in its finding of voluntary consent (para. B). |

Because Lisa still possessed the *right* to refuse the second request, her affirmative answer was *independent* of the first refusal.  The court treats each request as a distinct opportunity for consent; consent cannot be “carried over” from a prior refusal unless the officer continues to pressure the person.  The absence of any such pressure renders the second assent a *new* voluntary consent.

### 4.  Distinction from a coerced assent

A coerced assent would involve:

* **Physical restraint** (e.g., holding you down) or **threats** (e.g., “If you don’t let us in, I’ll take your kid”).  
* **Repetitive or high‑pressure arguments**, especially with a sense of “force” implied by the wording.  
* Lack of a clear *choice* to refuse (e.g., the officer tells you that refusing will lead to arrest or police will break down the door).

Here, Lisa’s refusal was clearly voiced, the officer’s tone was calm and concerned, and no threat or pressure was made.  The officer’s use of the word “need” was evaluated in light of the totality of circumstances and found *insufficient* to constitute coercion (contra **United States v. Hicks**, 539 F.3d 566, 570‑71, 2008).

---

### Bottom line for the student

1. **Consent is “freely and voluntarily”** under the federal standard.  
2. A *new* consent can be obtained *after* a refusal as long as the subject still retains the right to say “no” and the officer does not add pressure or threats.  
3. In this case, the second “okay” was treated as a *new*, independent voluntary consent because Lisa could have denied it again, and no coercion occurred.  
4. The court’s reliance on the *totality‑of‑circumstances* test, the absence of any coercive language or behavior, and the clear opportunity for Lisa to refuse again validates the second consent.  

Thus, the court correctly distinguished the new offer from any potential coercion.
