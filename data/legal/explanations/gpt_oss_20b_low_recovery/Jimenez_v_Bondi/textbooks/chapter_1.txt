Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 1: # 1. Case Architecture: Procedural Background and Legal Context

The case of **Jimenez v. Bondi** traces a classic trajectory through the U.S. immigration adjudicatory system, moving from the initial administrative hearing before an immigration judge (IJ) to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and ultimately to the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals under the statutory authority of § 1252. Understanding this procedural ladder and the statutory pillars that underlie it is essential for interpreting the court’s substantive findings.  

## Identity and Status of the Petitioners

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons are citizens of Colombia who entered the United States in December 2022 without a formal admission or parole proceeding. They remain “prisoners of international law” under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA): they were never admitted with a visa, yet they have been in the United States in a manner that makes them removable. Their status as non‑citizens without lawful immigration status sets the stage for the subsequent applications for travel‑and‑asylum relief.  

## Timeline of Filings: NTA, IJ Denial, BIA Affiliation

1. **Notice to Appear (NTA)** – Three months after arrival, DHS served the petitioners with individual NDAs charging each as removable under the INA. The NDAs constitute the legal mechanism by which DHS initiates removal proceedings against a non‑admitted non‑citizen.  
2. **Petition for Asylum, Withholding of Removal, and CAT Protection** – The petitioners’ attorneys filed applications for asylum and withholding of removal, together with a derivative claim for their children. A second application sought protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT).  
3. **Immigration Judge (IJ) Decision** – The IJ denied all requests. The denial was based on the finding that the petitioners could not establish (a) past persecution, (b) a nexus to a protected ground, (c) internal relocation, or (d) the Colombian government’s unwillingness or inability to protect them.  
4. **BIA Appeal** – The petitioners appealed to the BIA. A single BIA member affirmed the IJ’s denial, concluding that the record contained no substantial evidence of the necessary asylum elements. The BIA also rejected the petitioners’ due‑process claim that the IJ violated their right to counsel.  

## Jurisdictional Ground: 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1) and (b)(9)

The Ninth Circuit’s review of the case rests upon statutory authority. Section 1252(a)(1) authorizes the courts to review final removal orders, which may include a consolidated review of asylum claims. Section 1252(b)(9) specifically authorizes appellate review of a final order of removal that consolidates immigration‑judge decisions with BIA orders. The Tenth Circuit, following the precedent set in **Singh v. Bondi**, applies a *substantial‑evidence* standard to the BIA’s findings of fact and a *preponderance of the evidence* standard to the BIA’s conclusions of law when the matter is on the appellate bar. The court’s jurisdiction, therefore, requires that the petitioners had a final order of removal that incorporated the denial of asylum; no independent removal order had been issued at the time of appeal.

## Scope of the Appeal and the Grounds Petitioners Successfully Contested (Asylum/Withholding Only)

The petitioners’ appeal revolved exclusively around the BIA’s denial of **asylum** and **withholding of removal**. They did not challenge the BIA’s denial of CAT protection, nor did they raise new issues concerning the application of the Convention itself. Since the docket reflects only the asylum and withholding claims, the Tenth Circuit’s review is confined to:

1. Whether the petitioners proved *past persecution* on the statutory basis of **protected ground** (political opinion or particular social group).  
2. Whether the petitioner’s claims met the *well‑founded fear* standard required for both asylum and withholding.  
3. Whether the “unwilling or unable” requisite of the *government‑protection* analysis was satisfied.  

The court’s decision therefore does not address the CAT claim, leaving that matter at an outside adjudicative level (unless the petitioner were to file a separate petition for CAT under § 1158(b)(2)).

## Relevant Statutory Definitions: Refugee, Protection Under the Convention Against Torture

For the appellate court to evaluate the asylum claim, it revisits the statutory definitions that form the foundation of the asylum regime:

- **Refugee (INA § 1101(a)(42)(A))** – A person who is outside his or her country of nationality and is unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a **well‑founded fear of persecution** on account of *race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion*. The definition obliges the claimant to demonstrate *past persecution* that *establishes a rebuttable presumption* of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.

- **Convention Against Torture (CAT)** – Under INA § 1158(b)(2), protection from serious or prolonged torture (or cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment) is granted to any person who meets the definition of “country of particular concern” and has *substantial evidence* that the government is unwilling or unable to prevent or respond to such torture. Because the petitioners did not appeal the BIA’s denial of CAT protection, that statutory construct remains unexamined here.

By anchoring the procedural narrative in these statutory anchors, the court situates its subsequent legal analysis — from the standard for review to the evidentiary thresholds — within a framework that is both precise and grounded in the INA’s language. This clarity is indispensable for a law student navigating the complex interplay between procedural posture and substantive asylum law.
