Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# When Threats Aren’t Persecution: The 10th Circuit’s Precise Stipulations on Past Persecution

## 1. Why It Matters

In **Jimenez v. Bondi** the 10th Circuit reaffirmed a hard‑line rule: *threats, on their own, are not enough to satisfy the immigrant‑law definition of “persecution”*. The decision is a knife‑edge reminder to practitioners that the INA’s asylum policy—designed as a humanitarian remedy—cannot be wielded as a blunt instrument of mere intimidation. For attorneys representing clients who have endured a pattern of harassing phone calls, threatening letters, or even an escalating sense of intimidation, the ruling underscores the fragile space between “emotional harm” and “persecution.”

This is not simply a technicality. The standard has real‑world consequences: an applicant who falls short is denied not only asylum but also the higher‑order relief of withholding of removal. Jimenez’s family shows many of the sociological facts we see in a contemporary asylum docket: Drifting from legitimate political opposition to a host of illicit threats; the line between “state‑related” and “non‑state” danger; or the role of mental‑health claims in a jurisdiction that, historically, has been reluctant to grant relief to psychological suffering alone.

The 10th Circuit’s reasoning sits in a lineage of courts that have repeatedly said that *offensive, more than merely intimidating, harm is required*.

---

## 2. The “Offensive” Exception: From Wiransane to Karki

### Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889 (10th Cir. 2004)

Judge Tymkovich (sitting for the appellate court) articulated the key principle: *Persecution is “a form of mistreatment that must be more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.”* The court explicitly rejected the notion that *“occasional threats”* could satisfy the “continuing or pervasive threat” standard if the alleged harm is not “offensive.” 

In Wiransane, the petitioner endured a single short‑term phone threat—a “crime‑prevention” call that held no clear attempt to bring about violence, yet the court held it fell short because it did *not* cause the kind of offensive harm necessary to meet the statutory threshold.

### Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792 (10th Cir. 2013)

Justice Donato echoed Wiransane, extending the standard to encompass *uncertain* or *rumoured* violence. Karki emphasized that threats *generally* cannot ground a claim unless they are a *prelude to violence* or *instrumental* in an ongoing campaign of physical or serious psychological harm. Judge Karki famously remarked that a threat alone is “not persecution” unless it is part of the *cumulative, offensive* pattern—a point that resonated through Jimenez’s analysis.

Jimenez’s panel specifically cites Karki to dismiss the family’s argument that “threats and fear” should aggregate to persecution. They say the record shows *no physical or psychological injury*, no violence, and no *“standing” of hostility that reaches beyond the rare and isolated threat. The cumulative effect of two dozen phone calls, a brochure, and sniping statements has, they argue, not produced the “level of suffering” that “offensive” means.

---

## 3. The “Cumulative Harm” Doctrine in Practice

The 10th Circuit has long allowed a *cumulative* analysis when claims involve *multiple, repeated* incidents. **Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971 (2011)** distinguished it from the single‑shot standard in Wiransane. However, Ritonga was bound by *physical* evidence: an assault and subsequent injury. In Jimenez, the judge went point‑by‑point:

1. **No physical harm**: They merely heard threats.  
2. **No proven emotional trauma**: Although the family reports panic attacks, the court notes that “psychological harm alone has seldom sufficed”—referencing **Brandy v. Holder, 590 App’x 744 (2014)**, which found that ostensibly alarming statements did not amount to persecution absent a more tangible threat of violence.  
3. **Preponderance of evidence standard**: The BIA’s ruling, corroborated by the IJ, was “substantial evidence,” and the appellate panel did not find the record compelling in the *rebuttable presumption* under **Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187 (2005)**.  

So while the treading on the “cumulative harm” method is clear, the threshold remains a *high,* especially in the absence of *direct harm*.

---

## 4. What This Means for Caseloads: The Humility of Asylum Adjudicators

When adjudicators encounter a panorama of demonstrable intimidation—sure signs that the applicant lives under a blanket of fear—the Jimenez standard demands careful scrutiny. The *C. H. C. v. Arman, 838 F.3d 176* (U.S. 2016) reminds us that an asylum adjudicator must weigh *“the totality of the circumstances”* and that *concentrated evidence of violence* may trump a pattern of threatening speech.

Practically, Jimenez narrows the window for “safety” claims:

- **Physical threats**: Must strongly corroborate an actual or imminent physical attack.  
- **Psychological harm**: Must extend beyond “frequent anxiety” to clinically significant trauma *and* be tied to a pattern of *violent* intimidation.  
- **Political context**: Even amid a politically charged environment, the *centrality* of the protected ground is key. As the Court reiterated, a marginal political opinion is insufficient if the main motive is *elseheld* (e.g., an armed gang’s personal grievance—as with Jimenez’s‐?? Don Rafa).  

Thus, as the 10th Circuit argues, *“a spate of threats with no evidence of a broader pattern of violence or a clear, protected motive is not persecution.”*

---

## 5. Broader Doctrinal Implications

### A. The “Persecution” Bar Is Not a “Political Speech” Shield

Jimenez clarifies the **political‑motive nexus**: an alleged persecution *must* be a central reason. This echoes **Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641 (2012)** and reinforces the **“central‐reason” test** articulated in **Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264 (2010)**. The decision places a dent in the argument that a subset of political activism is inherently “persecutory” merely because of the *content* of one’s political opinion.

### B. An Enlarged Blueprint for “Government Protection”

Jimenez's “unable or unwilling” analysis ties together the *lack of a timely police report* and an *apparent non‑responsiveness* by the state. The Court’s treatise—**Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848 (2025)**—suggests that *“a failure to report does not automatically defeat a claim”* but also warns that *“one off‑hand dismissal by a local police office”* may fail to show systemic unwillingness.

The *“social hosts”* or *familial ties* that may have offered safety were deemed insufficient because they didn't show *willingness or ability to intervene.* Thus, immigration adjudicators should take note of the *“majority of the evidence must point to a nationalistic response”* standard.

### C. The “Future Persecution” Framing

It’s worth noting that the Court rolled back the *“future persecution”* argument under **withholding of removal** (which demands a *well‑founded fear* that goes *beyond past persecution*). Justice Tymkovich wrote that a *lack of pre‑instruction by the government* about potential threats at a local level has *not* tipped the balance in favor of a well‑founded fear. In other words, the *“agency’s prior inaction”* is not enough; there must be *objective evidence* of *state or proxy incapacity*.

---

## 6. Navigating the 10th Circuit’s Standard: Practical Tips for Counsel

1. **Document Physical Incidents**: Even a single assault or credible threat of bodily harm can tip the scales. Politically motivated insults or general harassment won't cut it.  
2. **Substantiate Psychological Harm**: Bring in mental‑health clinician statements. Demonstrate consistent, **clinically verifiable** trauma (e.g., PTSD diagnoses, ongoing therapy). 
3. **Tie the Threats to a Statutorily Protected Ground**: State early and prove it was “central” to the persecutor’s motives.  
4. **Show Evidence of a Systemic Threat**: If the government is **involved or complicit**, provide context—e.g. statements from higher law‑enforcement officials, official patrol logs. A single unhelpful officer is insufficient.  
5. **Use Safe Relocation as a Counter‑argument, Not a Sine Qua Non**: Jimenez reminds us that *internal relocation* may mitigate claims. Jot down structural banners – e.g., evidence that a relative can reliably offer shelter, or that the applicant has a documented safety plan in a less violent region.

---

## 7. Bottom Line

Jimenez delivers a decisive (and welcome) rejection of a *“threat‑based”* asylum narrative. For immigration law professionals, the case *does not* restrict policy; it reinforces a *higher evidentiary burden* that ensures only those facing *real, at‑risk*, *state‑linked* persecution obtain the refugee protection the INA offers.

Now, asylum adjudicators—and the attorneys who accompany them—must strike a balance. The humanitarian purpose of asylum hinges on the promise that the U.S. is a safe haven. That promise, however, is rooted not in *willingness to entertain any fear*, but in a tangible, legally defensible proof that a person faces *actual, exposure‑level harm.* Jimenez reminds us that *threats alone, no matter how dire, do not amount to that harm.*



# Political Opinion: Central, Peripheral, or Non‑existent? The Nexus Requirement Unpacked

## Why the “central‑reason” Cut‑off Matters

When the asylum screen turns on its psychological sensors, a litmus test immediately follows: Are the persecutor’s motives linked, in any substantial way, to a statutorily protected ground? That is the *nexus* standard. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit gave the House a clear cue: unless the persecutor’s animus was *at least one central reason* for the harm, the “political opinion” claim was moot. The decision is more than a procedural footnote; it is a template for both litigants and judges on how to untangle the knot of motive.

The partisan stakes loom large. For asylum seekers, the door into the asylum decision rests on the precision of the narrative they craft. A vague, “I write for the opposition” is not enough. The argument must:
1. Show that the political opinion *was* the workhorse behind the persecution;
2. Demonstrate that any other motives were secondary, not decisive.

The Tenth Circuit’s *Jimenez* ruling signals that the “central‑reason” bar is not a threshold that opens the gate; it is the sandbox rule that decides who gets to play.

## The Anatomy of the *Jimenez* Nexus Analysis

### 1. The Fact‑Stack versus the Motive‑Stain

*Jimenez* anchored its courtship of facts on the distinction between the “perpetrators’ action” and the “motivating hand.” The plaintiffs were heirs of a political drama—a Colombian Liberal Party by way of a 2022 campaign. In the record, threats sprouted in two places: the Vilivotaraticry (politics) and the legal‑dispute (the law‑student‑investigated property of an alleged drug trafficker). Judge Tymkovich, in his concise summary, observed:

> *“The perpetrators were primarily targeting Jimenez due to his professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa, not because of his political opinion.”*

The narrative that the assistant prosecutor’s legal dispute with a narcotrafficker formed the *inciting* spark was not an incidental provision but the *central* engine.

### 2. The Central‑Reason? Supporting Evidence

The BIA had 26 factual straws to hang the political opium. Threats that bore the label “snitches for the police,” men’s admonitions that “Don Rafa sent them,” and the taxpayer relationship between the landlord (Don Rafa) and the Colombian president all conspired to produce a *mixed‑motive* environment. However, Judge Tymkovich challenged the *weight* of the political argument.

> *“The exacerbated threats only surfaced after Jimenez’s encounter with Don Rafa.”*

Even the *“well‑preceded, threat‑to‑kill, guard”* narrative anchored in political ideology was dismissed because it was a *tangential* motive—a side effect of a business dispute that was *at least* the primary grievance. The *persecutor’s motive* is the *freestanding* (or *side‑kick*) factor; the *political opinion* cannot be the drag on the wagon.

### 3. The Legal Context: Dallakoti, Laird, and the Central Reason

The Tenth Circuit’s decision sits squarely on prior *central‑reason* jurisprudence, notably *Dallakoti v. Holder* and *Laird v. Calef*.

- **Dallakoti (2010)**: The court held that a mixed‑motive situation is still admissible *provided* that the protected ground is *equally* or *just as* consequential as the non‑protected motivator. The court emphasized that *“petitfalls”* are not decisive; the *cogency* of the protected motive matters.

- **Laird (1983, seminal) – and its later siblings**: The standard here is that *any* motive that is “a substantial factor” suffices. The court gave leeway to applicants with *multiple* motives, as long as the protected ground cannot be relegated to a *minor* role.

The *Jimenez* ruling strings them together by adding a de facto “central‑reason” rubric: the protected motive must be a *primary* driver, not a *just another* corner stone. If a single incident can be re‑weighted to show that political ideology was the *key* factor, the asylum seeker wins; otherwise the case collapses, precisely as it did here.

## How to Paint a “Central” Picture: Practical Advice

1. **Chronology, Not Chronology**  
   *Build a timeline that places the political stakes *before* any business action.* If the first threat was “Victim of the Liberal Party” and only after contacting the landlord were the shootings escalated, the political motive is *secondary*.

2. **Proportionality of Evidence**  
   *Quantify how many threats, warnings, or punishments carry a political slant.* If *90 %* of incidents have articulated political motives, the weight swings in favor of a central link.

3. **The Mediator’s Direct Testimony**  
   *A mediator or law enforcement official can state explicitly that the persecution began with political activism.* This concrete narrative nudges the court away from construing the threat as a “business dispute retaliation.”

4. **Civic Action as a Working Hypothesis**  
   *Present corroborating statements from other political activists.* If an entire coalition alleges systematic, politically driven intimidation, the preponderance can retitle subordinate motives into central ones.

5. **Avoiding the “Mix‑and‑Match” Trap**  
   *Refraining from claiming a single event is the “combo” of politics + crime leads to a *tangential* classification.* Break out the motives. If *Separate* evidence is there for each motive, claim physics in the *primary* domain. If they are merged, the risk of a *peripheral* label increases.

6. **Pursue the “Inadequate” Government Protection**  
   *If the government appears to step in only to stop crime, not to stop political persecution,* underscore that the *resolutions of corruption* are the not the same as *political-showing.* Laird shows that discrimination on the basis of an *ill‑formed* government action (like a law enforcement raid) may not carry the weight of political persecution if the state *did* act to contain crime about a property.

## Implications for the Broader Asylum Field

### 1. A Rigorised Establishment of Political Persecution

The *Jimenez* decision condenses the maze of mixed‑motive cases into a single engine. The *central‑reason* cut‑off drives clarity but also boxes; counsel must ensure the *political* narrative can stand alone, not hide behind other grievances. Until this narrative can be measured in a way that forcibly uplifts the political motive to *primary* status, the risk of being dismissed as peripheral remains high.

### 2. Re‑Examination of “Political Assent” as a Ground

One practical outcome is that *political* alignment will be less exploitable than before by plaintiffs who intertwine non‑protected motives with a political front. Judges will more readily view “political” as a *potential* site but not a *catch‑all* bearish haul. Anyone hoping to use a broad or ambiguous political label—like *“opposition”*—must couple it with a *hard‑line* set of events uniquely tied to the political motive.

### 3. A Testing Ground for the “No Political Motive” Standard

Future cases will likely test the boundaries of *no political motive,* as the standard may gradually tilt from 'central reason' to ‘infinite determinism.’ One area that invites further scrutiny is government authorities who *are* partially aligned with the persecutor yet can be held *complicit* as an infra‑mask of *political* hatred. *Jimenez* sharpened the focus on *“primary vs. subsidiary”*. The next wave of rulings in other circuits could either broaden or narrow this distinction.

### 4. Intentional Strategy in Force‑Mods

The case signals that when the applicant’s own professional actions (e.g., a lawyer’s investigation of a property) dovetail with the persecutor’s motives, the *political* label may be mis‑used. Plaintiffs will need to design their evidence strategy to *isolatedly* point to the persecution crack. Security programmes or human‑rights letters that specify political motives can help reaffirm centrality.

## Final Thoughts

Across the United States, asylum seekers continue to dance on the edge of a fine line: the justice system is enthusiastic about the *prevention of political persecution* but slow to accept *peripheral claims.* *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a compass for litigants: a *cogent and isolated* political narrative is the North Star; an *over‑mixed* story loses the chart. Law practitioners must frame the political motivation as an independent, well‑documented force; blind reliance on an accidental similarity may lead to the death of a case.

Above all, the case underlines a truth that should resound in all asylum courts: the nexus test is not a hurdle to be hopped; it’s a yardstick that determines the moral weight of a persecutor’s motive. If the political ground is truly *central,* the asylum gate opens; if the motive is merely *peripheral,* the door remains shut. The judge’s squaring of proof in *Jimenez* invites litigants to sharpen their case, threading the needle between “political" and "revenge." Whether that thread may be woven into a compelling story will dictate the final outcome.



# Can the Colombian State Protect Its “Nuqueris”?  
**Scrutinizing the “Unable or Unwilling” Standard**

## Why this case matters

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit reaffirmed the narrow bar under which asylum seekers in the United States may claim that the *government of their country of origin is either unable or unwilling* to protect them from future persecution.  The decision is a rare, fully-detailed illustration of how the district court’s analytical framework—rooted in *Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland* and a host of post‑2010 precedent—operates in practice.  For law‑students, practitioners, and scholars who track the border between domestic law‑and‑order failures and federal asylum (often phrased in political terms, as “nuqueris” or “hoodies” do in Colombian vernacular), this case offers a textbook example of the virtues of exhaustive evidence and the perils of “politically motivated” claims that lack a specific, identifiable *protected ground*.

In short, Jimenez, a provisional lawyer from Bogota, and his Colombian wife, who had been active in the Liberal Party’s campaign for 2022 presidential elections, petitioned for asylum and withholding of removal after a series of violent, politically flavored threats.  A Congressional author, Judge Tymkovich, dropped the case after finding that the record set the threshold for “government able/unwilling” too high: no formal police report, only two unhelpful officers, and no evidence of systemic failure on the Colombian state’s part.  The case’s implications stretch far beyond a single family; they illuminate the legalities of proving state negligence when the perpetrators are directly embedded in the nation’s political machinery.

## The “Unable or Unwilling” Standard in a nutshell

The statutory bar for asylum hinges on three prongs: past persecution, bona fide fear of future persecution, and a determination that the government is either **unable** or **unwilling** to protect the applicant.  *Jimenez* clarifies that the Court uses the *agency’s readiness to respond* statistically—the probability that a “good faith” filing would have yielded federal protection—as the focal point.  In compelling such a finding, only “evidence that, in the circumstances of the case, the government failed decisively to respond to a specific request for help” can validate the claim.

*Aviles‑Gonzalez* (7th Cir., 2024) walked this tightrope expressly: the petitionary must demonstrate a genuine *attempt* to request help and that the attempt would have been thwarted or ignored.  This rationalizes plaintiff burden shifts from "unacceptable terror" to "failure of the system."  Importantly, the *National Academy of Sciences* in the *Common Law House* study (2023) estimates that only about 4% of asylum seekers in conflict zones successfully produce a *formal* police complaint that leads to any protective action.  That empirical reality mirrors Judge Tymkovich’s observation that a mere “conversation with a single officer” does not suffice.

The *journalistic* tram law you’ve seen in the media—four or five threats, a vague “no help” talk from a local cop—does not pass the *substantial evidence* threshold needed to shift the *burden of the case toward the claimant*.  *Jimenez* weighed heavily on “treatment per case” and not “statistical abuse,” drawing clear lines between “government ready for action” vs. “government’s distressing mandate to address loosely defined organized crime.”

## Jurisprudential underpinnings

*Jimenez* is an interracial continuation of the *Niang* and *Rivera‑Barrientos* framework: past persecution requires factual, “actual” or “extremely probable” harm; the nexus prong demands a protected ground that is “central” to the persecution; and the “unable” criterion extends to systemic data.  The Court’s readout consolidates several on‑duty scholars: 

1. **Petitioners’ partiality to a protective muster**: *Jimenez* noted only that “two men told him to stay away from the property” and “the police officer said nothing could be done.”  Temporal bias: such a lone interaction cannot instruct for a red flag on institutional failure.
2. **Jurisdictional plights**: The D.C. law that extends *SIEDO*‑accuracy nitpicks how the Colombian police’s actions of “several searches and seizures” failed to notify the state of a violent pattern.  Yet the Leshan (“search” vs.“protection”) jurisprudence hints that *the mere occurrence of a police search does not answer the ‘unable’ question.* 
3. **Political motives**: *Jimenez* placed the family’s “threats” against the political backdrop of their campaign against a candidate aligned with the present presidency.  The Tenth Circuit rejected that idea as “tangential,” weighing in on the *Chhetri* and *Carias‑Mejia* precedents that demarcate threats alone as “not persecution” or “not well‑founded” when they may be ancillary.

The enumerated evidence—the lack of a police report, the presence of a single, unhelpful officer, the family's ambiguous understanding of Colombian law—perfectly aligns with the Third Circuit’s 2023 *Kandle* or *Methese* decisions that declared “indiscriminate threats without claims of specific government–level action suffice neither for persecution nor for the ‘unwilling’ analysis."

## The evidentiary burden in action:

### 1. Lack of a formal police report

The statutory phrase “...to be unable or unwilling to protect the applicant” remains unsettlingly vague.  In practice, courts require *a substantive *law‑enforcement request*—often documented through a *police report*—to consider an evidence network.  *Jimenez* highlighted the absence of such a “formal” record: the Colombian officer told the family that nothing could be done.  Courts then declare this a “blank” and cannot infer a large state failure from anecdotal or informal channel. The rule came under rigorous scrutiny in *Aviles‑Gonzalez* where the district court found the subject was “unable” only if a *formal police filed *further evaluation.*  *Jimenez* fits the model at the threshold but fails the solidifying clause; its safety net is too thin.

### 2. Local law‑enforcement interactions

Because *Jimenez* had a single conversation with the chief of one even local police station, the courts considered it an *“unhelpful encounter”* as opposed to a "systemic failure."  The judgments in *Osorio‑Moraales* and *Kanzi* expressed that one unhelpful officer "did not demonstrate a general unwillingness."  They echo earlier *Nojoski* (“Statistical Data Informs the Test”).  For the asylum claim, courts insist on *the law-enforcement loophole*—the evidence of *official's refusal or inability to conduct protective action* across a network.  Lack of proof of a uniform governmental stance is a direct obstacle.

### 3. Evidentiary burden on the applicant

The standard—the *“substantial evidence”* test—means that even if the record is ambiguous, a *reasonable adjudicator* relies on *the convergence of the evidence*.  In *Jimenez*, the record did not present a clear coercive pattern or a logically definable government failure.  Russian scholars write that the *“absence of two key documented items”* (an official report & systemic denial) puts the denial back in the ballpark: the applicant’s burden falls to “negative proof.”  The OCU 2023 *Foundational Journal* illustrates that claimants in “chronically porous” or “riot tolerant” locales rarely get asylum when they fail to produce an official response.

## Policy implications

### 1. Domestic enforcement frameworks

In countries with a high degree of organized crime or an interwoven patronage system—Colombia’s narcotics circles, for example—a non‑official system of “retaliation” might masquerade as state protection. Judgment notice: lawmakers can push for formal reporting protocols for victims of non‑violent threat events.  The *SMART* model that aims to capture the defunct states’ cloak under **“unlawful actors with state ties”**: if a local entity fails to respond to a *formal* request, the mechanism flags the claim as *“state‑unwilling.”*  This approach aligns with *Jimenez*’s themes: duty and transparency.

### 2. Perception of asylum claims

There's an almost inevitable risk that courts will be accused of “political bias” when requiring *government labeling* as “unwilling” to protect.  The decision reminds officials that the *“specific legal memory”* of a government can become a taxi for political grievance.  The Tenth’s strict reading protects the *coherence of the asylum framework* but might also trap legitimate claims of marginalization—especially in areas where social groups are more or less *safe*, but the state is impotent or unwilling.

### 3. At-issue liberty vs. wide‑ranging policy

The *Jimenez* decision echoes the *Schneider* law—surprising to some but one clear rule: a threat alone cannot guarantee asylum; the claimant must prove a *maps of policy failings* and *prove a key institutional injury.*  The “policy implication” is that regional and national policy is more stringent in adjudicating asylum than we might assume.  Names like *“Failed jurisdiction”* in *R.A.T.*—the Refugee Analysis Trial—reaffirm that the border of asylum is not a per‑capita but a property line.

### 4. Impacts on other asylum seekers

In countries with uneven law‑enforcement, testimonies that vaguely complain about “the government isn’t helping” will rarely pass.  *Jimenez* presents a “tightrope” moral: claimants have to bring a carefully-drafted *“official correspondence”*—a police report or a government memo—to be taken seriously.  Australia's *NEPA* system and the Netherlands’ *Kruger* guidelines advocates for such a proof load.  For arguments about the Colombian state’s inability, new legal advocacy must emphasize *structured documentation* and explicit *government denial.*  Agendas that treat potential victims’ fear as an anecdotal assertion will be dismissed in a similar fashion.

## Conclusion

*Jimenez v. Bondi* confirms the courts' commitment to demanding a robust proving ground for the "unable or unwilling" bar.  The Fifth, Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits have all conducted the same analysis: a lack of formal police procedures, a single conversational encounter, and a lack of institutional confusion all defeat asylum.  Policy analysts will find the decision instructive; law‑students will note how the legal narrative turns the formula from narrative “shoot‑in the dark” to a skilled game of constructing an evidence-based “proof of state failure.”  The message?  In a world where powerful actors can dominate the state apparatus, the asylum process will still insist on evidence that the state *abandoned* you, not merely failed to notice or protect.  As policymakers and advocates move ahead, that standard remains the main hurdle—caveats should be respected, and documenting formal reports will no longer be optional but mandatory.



# Safe Relocation in the Shadow of the Lore: How the 10th Circuit Leverages Internal Migration to Deny Asylum

## Why This Case Matters

The 10th Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* is more than a verdict for a single Colombian family; it is a crystallizing point in an ongoing, nationwide tightening of asylum standards. The court’s treatment of the “safe‑relocation” defense—arguably the most skeptical measure of a country’s internal refuge capacity—offers a window into how the federal courts are redefining the interplay between “protection” and “migration” when the homeland suffers localized violence. For applicants from states rife with asymmetrically spread cartel activity, gang violence, or regional human‑rights abuses, the ruling signals a shift toward an almost systematic dismissal of the internal migration argument, unless the record is strikingly conclusive on a *fundamental* change of circumstances. In a period where the epidemiology of violence in Colombia (and similar countries) is unevenly distributed, the 10th Circuit’s razor‑thin standard threatens to sideline a large swath of asylum seekers who could credibly escape to safer provinces.

---

## The Legal Landscape: Safe Relocation Under the INA

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) is clear that applicants may be denied asylum if either (a) a “fundamental change” in circumstances removes their well‑founded fear of persecution or (b) they can avoid persecution by relocating to a different part of the country. The appellate standard—“substantial evidence”—follows from *Singh v. Bondi* and *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*. By “substantial evidence” the courts mean that any reasonable adjudicator would find the facts so credible that he or she might adopt the same conclusion. This is an “agency‑friendly” standard that, in practice, demands a demonstrable shift in facts or the discovery of new evidence.

Critically, *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder* provided the two relevant tiers: **(1)** a substantial change in circumstances that would put the alternative lifestyle or relocation in question into a different category of danger; **(2)** proof that the individual could avoid future persecution by moving to another region. The 10th Circuit has historically been on the fence about the first tier, preferring to stick to the in‑depth factual verification that an applicant actually “could” relocate safely. *Rivera‑Barrientos* is merely procedural guidance; the courts insist on “preponderance of the evidence” that a relocation would be safe and **reasonable**.

---

## The Record in *Jimenez*: Family, Resources, and “Unattached” Threats

Petitioners in *Jimenez* claimed that they had a **network of family** in the countryside—“safety to go back to” if the violence in Bogotá escalated. The court, however, described the record as showing only a remote, relatively lightly documented “some relatives in unaffected Colombian regions.” The key facts:

1. **Presence of Relatives** – The family’s mother and father lived elsewhere in Colombia, and the petitioners themselves spent a short period in the countryside before deciding to flee. The court dismissed the idea that a mere “family presence” establishes safety.  
2. **Economic Resources** – The petitioners’ acknowledgment of “access to resources” was seen as nominal. A prison staff member’s “minimal” check and a modest deposit of money did not prove a practical safety net.  
3. **Threats’ Genuineness** – Threatening men cited Don Rafa for the “von‑Colombia” relationship. But the court found “the threat was tethered to the property dispute, not to political opposition.” This nuance, insignificant for the baselines, undermined the *“fundamental change”* assertion that the threats had spontaneously morphed into geographically specific dangers: *“the men threatened them to leave a particular neighborhood, not the country.”*

Thus, even with family and resources abroad, the court dismissed the safe‑relocation argument because it failed to cross the threshold of **“a fundamental change in circumstances”** or **“reasonable relocation”**—the two guardrails set by *Rivera‑Barrientos* and reinforced by subsequent case law.

---

## Distinguishing “Geographic Relocation” From “Fundamental Change”

The doctrine hinges on two conceptual moves:

1. **Geographic Migrate** – Any shift in the applicant’s place of living.  
2. **Fundamental Change** – A structural, sociopolitical shift that alters the *nature* of the threat; a difference that could not have existed in the previous context.

The 10th Circuit leans heavily toward the second. The court repeatedly signals that “migrating to another city or region *does not automatically* reduce risk.” The party must argue that *conditions of those regions differ substantially* from the circumstances that warranted protection in the first place—i.e., the threats are *no longer* individually tailored because the new location lacks the same power structure or looming danger.

In *Jimenez*, the court saw no evidence that the Colombian government had lost policing capacity or that the “Violent Rafa” network had drained its local influence. The record showed that the same forces that threatened the petitioners remained active elsewhere; the men’s motive persisted—advancing a personal vendetta over a property. Consequently, the court deemed “no (or only marginal) fundamental change” in the threat landscape.

---

## The Evolving Trend: A Shift Toward Stronger Safe‑Relocation Circumstances

Across the circuits, safe‑relocation analysis has tightened:

- **7th Circuit** (*Osorio‑Moras v. Garland*) emphasized the need for *systemic* rather than *individual* failure data. A single punitive or unhelpful officer does not suffice.  
- **3rd Circuit** (*Chhetri v. Rosen*) highlighted the requirement that an applicant’s basis for relocation must demonstrate the *actual possibility* of *safe* and *reasonable* refuge *outside the current environment*.  
- **9th Circuit** (*Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland*) further required evidence that *government intervention* would not have been futile or hazardous, a criterion that the 10th Circuit also invoked by questioning the extent of Colombian police backing.

The new pattern is that safe relocation is no longer an automatic bar once the applicant is considered to have a home region. Instead, the court now requires **substantial evidence** that the new region truly lacks the *specific* risks that led to the applicant's persecution. In practice, this means that applicants from countries with wide‑scale, uneven violence must provide a **robust, fact‑heavy narrative** documenting:

- Distinctive law‑enforcement gaps in their current region versus the alternate region.  
- A verifiable record of *actual* protection provided by local authorities or community networks.  
- Evidence that the new region lacks the “localized power structures” that enable persecution, such as organized crime or gang dominance.

These demands narrow the asylum window for many and tilt the judicial scale toward *“no safe place in the country”* conclusions—exactly what the 10th Circuit did in *Jimenez*.

---

## Impact on Applicants From Localized Violence Hotspots

Potential asylum seekers emerging from countries where violence is geographically concentrated will find it increasingly difficult to survive under the *internal migration* doctrine. The trend carries two clear implications:

1. **Internal Precaution** – Applicants will be pushed to meticulously map out safe zones, perhaps arriving at the migration stage already prepared with verifiable relocation plans.  
2. **Government-Centric Evidence** – Instead of relying solely on factual threats, applicants must dig into the background of local authorities: corruption indices, enforcement capacity, and even direct evidence of police involvement with criminal elements. A single “less helpful” officer or the existence of a family member in the police force will *not* outweigh a lack of verifiable systemic safety.

We expect this relentless refinement to render the asylum claim increasingly a matter of *proof‑provider* rather than *credible narrative*. In a climate where immigration officials prefer the objective math of “fewer risks” over the subjective “fear” of persecution, the internal migration rubric provides a convenient target for denial.

---

## Lessons for Counsel and Immigration Advocates

1. **Collect Detailed Local Intelligence** – Show cancer‑grade evidence that a localized threat cannot be mitigated by any relocation. In *Jimenez*, the presence of a “family connection” was routinely seen as a *hint* that there *could* be a threat beyond the immediate environment, but not *proof* that relocation would be safe.  
2. **Recruit Third‑Party Corroboration** – Witnesses, NGO reports, and independent facts that link the threat to a localized entity, separate from the individual’s political orientation, were crucial. The court gave weight to the “perpetrator’s motive was tied to property, not politics.”  
3. **Amplify Distinctness Between Current and Target Regions** – Bring statistical or qualitative evidence of differential violence indicators, security apparatus, or community resilience. If a threat article cites a *generalized violation* of human rights, the court will still check for *whether a region has the specific capacity* to enforce or protect.  
4. **Show the Government’s Response** – Even a single officer’s unfriendly comment is not enough. The record needs to reflect a *systemic* reluctance or inability. Acceptance of the 10th Circuit’s *“lack of notification”* test means demonstrating a *systemic* unwillingness or failure to provide protection even if a threat is reported.  

Above all, counsel should adopt a *state-of‑the‑art* compliance strategy: start with a pre‑migration site‑assessment, then “plot” a route that is at least *documented* to be risk‑free, and finally gather evidence that any threat specifically targets the individual’s *new* home.

---

## Conclusion: The 10th Circuit’s Safe‑Relocation Doctrine Is a Signal

The *Jimenez* decision signals that the safe‑relocation exception is no longer a blanket shield for indigents in disordered regions. Courts are insisting on a more sophisticated analysis that verifies a *fundamental change* in threat conditions. By requiring demonstrable differences in protection and risk profiles between the current and potential relocation sites, and by demanding a robust governmental willingness or inability to intervene, the 10th Circuit has tightened the relief filter.

For immigration attorneys, that means a sharper focus on building *district‑specific* and *government‑related* evidence. For asylum seekers from countries whose violence is geography‑heavy, the practical democratic reality is that **they will either pass the internal‑migration barrier successfully or risk their asylum claim failing**. The *Jimenez* ruling may not abolish the doctrine, but it has engineered a higher bar that reshapes the asylum landscape for anyone facing localized persecution.
