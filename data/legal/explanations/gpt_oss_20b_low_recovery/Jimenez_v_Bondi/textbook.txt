Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# 1. Case Architecture: Procedural Background and Legal Context

The case of **Jimenez v. Bondi** traces a classic trajectory through the U.S. immigration adjudicatory system, moving from the initial administrative hearing before an immigration judge (IJ) to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and ultimately to the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals under the statutory authority of § 1252. Understanding this procedural ladder and the statutory pillars that underlie it is essential for interpreting the court’s substantive findings.  

## Identity and Status of the Petitioners

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons are citizens of Colombia who entered the United States in December 2022 without a formal admission or parole proceeding. They remain “prisoners of international law” under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA): they were never admitted with a visa, yet they have been in the United States in a manner that makes them removable. Their status as non‑citizens without lawful immigration status sets the stage for the subsequent applications for travel‑and‑asylum relief.  

## Timeline of Filings: NTA, IJ Denial, BIA Affiliation

1. **Notice to Appear (NTA)** – Three months after arrival, DHS served the petitioners with individual NDAs charging each as removable under the INA. The NDAs constitute the legal mechanism by which DHS initiates removal proceedings against a non‑admitted non‑citizen.  
2. **Petition for Asylum, Withholding of Removal, and CAT Protection** – The petitioners’ attorneys filed applications for asylum and withholding of removal, together with a derivative claim for their children. A second application sought protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT).  
3. **Immigration Judge (IJ) Decision** – The IJ denied all requests. The denial was based on the finding that the petitioners could not establish (a) past persecution, (b) a nexus to a protected ground, (c) internal relocation, or (d) the Colombian government’s unwillingness or inability to protect them.  
4. **BIA Appeal** – The petitioners appealed to the BIA. A single BIA member affirmed the IJ’s denial, concluding that the record contained no substantial evidence of the necessary asylum elements. The BIA also rejected the petitioners’ due‑process claim that the IJ violated their right to counsel.  

## Jurisdictional Ground: 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1) and (b)(9)

The Ninth Circuit’s review of the case rests upon statutory authority. Section 1252(a)(1) authorizes the courts to review final removal orders, which may include a consolidated review of asylum claims. Section 1252(b)(9) specifically authorizes appellate review of a final order of removal that consolidates immigration‑judge decisions with BIA orders. The Tenth Circuit, following the precedent set in **Singh v. Bondi**, applies a *substantial‑evidence* standard to the BIA’s findings of fact and a *preponderance of the evidence* standard to the BIA’s conclusions of law when the matter is on the appellate bar. The court’s jurisdiction, therefore, requires that the petitioners had a final order of removal that incorporated the denial of asylum; no independent removal order had been issued at the time of appeal.

## Scope of the Appeal and the Grounds Petitioners Successfully Contested (Asylum/Withholding Only)

The petitioners’ appeal revolved exclusively around the BIA’s denial of **asylum** and **withholding of removal**. They did not challenge the BIA’s denial of CAT protection, nor did they raise new issues concerning the application of the Convention itself. Since the docket reflects only the asylum and withholding claims, the Tenth Circuit’s review is confined to:

1. Whether the petitioners proved *past persecution* on the statutory basis of **protected ground** (political opinion or particular social group).  
2. Whether the petitioner’s claims met the *well‑founded fear* standard required for both asylum and withholding.  
3. Whether the “unwilling or unable” requisite of the *government‑protection* analysis was satisfied.  

The court’s decision therefore does not address the CAT claim, leaving that matter at an outside adjudicative level (unless the petitioner were to file a separate petition for CAT under § 1158(b)(2)).

## Relevant Statutory Definitions: Refugee, Protection Under the Convention Against Torture

For the appellate court to evaluate the asylum claim, it revisits the statutory definitions that form the foundation of the asylum regime:

- **Refugee (INA § 1101(a)(42)(A))** – A person who is outside his or her country of nationality and is unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a **well‑founded fear of persecution** on account of *race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion*. The definition obliges the claimant to demonstrate *past persecution* that *establishes a rebuttable presumption* of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.

- **Convention Against Torture (CAT)** – Under INA § 1158(b)(2), protection from serious or prolonged torture (or cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment) is granted to any person who meets the definition of “country of particular concern” and has *substantial evidence* that the government is unwilling or unable to prevent or respond to such torture. Because the petitioners did not appeal the BIA’s denial of CAT protection, that statutory construct remains unexamined here.

By anchoring the procedural narrative in these statutory anchors, the court situates its subsequent legal analysis — from the standard for review to the evidentiary thresholds — within a framework that is both precise and grounded in the INA’s language. This clarity is indispensable for a law student navigating the complex interplay between procedural posture and substantive asylum law.

# 2. The Governing Legal Standards for Asylum

The United States furnishes asylum as a discretionary form of relief under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Every denial of asylum that flows from a Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) order is susceptible to appellate review. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit reaffirmed the doctrinal framework that a claimant must satisfy in order to survive such a denial: the claimant must first prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he or she is a refugee; then the BIA must find that the claimant suffered past persecution; that the persecution was on account of a statutorily protected ground; and that the government of the claimant’s country is either unwilling or unable to protect the claimant from future persecution. Where all those elements are met, the BIA has found that the claimant has a well‑founded fear of future persecution. Finally, the claimant must show that safe relocation within the country is either not available or would be unreasonable.

Below that brief: (1) the discretionary nature of asylum under § 1158(b)(1); (2) the burden of proof; (3) the statutory definition in § 1101(a)(42)(A); (4) the three‑step past‑persecution framework; (5) the well‑founded fear and “unable or unwilling” test; and (6) the safe‑relocation test.

## Discretionary Nature of Asylum under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) states that the Attorney General may grant asylum to qualified noncitizens. The statute makes no substantive requirement for eligibility beyond the refugee definition (see § 1158(b)(1)). Rather, it places the power entirely in the hands of the Executive. Consequently, the BIA, the final administrative adjudicator, is empowered to apply the same discretion that the Attorney General does: even if a claimant meets every statutory criterion, the Board can deny asylum if it believes, based on the record, that awarding asylum is not in the public interest. *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) affirms that the BIA’s discretion is a property of the statute and not subject to review unless the Board’s findings of fact are not supported by substantial evidence.

## Burden of Proof and Preponderance of Evidence Standard

The homogenized standard in asylum matters gives the claimant the burden to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she is a refugee. The *preponderance* test requires that the claim be more likely than not to be true. The *Jimenez* opinion reiterated that the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the appellate court finds that the administrative record, viewed by a reasonable adjudicator, compels a different conclusion. *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012). Thus, discretion is not merely a “means” but a “burden”-bearing exercise: the claimant must convince the Board, and the Board’s decision is only reviewable for lack of substantial evidence.

## Section 1101(a)(42)(A) – Definition of a Refugee

Section 1101(a)(42)(A) is the statutory yardstick by which the BIA measures asylum. The statute reads:

> **“A refugee is a person who is outside the country of his or her nationality who is unable or unwilling to return there because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.”** *INA § 1101(a)(42)(A).*

Two things matter. First, a claimant must be **outside** his or her country of nationality at the time of the asylum claim (i.e., not present in that country). Second, the claimant must establish that he or she is **unable or unwilling** to return because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on a protected ground.

In *Jimenez*, the Court noted that this standard is the gatekeeper for the entire asylum process. Without satisfying this definition, the claimant is dismissed entirely, and no further analysis of past persecution or government protection is required.

## Three‑Step Framework for Past Persecution

The BIA’s determination of past persecution follows a triad established by the case law. A claimant must show:

1. **An Incident (or Incidents) that Rise to the Level of Persecution.** The incident must be such that the recipient suffered physical, emotional, or psychological harm. Courts distinguish simple threats or police harassment from persecution, requiring that the harm be **offensive** and more than a mere restriction of liberty or the mere threat of violence. *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004). The *Jimenez* opinion relied heavily on *Karki v. Holder* and *Brandy v. Holder* to clarify that solitary threats, absent accompanying physical harm or a pattern of harassment, do not constitute persecution in isolation. The court introduced the idea of a **cumulative** analysis: even if no single incident rises to the level of persecution, the aggregation of multiple incidents may. *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (2011).

2. **That the Incident is on Account of a Statutorily Protected Ground.** The claimant must demonstrate that the perpetrator’s motive was—at least in part—linked to one of the protected grounds in § 1101(a)(42)(A): race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. A protected ground may not be merely incidental or superficial. *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d 646, 647 (quoting § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In *Jimenez*, the Court found that the claimant’s political activity was a **tangential** rather than **central** reason for the alleged persecution. The Board’s analysis of why the Muslims were targeted—whether because of political activism or because of the landlord’s crime—was decisive.

3. **That the Persecution was Committed by the Government or by a Group the Government Controls or is Unable or Unwilling to Control.** The protected incident must have involved the state (directly prosecuting, arresting, or executing any wrongdoing) or a group that the state controls or fails to restrain. In *Jimenez*, the Court turned to the alleged involvement of *Don Rafa*, an informally described leader of a narcotrafficking guerrilla aligned with the president. The question then was whether this group was under the government's aegis. The grievance “withholding of removal” anchored the Board’s request that the government be rendered “unwilling or unwilling” to protect the claimant.

Because the BIA’s decision is summary, the appellate court, in *Jimenez*, affirms that the Board did not err where the BIA’s findings of fact were corroborated by substantial evidence.

## Well‑Founded Fear and the “Unable or Unwilling” Test

Once past persecution is established, the claimant is then judged for a **well‑founded fear of future persecution**. The “well‑founded fear” is a higher bar than past persecution; it requires more than a vague fear or a desire for safety. The statutory statute lumps this step together with the “unwilling or unable” test: *“It is presumed that the government is unwilling or unable to protect the applicant from persecution.”*

The Court has identified two key strands:

1. **Government Response to Past Persecution.** The Board must weigh evidence regarding whether the government tried or would have tried to prevent the actual persecution. Elements include law‑enforcement response, prosecution of the perpetrators, and protective measures. *Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861. The presence of a police attempt to raid the property or investigations into the landlord’s conduct weighed against a finding that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect the claimant. Likewise, *Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland* noted that the testimony that a police officer told the claimant “you should not seek trouble” did not constitute proof of a systemic unwillingness.

2. **Reporter of Persecution to the State.** If a claimant reports persecution, the government may rebut the presumption that it is unwilling or unable to protect the claimant. The court held that the mere failure to report, as long as it is not outweighed by clear evidence that reporting would be futile or dangerous, can kill the claim. *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, id. at *2. In *Jimenez*, the claimant’s failure to file a formal police report coupled with the absence of evidence that a formal report would have been futile made it impossible to locate a factual basis for the “unwilling or unable” conclusion.

If the BIA finds that a claimant has a **well‑founded fear of future persecution** and the government is **unable or unwilling** to protect him, then asylum is a viable path. In *Jimenez*, the Board and the Court concluded that the claimant’s fear was **not well‑founded** because the threat was incidental to the landlord’s drug‑related activity and not directly tied to the claimant’s political stance.

## Safe Relocation (Fundamental Change/Test) and its Evidentiary Burden

The “safe relocation” exception is a **positive assurance** that a claimant will not suffer future persecution in any part of his or her country. It is triggered in two contexts:

- **Fundamental Change**: The claimant demonstrates that circumstances have altered in a way that removes the basis for future persecution. 
- **Relocation Test**: The claimant can show that he or she can reasonably relocate to a region in which the threat does not exist.

Both require **preponderance of the evidence**. *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646. The Board can negate the well‑founded fear *only* if it proves that future persecution is avoidable by relocation. In *Jimenez*, the Court found that the claimant, who had family residencies in Bogotá and previously lived in the countryside for weeks, demonstrated that relocation in Colombia was possible. The Court also noted that the threat was exclusively from a neighborhood recruiter, not an organized, state‑backed entity, so the claimant's social environment remained unchanged by relocation.

In sum, the safe‑relocation test is a stringent hurdle that often falls short for claimants whose threat is of a localized, non–governmental nature. *Jimenez* recognized that the presence of family does not automatically provide safety, but that safe relocation, fortified by records, can create a valid defense. However, due to the inadequate evidence in the *Jimenez* record, the Board could not find any risk that exempted the claimant.

---

**Take‑away for the Law Student:** In a denial of asylum, the BIA’s analysis starts with the rubric in § 1101(a)(42)(A). If the claimant fails the refugee definition, the denial is immediate. If the claimant satisfies “unable or unwilling” and the three steps for past persecution, the BIA may give the claimant a *well‑founded fear of future persecution*. The BIA must also address whether the claimant can relocate safely. Only when *all* those analyses culminate in a finding—and when the record supports each element—does a refugee status excuse the removal. In *Jimenez*, the Court found that the claimant failed to meet the refugee definition because he did not establish past persecution on a protected ground connected to a government‑controlled threat; consequently, the Board’s denial was upheld, and the “well‑founded fear” and safe‑relocation arguments were not legally actionable.

# 3. Fact Pattern and Evaluation of the Past Persecution Claim

In this chapter we parse the facts that the court found contested by the petitioners, explain how those facts were weighed through the cumulative‑effects doctrine, and show why the court concluded that the petitions’ alleged persecution failed to rise to the level required by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).  

## Description of threats, property dispute, and subsequent intimidation incidents

The record illustrates a series of threats and intimidation directed at petitioner Wilson Jefferson Canon Jiménez and his wife Angelica Parada Duarte.  At the outset, the two were active participants in the Colombian Liberal Party, campaigning for candidate Federico Gutierrez in 2022. Beginning in late May, Jiménez repeatedly received phone calls that threatened his life because of his party affiliation.  In early June a fellow campaigner was abducted and killed despite previous warnings, a event that did not deter Jiménez and his family from continuing their political work.  

A first diverging episode involves a property dispute in June. Jiménez, a provisional lawyer, was asked by a woman in San Bernardo to mediate a rent issue against her former, now ex‑husband, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, popularly known as “Don Rafa.” The tenant was the leading member of a narco‑trafficking guerrilla group that aligned itself with President Gustavo Petro.  Jiménez discovered that the property had been shut down by the health department, was being used by migrants, and was a known base for illicit activity.  During his visit, a local police officer warned him to stay away.  That same month a brochure at Jiménez’s furniture factory threatened “death to snitches from Liberal Party.”  In November (the second month of threats) two men forcibly entered the factory, threatened him at gunpoint, and labeled him a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office; approximately a month later, the same men assaulted a factory employee, demanded the location of the couple, threatened them with death, and cited Don Rafa as the source of command.  In December a man tried to stop the couple’s young son from leaving school by physically restraining him, only to be pushed away by Jiménez.  These threats culminated in the couple seeking safety at Jiménez’s mother’s home in rural Bogota and ultimately deciding to leave the country.

The facts are thus a chain of pervasive intimidation.  They include phone threats, a hostile brochure, gun‑point threats to a factory, assault on a factory employee, an attempt to detain a child, and an ongoing creep in the neighborhood.  Crucially, none of the petitioners suffered torture, kidnapping, or direct violence that amounted to bodily injury; the threats were never followed through by a physical violation.

## Court’s doctrine on threats versus persecution (Karki, Brandy, Chhetri, Carias‑Mejia)

The court applied the standard established in the literature: **threats alone do not amount to persecution**.  *Karki v. Holder* (10th Cir. 2013) clarified that the threat element must be coupled with actual harm that is “offensive” and “more than merely a restriction or sporadic threat.”  The *B’land'* case of *Brandy v. Holder* (10th Cir. 2014) reaffirmed that emotional or psychological anxiety absent overt violence fails to satisfy the definition.  *Chhetri v. Rosen* (10th Cir. 2021) illustrated that “four telephone threats and one vague in‑person threat” were insufficient; *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland* (10th Cir. 2021) held that threats conditioned on compliance did not amount to persecution.  

These authorities do not require that *all* threats be substantiated—some may be incidental—but they require at least one instance of actual, offensive harm to trigger the statutory threshold.  The court consequently examined whether any of the recorded incidents involved real injury or violence.  Up to the record, Jiménez and Duarte reported that no physical injuries had occurred, and when petitioners acknowledged that they suffered threats, they admitted no lasting physical harm either.  A single threat of death, a brochure warning of death, a menacing gun‑point confrontation—none actually resulted in violence.  The courts subsequently concluded that the “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.”

## Assessment of cumulative effects per Wiransane, Ritonga

The INA recognizes that persecution is a *fact question* and may be the product of a series of incidents.  *Wiransane v. Ashcroft* (10th Cir. 2004) framed the test: the suffering must be “offensive in nature” and not merely a splinter of restrictions; *Ritonga v. Holder* (10th Cir. 2011) taught that a government should look at the *cumulative* effect of multiple events.  

The court applied the cumulative‑effects doctrine by looking at the entire sequence presented: repeated phone threats, a hostile pamphlet, a gun‑point confrontation, an assault on a factory employee, the attempt to capture a child, and the ongoing intimidation.  On the surface, the tally might appear grave.  However, cumulative effects can apply only where each individual event individually rises to menace or threat that is “offensive.”  Because none of the incidents involved actual violence, the cumulative counterfeit harm remained below the threshold of *persecution*.  The court emphasized that threats that are “infrequent or sporadic” do not accumulate into a theoretically “offensive” level of persecution unless they are part of a pattern of violence or a history of actual physical damage.  *Wiransane* clarified this: “In the current case, because the petitioner’s experience is limited to threats that did not materialize into any bodily injury or violent act, we cannot find persecution.”

## Physical vs. psychological harm: when does it make the “persecution” threshold?

The doctrine distinguishes **physical harm** (torture, kidnapping, beatings) from **psychologic distress** (panic attacks, insomnia).  The courts, following *Brandy* and *Chhetri*, hold that “psychologic harm by itself, unless it is the result of a longer or more severe pattern of violent victimization, does not reach the persecution threshold.”  *Carias‑Mejia* elaborated that mere “fear” or "anxiety" is “not enough” without a pattern of violence or an official protective response.  

In this case, Duarte testified that she could not sleep and that her son had panic attacks, but that is **correlated** with the continuous threat environment.  The courts require evidence of “actual violence or a credible threat that became a real physical act” to confirm the psychological harm is an effect of persecution, not merely a normal reaction to a hostile environment.  Since the threats did not culminate in violence, the psychological trauma was deemed a normal response rather than a statutory persecution.

## Petitioners’ failure to demonstrate that threats rose to persecution per precedent

Applying the preceding precedential framework, the court found that Jiménez and Duarte's record constituted purely *threats* without accompanying violence.  The threats were “incidental” in the sense that they did not culminate in actual harm.  The court noted:

1. The impugned threats (phone calls, brochure, gun‑point confrontation, assault on a factory employee, intimidation of a child) were unsustained; none resulted in physical injury or direct violence.  
2. The record provided **no evidence** of an actual assault that resulted in bodily harm or enforceable injury.  
3. The cumulative effects doctrine, invoked through *Wiransane* and *Ritonga*, was not satisfied because the individual events did not individually reach the “offensive threshold.”  
4. The psychological distress reported by Peter's wife and son could not be linked to violence; thus, it fell short of *persecution* as defined in the INA.  

Hence, the court concluded that the alleged past persecution failed to meet the statutory definition.  Under the substantial‑evidence standard, the BIA’s finding that the petitioner’s experiences did not constitute persecution was conclusive, and the appellate court upheld that determination.

By carefully applying the doctrinal framework, the court demonstrated that the line between lawful intimidation and “persecution” is drawn not merely by the presence of threats but by the presence of actual, offensive harm that is verified either by physical injury or a credible pattern of violence.  In the absence of such harm, the asylum claim cannot advance.

# 4. Nexus Analysis: Political Opinion versus Other Motives

In asylum adjudication, a central hurdle is the *nexus test*: the applicant must show that any past persecution suffered was *on account of* a statutorily protected ground. The court’s reasoning in **Jimenez v. Bondi** focuses on this very element. The petitioners alleged that the threatening incidents were retaliatory for their political activity. However, the panel held that the evidence pointed to a different primary motive—namely, the petitioners’ involvement in a property dispute with a narcotics‑trafficker who was linked, purportedly, to the Colombian government. This chapter walks through the doctrinal framework the appellate court applied and explains how the evidence was weighed against that framework.

## Defining a Protected Ground and the Political Opinion Requirement

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i), political opinion is a protected ground. The *Interior Department* requires that alleged persecution be tied to *one or more* of the protected categories (race, religion, nationality, particular social group, or political opinion). A political‑opinion claim is viable only when the persecutor’s animus or otherwise *targets* the applicant because of their political ideology or activity. Crucially, “political opinion must be an **at least one central reason** for the persecution.” This means that if a victim is harassed for several reasons—say, for telling a crime story, for being a minority, or for running in a campaign—then the political‑opinion factor must be the *dominant* or *seed* reason for the harm. The Ninth Circuit’s *Rivera‑Barrientos* doctrine codifies this centrality test.

## Mixed‑Motive Framework and Centrality Test (Rivera‑Barrientos, Dallakoti)

When multiple motives are purported, the “mixed‑motive” framework requires courts to examine how the persecutor’s reasons combine to produce the attack. The *centrality* test presumes that each motive is a *relevant* element, but only those grounds that are *central* to the persecutor’s decision to act may support asylum. The standard is not *exclusive*—a protected ground can be *one of several causes*—but it cannot be *minor, incidental, or tangential*. In *Dallakoti v. Holder*, the court emphasized that the applicant must show that the protected ground was “not merely a peripheral cause” but a *core motivator*.

The contention of *Jimenez* was that the travel partners were targeted because Jimenez, a veteran of the Liberal Party, was seen as a threat by the cartel. The panel, however, applied *Rivera‑Barrientos* and found the statements—or at least the evidence—did not establish that political opinion was central.

## Evidence Suggesting Dominant Motive: Property Dispute with Don Rafa

The record shows that Jimenez, while acting as a provisional lawyer, engaged in a negotiation at a property owned by the *ex‑husband* of a client. The tenant—identified as *Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros* (“Don Rafa”), a known narcotics trafficker aligned with the president—returned the property to the client. Jimenez’s attempt to negotiate an eviction turned hostile; the tenant confronted him, asserting that Jimenez’s presence could jeopardize the drug operation. 

Subsequent interactions involved men holding Jimenez’s family at gunpoint, harassing him for “snitching for the prosecutor’s office.” These acts were *directly* linked to Don Rafa, who allegedly directed the affiliates to intimidate the petitioners. The court noted that these incidents *followed* the property dispute, whereas the earlier politically‑motivated threats (phone calls, campaign enemy’s kidnapping) occurred *before* Jimenez’s involvement with Don Rafa.

In light of this chronology, the appellate panel inferred that the cartel’s hostility was *initiated* by Jimenez’s interference with their property interests, not by political bias. The evidence did not show that Don Rafa’s threats were a response to the petitioners’ Liberal Party affiliation. The presence of a *neutral* event—the property dispute—between the petitioners and the cartel provided a more plausible *primary* motive.

## Petitioners’ Lack of Proof that Political Opinion was Central

Petitioners charged that the cartel’s actions were retaliation for political reasons, asserting that the men who threatened them in the factory were “snitches” for the prosecutor’s office and “in politics.” Yet the petitioners offered no contemporaneous or documentary proof that the cartel’s animus was based on political opinion. They did not identify any specific political ideology, nor did they present evidence that Don Rafa was motivated by the political figurehead of an opposition party. Instead, the priests’ argument rested on a general claim that the cartel “aligned with the government” and therefore harboring political sentiment. The court noted that linking a criminal organization to a prescription of *political* motivations required a *higher evidentiary threshold*; mere association is insufficient.

Moreover, the petitioners’ *own* testimony demonstrated that the threats intensified only after Jimenez’s involvement with Don Rafa, thereby suggesting a *proximate* causation by the threat of property damage rather than political critique. The court observed that the *interview footage* from the factory incident, as well as the accompanying police reports, framed the offenders as “snitches” but did not reference the Liberal Party or any electoral stance. Accordingly, the court concluded the petitioners failed to meet the *centrality* requirement.

## Court’s Decision to Reject the Nexus Claim

Given the *preponderance of evidence* weighing that the cartel’s intimidation stemmed from a property dispute, the court held that the petitioners **did not satisfy the nexus requirement**. The *mixed‑motive* analysis found that the alleged political opinion was *subordinate* to the certifiable motive of narcotics‑related property protection. Accordingly, the appellate panel upheld the BIA’s denial of asylum relief, because a failure to establish a protected‑ground nexus invalidates a claim of past persecution.

In sum, the court reinforced that a *political‑opinion nexus* cannot be inferred from an *indirect* or *secondary* connection. The evidence must show that the persecutor *deliberately targeted* the applicant because of *political views* *in and of itself*. In *Jimenez*, that evidentiary bar was not satisfied, leading the court to reject the petitioners’ nexus argument.

# 5. Government Protection, Safe Relocation, and Final Holding  

In this chapter we trace how the Tenth Circuit applied the statutory framework for asylum and withholding, focusing on the policy that requires an applicant to show that a persecutor is “unable or unwilling” to protect her or him. We then examine how that “lack of protection” analysis fed into the safe‑relocation inquiry and ultimately produced the court’s denial of the petition for review.  

## Government Protection Analysis: Notice, Response, Systemic Failure  

The INA requires the applicant to demonstrate that the government has a “notice of the past persecution” and that it failed or could not provide protection. The Seventh Circuit’s *Osorio‑Mora­les* and the Ninth Circuit’s *Aviles‑Gonzalez* decisions teach that evidence of a single unhelpful police officer is insufficient; the court looks for a pattern of systemic failure.  

The Board’s record offers only two incidents. First, Jimenez reported a threatening brochure to a police patrol in Bogotá. The officer answered that nothing could be done and advised the petitioners to leave the city. Second, the petitioners claimed that two men, who later said they were “sent” by Don Rafa, held a factory worker at gunpoint and demanded that the petitioners leave the neighborhood. In each case the petitioners sought help from the local law‑enforcement apparatus. The courts, however, see this as a private encounter with an officer whose remarks do not reflect the Colombian government’s overall policy or capacity. *Osorio‑Mora­les* stresses that a single officer’s statement must be weighed against evidence of a wider institutional failure. The petitioners failed to offer any evidence of prosecutions, protective custody, or systematic police indifference in Bogotá or elsewhere in Colombia.  

Because the petitioners did not file any police report of persecution, the courts regard the record as lacking sufficient “notice.” The *Aviles‑Gonzalez* decision makes clear that, absent notice, the government’s willingness or ability cannot be evaluated. Accordingly, the Tenth Circuit found no “systemic evidence” that the Colombian authorities had either stopped or attempted to stop the persecutors, nor did they show a pattern of refusing to protect political opponents. The record merely confirms the petitioners’ fear that “the government was closely aligned with the criminals,” an assertion that the court also deems unsubstantiated.

## Application of *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and *Osorio‑Mora­les* Precedent  

The court’s treatment of the petitioners’ claims mirrors the reasoning in *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and *Osorio‑Mora­les* in two ways:

1. **Notice Requirement** – In *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, the BIA rejected an asylum claim because the applicant had not reported persecution to the police. The Tenth Circuit followed that logic, noting the petitioners’ one‑off conversation with the patrol officer did not provide the necessary notice.

2. **Systemic Failure Standard** – *Osorio‑Mora­les* urged that the burden is on the applicant to show repeated systemic lapses by the government. The petitioner’s evidence is limited to isolated anecdotes; the court rejected the claim as “insufficient to prove an unwilling or unable government.”

By applying these precedents, the court affirmed that the petitioners lacked the evidence required to establish a “willing or able” Colombian state.

## Evaluation of Safe Relocation  

Safe‑relocation is a secondary obstacle that a denial of asylum or withholding must satisfy. The Board and, therefore, the appellate court, look for a “principal, permanent, and perpetually risky regional threat” that precludes internal movement. The petitioners attempted to argue that they could not relocate safely because of “administrative and economic burdens” and because their persecutors have ties to the president. The courts rejected these claims for three reasons:

* **Stable Family Network** – The petitioners have relatives who remain safely in Bogotá and in the countryside for at least three weeks after the assaults, demonstrating that a temporary relocation is feasible.

* **Prior Rural Residence** – Jiménez’s mother’s house in the Bogotá countryside served as a safe refuge for the family’s three sons. This prior safe residence suggests that political persecution was not intolerably widespread in other Colombian districts.

* **Lack of a Credible Future Persecution Claim** – Since the court found no well‑founded fear of future persecution, the safe‑relocation inquiry is moot. In *Carias‑Mejia*, the court emphasized that a denial of asylum that precedes a safe‑relocation judgment falls outside the scope of its review.

The court concluded that the petitioners could relocate without enduring continued persecution and that the government was not complicit in preventing such relocation.

## Implications of Lacking a Well‑Founded Fear of Future Persecution  

A well‑founded fear is a prerequisite for both asylum and withholding. The court’s failure to find a well‑founded fear in *Jimenez v. Bondi* had a twofold effect:

1. **Denial of Asylum** – Because the petitioners could not demonstrate past persecution, nexus, or a well‑founded fear, the asylum grant was denied under the “three‑prong” test in *Niang v. Gonzales*.

2. **Denial of Withholding of Removal** – Withholding is “higher‑handed” than asylum. The court ruled that, since the petitioners did not satisfy the asylum “past persecution” criterion, the BIA was also correct in holding that they lacked the “well‑founded fear” required under *Carias‑Mejia*.

In both cases, the absence of well‑founded fear implied that the petitioners could be returned to Colombia without danger, negating the need to apply the safe‑relocation standard or seek police protection.

## Court’s Final Decision and Its Reasoning Hierarchy  

The appellate decision culminated in the order to deny the petition for review. The court articulated a clear hierarchy in its reasoning:

1. **Review of the BIA’s factual findings** – Under the substantial‑evidence standard, the court found the BIA’s findings regarding past persecution, nexus, and government protection to be supported by the record.

2. **Legal assessment of the asylum framework** – The court applied *Niang*, *Rivera‑Barrientos*, and *Karki* to conclude the petitioners could not establish past persecution or a nexus to a protected ground.

3. **Withholding determination** – Leveraging *Carias‑Mejia* and the absence of well‑founded fear, the court held that the petitioners failed to meet the higher standard for withholding.

4. **Safe‑relocation footnote** – Because the key prerequisites for asylum and withholding were missing, the safe‑relocation inquiry was effectively precluded.

5. **Summary denial** – The court, therefore, denied the petition for review, affirming the BIA’s denial.

The decision underscores a strict adherence to the legal framework for asylum relief and demonstrates that, absent a robust record showing both past persecution and a well‑founded fear tied to a protected ground, applicants cannot rely on the government’s willingness or inability to protect them. The Tenth Circuit’s use of *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and *Osorio‑Mora­les* signals that future petitioners will need a more comprehensive evidentiary showing of systemic state failure and concrete reports of persecution to succeed.
