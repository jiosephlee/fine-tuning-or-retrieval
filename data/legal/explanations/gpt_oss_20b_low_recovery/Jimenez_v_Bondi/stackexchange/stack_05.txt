Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What does ‘safe internal relocation’ mean in asylum law, and why did the court reject the petitioners’ claims?
Question:
The opinion dismisses the petitioners’ argument that they cannot safely relocate within Colombia, citing that they lived safely with relatives for three weeks. How is ‘safe internal relocation’ evaluated, what evidence is required, and why did the court find relocation to be ‘reasonable’ despite the petitioners’ claims of insecurity?
Answer:
**Answer**

**Short answer:**  
“Safe internal relocation” is a *requirements‑test* that the asylum applicant must satisfy to rebut the de facto presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution. The test asks whether the applicant could **reasonably** (i.e., based on facts available in the record) move to *another part of the same country* and avoid the threat posed by the persecutor(s). In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the court found that the petitioners could have “safely and reasonably relocated” to the countryside (where they stayed for three weeks) and that their alleged threats were directed only at a particular neighborhood, not at the country as a whole. The court therefore concluded that safe internal relocation was possible and that the petitioners’ claims of insecurity were insufficient to override the ordinary presumption that a person who can relocate safely is unlikely to face future persecution.

---

### 1. What the standard actually asks

The Court repeats the *Rivera‑Barrientos* framework:  

> “The government may rebut the presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution… *if, by a preponderance of the evidence, the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.*” ( *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646, citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Thus, **two elements** are required:  

1. **Feasibility** – is there a *practical* place where the applicant could live that is outside the persecutor’s influence?  
2. **Reasonableness** – does the record show that the applicant *could actually* make that move; i.e., do they have family ties, economic means, or credible evidence that they would *safe* there?

The Court’s analysis is *fact‑specific*; the record must show that the applicant *could* mitigate the threat by moving.

---

### 2. Evidence the Court considered

| Evidence | How it helped the Court find relocation *reasonable***
|----------|-------------------------------------
| **Three‑week stay in the Bogota countryside** | Shows the petitioners *actually lived* there safely after the last threat; demonstrates that the countryside is a viable, **non‑threatened** area. |
| **Family ties (mother, father, relatives)** | The presence of family in the country, and no reports that those relatives had the same political opinions or were under threat, indicates that the petitioners would have local support if they returned. |
| **Threats were localized** | The threats came from men tied to “Don Rafa” and were *directional* to a neighborhood, not the entire country; the men never told the family to “move out of the country.” |
| **No evidence of broad government cruelty** | The petitioners never claimed the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them; the Court noted that “the government had attempted several searches and seizures,” suggesting at least *some* level of control over the perpetrators. |
| **Petitioners’ own action** | They voluntarily went to their mother’s house after the last threat. Their decision to leave for the U.S. happened only after they **tried** a domestic refuge that proved inadequate. |

Because the record *demonstrated* that a **safe place existed** and that the petitioners had *actually used* that place, the Court concluded that safe internal relocation was **available and reasonable**.

---

### 3. Why the court rejected the petitioners’ claim of insecurity

* The Court emphasized that *“security” is a *function* of the specific threat, not an assumption of country‑wide indiscriminate danger.* The plaintiffs’ own evidence—the three‑week stay—showed that the area they chose was safe.  
* The Court noted that **parking a threat on the whole country is not a prerequisite** for a refusal under the safe‑relocation bar. It is enough that the threat could be *avoided by moving to a safer region*, which the record supports.  
* The petitioners’ narrative that the persecutors were “closely aligned with the Colombian government” was contradicted by the fact that *the police had already made several searches at the property* and that the petitioners themselves were *not able to get help* from a single “unhelpful” officer—an isolated incident that the Court did not find sufficient to establish that the government was “unwilling or unable” to protect them.

In sum, the Court held that the **jurisprudence requires the record to show a *reasonable plan* for relocation; the petitioners proved exactly that**—they lived safely in the countryside, had family support, and had no evidence that relocation would be impossible or unsafe.

---

### 4. Bottom line for you

If you are a student looking at this ruling, remember:

* **Safe internal relocation is *not* an automatic bar**; it is a *fact‑specific* test that focuses on whether the applicant can *avoid future persecution* by moving elsewhere in the country.  
* The Court expected *concrete evidence* that the applicant *could and would* relocate safely (family ties, transient stays, economic resources).  
* In *Jimenez*, the record showed that the petitioners *actually did* move to a safe place, rendering their fears of state‑linked persecution irrelevant to the safe‑relocation bar.

That’s why the court dismissed the petitioners’ argument and concluded that safe internal relocation was indeed “reasonable” despite their claims of insecurity.
