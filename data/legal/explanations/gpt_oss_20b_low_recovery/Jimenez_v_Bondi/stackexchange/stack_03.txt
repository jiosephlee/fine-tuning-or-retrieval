Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What is the standard of review for the BIA’s findings of fact under § 1252(b)(4)(A)?
Question:
The opinion states that the court reviews the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard. I’ve seen the term used in other contexts, but can anyone clarify what “substantial evidence” means in this particular review, and how it differs from the standard used in merits‑review decisions by immigration judges?
Answer:
**Answer**

The 10th Circuit is clear that, under § 1252(b)(4)(A), the court reviews the BIA’s *findings of fact* “under a substantial‑evidence standard.”  That means the court gives the BIA’s factual determinations the benefit of the doubt; those findings are *conclusive* unless the record shows that *any reasonable adjudicator* would have reached the opposite conclusion.  See *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012).

### What “substantial evidence” means in this context

In practice, the substantial‑evidence test is less deferential than the “substantial evidence” standard used in most ordinary administrative appeals, but it is not merely “the same evidence” as the BIA’s.  The standard requires that:

1. **The record actually contain evidence** that a reasonable adjudicator would have found.  
2. **That evidence is not merely circumstantial or weak** but, taken together, supports the factual conclusion.  

The BIA’s facts are not “pre‑certified” or presumed to be beyond question; the court must still look at the evidence the BIA considered.  The court consults the IJ’s “more complete explanation” of the facts when the BIA relied on the IJ (as the opinion notes), but it does not re‑weigh the evidence in a new light.  See *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

### How this differs from the standard used in merits‑review decisions

On a “merits‑review” of the IJ’s decision (the ordinary review of an asylee’s claim), the court also uses a substantial‑evidence standard, but it applies it to *both* factual findings and legal conclusions drawn from those findings.  The difference here is that the 10th Circuit is reviewing the BIA’s *final* order under § 1252(b)(4)(A).  The procedural posture is a *final‑order* review, not a merits‑review:

* **Merits review** – the IJ’s findings of fact are reviewed de novo under substantial evidence, and the court can consider new evidence or re‑evaluate the IJ’s facts.  
* **Final‑order review** – the court reviews the BIA’s findings as part of a single, final administrative decision.  The standard is still substantial evidence, but the court is restricted to the record that supports the BIA’s *final decision*, and it gives the BIA’s determinations the benefit of the doubt.

Thus, although the formal standard is the same word (substantial evidence), the scope in a final‑order review is narrower and the court is expressly directed not to re‑weigh evidence or read the BIA’s findings differently—a “catch‑all” limitation that is not present in a merits review.

### Bottom line

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court correctly applied the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings under § 1252(b)(4)(A).  The court found that the BIA’s findings were supported by the record and therefore were conclusive, and it refused to overturn them.  The standard is the same phrase used elsewhere, but in a final‑order context the court is even more deferential to the agency’s factual conclusions.
