Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the ‘central reason’ test apply to political-opinion persecution?
Question:
Petitioners allege that they were targeted because of their political opinion, yet the court found that the violence was primarily motivated by Jimenez’s conflict with Don Rafa. The court refers to the ‘central reason’ test from Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti. Could someone explain how the central‑reason test is applied when a persecutor has multiple motives, and what evidence is necessary to satisfy that one protected ground is a central reason?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
The “central‑reason” test is satisfied when the record shows that, *at least one* protected‑ground motive was the *dominant* or *primary* driver of the persecution, even if other motives are present.  In mixed‑motive cases the adjudicator must weigh all facts, but if the majority of the evidence points to a different motive, the protected ground is only “tangential” and the applicant fails the nexus prong.  To meet the test, the applicant must demonstrate that:  

1. **There is at least one incident or **a pattern** of incidents** that can be clearly linked to the protected ground.  
2. **The persecutor’s motive in the incident(s) can be proven** to have been, or was, *at least one central reason* for the harm.  
3. **The evidence for other motives is not sufficient to tip the balance** to a different primary motive.  

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the court applied Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti exactly in this way.  The record was “replete with evidence that Jimenez’s conflict with Don Rafa Lancheros—his association with the narcotics‑trafficking group aligned with the president—was the principal reason the attacks were directed at him and his family.”  The court rejected the claim that political opinion was the primary cause because Jimenez’s politically‑motivated threats *pre‑dated* the escalation that followed his involvement in the property dispute, and the “escalated threats” were linked explicitly to Don Rafa’s protest that Jimenez had “inspected” his property.  The adjudicators therefore found that the protected‑ground motive was “tangential, subordinate, or a minor role” in the persecution (see *Rio v. Freedom*, 993 F.3d at 1514‑15).

Below is a concise, step‑by‑step guide on how the central‑reason test is applied in mixed‑motive cases, with the standard–of‑evidence structure that the court used in *Jimenez*.

---

## 1.  Identify the protected‑ground claim

The applicant must first state the protected ground (e.g., political opinion, particular social group, race). In *Jimenez*, the claim was political opinion (“membership in the Colombian Liberal Party”).

---

## 2.  Establish that the protection‑ground was **at least one central reason**

### a.  Look for *ordered or patterned* evidence

The court says the protected ground must be “a central reason” and not “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate.”  The record must contain at least one incident (or a consistent pattern) that can be causally linked to the protected ground.  The “at least one” requirement is *critical*—the existence of a single, clearly connected incident is enough, even if other unrelated incidents also occurred.

### b.  Evaluate *time‑to‑time* sequence

In *Jimenez*, the court noted that the threats and harassment escalated **after** the property dispute with Don Rafa.  The timing was crucial: the contested attacks were not happening *because* of Jimenez’s party work but because he *interfered* with Don Rafa’s illicit operations.  The court therefore found the political‑opinion motive had *no causal role* in the escalation.

### c.  Examine the persecutor’s statements

Statements made by the persecutor (e.g., “Don Rafa sent them”) are a key piece of evidence.  In *Jimenez*, the men who threatened the family explicitly said they had been sent by Don Rafa and were angry that Jimenez had “made trouble” for “snitches.”  That testimony placed Don Rafa’s personal grievance—not political opinion—at the heart of the persecution.

### d.  Consider *corroboration* from third parties and context

- **Other victims**: The court looked at the fate of other Liberal Party members who received threats *before* Jimenez’s dispute.  Their continued campaigning suggested the threat level was not rooted in political affiliation.  
- **Local police**: The officer’s statement that “the Colombian government is contributing to the problem” is *not* evidence of a political‑opinion motive.  
- **Crime‑control context**: The fact that Don Rafa was a narcotics‑trafficking leader aligned with the current president added weight to the “personal grievance” motive.

All of these points helped the court conclude that the political‑opinion motive was “minor” in the context of the specific persecution.

---

## 3.  Weigh the evidence: *Central vs. Peripheral*

The central‑reason test requires a *substantial‑evidence* (not "preponderant") determination that the protected ground was a *principal* motive.  The court therefore asked:

- **Did the majority of the evidence point to Don Rafa’s personal grievance?**  
  Yes.  
- **Are there any facts that show political opinion was the driver?**  
  No.  Threats before Jimenez’s dispute were “politically affiliated,” but they did not link to the *actual* violence that followed.  

Because the evidence tilted decisively toward another motive, the court found the protected‑ground claim was *tangential*.

---

## 4.  Evidence required to satisfy the test

| Evidence | Why it matters |
|---------|----------------|
| **A specific incident or pattern directly tied to the protected ground** | Shows the persecution is rooted in that ground. |
| **Persecutor’s statements or documents explaining motive** | Provides direct evidence of motive. |
| **Temporal sequencing that links the protected‑ground actions to the persecution** | Demonstrates causal relationship. |
| **Contextual data (e.g., other victims, political climate)** | Context can show whether similar actions were politically motivated. |
| **Ruling on other motives being stronger or equal** | If other motives outweigh the protected ground, the test fails. |

---

## 5.  Practical application in *Jimenez*

1. **Claim**: Political opinion (Liberal Party membership).  
2. **Incidents**: Threatening phone calls, vandalization, and armed harassment.  
3. **Persecutor statements**: Men explicitly said they were “sent by Don Rafa” and were angry at Jimenez for “snitching.”  
4. **Timing**: Escalation occurred *after* the property dispute.  
5. **Other motives**: Don Rafa’s business interests, his alignment with the president, and the nativist criminal context.  
6. **Conclusion**: Protected‑ground motive was *not central*; instead, the primary motive was personal grievance with Don Rafa over the property dispute.

Thus, the court rejected the nexus claim.

---

## Bottom line

*If you assert a mixed‑motive case, you must prove the protected ground was *the dominant* or *principal* reason for the persecution.  A single incident clearly linked to that ground suffices, but **all other evidence must not press the persecutor to another motive as the stronger one**.  In *Jimenez*, the evidence overwhelmingly favored Don Rafa’s personal grievance, making the political opinion claim peripheral and insubstantial.*
