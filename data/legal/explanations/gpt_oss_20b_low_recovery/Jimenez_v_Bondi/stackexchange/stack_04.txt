Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why did the court refuse to consider the petitioners’ fear of future persecution under the ‘unable or unwilling’ test?
Question:
The opinion disclaims that the petitioners failed to show that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them, citing insufficient reporting. How does the ‘unable or unwilling’ analysis weigh evidence of the government’s response to past persecution, and why is a lack of police reporting not fatal in other cases?
Answer:
**Why the court could not apply the “unable or unwilling” prong to the petitioners’ case**

The “unable or unwilling” test is part of the **well‑founded fear of future persecution** prong of asylum.  
When that prong is asserted the applicant must show that, *if* the authorities were notified of the past persecution, they would **not** be able or would refuse to provide protection.  (See *Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861.)

---

### 1.  What the courts look for

The *Tenth Circuit* acknowledges two kinds of evidence that weigh heavily on the government‑response analysis:

| Evidence | What it signals | How it is applied |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Police action to stop the persecutors.** | That the state is willing to intervene. | If the police actually foiled a threat, the “unwilling” claim collapses. |
| **Offer of protection (e.g., protective custody).** | That the state is able to shield the applicant. | If such a protection was offered, the “unable” claim is negated. |

The court also stresses that the **first question** in the inquiry is whether the applicant denied the state knowledge of the past persecution.  “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped” (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861–62).  The *absence of a reporting* *can* undermine a claim, but it is *not fatal*; the applicant can still satisfy the prong if it can show that filing a report would have been futile or dangerous.

---

### 2.  How the record in *Jimenez v. Bondi* weighs

1. **No formal police report.**  
   The petitioners only spoke informally with two officers who told them they should “not seek trouble.”  No written complaint was filed.  
   The court accepted that this *lack of reporting* makes it hard to assess how the police would respond, but noted that *the fact alone is insufficient to prove the government was unwilling or unable* (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861; *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2*).

2. **Evidence of police intent to act.**  
   The court pointed out that the government had *already* attempted “several searches and seizures” of Don Rafa’s property.  That shows the police were aware of the situation and had taken concrete steps, undermining the petitioners’ assertion that the government would “refuse or be unable to stop the perpetrators.”  The court stated that “the evidence counters Petitioners’ argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government and would be unwilling or unable to stop the persecutors” (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861).

3. **No systemic evidence of state failure.**  
   The court explained that *one unhelpful officer is not proof of systemic unwillingness* (“…a one‑off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” *Osorio‑Mora⁎, 72 F.4th at 743).  The petitioners failed to produce evidence that the Colombian police, or any Colombian authority, routinely failed to protect the cartel’s informants or to investigate crimes against political opponents.

4. **Argument that contacting the state would have been futile is unsupported.**  
   The petitioners’ claim that the government would “not be able to stop the persecutors” because they were aligned with the president is *not proven* in the record.  The court explicitly rejected that proof: “Petitioners cannot show that the Colombian government was, or would be, unable or unwilling to protect Petitioners had it known about the threats they received” (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861). The court treated the threat that “the men told them to move out of the neighborhood” as not extending to the country as a whole, so a government‑wide “unwillingness” could not be inferred.

---

### 3.  Why a lack of police reporting is “not fatal”

The court cites *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and *Singh* to explain that a missing report does not automatically defeat the claim.  A petitioner may still satisfy the “unwilling or unable” prong by showing that *reporting would have been futile or dangerous* (e.g., “the state would have turned a blind eye” or “the police would have protected the trafficker”).  In *Jimenez*, however, the record *does not contain* an inference that reporting would have been futile: the police had *already* tried to intervene in the property dispute, and the petitioners did not show that the authorities would have protected them if they had come forward.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for the petitioners

Because the record lacks evidence that:

* The petitioners *tried* to involve the state in a formal way (no police filing), **and**  
* The state *would not have acted* successfully or was *systemically unwilling* to protect them,

the court concluded that the “unable or unwilling” analysis could not be satisfied.  Consequently, the petitioners could not establish a well‑founded fear of future persecution.  This conclusion, coupled with the earlier denial of past persecution, led the court to deny review of the immigration judge’s decision.

In sum, the court refused to consider the petitioners’ fear under the “unable or unwilling” test because the record did not support the key elements—lack of a police report *and* evidence that a state‑based intervention would have failed—necessary to satisfy that part of the asylum standard.
