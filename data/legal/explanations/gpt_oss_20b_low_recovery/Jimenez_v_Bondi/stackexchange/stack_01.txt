Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What distinguishes a threat from persecution in asylum cases?
Question:
In the opinion, the court says that threats alone are insufficient to establish past persecution. I’ve read that asylum requires ‘more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty’, but I’m unclear on how the courts determine what level of harm crosses that threshold. What factors are considered when evaluating whether a threat, or a series of threats, constitute persecution?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the court reiterated that *threats alone do not rise to the level of “persecution”* required for asylum.  When the court evaluates whether a threat (or series of threats) amounts to persecution it looks for **(1)** that the threat is **“offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats”** (Karki, § C, ¶ 17‑18), **(2)** that the threat is **part of a broader pattern of mistreatment** that creates a **cumulative risk of harm**, and **(3)** that the threat is **an instrument of state‑level or state‑aligned violence** that the government is either unable or unwilling to curb.  If any of those elements are missing, the court will conclude the applicant has failed the past‑persecution prong.

---

### 1.  Threats are not “persecution” unless they are part of a broader pattern

> “We have held that a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm ... and must entail more **than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty**.” *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (emphasis added) – *Jimenez*, § C paragraph 17.  

The court emphasizes that **individual threats, even if violent in tone, do not automatically meet this threshold**.  In *Karki* the court said:

> “**Threats alone are insufficient** to constitute persecution.” *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013).  

Thus, a single threatening phone call or a brochure saying “death to snitches” does not count as persecution.  The threat must be embedded in a larger context of intimidation, violence, or criminal activity.

### 2.  Cumulative, ongoing threats can be treated as persecution

The court stresses that it looks at **the collection of incidents, not an isolated event**:

> “We do not look at each incident in isolation, but **consider them collectively**, because the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution.” *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citing *Jimenez*, § C, ¶ 18).  

In *Jimenez*, the family documented repeated phone calls, a threatening brochure, a gun‑point assault at the factory, a threat of murder from men “under the order of Don Rafa,” and an attempt to hold one of their children at gunpoint.  While the judge noted no physical injury was reported, the court said the **aggregate of these threats and the ongoing danger they created failed to meet the “offensive” standard** needed for persecution.  The court cited *Chhetri v. Rosen* and *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland* to show that **four telephone threats and one vague in‑person threat, or threats that were contingent on compliance, were insufficient**.

### 3.  The threat must be linked to a protected ground and to state control

Even when threats are cumulative, the court requires that they be **“on account of”** a protected ground and that they be **caused or condoned by the government or a group the government cannot control**:

> “The petitioner must establish that **the persecution was ... on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds**.” *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012).  

In *Jimenez*, the petitioner argued the threats were political in motive.  The court found the predominant motive was a personal dispute with **Don Rafa** (a narcotics‑timed operator aligned with the president), not the petitioner’s political opinion.  The court held that **the political opinion was merely “tangential”** and “subordinate to the main reason” for the harassment (see *Jimenez*, § D, paragraph 3).  Thus the threats could not be tied to a protected ground.

### 4.  A threat must be barred by the state’s unwillingness or inability to protect

If an applicant shows that a threat is part of a pattern of persecution by a government or a law‑enforcement body, the court also checks whether the **government failed or was unwilling to intervene**:

> “Whether a noncitizen has proved past private persecution … is a **fact question**.” *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 859‑60 (10th Cir. 2025).  

In *Jimenez*, the court found the applicant had **not sought help** beyond two informal conversations with police officers, and no evidence that the government had actually responded to the threats.  The court therefore deemed **no proof of government unwillingness or inability** (see *Jimenez*, § E).  Without this element, the threat sequence cannot constitute persecution.

---

### Bottom line for a student

1. **Single threats = not persecution.**  
2. **Multiple, ongoing threats + evidence of retaliation or violence can be persecution,** but only if the pattern is **“offensive and more than sporadic.”**  
3. The threats must be **tied to one of the protected bases (race, religion, nationality, particular social group, political opinion).**  
4. The threats must be **by or associated with the government or a group the government cannot control.**  
5. If the government is **unable or unwilling** to stop the persecution, the threat can also satisfy the *unable‑or‑unwilling* prong for “future persecution.”  

If any of those steps fail, the court, as in *Jimenez*, will reject the claim under the past‑persecution prong of asylum.
