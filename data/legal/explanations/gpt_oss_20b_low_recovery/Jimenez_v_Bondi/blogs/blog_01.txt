Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# When Threats Aren’t Persecution: The 10th Circuit’s Precise Stipulations on Past Persecution

## 1. Why It Matters

In **Jimenez v. Bondi** the 10th Circuit reaffirmed a hard‑line rule: *threats, on their own, are not enough to satisfy the immigrant‑law definition of “persecution”*. The decision is a knife‑edge reminder to practitioners that the INA’s asylum policy—designed as a humanitarian remedy—cannot be wielded as a blunt instrument of mere intimidation. For attorneys representing clients who have endured a pattern of harassing phone calls, threatening letters, or even an escalating sense of intimidation, the ruling underscores the fragile space between “emotional harm” and “persecution.”

This is not simply a technicality. The standard has real‑world consequences: an applicant who falls short is denied not only asylum but also the higher‑order relief of withholding of removal. Jimenez’s family shows many of the sociological facts we see in a contemporary asylum docket: Drifting from legitimate political opposition to a host of illicit threats; the line between “state‑related” and “non‑state” danger; or the role of mental‑health claims in a jurisdiction that, historically, has been reluctant to grant relief to psychological suffering alone.

The 10th Circuit’s reasoning sits in a lineage of courts that have repeatedly said that *offensive, more than merely intimidating, harm is required*.

---

## 2. The “Offensive” Exception: From Wiransane to Karki

### Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889 (10th Cir. 2004)

Judge Tymkovich (sitting for the appellate court) articulated the key principle: *Persecution is “a form of mistreatment that must be more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.”* The court explicitly rejected the notion that *“occasional threats”* could satisfy the “continuing or pervasive threat” standard if the alleged harm is not “offensive.” 

In Wiransane, the petitioner endured a single short‑term phone threat—a “crime‑prevention” call that held no clear attempt to bring about violence, yet the court held it fell short because it did *not* cause the kind of offensive harm necessary to meet the statutory threshold.

### Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792 (10th Cir. 2013)

Justice Donato echoed Wiransane, extending the standard to encompass *uncertain* or *rumoured* violence. Karki emphasized that threats *generally* cannot ground a claim unless they are a *prelude to violence* or *instrumental* in an ongoing campaign of physical or serious psychological harm. Judge Karki famously remarked that a threat alone is “not persecution” unless it is part of the *cumulative, offensive* pattern—a point that resonated through Jimenez’s analysis.

Jimenez’s panel specifically cites Karki to dismiss the family’s argument that “threats and fear” should aggregate to persecution. They say the record shows *no physical or psychological injury*, no violence, and no *“standing” of hostility that reaches beyond the rare and isolated threat. The cumulative effect of two dozen phone calls, a brochure, and sniping statements has, they argue, not produced the “level of suffering” that “offensive” means.

---

## 3. The “Cumulative Harm” Doctrine in Practice

The 10th Circuit has long allowed a *cumulative* analysis when claims involve *multiple, repeated* incidents. **Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971 (2011)** distinguished it from the single‑shot standard in Wiransane. However, Ritonga was bound by *physical* evidence: an assault and subsequent injury. In Jimenez, the judge went point‑by‑point:

1. **No physical harm**: They merely heard threats.  
2. **No proven emotional trauma**: Although the family reports panic attacks, the court notes that “psychological harm alone has seldom sufficed”—referencing **Brandy v. Holder, 590 App’x 744 (2014)**, which found that ostensibly alarming statements did not amount to persecution absent a more tangible threat of violence.  
3. **Preponderance of evidence standard**: The BIA’s ruling, corroborated by the IJ, was “substantial evidence,” and the appellate panel did not find the record compelling in the *rebuttable presumption* under **Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187 (2005)**.  

So while the treading on the “cumulative harm” method is clear, the threshold remains a *high,* especially in the absence of *direct harm*.

---

## 4. What This Means for Caseloads: The Humility of Asylum Adjudicators

When adjudicators encounter a panorama of demonstrable intimidation—sure signs that the applicant lives under a blanket of fear—the Jimenez standard demands careful scrutiny. The *C. H. C. v. Arman, 838 F.3d 176* (U.S. 2016) reminds us that an asylum adjudicator must weigh *“the totality of the circumstances”* and that *concentrated evidence of violence* may trump a pattern of threatening speech.

Practically, Jimenez narrows the window for “safety” claims:

- **Physical threats**: Must strongly corroborate an actual or imminent physical attack.  
- **Psychological harm**: Must extend beyond “frequent anxiety” to clinically significant trauma *and* be tied to a pattern of *violent* intimidation.  
- **Political context**: Even amid a politically charged environment, the *centrality* of the protected ground is key. As the Court reiterated, a marginal political opinion is insufficient if the main motive is *elseheld* (e.g., an armed gang’s personal grievance—as with Jimenez’s‐?? Don Rafa).  

Thus, as the 10th Circuit argues, *“a spate of threats with no evidence of a broader pattern of violence or a clear, protected motive is not persecution.”*

## 5. Broader Doctrinal Implications

### A. The “Persecution” Bar Is Not a “Political Speech” Shield

Jimenez clarifies the **political‑motive nexus**: an alleged persecution *must* be a central reason. This echoes **Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641 (2012)** and reinforces the **“central‐reason” test** articulated in **Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264 (2010)**. The decision places a dent in the argument that a subset of political activism is inherently “persecutory” merely because of the *content* of one’s political opinion.

### B. An Enlarged Blueprint for “Government Protection”

Jimenez's “unable or unwilling” analysis ties together the *lack of a timely police report* and an *apparent non‑responsiveness* by the state. The Court’s treatise—**Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848 (2025)**—suggests that *“a failure to report does not automatically defeat a claim”* but also warns that *“one off‑hand dismissal by a local police office”* may fail to show systemic unwillingness.

The *“social hosts”* or *familial ties* that may have offered safety were deemed insufficient because they didn't show *willingness or ability to intervene.* Thus, immigration adjudicators should take note of the *“majority of the evidence must point to a nationalistic response”* standard.

### C. The “Future Persecution” Framing

It’s worth noting that the Court rolled back the *“future persecution”* argument under **withholding of removal** (which demands a *well‑founded fear* that goes *beyond past persecution*). Justice Tymkovich wrote that a *lack of pre‑instruction by the government* about potential threats at a local level has *not* tipped the balance in favor of a well‑founded fear. In other words, the *“agency’s prior inaction”* is not enough; there must be *objective evidence* of *state or proxy incapacity*.

## 6. Navigating the 10th Circuit’s Standard: Practical Tips for Counsel

1. **Document Physical Incidents**: Even a single assault or credible threat of bodily harm can tip the scales. Politically motivated insults or general harassment won't cut it.  
2. **Substantiate Psychological Harm**: Bring in mental‑health clinician statements. Demonstrate consistent, **clinically verifiable** trauma (e.g., PTSD diagnoses, ongoing therapy). 
3. **Tie the Threats to a Statutorily Protected Ground**: State early and prove it was “central” to the persecutor’s motives.  
4. **Show Evidence of a Systemic Threat**: If the government is **involved or complicit**, provide context—e.g. statements from higher law‑enforcement officials, official patrol logs. A single unhelpful officer is insufficient.  
5. **Use Safe Relocation as a Counter‑argument, Not a Sine Qua Non**: Jimenez reminds us that *internal relocation* may mitigate claims. Jot down structural banners – e.g., evidence that a relative can reliably offer shelter, or that the applicant has a documented safety plan in a less violent region.

---

## 7. Bottom Line

Jimenez delivers a decisive (and welcome) rejection of a *“threat‑based”* asylum narrative. For immigration law professionals, the case *does not* restrict policy; it reinforces a *higher evidentiary burden* that ensures only those facing *real, at‑risk*, *state‑linked* persecution obtain the refugee protection the INA offers.

Now, asylum adjudicators—and the attorneys who accompany them—must strike a balance. The humanitarian purpose of asylum hinges on the promise that the U.S. is a safe haven. That promise, however, is rooted not in *willingness to entertain any fear*, but in a tangible, legally defensible proof that a person faces *actual, exposure‑level harm.* Jimenez reminds us that *threats alone, no matter how dire, do not amount to that harm.*
