Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When Expert Testimony Breaks the Law: The Case of Dr. Wells and the Failure of “Signals of Interest”

## Why This Matters

Less than a year after the jury in the Eastern District of Texas found Finesse Wireless sufficiently proved that Nokia’s radios infringed its two patents on mitigation of intermodulation products (IMPs), the Federal Circuit threw out that verdict as a matter of law.  At the heart of the decision was the court’s assessment of the patent‑prosecution expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, whose testimony about how a receiver’s analog‑to‑digital converter (ADC) samples “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” simply could not be sustained.  The ruling illustrates why, in high‑tech patents, experts must speak with surgical precision—not simply a broad brush of “this is how the thing works.”  It also reminds litigators of the tightrope they walk between persuasive evidence and the “substantial evidence” bar.  In an era where circuit‑level schematics are buried in thousands of pages of company documents, the Wells decision is a reminder that the ledger of expert opinion must be as clear as the spec in the patent itself.

## The Fifth Circuit’s Legal Framework

The court begins by setting out the standard its review hinges on.  Denials of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) are reviewed de novo.  The Fifth Circuit follows the precedent in *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*:  the panel must find that the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes reasonable jurors *could not* arrive at any contrary conclusion.”  (See *Wi‑LAN*, 811 F.3d at 461.)  The United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit then brings the “substantial‑evidence” standard, drawn from *Apple v. Wi‑LAN*, to adjudicate a jury’s infringement finding.  If a reasonable jury could not find infringement in light of the evidence presented, the appellate court may reverse.

This is not a question of “could the jury have found infringement” in a vacuum; it is a question of whether the factual record—experts, documents, and expert testimony—satisfies a high evidentiary threshold.  The Fifth Circuit has long held that the fact‑finder’s monolithic decision must be buttressed by credible, detailed evidence, especially where the parties have eschewed a standard “expert statement” model in favor of obscure signal‑path illustrations.  In *Wi‑LAN*, the court found the district court had failed to see the “de facto” evidence that the affected signals were indeed sampled.

In *Finesse*, it was the opposite that emerged: the alleged evidence was too thin.

## The Heart of the Argument: Are the Signals “Real” at the ADC?

The ’134 patent’s key limitation—“over‑sampling a passband of received signals *to create a bit stream* that includes both signals of interest and interference‑generating signals”—imposes a concrete requirement: the receiver’s ADC must see both kinds of signals *before* they are processed.  Dr. Wells’ testimony formidably wrapped his argument around the figure in the Nokia document that labels a double‑red path: “DL(TX) reference & modeled PIM path.”  When pressed, he insisted the two signals on that path were the very signals the claims required.

On cross‑examination, the prosecution counsel plumbed a simple yet fatal technicality: the modeled PIM path is, by definition, generated *after* sampling occurs.  A signal can’t be sampled until it exists.  The district court recognized that point but rested its ruling on the prosecution’s insistence that the “modeled PIM path” was not the interference‑generating signal the claims demanded, thereby concluding the ADC indeed sampled both required signals.

The appellate court, however, waters down the prosecution’s own reasoning.  It read the prosecution’s argument literally and concluded Dr. Wells never, at any point, made a definitive mapping of the two red‑path signals to the required categories.  He did not assert “x1 is the signal of interest” and “x2 is the interference‑generating signal.”  He repeatedly says “x1 and x2 are the two inputs on the red path” without qualifying which input stands for what the claim calls for.  The only names we get from the defendant’s side are “downlink TX reference” and “modeled PIM path,” but those labels themselves belong to a *legend* created by Nokia and, more importantly, were applied to *computed* signals that only exist downstream of the ADC.

When the court splits itself between “the claims require a signal at the ADC that doesn’t exist there” and “the ADC does sample the signals,” it ultimately picks the former because the prosecution’s presentation is too equivocal.  The evidence is “inconsistent, self‑contradictory, and not at all consistent with the claim’s technical requirement,” leading the court to conclude that no reasonable jury could find infringement.  Since the crux of the evidence – the mapping of signals – is missing, the *substantial‑evidence* bar is not met.

## Why “Substantial Evidence” Was the Key

Here’s a finer point that’s easy to miss.  The Fifth Circuit’s *substantial‑evidence* standard is not quite the same as the “reasonable man” threshold used for crisp factual decisions.  It requires evidence that a reasonable jury *could* find infringement based on the record.  In *Finesse*, that turned out to be a matter of whether or not the prosecution literally told the jury that the ADC sees a signal the patent calls the “interference‑generating signal.”  The answer was “no.”  Whether “mapping x1 to S1” or “x2 to S2” was sufficient was moot because the prosecution never told the jury that those fundamental cross‑sections existed.

In a similar vein, the court had to consider that the prosecution’s key claim amendment – to require *three* separately identifiable signals – was only intended to disallow *two* signals as a “scope expansion” and not to preclude a situation where the accused device *did* use only two unique signals.  The approval of that amendment is harmless; the problem is the later evidence insists the accused used only two unique signals whereas the claim calls for three.  Even if the court indulged that argument, the jury’s finding would have still lacked evidence that the accused achieved the *seven* multiplications claimed for *S1 × S1 × S2*, *S2 × S2 × S3*, etc.  Instead, the logic stops at three distinct multiplications asserted in the Nokia documentation.  The court thus saw no “substantial evidence” that the accused performed the claimed seventh multiplication.

In other words, the evidence *was not** in the record to support the required claim construction, rendering the jury’s finding unsupported.

## The Broader Significance for Experts and Litigators

1. **Clarity of Matching Claims to Artifacts**  
   Dr. Wells’ testimony shows how easy it is to slip into a “we’re using this diagram” trap.  Plaintiffs must ground every term in the claims to a verifiable artifact in the technical construction.  More than point‑wise mapping, the expert must show how the artifact *pre‑exists* (i.e., sits in the ADC) and participates in the claimed process.  Oversights or ambiguous labeling can evidence the Wrecking Ball per *Finesse*.

2. **The “Self‑Contradictory” Test**  
   The court cited *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* for the rule that a self‑contradictory expert opinion cannot underpin a jury verdict if it cannot satisfy a reasonable jury under the “substantial‑evidence” test.  In modern electronics litigation, experts routinely shift between multiple signal semantics—e.g., “modeled PIM path” vs. “actual generator of interference.”  That dualism may not amount to a substantive difference if the expert never settles on a mapping, but a defendant could easily point out the contradiction and upset the jury’s notion that the evidence is solid.

3. **The Burden of Proof Is the Litigator’s Kettle**  
   In technology‑heavy patents, a plaintiff’s burden of proving infringement includes deploying *unambiguous* expert testimony.  It is not enough to weaponize a diagram and then argue “the malware is there.”  Courts demand that the expert either show the claimed signal directly in the right plane of a device or, at minimum, can help the jury imagine a plausible circuit for the claim.  A failure to trace the signal trajectory from “incoming RF” to “bit stream” and on through the cancellation chain can break the entire case.

4. **Damage Recalculation**  
   Because both the JMOL and the damages award were vacated, the new trial will likely involve a different damages analysis (probably reduced or nullified).  Even if the final verdict is reinstated, re‑proof of infringement may entail a far different damage calculation.  Plaintiffs should prepare to argue that the company’s error was “inadequate" for the original damages amount, a process that typically requires clear evidence of the loss and a demonstrable risk of repeated infringement.

## Downstream Doctrinal Implications

- **Expert Testimony Is Not a “Wizard”**  
  *Finesse* demonstrates that the appraisal of the evidence is not a deferential “magical” process; an expert’s testimony must match the claim, or the jury will find no infringement.  This could echo in *Fed. Cir.*, *Finesse* underscores that courts can grade the sufficiency of an expert’s narrative, potentially extending the “experimental evidence” doctrine into patent litigation as a lower‑barrier tool.

- **Claim Construction Remains Anchored to Intrinsic Evidence**  
  While the court referenced *Wi‑LAN*’s de novo review of claim construction, it reinforced that the intrinsic evidence cannot be overridden by an expert’s argument if the expert’s testimony fails to tie their discussion to the patent’s own language.  The result sharpens the line that attorneys must follow: elaborate technical arguments bear no weight unless the lines of the patent are followed.

- **The “Substantial‑Evidence” Test Gets Stressed**  
  The case may encourage litigants to pay extra attention to reviewing the prosecution’s output, especially any “inconsistent or overlapping” statements.  A “substantial‑evidence” review will now, in light of *Finesse*, include rigorously vetting whether the expert’s statements *actually* meet each claim’s granular limitation.

## Final Take‑Away

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit did not so much hand a verdict on the technical merits of the Nokia radios’ PIM‑CN cancellation circuitry as it corrected a fundamental flaw in the plaintiff’s case: Dr. Wells’ expert testimony made an ambiguous, self‑contradictory claim about which signals the ADC really sampled.  Because the prosecution never proved that the patented “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” existed within the device as the patent required, the jury’s verdict was unsupported by substantial evidence.  The decision is a cautionary tale: in patent law, the burden of proof is on the plaintiff to tie every claim limitation to a clear, unambiguous piece of evidence—especially when that evidence is offered through an expert’s costly and court‑critical testimony.  Litigants and experts alike will find that the *Kuma* of this ruling will echo through future high‑tech patent litigation.



# Claim Construction in the Digital Domain: ‘Three Signals’ vs. ‘Two or Three Signals’ in the ’775 Patent

## Why this case matters

In the ever‑turbulent world of wireless interference, the mechanics of patent infringement can be as intricate as the radio‑frequency circuits they aim to protect. The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*—particularly the analysis around the ’775 patent—provides a prescient look at how courts grapple with the interplay between claim language, prior‑art allowance, and expert testimony. While the lower court blew the jury verdict a hole in the head with an unsubstantiated “seven‑multiplication” instruction, the appellate panel offered a sober _claim‑disavowal_ test and reaffirmed that an expert’s slippery testimony cannot magically bridge engineering gaps.

The stakes are real: if a patent clause is built on the shaky proposition that a device multiplies *three* signals in *seven* unique ways, then any miss‑match between what the claims require and what the accused device actually does can wipe the infringement analysis clean. That has downstream ramifications for the entire ecosystem of mobile‑device litigation, from the drafting attorneys who draw firm claim borders to the engineers who map claim requirements onto firmware and silicon.

## The ’775 patent at a glance

Patent No. 9,548,775 claims a **digital‑domain passive‑intermodulation (PIM) cancellation method**. The type‑of‑signal language Kicking off the method is a “three‑signal” requirement:

> “…in a receiver, with a transmitter and the receiver being co‑located … the method comprising… generating interference cancellation signals (ICSs) … by *digitally multiplying three signals in seven multiplications* to generate clicks…” (Claim 1).

The crux for infringement is twofold:  
1. **Number of signals**—The device must present **three *separately identifiable* signals** (S1, S2, S3).  
2. **Number of multiplications**—It must perform **seven** distinct third‑order products as specified.

Finesse had a history of arguing that its prior‑art deductions—especially the Filipovic reference—had previously taught *two*‑signal approaches, so it narrowed in the specification and claim amendments to accept a *three‑signal* requirement. At the same time, the amendment explicitly sticks to “three signals … in seven multiplications,” seemingly shoring up the scope. The question is: did the amendment *disavow* the possibility of a two‑signal or three‑signal scenario, or does it still allow the use of two unique inputs as well?

## “Three signals” isn’t *the only* signal

At the heart of the appellate decision lies a claim‑safeguarding doctrine: a claim amendment disavows a scope only when that disavowal is *clear, explicit, and unambiguous* (see *Cont’l Circuits v. Intel*). The ’775 patent’s new language—*digitally multiplying three signals in seven multiplications*—is a *quantitative* shift, but it does not expressly outlaw using fewer *unique* signals. The court examined the old and new wording side by side and concluded that the amendment stressed the need for *three distinguishable signals*, but still left room for the *determinant* of how many *unique* signals those could be. In other words, the claim could be satisfied by a prosecution that used *two* physically different signals plus a copy of one of them (e.g., x1, x2, x2') because those are still *separately identifiable*.

The court drew a useful line between “two unique signals” and “two unique signals plus a copy of one.” The differentiation is subtle. A copy of a signal is *not* a distinct unique signal because it is a mathematical transformation, not a new source. Finesse’s amendment fails to establish that a *unique* third signal must be supplied. That interpretation generally aligns with the historic patent‑law principle that a three‑signal parameter clause means “three *slots* for signals” rather than “three *different* physical sources.”

## The seven‑multiplication gap

The second pillar of infringement collapsed for a more blatant reason: the accused radios simply did not **perform** the full suite of seven third‑order multiplications that the claim enumerated. The decision’s authors spent a great deal of time dissecting Dr. Wells’s expert testimony:

* The Nokia technical document employed as the evidentiary backbone never listed all seven multiplications.  
* Instead it enumerated **only three distinct multiplication operations** (e.g., x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|), each involving either a real or conjugated signal or a modulus.  
* The document made no claim to repeating any of those multiplications, such that the *seven* combinations (x1⋅x1⋅x2, x1⋅x2⋅x2, …) were never carried out.  
* Crucially, the mapping from those three operations to the seven claimed ones was never explained.

Because patents are factual, not purely doctrinal, “substantial evidence” rests on whether a reasonable jury could see that the accused device performed the claimed multiplication sequence. The Federal Circuit wrote that without evidence of all seven multiplications, *the jury’s infraction verdict is unsupported*.

Why was this glaring omission allowed into the trial record at all? The judge accepted the expert’s testimony that Dr. Wells had “discovered” the mapping from the Nokia document’s three multiplications to the seven claimed ones. The court deemed it a *puzzle piece fit*—truly a fine line. But in absence of any credible mapping, the appellate panel ruled the district court was “too wide” in interpreting the expert’s opinion, bringing the infringement analysis to a halt.

## Doctrinal implications

### Claim scope and claim disavowal

The *finesse* of the claim‑construction reasoning here goes beyond the specific case. It reinforces that:

1. **Quantitative amendments** need to be *explicitly* framed as *disavowals* if they intend to tighten scope.  
2. Even a seemingly clear “three signals” clause may not hold if *legal context* (e.g., prosecution history) shows the patent can be satisfied by a *smaller set* of unique inputs.

This has an echo in future drafting: beware of “three signals” without specifying that the signals *must be unique*. If the aim is to preclude a two‑signal economy, the draft must say clearly “three *distinct* signals” and perhaps also highlight that *copies* do not satisfy the claim.

### Expert testimony and “cognitive mapping”

Where patents ask for *specific functional* or *mathematical* operations, the burden on the plaintiff lies not just on the existence of the operation but also on the *mapping* from the accused device’s internal operations to the claim language. If the expert’s mapping is highly speculative or unexplained, courts will refuse to give it evidentiary weight. The decision, therefore, reiterates the chilling weather that surrounds *ad‑hoc mapping* without hard data points.

### Convergence with prior cases

The reasoning aligns with older commercial‑telecommunications cases—*Wi‑LAN v. Apple* and *Intel v. Cont’l Circuits*—but casts a fresh light on *digital‑domain* internal signal layers. In the final paragraph, the panel essentially argued that the act of requiring “seven” distinct multiplications is a *non‑falsifiable* claim unless the plaintiff can provide *concrete* evidence of those operations. That signals an impatience with intellectual gymnastics that have historically been used to mask the lack of engineering reality.

## What this means for future litigations

1. **Drafters** should pay close attention to the verb “distinct” when the claim scope depends on signal variety. The phrase “three signals” alone can be ambiguous.  
2. **Engineers** must supply *empirical demonstrations* (firmware traces, component schematics) of each multiplication, especially when the patent claims a specific count.  
3. **Litigators** have an open field to challenge missing function, even if expert testimony appears in court. As the decision shows, if the jury’s verdict rests on a loose, uncommented mapping, the appellate court has plenty of room to step in.  
4. **Res judicata** in this niche area is likely to be restrained. Because the claims can be decided by *broad but unspecified* language (e.g., “three signals”), a court’s later recognition of claim breadth may open new infringement possibilities—even for patents whose claims have otherwise gone on the shelf.  

In sum, the Federal Circuit’s decision neatly reiterates a familiar theme: if the claim says “three signals” and “seven multiplications,” the prosecution history, wording, specifications, and executable evidence must support that demand. Otherwise, any jury verdict built on shaky ground gets overturned. Patents—especially in the high‑stakes domain of wireless interference—must now face a more rigorous standard: engineers cannot simply make an educated guess about what the claim means—proof must be as precise as the claim itself.



# Damages Frozen by Infringement Uncertainty: A Wake‑up Call for Patent Litigation Damages Calculations

## Why This Case Matters

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit reversed a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of non‑infringement for two of Finesse’s patents and vacated a $166 million damage award that had been rendered on the basis of that verdict. The decision is striking for two reasons. First, it punctures the comfort level that litigants and scholars have felt about the “finality” of a jury verdict that carries a substantial financial penalty. Second, it forces us to re‑examine the interplay between infringement findings, damages calculations, and the appellate pathway for challenging those damages when a jury’s verdict is not supported by *substantial evidence*. The blowover is real: damages that once seemed locked in are now vulnerable to fluctuation or total reset whenever an infringement finding falters.

## The Damages Story: From Judgment to Appeal

Under the 35 U.S.C. § 284, a defendant may move for a new trial or for JMOL on damages after a verdict, but the defense’s challenges must be constrained by the standard set in *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.* Each jurisdiction applies its own “new‑trial” standard, but the Fifth Circuit’s governing rule is straightforward: the appellate court will grant a new trial only if the district court abused its discretion, and the plaintiff’s remedy has to prove there is egregious error. Because damages are consequential, the court goes even further: *the defendant must show the jury’s damages award was “shocked by the evidence” or was not supported by substantial evidence*.

In *Finesse*, the district court denied the defendants’ JMOL on damages (and also denied a motion for a new trial) after finding all of the patent claims infringed. Importantly, the defendants had already moved for JMOL of non‑infringement at the suggestion of the jury’s verdict, a claim that copes with the evidentiary standard for infringement. The appellate court, however, found that the evidence presented to the jury **did not support a credible infringement verdict for either of the patents**. The jury’s findings were, at best, *substantially lacking* in evidence. Because the foundation of the verdict was shaky, the damages award derived from that verdict could no longer be upheld.

## The Substantial‑Evidence Trigger

The central standard is perhaps best explained with the doctrine’s two parts:

1. **Substantial evidence**: A factual finding is supported if a reasonable jury could have found it in light of the evidence presented at trial. This is the standard used for determining infringement. 
   
2. **The “shock” or “lack of evidence” trigger**: The defendant’s motion for a new trial on damages (or JMOL on damages) must show that the award is “shockingly unsupported” or that the proof was not *down the line*.

The appellate court’s reversal shows that a failure to meet the substantial‑evidence bar for infringement *knocks the entire damages tower on its head*. Even if the jury had a valid method for calculating damages—like the unit‐price or “gold‐standard” method under § 284—if the **instrument of that calculation (the infringement verdict) is not credible, the calculation itself has no legal basis**.

In practice, the court’s “substantial evidence” threshold is *not* an *absolute* bar; we are dealing with a *reasonably credible* fact pattern. In *Finesse*, the problematic expert testimony (formerly Dr. Wells) contradicted itself and seemed to conflate internal signals (x1, x2) with external “interference‑generating signals.” The jury was, therefore, led by a testimony “more a well‑sprung confusion than a well‑sprung factual map.” Since the jury’s verdict was unsupported, no damage amount stands.

## What This Means for Future Litigation

### 1. Infringement Findings Must Stand on Their Own

The realignment of damages to the infringement standard means defendants can no longer rely on a “good‑faith” damages calculation that presupposes infringement. Whether a plaintiff uses a “statistical” or “unit‑value” approach, the court will now look first at whether *any* reasonable jury could have found infringement. If not, the damages roll back to the *pre‑verdict* stage – *liquidated damages* or *lost profits* defenses might get a second look, pricing under § 282, or even a *partial damages* view of the claim.

### 2. The “Lump‑Sum” vs. “Unit‑Value” Debate

Prior to *Finesse*, plaintiffs in patents relating to high‑tech equipment often favored lump‑sum damages because they were easier to argue and more punitive. The court’s decision suggests that a lump‑sum may still be vulnerable once infringement is under question because the court view is that the plaintiff must first prove *infringement* and *products affected*. If a plaintiff's only evidence is a lump‑sum figure that relies on unproven media‑quality mapping of signals, the appellate court can put that figure on hold. The onus is on plaintiffs to tie the damages directly to *specific* infringing acts rather than general, unit‑value approaches that might be too speculative in a high‑tech context.

### 3. Appellate Power to Override Verdict Calculations

Because the appellate court now has the power to override a jury’s damages determination when infringement is unsubstantiated, litigation strategists should focus on the early phases of the trial. This means:
- Protecting the integrity of expert testimony by requiring clear mapping of signal‑flows to claim limitations.
- Using “dual‑certification” experts who can cross‑link hardware schematics to claim claim interpretations.
- Explicitly addressing “unique inputs” or “third‑order multiplications” in the trial record.

Failure to do so invites a reversal like *Finesse*, potentially wiping out a multi‑hundred‑million–being award.

### 4. The Role of Rule 50(a) “Clear and Unambiguous” Challenges

The defendants in *Finesse* argued that claim scope had been surrendered by amendment. Because the defendants did not raise this argument at Rule 50(a) stage (the initial pleading for JMOL), the court was reluctant to reconsider it. The court’s analysis about whether they “waived” claim scope because of earlier amendments sheds light on the importance of timely, procedural precision. The point is that *changing the shape of the claim body and then using that modified shape to defend non‑infringement can be legally “waived”* if not raised early.

Because a new trial for damages can be denied simply for *failure to raise valid defenses at the appropriate pleading stage,* litigants should remember that a new trial is not an open field but a tightly linked procedural ladder. The *Rivard* test in *Wi‑LAN* insists that the court will only grant a new trial if it can find the district court abused its discretion, which rarely happens when the defendant simply did not raise evidence in time.

## Potential Downstream Effects

1. **Settlements May Be Re‑evaluated**: Once an award has been vacated, the parties may be forced to renegotiate. Parties who settled based on a senior jury verdict may see that offer invalidated or must be re‑struck.
   
2. **Defendant Payouts Capped**: If a plaintiff appeals and the court vacates the award, defendants will be able to “roll back” the damages percentage and potentially avoid a full payout. This might also encourage the use of *damage‑rollover* clauses where the defendant pays only a % of a reversed award.
   
3. **Re‑emphasis on Patent Clarity**: The construction path will suffer a boost. Late‑stage amending of claims alters the construction but may not protect a defendant’s interests in the event of an appeal. Claim drafting will involve a careful balance between broad coverage and enamel‑quality evidence for infringement.

4. **Future Tactics for `“Interference”` Patents**: The high‑tech semiconductor and antenna‑engineering arena will need to carefully craft both the technical and legal narratives. The failure in *Finesse* hinged on the failure to concretely map the physical signals to the claim language. The cautionary tale is clear: piecing together a horn string of analog signals into a “signal path” that orbits around “interference‑generating” signals can fade into noise. Patent owners now face a larger evidentiary burden to keep infringement clear.

## Bottom Line

The *Finesse* decision puts a fresh spotlight on the nexus between infringement and damages. It reminds litigators, patentee owners, and the courts that there’s no escape from the *expectations of substantial evidence* when deciding how to pick a jury’s verdict. The consequence for an unshaken damages award is that its validity depends squarely on the integrity of the infringement finding. In the space of **patent litigation, especially in the high‑tech domain, the “no‑budget” guess‑works–jei**—increasingly, the *infringement certainty* base is a crucial prerequisite for a *damages calculation staircase* that does not collapse itself under the weight of misinformed technical testimony.



# The Fifth Circuit’s Two‑Step Standard for JMOL in Patent Litigation  
### Lessons from *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*

## 1. Why This Case Matters

In *Finesse Wireless*, the Federal Circuit reversed a massive damages verdict and vacated a finding of infringement for two of Finesse’s PIM‑cancellation patents. The reversal rested on two elements of the Fifth Circuit’s legal framework: first, a de novo review of a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on infringement; second, the substantial‑evidence test for a jury’s factual findings. At first glance, the case might seem like a routine post‑trial challenge, but its doctrinal consequences ripple through the entire telecommunications patent ecosystem. Law‑makers and litigators can no longer presume a jury’s verdict will stand if the evidence’s “factual backbone” is shaky, even when claim construction and technical analysis appear to support infringement.

## 2. The Two‑Step Test in Practice

### 2.1 De novo Review of JMOL

The Fifth Circuit’s scaffold for JMOL has long been a touchstone for appellate courts. In *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, the court made clear that a district court’s denial of JMOL must be reversed if, when viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, “the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” This standard gives the appellate court latitude to look beyond the court‑room record—as long as the evidence the jury considered is compelling.

In *Finesse*, the appellate majority interpreted this de novo standard to mean that a written jury verdict cannot survive if the evidence the jury ate up was itself “confusing and contradictory.” The court disbelieved the jury’s conclusion that the RF ADC in Nokia’s radios sampled both a “signal of interest” and an “interference‑generating signal” because the plaintiff’s expert, Dr. Wells, never mapped those signals in a way that satisfied the technical limitation. The court’s language echoed the *Johns Hopkins v. Datascope* ruling, where the court rejected an expert’s contradictory testimony as “no reasonable jury” could have found infringement.

### 2.2 The Substantial‑Evidence Standard

Once the appellate court is convinced the district court made a mistake, the second step is whether the jury’s factual findings themselves are supported by substantial evidence. In *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN*, the court holds that “a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” This has a particular peril for patent litigation: even if claim construction is favorable, a jury can still be reversed if the underlying facts—such as what signals were actually being sampled or multiplied—lack corroboration.

In *Finesse*, the appellate panel found no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications or that the interference‑generating signals pre‑existed the ADC sampling phase. The expert’s testimony omitted key mapping details and relied heavily on a technical manual that did not demonstrate the claimed operations. The jury, therefore, failed the substantial‑evidence bar, and the damages award—anchored on a failed infringement conclusion—was vacated.

## 3. Doctrinal Implications

### 3.1 Re‑Enforcement of the Receptive Evidence Rule

A central tenet of patent litigation is that there must be “receptive evidence” – that is, truth‑finding evidence that can be examined and probed under discovery and trial. The *Finesse* decision reiterates that appellate courts will not be satisfied with mere “expert opinion” that is not anchored in observable engineering practice. This signals that future cases involving sophisticated telecommunications technologies, where experts may lean on internal specs or non‑public documents, will be scrutinized more fiercely.

### 3.2 Claim Construction vs. Technical Proof

This opinion reminds litigators that claim construction, while crucial, is only the tip of the iceberg. The Fifth Circuit, echoing the Supreme Court’s approach in *Chevron* and *Harris v. Holbrook*, insists that a patent’s scope cannot be stretched beyond the evidence that “demonstrates” support for the accused implementation. The *Finesse* case neatly separates the appellate court’s de novo review (no new evidence) from the jury’s factual findings (substantial evidence) and shows how a misreading of claim language can be outweighed by the absence of supporting data.

### 3.3 Precedent for the Fifth Circuit and Beyond

Because the Fifth Circuit’s law on JMOL remains distinctive within the federal appellate system, *Finesse* carries weight in other Fifth Circuit venues—Texas, Arkansas, Louisiana, and Mississippi. Telecommunications firms operating in these states will now be more cautious about relying on a “broad” expert testimony that does not directly tie into observable circuitry unless they can show, on the record, that the accused device’s operations truthfully implement the patented steps.

## 4. Potential Downstream Effects

### 4.1 Shift Toward “Signal‑by‑Signal” Evidence

Manufacturers may need to capture and preserve more granular evidence, perhaps through hardware‑in‑the‑loop recordings, firmware logs, or direct circuit diagrams, rather than relying on field‑specs or internal design memos. Courts will treat these materials with less skepticism, thereby tightening the evidentiary threshold for cases involving signal processing patents.

### 4.2 Greater Emphasis on Third‑Party Witnesses

Where internal expertise is murky, expert witnesses who have independently verified a product’s architecture will carry more weight. In *Finesse*, the appellate court found Dr. Wells’s reliance on a Nokia document insufficient. Third‑party engineers who can provide corroborating test results or independent simulations stand a better chance of supporting the jury’s findings.

### 4.3 Strategic Settlement Considerations

Lawsuits that thus far have hinged on contested expert testimony may find settlement more attractive. By negotiating outside court, parties can avoid the risk that a judge will throw out a verdict that is not underpinned by robust evidence. The *Finesse* outcome may encourage courts to be more terse in encouraging settlements when the evidence is thin.

## 5. A Cautionary Note for Litigators

The appellate panel’s reversal underscores that each element of a patent claim remains gatekeeping. A well‑drafted claim that requires three distinct, separately identifiable signals and seven specific multiplications will still be subject to the “only if evidence demonstrates” requirement. Petitioner firms, therefore, should:

1. **Document Every Implementation Detail**: From the first sampling stage right through to the last cancelation step, forensic evidence must be at hand.
2. **Avoid “Legend‑Based” Interpretations**: As *Finesse* showed, mapping signals to claims via a document legend is fragile; the mapping must be explicit in both the technical evidence and the expert testimony.
3. **Cross‑Check Against Independent Knowledge**: Experts should verify that their assertions are not merely echoing internal documents but are substantiated by independent analysis or testing.

## 6. Final Thoughts

*Finesse Wireless* is a textbook illustration of the Fifth Circuit’s insistence on the two‑step JMOL review. In the age of increasingly complex signal‑processing patents, claiming infringement is no longer a matter of “does the claim read right?”—it is also about “do we have the honest, testable evidence that the accused device actually does this.” As telecommunications firms and patent holders adjust to this reality, the Seventh‑and‑Above Courts will be better served by tightening the evidentiary fabric that supports a jury’s verdict, ensuring that only those claims truly backed by clear, observable facts survive appellate scrutiny.
