Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What is the significance of the court’s analysis of the seven third‑order multiplications in the ’775 patent, and how does the lack of a mapping to the Nokia document’s operations affect the infringement verdict?
Question:
The opinion discusses how Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document that listed only three multiplications. I’m not clear on why showing that the accused device performs ‘any’ of the seven multiplications is insufficient for infringement when the court says the device must perform *all* of them. How does the claim wording enforce that requirement?
Answer:
**Answer**

The question hinges on the claim‑construction of the ’775 patent’s key limitation—*“generating the n‑th order IC​S by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3 and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer”* (claim 1, 16‑54‑17).  

The *“seven third‑order multiplications”* are **not** a per‑multiplication limitation; they are the *comprehensive* set of multiplications that the claim requires the method to execute. The claim itself says that **all** seven specified products must be formed. That is why the court treats the limitation as “the seven multiplications must take place” (see the paragraph beginning “The key claim limitation at issue on appeal is that seven third‑order multiplications of the three signals must take place to meet the claim limitation.”).

Because the claim enumerates every multiplication, a device that performs **any** subset of those products is insufficient to satisfy limit­ation 1[d]. The court therefore treats the claim as a “whole‑product” restriction that cannot be satisfied by merely showing the device performs one or two of the multiplications. The language of the claim ("**including** generating an n‑th order IC​S **by** digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3 and S2×S3×S3") leaves no room for interpretation that the device “generates at least three of the seven” or “generates any three of the seven.” The claim explicitly lists *all* seven, and the court characters it as a “comprehensive set.” See the court’s discussion that follows the claim: “the key claim limitation at issue is that seven third‑order multiplications … must take place… The jury found all asserted claims of the ’775 patent infringed” (J.A. 169). The court proves that the burden of showing **all seven** was not met by the evidence presented at trial.

---

### Why the lack of a mapping between the device’s operations and the seven claimed multiplications matters

At trial, Dr. Wells used a Nokia document that listed **three distinct multiplications**:

1. \(x_1\cdot |x_1|\cdot |x_1|\)  
2. \(x_1\cdot x_1\cdot x_2'\)  
3. \(x_1\cdot |x_2|\cdot |x_2|\)  

Those operations do not match the textbook multiplications enumerated in the claim. The court pointed out that the Nokia document “does not show the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications” and that “there is no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed” (J.A. 26486).  

Because the claim requires **all seven** particular products, the court required that the expert testify not only that the device performs the claimed multiplications but also *how the device’s actual calculations map onto each of the seven products*. In the opinion, the court notes that Dr. Wells “did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications” (J.A. 14966–70). This omission renders the expert’s testimony an *inadequate basis* for the jury’s inference that the device performed the full set of needed multiplications.  

In patent litigation, the jury’s burden is to find infringement **based on the construction of each claim limitation**. If the court constructs a limitation to mean “all seven multiplications must take place,” a single or partial performance of the claim’s essential function is insufficient. The expert therefore must produce *correspondence data* that demonstrates how the device’s actual algorithm implements each of the seven claim‑specified multiplications. The Nokia document, lacking that correspondence, did not satisfy the requirement that the device “generates the n‑th order IC​S by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering **all** of the seven products.” That mapping was required, because the claim uses absolute language: “including generating an n‑th order IC​S by … and **S2×S3×S3**,” not “by generating an n‑th order IC​S by generating at least one of ...”.  

---

### Bottom line

1. **Claim construction**: The ’775 claim expressly enumerates seven distinct third‑order multiplications that *must* all occur. The language of the claim and the court’s construction treat the requirement as a *comprehensive* set, not a “select several” or “any subset.”  

2. **Expert evidence**: The expert’s testimony was insufficient because it identified only three multiplications from a Nokia document that do not correspond to the seven claim‑required products, and it did not provide an explicit mapping of those operations to each of the seven required multiplications. The lack of such a mapping prevents the jury from concluding that the accused device satisfied the claim limitation.  

3. **Infringement verdict**: Because the evidence did not support that the accused device performed *all* seven multiplications, the jury’s infringement verdict cannot be sustained. The court therefore reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award.  

In short, the significance of the “seven third‑order multiplications” is that the claim requires *every* one of them. The expert’s failure to map the device’s real‑world operations onto those seven products makes the infringement finding unsupported.
