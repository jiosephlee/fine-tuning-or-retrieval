Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What standard does the Fifth Circuit use when reviewing a district court’s denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) on infringement?
Question:
The opinion cites Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc. for de novo review. I’m trying to understand whether the court still checks whether the evidence is substantial, or if the de novo review replaces that requirement. How exactly does the court evaluate ‘substantial evidence’ in the context of JMOL versus a factual finding?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit’s review of a district court’s denial of *Judgment as a Matter of Law* (JMOL) for infringement is *de novo*.  The court applies the *de novo* standard set forth in *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016).  The de novo review does not eliminate the substantial‑evidence requirement; rather, it simply gives the appellate court the same evidentiary standard that a jury would face, but in a more “light‑favorable‑to‑the-verdict” view.

### 1.  De novo review

In *Wi‑LAN* the court held that when a district court denies a motion for JMOL, the appellate court must:
> “review the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict and determine whether the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” 811 F.3d at 461.

Thus the appellate court is not bound by the district court’s finding; it may look anew at all of the evidence in the record—including jury instructions, exhibits, expert testimony, and the parties’ arguments—to decide whether, under the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence supports the findings.

### 2.  Substantial‑evidence requirement

The de novo standard is applied only to the *same* standard that governs a jury’s factual findings: substantial evidence.  As the Ninth‑Circuit case *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022) explains, a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a “reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.”  The court in *Finesse Wireless* adopts this test explicitly:

> “We review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence.”  (par. 24)

In the instant case the appellate court first considered whether the evidence *supported* the jury’s infringement convictions.  The jury had found the ’134 and ’775 patents infringed, but the court found the evidence was insufficient: Dr. Wells’ testimony was contradictory, he did not clearly map the required signals, and the Nokia documents did not demonstrate the requisite multiplications.  Under the substantial‑evidence test, the court concluded that “no reasonable jury could have found” infringement and so reversed the denial of JMOL.

### 3.  What the court actually looks for

Under the de novo, substantial‑evidence framework, the court asks:

1. **Is there any evidence that could support the verdict?**  
   The court examines all evidence that the trial court deemed admissible and considers it in the light most favorable to the verdict.

2. **Does the evidence point “so strongly and overwhelmingly” to a particular outcome?**  
   The court must be convinced that *reasonable jurors* could not reach a contrary conclusion.  If the evidence is ambiguous or contradictory—as in the Dr. Wells testimony—the court will treat it as insufficient.

3. **Did the trial court err in its factual findings?**  
   The appellate court may consider claims of clear or evident error (e.g., if the trial court accepted testimony that logically contradicted other evidence).  The *Wi‑LAN* court allows this when the evidence shows “no reasonable jury could have found” the contrary conclusion.

In *Finesse*, the appellate court applied all three steps.  It found that Dr. Wells’ testimony was “confusing and unclear” and that the expert’s contradictory statements made the evidence “insufficient to satisfy the substantial‑evidence standard.”  Consequently, even though the appellate court was reviewing the action *de novo*, it concluded that the evidence did not support a reasonable jury’s infringement finding.

### 4.  Bottom line for a student

* The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL **de novo**, not unaided by the district court’s reasoning.  
* The appellate court still applies the **substantial‑evidence** standard that governs a jury’s factual findings.  
* The de novo test does **not** replace the substantial‑evidence requirement; it simply gives the appellate court the authority to apply that requirement afresh, in the most favorable light to the verdict.

So when you see a court reverse a denial of JMOL, read the opinion carefully to see whether the court found the evidence “pointed so strongly and overwhelmingly” in favor of one party and whether it applied the substantial‑evidence standard under that light.  In *Finesse*, that was exactly what happened: the evidence was deemed too weak to support an infringement verdict, and the denial of JMOL was reversed.
