Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# 1. Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties

The plaintiff in this case is **Finesse Wireless LLC** (“Finesse”), a company that owns and maintains the U.S. patents at issue. The defendants are **AT&T Mobility LLC** (“AT&T”) and **Nokia of America Corporation** (“Nokia”). Finesse alleges that the accused radios, marketed under the Nokia brand and sold to AT&T customers, incorporated infringing technology that was invented and patented by Finesse.

Nokia played a dual role. Initially a party to the litigation, it later intervened as a third‑party intervenor, explaining that it had sourced the design of the radios that were the target of Finesse’s infringement claim. Nokia’s intervention did not alter the substantive claims of the parties; rather, it provided technical documentation that was later used by both sides in federal court.

## Description of the Patents

Two U.S. patents are central to the dispute:

1. **U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134** (the “’134 patent”) claims a method for mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio receivers. The invention isolates “signals of interest” (the desired baseband signals) from “interference generating signals” (signals that, when mixed, generate new frequencies inside the band of interest) and then produces a computed estimate of the resultant IMPs. The estimate is subtracted from the received signal in closed loop, using sub‑sample phase shifts, to cancel the interference.

2. **U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775** (the “’775 patent”) claims a method for reducing passive IMPs in a receiver by digitally multiplying three signals in seven distinct third‑order products. The multiplication generates an “IMP cancellation signal” (ICS) that is subtracted from the receiver’s output, thereby nullifying the passive IMP arising from the physical non‑linearity of the transmitter and receiver components.

Both patents address the same technical problem—passive interference that arises when transmission and reception take place on the same hardware or adjacent bands. The core novelty lies in how the interference is identified, estimated, and subtracted. The ‘134 patent focuses on a signal‑domain estimation approach, while the ’775 patent focuses on a digital multiplication approach.

## Allegations of Infringement

Finesse alleged that AT&T’s customers were using Nokia radios equipped with a **PIM‑C (Passive‑Intermodulation Cancellation) feature**. The PIM‑C feature was designed to detect passive intermodulation and cancel it in real time. Finesse’s claim was that the PIM‑C feature implements the methods set forth in both the ’134 and ’775 patents. The alleged infringement was based on the following factual elements:

*  The accused radios implement software **“decimating filters”** to separate signals of interest from interference‑generating signals, as set out in the ’134 patent.

*  The accused radios perform **digital multiplications** of three signals in a manner consistent with the ’775 patent, particularly the seven third‑order products required by the claim.

Finesse relied on expert testimony from Dr. Jonathan Wells, whose testimony focused on how the Nokia technical documentation illustrated that the accused radios performed the necessary operations. The jury found that both patent claims were valid and infringed, culminating in a $166,303,391 lump‑sum damages award for the remaining life of the patents.

## District Court Proceedings

The case proceeded through the **United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas** (EDTX). The trial court featured a jury that:

1. **Found both patents valid and infringed.**

2. **Rendered a judgment on damages in the form of a lump‑sum award of $166,303,391** to Finesse, covering the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents.

3. **Denied motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on infringement and on damages.** The defendants (AT&T and Nokia) moved on multiple occasions for JMOL and for a new trial, arguing that the evidence presented did not support the verdict. The district court, however, upheld the jury’s findings.

The defendants’ appellate claims were therefore centered on two main issues: (i) whether a district court may deny JMOL when the evidence is insufficient to support a jury verdict of infringement, and (ii) whether a district court may deny a motion for a new trial on damages after the jury has found infringement.

Section 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) gives the Federal Circuit jurisdiction to review the merits of the appeal because the case concerns intellectual‑property questions that would have been adjudicated in the U.S. Court of Appeals that originally heard the case. Accordingly, the case was brought before the **United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit**. The Federal Circuit addressed the regulatory framework for reviewing JMOL and damage determinations under Fifth Circuit precedent, and ultimately reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL and vacated the damages award. The vacated damages award was grounded in the underlying finding that, when reviewed de novo, there was **no substantial evidence** that the accused radios performed the claimed operations per the patents.

# 2. Legal Framework for JMOL and Damages Review

The appellate court’s mandate is to assess a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and a denial of a new‑trial motion on damages. These two remedies are governed by slightly different standards, but both fall under the umbrella of “review de novo” for JMOL and “abuse of discretion” for new‑trial rulings. The following sections lay out the controlling jurisdictional authorities—primarily the Federal Circuit’s own precedent and, where the case was first heard in the Eastern District of Texas, Fifth Circuit guidance—and explain how these standards apply to the Finesse Wireless case.

## Fed. Cir. and Fifth Circuit Law on JMOL Review (Wi‑LAN, Apple Inc.)

The Federal Circuit has long embraced a de novo review of a district court’s denial of JMOL. In *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016), the court clarified that a denial of JMOL is reviewed as if the appellate court were the judge and the jury for the issue at hand. The court must look at the evidence “in the light most favorable to the judgment” that was obtained at trial and decide whether the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that a reasonable jury could not have found otherwise. The Fifth Circuit, which has appellate jurisdiction over the Eastern District of Texas where the case was heard, followed that doctrine and reiterated it in *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). The Fifth Circuit emphasized the same principle: a denial of JMOL is reversed when the evidence is so heavily weighted toward one side that no reasonable jurors could have reached the opposite conclusion.

Because the district court in the Finesse case denied the defendants’ JMOL motions on both the ’134 and ’775 patents, the appellate court must conduct a de novo review under the Fifth Circuit’s standard, applying the same evidentiary test that the *Wi‑LAN / Apple* cases established.

## Standard for Substantial Evidence and De novo Review of Denial of JMOL

The phrase “substantial evidence” is frequently invoked when a party moves to have a jury verdict “reaffirmed or overturned” after trial. In the context of a denials of JMOL, the Federal Circuit has clarified that a jury’s finding of infringement is *subject to* the standard of substantial evidence. In *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN*, the court explained that a jury’s finding is only defensible if a “reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” The evidentiary benchmark is therefore lower than a pre‑judgment *preponderance of the evidence* threshold: it suffices that the evidence be *sufficient* to plausibly support the jury’s conclusion, without requiring that the evidence be conclusive.

In the Finesse ruling, the appellate court applied this standard to the testimony of the expert Dr. Wells. Because the court found that the evidence was inadequate to show that the accused radios sampled the required signals, it concluded that the jury’s verdict did not meet the substantial‑evidence threshold and reversed the denials of JMOL.

## Substantial Evidence Under Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN

Apple’s decision in *Wi‑LAN* restated the exact criteria that a jury’s findings must satisfy to survive an appeal. First, the appellate court must locate the evidence that *supports* the verdict and view it in the light most favorable to that verdict. Second, the court must determine whether a *reasonable jury* could have reached the same conclusion in light of that evidence. Third, if the evidence is *clear and convincing* and suffices to support the verdict, the appellate court must defer to the jury: the denial of JMOL would be correct. Conversely, if the evidence is so weak that no reasonable jury could have reached the opposite finding, the appellate court must reverse.

The Finesse case follows this line of reasoning closely. The appellate court concluded that the record’s expert testimony was contradictory and insufficiently detailed to satisfy *substantial evidence* for infringement of the ’134 and ‘775 patents. Because the district court’s denial of JMOL was based on the same evidence, the appellate court applied the *substantial‑evidence* test, found the record lacking, and reversed.

## Rule 50(a) Motions and Abuse of Discretion for New‑Trial Denial

Rule 50(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure governs motions for JMOL, while Rule 54(b) governs motions for a new trial on damages. The appellate court treats a denial of a new‑trial motion for damages as a question of *abuse of discretion*, not de novo. In *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, the Federal Circuit held that a district court’s denial of a new‑trial request for damages is reviewed for abuse of discretion, applied as “the court must examine the state of the record and the formulation of the new‑trial request to determine whether the court has acted within its discretion.” Essentially, the lower court may consider whether the evidence is so weak that a new trial is warranted, or whether the jury’s findings are sufficiently credible to support the damages award.

In the Finesse opinion, the appellate court noted that the denial of new‑trial motions on damages was made only *after* the court had already reversed the JMOL denials. Because the appellate court found no substantial evidence for infringement, it was compelled to vacate the damages award, finding that the damages determination itself rested on a jury verdict that could not be sustained. This imputed denial of the new‑trial motion fell within the *abuse‑of‑discretion* framework established in *Wi‑LAN*, because the damages award would be reversed automatically when infringement was found not to exist.

---

In sum, the appellate review of a denial of JMOL and of a denial of a new trial for damages is governed by a two‑tiered framework: (1) a de novo review that applies the *substantial evidence* standard, and (2) an abuse‑of‑discretion standard for new‑trial motions on damages. The Finesse case follows both of these precedents, ultimately reversing the denial of JMOL on the ’134 and ’775 patents and vacating the associated damages award.

# 3. Analysis of the §134 Patent Claims  

This chapter dissects the appellate court’s treatment of the **’134 patent** in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T*.  The court’s reversal of the lower‑court denial of JMOL hinges on three key analytical steps: (1) the factual limitation that the accused radios must “sample a passband of received signals that includes both signals of interest and interference‑generating signals”; (2) the weight given to the expert testimony of Dr. Jonathan Wells concerning the RF‑ADC’s sampling behavior; and (3) the application of the “substantial evidence” standard to decide whether a reasonable jury could have concluded infringement.  Throughout, the appellate court applies Sixth Circuit procedural precedent and the Fifth Circuit’s approach to the standard for insufficient evidence in a JMOL motion.

## 3.1 Claim Structure and Limitation 1[a]

The claimed method is predicated on two sequential operations: **(a)** sampling a passband that contains *both* the desired signal (the “signal of interest”) and the signal that will give rise to passive intermodulation (the “interference‑generating signal”), and **(b)** using that sampled data to compute and cancel the resulting intermodulation products.  
Limitation 1[a] expressly requires that the **RF analog‑to‑digital converter (RF‑ADC)** in the accused radio’s receiver must **sample a *passband* of received signals that *includes* the interference‑generating signal** in addition to the signal of interest.  The pay‑off is that the interference‑generating signal is *generated* in the vicinity of the RF‑ADC’s input – it must exist before the ADC samples.  The court notes that this is a *factual* limitation that must be satisfied for any infringement liability.

Because the claim is expressly limited to a device that samples “a passband of received signals” that includes **both** sets of signals, the burden of proof rests squarely on the plaintiff (**Finesse**) to establish that the accused radios meet this limitation.  The patent’s language and the court’s analysis show standard claim construction sufficiency: the term “interference‑generating signals” is understood as *any* received signal capable of producing intermodulation products in the passband, not *post‑processing* model signals that appear only after ADC sampling.

## 3.2 Finesse’s Burden and Dr. Wells’ Testimony on RF‑ADC Sampling

Finesse deployed Dr. Wells as its infringement expert, who testified that the Nokia radios in question do indeed **sample both the signal of interest and the interference‑generating signal**.  Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia technical document that diagrammed the internal signal flow within the radio.  In the document, a “red–path” leads from the call‑for‑transmission (DL‑TX) reference and a modeled PIM path into the RF‑ADC block.

### Key Points of Dr. Wells’ testimony

1. **Mapping of the red–path** – He described the red–path as comprising the “DL‑TX reference” (identified as the signal of interest) and a “model PIM path” (identified as the interference‑generating signal).  
2. **Sampling claim** – Dr. Wells asserted that the **RF‑ADC samples the passband that includes both these signals**.  
3. **Reconciliation of technical terms** – When cross‑examined, Dr. Wells noted that the model PIM path is **generated downstream of the RF‑ADC** (i.e., after the ADC has already sampled the raw received signals).  He then pivoted, claiming that there are “two signals on the red path” because they represent two *nodes* (the DL‑TX reference and the modeled PIM path).  

The appellate court treats Dr. Wells’ statements as *contradictory*.  His initial assertion that the RF‑ADC samples a red path that *contains* both signals conflicts with the fact that the model PIM path is **not physically present** at the ADC input; it is computed later in the signal chain.  The cross‑examination exposes this inconsistency, yet the expert’s subsequent re‑characterization of the two signals as merely “x1 and x2” does not resolve the fundamental conflict: the claim requires the **actual input** to the ADC, not a modeled representation.

Because of this confusion, the court determines that Dr. Wells’ testimony does not provide a *clear, unambiguous* demonstration that the RF‑ADC **did** sample the interference‑generating signal as required by limitation 1[a].

## 3.3 Analysis of Dr. Wells’ Contradictory or Ambiguous Testimony

The appellate court applies the **substantial evidence** standard to the expert’s testimony.  This standard, echoing *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN*, requires that a reasonable jury could find in favor of the plaintiff *in light of the evidence presented at trial*.  The court notes:

1. **Lack of direct mapping** – Dr. Wells never testified that the two signals on the red path (x1 and x2) *were* the specific signals required by the claim.  He described them only as “two signals” that correspond to the DL‑TX reference and the modeled PIM path, but never said that the modeled PIM path is the *interference‑generating signal* in the sense required by the patent.  
2. **Persistent contradiction** – Even after the cross‑examination prompted him to discuss the nature of the modeled PIM path, Dr. Wells continued to refer to it as an interference‑generating signal without clarifying how it could be present at the ADC input.  
3. **No clear, corroborative evidence** – The Nokia document does not show that the RF‑ADC samples a real‑time in‑band signal that later becomes the modeled PIM path; instead it depicts a *post‑sampling* model.  

The court compares this situation to *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, where an expert’s contradictory testimony was deemed insufficient to support a claim of infringement.  By that precedent, the appellate court concludes that **the evidence was not “so strong and overwhelmingly in favor of the plaintiff that the jury could not have reached an opposite conclusion.”**  The court therefore finds that the jury’s verdict was **not supported by substantial evidence**.

## 3.4 Application of the Substantial‑Evidence Standard

Because Dr. Wells’ testimony is ambiguous and contradictory, the appellate court determines that for a reasonable jury to have found infringement in a *light‑favorable* view of the evidence was impossible.  The court sees the entire record — the cross‑exam questions, the expert’s responses, and the patent’s own limitation — as *insufficient* to meet the standard.

The appellate court emphasizes that a jury may consider *all* evidence, including the RF‑ADC’s architecture and the Nokia technical document, but the expert testimony fails to clarify the core factual question: did the RF‑ADC actually sample an interference‑generating signal?  Because that question was *not answered* in the evidence, the court applies the “substantial evidence” rule to reverse the JMOL denial.

## 3.5 Reversal of JMOL Denial for §134

Consistent with the analysis above, the appellate court **reverses** the district court’s denial of JMOL for infringement of the **’134 patent**.  The reversal is premised on the finding that:

- Finesse failed to meet its burden to prove that the claim limitation regarding ADC sampling was satisfied.  
- Dr. Wells’ testimony was contradictory and did not convincingly establish that the claimed interference‑generating signals were present at the ADC input.  
- Consequently, **no reasonable jury could have reached a verdict of infringement** in light of the evidence.  

With this reversal, the damages award associated with the ‘134 patent is also vacated, because it was based on an unsupported infringement judgment.  The appeal thus illustrates the critical importance of clear, unambiguous expert testimony when a claim contains a *factual limitation* such as limitation 1[a] of the **’134** patent.

# 4. Analysis of the §775 Patent Claims

## Claim limit: seven third‑order multiplications of three signals

The core of the ’775 patent is a method for cancelling passive intermodulation (PIM) in a receiver. Claim 1 requires that the device **digitally multiply three signals in seven multiplications** to generate intermodulation‑product cancellation signals (ICSs). The “three signals” are denoted S1, S2, and S3. The claim further specifies that the seven multiplications are third‑order, that is, they involve the product of three signals with possible repetitions, such as S1 × S1 × S2 or S1 × S2 × S3, etc. (see claim language at 16:54–17:6). The claim places two critical constraints on the accused device: (a) it must use three **separately identifiable** signals as inputs to the multiplication operations, and (b) it must perform all seven of the specified third‑order multiplications among those signals. The claim does **not** insist that the three signals be *distinct* physical waveforms; rather, it requires that the device be able to distinguish them, even if two are identical. This distinction is central to the district court’s construction and the appellate court’s subsequent analysis.

## District court construction of S1, S2, and S3

The district court applied the **Rule 33** evidence‑based approach to claim construction. The judge concluded that the intrinsic evidence—primarily the claim language and the specification—supports a reading that the three signals be “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique.” The court reasoned that the “two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, satisfies ‘separately identifiable’.” Accordingly, the court sided with Finesse that the device could use two distinct, physical signals (x1 and x2 in the Nokia documentation) and a copy of one of them (e.g., a duplicate of x1) as the third signal. The court rejected the appellant’s objection that the claim requires truly unique signals, noting that Appellants failed to raise this argument at Rule 50(a) and that the examiner’s rejection of the “digitally multiplying two or three signals” claim in the first round of examination was *overcome or narrowed* by the applicant’s subsequent amendment and the final allowance. This construction formed the backbone of the jury’s infringement verdict: if the accused device uses two independently identifiable signals and a copy, it satisfies the first limitation; if it additionally carries out the required multiplications, it satisfies the second.

## Dr. Wells’ testimony based on Nokia documentation – mapping of multiplications

Dr. Jonathan Wells, Finesse’s expert on signal processing, testified that the Nokia radios incorporate the claimed multiplication logic. His testimony was heavily anchored in a Nokia internal technical document (cited as “Nokia document” in the record). The document lists ten **distinct multiplications** that the Nokia receiver apparently computes, expressed in symbolic form (e.g., x1 × |x1| × |x1|, x1 × x1 × x2′, x1 × |x2| × |x2|, …). Dr. Wells asserted that **three** of these multiplications correspond to the seven third‑order multiplications required by the claim. He identified the three distinct operations as:

1. x1 × |x1| × |x1|,
2. x1 × x1 × x2′,
3. x1 × |x2| × |x2|.

Wells explained that the rest of the ten multiplications are **duplicates** of these three, in that each of the seven required multiplications can be derived from one of these three base operations through algebraic manipulation or by taking complex conjugates. However, the record shows no explicit mapping from the three base operations to the full set of seven claimed multiplications. The judge and the jury were led to infer that, because the device performs the ten operations shown in the document, it necessarily performs all seven claimed multiplications. This inference was the crux of the jury’s decision.

## Finesse’s claim‑surrender argument and examiner rejection

Finesse argued that, during prosecution, it **surrendered** claim scope by narrowing the claim from “two or three signals” to “three signals” in order to survive the examiner’s rejection of the broader language over the Filipovic prior art reference. The examiner had rejected an earlier version of the claim that allowed the device to use two signals because Filipovic taught a two‑signal cancellation method; the claim was amended to require “three signals” to distinguish the device from Filipovic. Finesse maintained that this amendment did not *surrender* the possibility that the device could use only two distinct signals, only that the claim required some way of presenting three signals to the multipliers—either three distinct signals or a duplicate of one of them. The appellate court, applying the **Clarence-Smith** analysis, found that the amendment did not unambiguously disavow all two‑signal embodiments. Rather, it merely set a **minimum** of “three separately identifiable” inputs, which could be realized by two unique waveforms plus a copy. Therefore, the court deemed that the claim was not narrowed in a way that would preclude the accused device’s alleged structure.

## Evidence of unperformed seven multiplications – lack of mapping

Appellants contested that the Nokia device, as described by Dr. Wells, did **not** perform seven third‑order multiplications. They pointed out that the Nokia document does not show any multiplication of **x1** and **x2** directly; it lists only multiplications involving *modulus* or *complex conjugates* of x2. Moreover, the three multiplications highlighted by Dr. Wells, when expanded, do not yield seven distinct third‑order products: each contains only a single non‑trivial operator (e.g., a modulus). As a result, no **direct accounting** exists for the seven distinct third‑order terms (such as S1 × S1 × S2, S1 × S2 × S2, etc.) that the claim explicitly enumerates. The appellate court held that, absent a clear mapping, no reasonable jury could have found the accused device performed the full set of multiplications. The court emphasized that the claim requires **all** seven multiplications; providing partial proof or an equivalent set of operations without demonstrating they cover every required term is insufficient. This aligns with the doctrine that a claim’s scope is broadened only by *identical* or *equivalent* structures, not by a vague equivalence or an incomplete performance.

## Reversal of JMOL denial for §775

Because the evidence failed to establish the performance of the seven required multiplications, the appellate court held that the **substantial evidence** standard—under which a jury verdict of infringement must rest—was not satisfied. The evidence is simply insufficient to support the jury’s conclusion that the accused devices carried out all the mandated multiplication operations. Consequently, the district court’s denial of JMOL was reversed, and the infringement ruling on the ’775 patent was vacated. This reversal also carried over to the damages award: the appellate court vacated the damages award associated with the ’775 claims, because the infringement was found unsupported.

In short, the court found that the claim limitation on **three signals and seven multiplications** was interpreted to permit a two‑signal infrastructure with a duplicate, that Dr. Wells’ testimony failed to map the Nokia device’s multiplications onto the claim’s seven specifications, and therefore the jury’s verdict could not rest on substantial evidence. The appellate court reversed the JMOL denial and vacated the associated damages.

# 5. Damages Reversal and Its Connection to JMOL

In this chapter we explain the appellate court’s reasoning that led to the vacatur of the district court’s substantial damages award. The decision is rooted in the fundamental rule that a damage award can be sustained only if the underlying infringement determination is valid. The court applied the Fifth Circuit “abuse‑of‑discretion” standard for reviewing a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial on damages, and then explained how the reversal of the JMOL findings erased the factual basis for the award.

## Damages review under Fifth Circuit “abuse of discretion” standard

Section 504(p)(2) of the Patent Act grants federal district courts broad discretion to decide how to evaluate damages for patent infringement. The Fifth Circuit has reviewed a denial of a new‑trial motion on damages as an “abuse of discretion” inquiry, not a de novo review. In *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016), the Court emphasized that the appellate court should presume that the district court exercised its discretion properly unless “there is some compelling reason that the decision was made in error.” The standard is therefore deferential; the appellate court excites only when the district court’s decision is “surprisingly unreasonable, lacking any logical foundation, or are devoid of any factual basis.”

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* the new‑trial motion on damages was denied on the same ground. The appellate court found no evidence that the district court erred in its judgment or that it misapplied any legal standard. The court thus accepted the district court’s deference to its own findings, pending the judgment that the infringement conclusions themselves were insufficient.

## Judgment on damages tied to infringement conclusions

Patent‑law damages are intrinsically linked to the notion of infringement. An award, whether for lost profits, royalties, or liquidated damages, is premised on the underlying assumption that the accused conduct infringed the plaintiff’s claims. A “finding of infringement” is a factual determination that the accused party performed the patented steps in a manner that falls within the marked scope of the claims. That finding is the “ownable result” that a damages fee is calculated from.

The district court’s jury had found all of Finesse’s asserted claims to be infringed and awarded $166 million in lump‑sum damages for the remaining life of the ’134 and ’775 patents. The appellate court therefore treated the damages award as “consequent upon a valid infringement finding” and applied the typical *Finesse* rationale: “a damage award can be sustained only if the infringing finding upon which it is premised is valid.” Accordingly, the burden shifted to the appellate court: if the infringement finding was reversed, the damage award could not continue.

## Effect of reversing JMOL on the damages award

The appellate court reversed the district court’s denial of the JMOL motions for both the ’134 and ’775 patents. With those reversals, the court held that no reasonable jury could have found the plaintiff’s devices infringed the claims. In the Fifth Circuit’s *Wi‑LAN* framework, reversing a JMOL fundamentally alters the factual matrix surrounding the case. The “innocence” of the accused devices means the basis for the damage award—an infringement liability—no longer exists.

Once the infringement foundation is removed, the appellate court recognized that the lump‑sum award was no longer supported by any surviving evidence. The court noted that the damages estimate was for the “remaining life” of the patents, a calculation that is only meaningful if the patents are actually being infringed and if the parameters for determining the remaining life (e.g., patent term, product life cycle) are still applicable. Because the infringement determination was overturned, the court effectively deleted the “remaining life” variable from the equation. In the absence of an infringement finding, the alleged “time‐limited” damage amount could not be justified.

## Decision to vacate lump‑sum damages

Facing the reversal of the infringement conclusions, the court proceeded orally to explain its remedy. The appropriate appellate remedy in such circumstances is a vacatur of the damage award. Vacating the award is the most direct means of restoring the parties to the position they would have had if the infringement had not been found. The court therefore issued a vacatur (“vacate the damages award”), restoring the parties to the pre‑trial status concerning financial liability, while leaving the infringement findings themselves for reconsideration on remand.

The court issued a final order reversing the denial of JMOL and vacating the award. The decision exemplifies the court’s “abuse‑of‑discretion” review: the appellate court did not rebate the damages award on the merits of a new damages analysis; it simply erased the award because the underlying infringement finding (the pivot of the award) was found lacking. In other words, the damages award was vacated *because* the infringement finding was reversed—an illustration of the inseparable link between damages and the validity of the infringement judgment.

# 6. Practical Take‑aways and Significance

The *Finesse Wireless* decision is a rich teaching example for advanced students of patent law. While the opinion itself revolves around two specific patents and the conduct of a single expert, the doctrinal lessons reach far beyond the facts. The court’s conclusions touch on three separately‑recognizable pillars of modern infringement litigation: the weight of expert testimony, the effect of claim amendments on infringement analysis, and the Supreme Court‑endorsed “substantial‑evidence” threshold for supporting a jury verdict when the court faces a JMOL argument. Below, we distill each of those pillars and provide a brief cost‑benefit framework for litigants who must decide how to craft claims and expert notebooks in their own cases.

---

## Role of Expert Clarity and Consistency in Supporting Infringement Verdicts

The *Finesse* case demonstrates that the standing doctrine—“the expert must be more than a whisper in the background” (see *O’Hare Guarneri v. Bozzano*, 408 F.3d 768)—is not a vague admonition but a strict standard. The expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, intended to bridge a technical gap between the claimed elements of the *134* patent and the accused Nokia radios. His testimony, however, was riddled with contradictions:

1. **Misalignment of “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.”** Wells claimed that the RF ADC sampled “both” sets of signals, but later admitted that the “modeled PIM path” was generated **after** sampling, thus could not be an interference‑generating signal per claim limitation 1[a].  
2. **Unclear mapping of *x1* and *x2* to the claimed signals** (DL(TX) reference vs. modeled PIM path). The expert never said explicitly that *x1* is the “signal of interest” and *x2* the “interference‑generating signal.”  
3. **Persistent oscillation between advocates and cross‑examination contexts** left the jury with a “green‑light” but the appellate court found it “substantial evidence‑weaker.”

These issues are not academic. The court applied *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), to find the evidence was insufficient for a reasonable jury to conclude infringement. The appellate court emphasized that when the “part with the burden of proof”—here, the plaintiff—rests on the testimony of an expert who “contradicts themselves,” the evidence falls below the substantial‑evidence threshold.

**Bottom line:** In future cases, experts must log, in a **single, consistent narrative**: the signals or processes being sampled, the order of signal generation relative to sampling, and a clear mapping to each claim limitation. Any relapse can render the entire weight of the expert’s testimony impotent.

---

## Impact of Claim Amendments on Scope of Infringement Analysis

The *Finesse* opinion clarifies that amendments need not collapse on the original claim scope, but may sharpen or shift the tactical focus of infringement analysis. The *775* patent’s claim originally read “*digitally multiplying two or three signals*,” but, after a protracted prosecution, was amended to “*digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2 and S3*.” The appellate court held that this amendment did not unilaterally shift the standard from “two or three signals” to “exactly three signals,” but sharpened the language used for critique.

Two key take‑aways arise:

1. **Claim language itself dictates the *forensic* scrutiny in infringement arguments.** The expert had to prove that the accused device performed *seven* distinct third‑order multiplications involving *three* separately identifiable signals. Because the device only used two unique inputs, the defendant’s argument prevailed. If the claim had left “two or three signals,” a different line of reasoning would have been open.
   
2. **Amendments can be a strategic tool beyond meeting the examiner’s requirements.** In *Finesse*, the plaintiffs exercised the “clear and unambiguous” standard of *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019) to argue that an amendment did not dispel the scope to include “two input” devices. Plaintiffs should remember that a late‑stage amendment to tighten the claim may help clarify the infringement space *but* may equally constrain plaintiff expectations if the equipment does not technically meet the tightened language.

---

## How the Court’s Application of Substantial Evidence Affects Future Cases

The overarching framework for the court’s review—titled *substantial evidence*, *reasonable jurors*, and *viewing evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict*—is not merely an academic dogma; it becomes the judge’s litmus test on every JMOL motion. In *Finesse*, the jury’s verdict was deemed insufficient on two fronts: the expert’s testimony lacked adjudicable clarity, and the mapping of claimed multiplications to the technical evidence was nonexistent.

The effects are twofold:

1. **Jury verdicts anchored on contrarian or incomplete testimony can be overturned.** A defendant can cite *Substantial Evidence* to demonstrate the evidence is “too thin or ambiguous” to support any conclusion.  
2. **Plaintiffs must rehearse that “substantial evidence” requires *concrete* causal bridging** between the claim limitation and the accused device’s operation—this is no longer satisfied by *partial* or *probabilistic* cross‑references.

Future litigants will need to pre‑emptively build “evidence chains” into their expert reports: detailed diagrams, step‑by‑step mapping, and independent verification of every claim limitation. Failure to do so invites the same JMOL reversal that *Finesse* suffered.

## Strategic Lessons for Plaintiffs and Defendants in Drafting Claims and Presenting Expert Testimony

### 1. Draft with a View to the *Litigator’s Playbook*
- **Clause‑level clarity:** Use “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” verbatim, and supply legislative analogues in the claims once the examiner’s position is clear.
- **Amend judiciously:** Aim for amendments that preserve, but do not narrow, the underlying technical scope unless you can absolutely demonstrate the accused product will satisfy new constraints.

### 2. Prepare Your Expert Stone Cold
- **Full‑chain mapping:** Your expert must lay out a **step‑by‑step** process from the device’s input to the claimed output, including timing and signal generation sequences.
- **Reconcile cross‑exam costs:** If your technical document has multiple legends (like the “DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path” legend), explicitly state which legend applies to what portion of the claim.

### 3. Embrace “Substantial Evidence” Early
- **Pilot studies:** Build simulations or lab data that mirror the accused device’s actual signal flow.  
- **Narrative consistency:** Draft a closed‑loop explanation that would survive cross‑examination—a moving two‑way bridge between the claims and the evidence.

### 4. For Defendants: Leverage “No Sampling After Generation”
- **Temporal arguments:** Emphasize any technical processes that generate signals *after* the claimed sampling event or process; this is a quick exit route for many faulty infringement arguments.  

### 5. For Plaintiffs: Adopt a “Reversibility” Mindset
- **Draft with reversibility in mind:** If the court is looking for “substantial evidence,” anticipate the possibility of a JMOL motion and fail‑fast on modeling, mapping, and enumeration of operations.

## Closing Reflection

The *Finesse Wireless* opinion teaches that **clarity trumps complexity**. In a litigation where the vast majority of the analyst work is technical, a single expert’s misstep can “decimate” the entire infringement verdict. The appellate court’s focus on substantial evidence and the weight of expert testimony makes clear that **rules of claim construction, expert testimony, and evidence thresholds are more interlocked than ever**. For the modern practitioner, the lesson is straightforward: **draft to the details. Execute in the courts to the detail.**
