Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # 2. Legal Framework for JMOL and Damages Review

The appellate court’s mandate is to assess a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and a denial of a new‑trial motion on damages. These two remedies are governed by slightly different standards, but both fall under the umbrella of “review de novo” for JMOL and “abuse of discretion” for new‑trial rulings. The following sections lay out the controlling jurisdictional authorities—primarily the Federal Circuit’s own precedent and, where the case was first heard in the Eastern District of Texas, Fifth Circuit guidance—and explain how these standards apply to the Finesse Wireless case.

## Fed. Cir. and Fifth Circuit Law on JMOL Review (Wi‑LAN, Apple Inc.)

The Federal Circuit has long embraced a de novo review of a district court’s denial of JMOL. In *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016), the court clarified that a denial of JMOL is reviewed as if the appellate court were the judge and the jury for the issue at hand. The court must look at the evidence “in the light most favorable to the judgment” that was obtained at trial and decide whether the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that a reasonable jury could not have found otherwise. The Fifth Circuit, which has appellate jurisdiction over the Eastern District of Texas where the case was heard, followed that doctrine and reiterated it in *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). The Fifth Circuit emphasized the same principle: a denial of JMOL is reversed when the evidence is so heavily weighted toward one side that no reasonable jurors could have reached the opposite conclusion.

Because the district court in the Finesse case denied the defendants’ JMOL motions on both the ’134 and ’775 patents, the appellate court must conduct a de novo review under the Fifth Circuit’s standard, applying the same evidentiary test that the *Wi‑LAN / Apple* cases established.

## Standard for Substantial Evidence and De novo Review of Denial of JMOL

The phrase “substantial evidence” is frequently invoked when a party moves to have a jury verdict “reaffirmed or overturned” after trial. In the context of a denials of JMOL, the Federal Circuit has clarified that a jury’s finding of infringement is *subject to* the standard of substantial evidence. In *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN*, the court explained that a jury’s finding is only defensible if a “reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” The evidentiary benchmark is therefore lower than a pre‑judgment *preponderance of the evidence* threshold: it suffices that the evidence be *sufficient* to plausibly support the jury’s conclusion, without requiring that the evidence be conclusive.

In the Finesse ruling, the appellate court applied this standard to the testimony of the expert Dr. Wells. Because the court found that the evidence was inadequate to show that the accused radios sampled the required signals, it concluded that the jury’s verdict did not meet the substantial‑evidence threshold and reversed the denials of JMOL.

## Substantial Evidence Under Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN

Apple’s decision in *Wi‑LAN* restated the exact criteria that a jury’s findings must satisfy to survive an appeal. First, the appellate court must locate the evidence that *supports* the verdict and view it in the light most favorable to that verdict. Second, the court must determine whether a *reasonable jury* could have reached the same conclusion in light of that evidence. Third, if the evidence is *clear and convincing* and suffices to support the verdict, the appellate court must defer to the jury: the denial of JMOL would be correct. Conversely, if the evidence is so weak that no reasonable jury could have reached the opposite finding, the appellate court must reverse.

The Finesse case follows this line of reasoning closely. The appellate court concluded that the record’s expert testimony was contradictory and insufficiently detailed to satisfy *substantial evidence* for infringement of the ’134 and ‘775 patents. Because the district court’s denial of JMOL was based on the same evidence, the appellate court applied the *substantial‑evidence* test, found the record lacking, and reversed.

## Rule 50(a) Motions and Abuse of Discretion for New‑Trial Denial

Rule 50(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure governs motions for JMOL, while Rule 54(b) governs motions for a new trial on damages. The appellate court treats a denial of a new‑trial motion for damages as a question of *abuse of discretion*, not de novo. In *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, the Federal Circuit held that a district court’s denial of a new‑trial request for damages is reviewed for abuse of discretion, applied as “the court must examine the state of the record and the formulation of the new‑trial request to determine whether the court has acted within its discretion.” Essentially, the lower court may consider whether the evidence is so weak that a new trial is warranted, or whether the jury’s findings are sufficiently credible to support the damages award.

In the Finesse opinion, the appellate court noted that the denial of new‑trial motions on damages was made only *after* the court had already reversed the JMOL denials. Because the appellate court found no substantial evidence for infringement, it was compelled to vacate the damages award, finding that the damages determination itself rested on a jury verdict that could not be sustained. This imputed denial of the new‑trial motion fell within the *abuse‑of‑discretion* framework established in *Wi‑LAN*, because the damages award would be reversed automatically when infringement was found not to exist.

---

In sum, the appellate review of a denial of JMOL and of a denial of a new trial for damages is governed by a two‑tiered framework: (1) a de novo review that applies the *substantial evidence* standard, and (2) an abuse‑of‑discretion standard for new‑trial motions on damages. The Finesse case follows both of these precedents, ultimately reversing the denial of JMOL on the ’134 and ’775 patents and vacating the associated damages award.
