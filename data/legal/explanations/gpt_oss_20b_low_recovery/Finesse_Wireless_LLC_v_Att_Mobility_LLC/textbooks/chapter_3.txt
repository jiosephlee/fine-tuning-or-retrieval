Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 3: # 3. Analysis of the §134 Patent Claims  

This chapter dissects the appellate court’s treatment of the **’134 patent** in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T*.  The court’s reversal of the lower‑court denial of JMOL hinges on three key analytical steps: (1) the factual limitation that the accused radios must “sample a passband of received signals that includes both signals of interest and interference‑generating signals”; (2) the weight given to the expert testimony of Dr. Jonathan Wells concerning the RF‑ADC’s sampling behavior; and (3) the application of the “substantial evidence” standard to decide whether a reasonable jury could have concluded infringement.  Throughout, the appellate court applies Sixth Circuit procedural precedent and the Fifth Circuit’s approach to the standard for insufficient evidence in a JMOL motion.

## 3.1 Claim Structure and Limitation 1[a]

The claimed method is predicated on two sequential operations: **(a)** sampling a passband that contains *both* the desired signal (the “signal of interest”) and the signal that will give rise to passive intermodulation (the “interference‑generating signal”), and **(b)** using that sampled data to compute and cancel the resulting intermodulation products.  
Limitation 1[a] expressly requires that the **RF analog‑to‑digital converter (RF‑ADC)** in the accused radio’s receiver must **sample a *passband* of received signals that *includes* the interference‑generating signal** in addition to the signal of interest.  The pay‑off is that the interference‑generating signal is *generated* in the vicinity of the RF‑ADC’s input – it must exist before the ADC samples.  The court notes that this is a *factual* limitation that must be satisfied for any infringement liability.

Because the claim is expressly limited to a device that samples “a passband of received signals” that includes **both** sets of signals, the burden of proof rests squarely on the plaintiff (**Finesse**) to establish that the accused radios meet this limitation.  The patent’s language and the court’s analysis show standard claim construction sufficiency: the term “interference‑generating signals” is understood as *any* received signal capable of producing intermodulation products in the passband, not *post‑processing* model signals that appear only after ADC sampling.

## 3.2 Finesse’s Burden and Dr. Wells’ Testimony on RF‑ADC Sampling

Finesse deployed Dr. Wells as its infringement expert, who testified that the Nokia radios in question do indeed **sample both the signal of interest and the interference‑generating signal**.  Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia technical document that diagrammed the internal signal flow within the radio.  In the document, a “red–path” leads from the call‑for‑transmission (DL‑TX) reference and a modeled PIM path into the RF‑ADC block.

### Key Points of Dr. Wells’ testimony

1. **Mapping of the red–path** – He described the red–path as comprising the “DL‑TX reference” (identified as the signal of interest) and a “model PIM path” (identified as the interference‑generating signal).  
2. **Sampling claim** – Dr. Wells asserted that the **RF‑ADC samples the passband that includes both these signals**.  
3. **Reconciliation of technical terms** – When cross‑examined, Dr. Wells noted that the model PIM path is **generated downstream of the RF‑ADC** (i.e., after the ADC has already sampled the raw received signals).  He then pivoted, claiming that there are “two signals on the red path” because they represent two *nodes* (the DL‑TX reference and the modeled PIM path).  

The appellate court treats Dr. Wells’ statements as *contradictory*.  His initial assertion that the RF‑ADC samples a red path that *contains* both signals conflicts with the fact that the model PIM path is **not physically present** at the ADC input; it is computed later in the signal chain.  The cross‑examination exposes this inconsistency, yet the expert’s subsequent re‑characterization of the two signals as merely “x1 and x2” does not resolve the fundamental conflict: the claim requires the **actual input** to the ADC, not a modeled representation.

Because of this confusion, the court determines that Dr. Wells’ testimony does not provide a *clear, unambiguous* demonstration that the RF‑ADC **did** sample the interference‑generating signal as required by limitation 1[a].

## 3.3 Analysis of Dr. Wells’ Contradictory or Ambiguous Testimony

The appellate court applies the **substantial evidence** standard to the expert’s testimony.  This standard, echoing *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN*, requires that a reasonable jury could find in favor of the plaintiff *in light of the evidence presented at trial*.  The court notes:

1. **Lack of direct mapping** – Dr. Wells never testified that the two signals on the red path (x1 and x2) *were* the specific signals required by the claim.  He described them only as “two signals” that correspond to the DL‑TX reference and the modeled PIM path, but never said that the modeled PIM path is the *interference‑generating signal* in the sense required by the patent.  
2. **Persistent contradiction** – Even after the cross‑examination prompted him to discuss the nature of the modeled PIM path, Dr. Wells continued to refer to it as an interference‑generating signal without clarifying how it could be present at the ADC input.  
3. **No clear, corroborative evidence** – The Nokia document does not show that the RF‑ADC samples a real‑time in‑band signal that later becomes the modeled PIM path; instead it depicts a *post‑sampling* model.  

The court compares this situation to *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, where an expert’s contradictory testimony was deemed insufficient to support a claim of infringement.  By that precedent, the appellate court concludes that **the evidence was not “so strong and overwhelmingly in favor of the plaintiff that the jury could not have reached an opposite conclusion.”**  The court therefore finds that the jury’s verdict was **not supported by substantial evidence**.

## 3.4 Application of the Substantial‑Evidence Standard

Because Dr. Wells’ testimony is ambiguous and contradictory, the appellate court determines that for a reasonable jury to have found infringement in a *light‑favorable* view of the evidence was impossible.  The court sees the entire record — the cross‑exam questions, the expert’s responses, and the patent’s own limitation — as *insufficient* to meet the standard.

The appellate court emphasizes that a jury may consider *all* evidence, including the RF‑ADC’s architecture and the Nokia technical document, but the expert testimony fails to clarify the core factual question: did the RF‑ADC actually sample an interference‑generating signal?  Because that question was *not answered* in the evidence, the court applies the “substantial evidence” rule to reverse the JMOL denial.

## 3.5 Reversal of JMOL Denial for §134

Consistent with the analysis above, the appellate court **reverses** the district court’s denial of JMOL for infringement of the **’134 patent**.  The reversal is premised on the finding that:

- Finesse failed to meet its burden to prove that the claim limitation regarding ADC sampling was satisfied.  
- Dr. Wells’ testimony was contradictory and did not convincingly establish that the claimed interference‑generating signals were present at the ADC input.  
- Consequently, **no reasonable jury could have reached a verdict of infringement** in light of the evidence.  

With this reversal, the damages award associated with the ‘134 patent is also vacated, because it was based on an unsupported infringement judgment.  The appeal thus illustrates the critical importance of clear, unambiguous expert testimony when a claim contains a *factual limitation* such as limitation 1[a] of the **’134** patent.
