Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 4: # 4. Analysis of the §775 Patent Claims

## Claim limit: seven third‑order multiplications of three signals

The core of the ’775 patent is a method for cancelling passive intermodulation (PIM) in a receiver. Claim 1 requires that the device **digitally multiply three signals in seven multiplications** to generate intermodulation‑product cancellation signals (ICSs). The “three signals” are denoted S1, S2, and S3. The claim further specifies that the seven multiplications are third‑order, that is, they involve the product of three signals with possible repetitions, such as S1 × S1 × S2 or S1 × S2 × S3, etc. (see claim language at 16:54–17:6). The claim places two critical constraints on the accused device: (a) it must use three **separately identifiable** signals as inputs to the multiplication operations, and (b) it must perform all seven of the specified third‑order multiplications among those signals. The claim does **not** insist that the three signals be *distinct* physical waveforms; rather, it requires that the device be able to distinguish them, even if two are identical. This distinction is central to the district court’s construction and the appellate court’s subsequent analysis.

## District court construction of S1, S2, and S3

The district court applied the **Rule 33** evidence‑based approach to claim construction. The judge concluded that the intrinsic evidence—primarily the claim language and the specification—supports a reading that the three signals be “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique.” The court reasoned that the “two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, satisfies ‘separately identifiable’.” Accordingly, the court sided with Finesse that the device could use two distinct, physical signals (x1 and x2 in the Nokia documentation) and a copy of one of them (e.g., a duplicate of x1) as the third signal. The court rejected the appellant’s objection that the claim requires truly unique signals, noting that Appellants failed to raise this argument at Rule 50(a) and that the examiner’s rejection of the “digitally multiplying two or three signals” claim in the first round of examination was *overcome or narrowed* by the applicant’s subsequent amendment and the final allowance. This construction formed the backbone of the jury’s infringement verdict: if the accused device uses two independently identifiable signals and a copy, it satisfies the first limitation; if it additionally carries out the required multiplications, it satisfies the second.

## Dr. Wells’ testimony based on Nokia documentation – mapping of multiplications

Dr. Jonathan Wells, Finesse’s expert on signal processing, testified that the Nokia radios incorporate the claimed multiplication logic. His testimony was heavily anchored in a Nokia internal technical document (cited as “Nokia document” in the record). The document lists ten **distinct multiplications** that the Nokia receiver apparently computes, expressed in symbolic form (e.g., x1 × |x1| × |x1|, x1 × x1 × x2′, x1 × |x2| × |x2|, …). Dr. Wells asserted that **three** of these multiplications correspond to the seven third‑order multiplications required by the claim. He identified the three distinct operations as:

1. x1 × |x1| × |x1|,
2. x1 × x1 × x2′,
3. x1 × |x2| × |x2|.

Wells explained that the rest of the ten multiplications are **duplicates** of these three, in that each of the seven required multiplications can be derived from one of these three base operations through algebraic manipulation or by taking complex conjugates. However, the record shows no explicit mapping from the three base operations to the full set of seven claimed multiplications. The judge and the jury were led to infer that, because the device performs the ten operations shown in the document, it necessarily performs all seven claimed multiplications. This inference was the crux of the jury’s decision.

## Finesse’s claim‑surrender argument and examiner rejection

Finesse argued that, during prosecution, it **surrendered** claim scope by narrowing the claim from “two or three signals” to “three signals” in order to survive the examiner’s rejection of the broader language over the Filipovic prior art reference. The examiner had rejected an earlier version of the claim that allowed the device to use two signals because Filipovic taught a two‑signal cancellation method; the claim was amended to require “three signals” to distinguish the device from Filipovic. Finesse maintained that this amendment did not *surrender* the possibility that the device could use only two distinct signals, only that the claim required some way of presenting three signals to the multipliers—either three distinct signals or a duplicate of one of them. The appellate court, applying the **Clarence-Smith** analysis, found that the amendment did not unambiguously disavow all two‑signal embodiments. Rather, it merely set a **minimum** of “three separately identifiable” inputs, which could be realized by two unique waveforms plus a copy. Therefore, the court deemed that the claim was not narrowed in a way that would preclude the accused device’s alleged structure.

## Evidence of unperformed seven multiplications – lack of mapping

Appellants contested that the Nokia device, as described by Dr. Wells, did **not** perform seven third‑order multiplications. They pointed out that the Nokia document does not show any multiplication of **x1** and **x2** directly; it lists only multiplications involving *modulus* or *complex conjugates* of x2. Moreover, the three multiplications highlighted by Dr. Wells, when expanded, do not yield seven distinct third‑order products: each contains only a single non‑trivial operator (e.g., a modulus). As a result, no **direct accounting** exists for the seven distinct third‑order terms (such as S1 × S1 × S2, S1 × S2 × S2, etc.) that the claim explicitly enumerates. The appellate court held that, absent a clear mapping, no reasonable jury could have found the accused device performed the full set of multiplications. The court emphasized that the claim requires **all** seven multiplications; providing partial proof or an equivalent set of operations without demonstrating they cover every required term is insufficient. This aligns with the doctrine that a claim’s scope is broadened only by *identical* or *equivalent* structures, not by a vague equivalence or an incomplete performance.

## Reversal of JMOL denial for §775

Because the evidence failed to establish the performance of the seven required multiplications, the appellate court held that the **substantial evidence** standard—under which a jury verdict of infringement must rest—was not satisfied. The evidence is simply insufficient to support the jury’s conclusion that the accused devices carried out all the mandated multiplication operations. Consequently, the district court’s denial of JMOL was reversed, and the infringement ruling on the ’775 patent was vacated. This reversal also carried over to the damages award: the appellate court vacated the damages award associated with the ’775 claims, because the infringement was found unsupported.

In short, the court found that the claim limitation on **three signals and seven multiplications** was interpreted to permit a two‑signal infrastructure with a duplicate, that Dr. Wells’ testimony failed to map the Nokia device’s multiplications onto the claim’s seven specifications, and therefore the jury’s verdict could not rest on substantial evidence. The appellate court reversed the JMOL denial and vacated the associated damages.
