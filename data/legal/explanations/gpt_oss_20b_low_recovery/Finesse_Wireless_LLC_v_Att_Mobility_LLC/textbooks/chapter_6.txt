Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 6: # 6. Practical Take‑aways and Significance

The *Finesse Wireless* decision is a rich teaching example for advanced students of patent law. While the opinion itself revolves around two specific patents and the conduct of a single expert, the doctrinal lessons reach far beyond the facts. The court’s conclusions touch on three separately‑recognizable pillars of modern infringement litigation: the weight of expert testimony, the effect of claim amendments on infringement analysis, and the Supreme Court‑endorsed “substantial‑evidence” threshold for supporting a jury verdict when the court faces a JMOL argument. Below, we distill each of those pillars and provide a brief cost‑benefit framework for litigants who must decide how to craft claims and expert notebooks in their own cases.

---

## Role of Expert Clarity and Consistency in Supporting Infringement Verdicts

The *Finesse* case demonstrates that the standing doctrine—“the expert must be more than a whisper in the background” (see *O’Hare Guarneri v. Bozzano*, 408 F.3d 768)—is not a vague admonition but a strict standard. The expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, intended to bridge a technical gap between the claimed elements of the *134* patent and the accused Nokia radios. His testimony, however, was riddled with contradictions:

1. **Misalignment of “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.”** Wells claimed that the RF ADC sampled “both” sets of signals, but later admitted that the “modeled PIM path” was generated **after** sampling, thus could not be an interference‑generating signal per claim limitation 1[a].  
2. **Unclear mapping of *x1* and *x2* to the claimed signals** (DL(TX) reference vs. modeled PIM path). The expert never said explicitly that *x1* is the “signal of interest” and *x2* the “interference‑generating signal.”  
3. **Persistent oscillation between advocates and cross‑examination contexts** left the jury with a “green‑light” but the appellate court found it “substantial evidence‑weaker.”

These issues are not academic. The court applied *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), to find the evidence was insufficient for a reasonable jury to conclude infringement. The appellate court emphasized that when the “part with the burden of proof”—here, the plaintiff—rests on the testimony of an expert who “contradicts themselves,” the evidence falls below the substantial‑evidence threshold.

**Bottom line:** In future cases, experts must log, in a **single, consistent narrative**: the signals or processes being sampled, the order of signal generation relative to sampling, and a clear mapping to each claim limitation. Any relapse can render the entire weight of the expert’s testimony impotent.

---

## Impact of Claim Amendments on Scope of Infringement Analysis

The *Finesse* opinion clarifies that amendments need not collapse on the original claim scope, but may sharpen or shift the tactical focus of infringement analysis. The *775* patent’s claim originally read “*digitally multiplying two or three signals*,” but, after a protracted prosecution, was amended to “*digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2 and S3*.” The appellate court held that this amendment did not unilaterally shift the standard from “two or three signals” to “exactly three signals,” but sharpened the language used for critique.

Two key take‑aways arise:

1. **Claim language itself dictates the *forensic* scrutiny in infringement arguments.** The expert had to prove that the accused device performed *seven* distinct third‑order multiplications involving *three* separately identifiable signals. Because the device only used two unique inputs, the defendant’s argument prevailed. If the claim had left “two or three signals,” a different line of reasoning would have been open.
   
2. **Amendments can be a strategic tool beyond meeting the examiner’s requirements.** In *Finesse*, the plaintiffs exercised the “clear and unambiguous” standard of *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019) to argue that an amendment did not dispel the scope to include “two input” devices. Plaintiffs should remember that a late‑stage amendment to tighten the claim may help clarify the infringement space *but* may equally constrain plaintiff expectations if the equipment does not technically meet the tightened language.

## How the Court’s Application of Substantial Evidence Affects Future Cases

The overarching framework for the court’s review—titled *substantial evidence*, *reasonable jurors*, and *viewing evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict*—is not merely an academic dogma; it becomes the judge’s litmus test on every JMOL motion. In *Finesse*, the jury’s verdict was deemed insufficient on two fronts: the expert’s testimony lacked adjudicable clarity, and the mapping of claimed multiplications to the technical evidence was nonexistent.

The effects are twofold:

1. **Jury verdicts anchored on contrarian or incomplete testimony can be overturned.** A defendant can cite *Substantial Evidence* to demonstrate the evidence is “too thin or ambiguous” to support any conclusion.  
2. **Plaintiffs must rehearse that “substantial evidence” requires *concrete* causal bridging** between the claim limitation and the accused device’s operation—this is no longer satisfied by *partial* or *probabilistic* cross‑references.

Future litigants will need to pre‑emptively build “evidence chains” into their expert reports: detailed diagrams, step‑by‑step mapping, and independent verification of every claim limitation. Failure to do so invites the same JMOL reversal that *Finesse* suffered.

## Strategic Lessons for Plaintiffs and Defendants in Drafting Claims and Presenting Expert Testimony

### 1. Draft with a View to the *Litigator’s Playbook*
- **Clause‑level clarity:** Use “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” verbatim, and supply legislative analogues in the claims once the examiner’s position is clear.
- **Amend judiciously:** Aim for amendments that preserve, but do not narrow, the underlying technical scope unless you can absolutely demonstrate the accused product will satisfy new constraints.

### 2. Prepare Your Expert Stone Cold
- **Full‑chain mapping:** Your expert must lay out a **step‑by‑step** process from the device’s input to the claimed output, including timing and signal generation sequences.
- **Reconcile cross‑exam costs:** If your technical document has multiple legends (like the “DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path” legend), explicitly state which legend applies to what portion of the claim.

### 3. Embrace “Substantial Evidence” Early
- **Pilot studies:** Build simulations or lab data that mirror the accused device’s actual signal flow.  
- **Narrative consistency:** Draft a closed‑loop explanation that would survive cross‑examination—a moving two‑way bridge between the claims and the evidence.

### 4. For Defendants: Leverage “No Sampling After Generation”
- **Temporal arguments:** Emphasize any technical processes that generate signals *after* the claimed sampling event or process; this is a quick exit route for many faulty infringement arguments.  

### 5. For Plaintiffs: Adopt a “Reversibility” Mindset
- **Draft with reversibility in mind:** If the court is looking for “substantial evidence,” anticipate the possibility of a JMOL motion and fail‑fast on modeling, mapping, and enumeration of operations.

---

## Closing Reflection

The *Finesse Wireless* opinion teaches that **clarity trumps complexity**. In a litigation where the vast majority of the analyst work is technical, a single expert’s misstep can “decimate” the entire infringement verdict. The appellate court’s focus on substantial evidence and the weight of expert testimony makes clear that **rules of claim construction, expert testimony, and evidence thresholds are more interlocked than ever**. For the modern practitioner, the lesson is straightforward: **draft to the details. Execute in the courts to the detail.**
