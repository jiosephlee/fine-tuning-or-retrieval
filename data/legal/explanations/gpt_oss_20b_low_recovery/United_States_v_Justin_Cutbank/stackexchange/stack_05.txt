Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### When can a district court rely on a PSR’s factual statements without objection to determine the guidelines range?
Question:
In the sentencing phase, the court 'adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions.' The defendant raises no objections. Is it standard practice for a court to accept the PSR’s facts at face value, or does the judge still need to verify them? What happens if later a new piece of evidence shows a conviction was actually for a misdemeanor?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the district court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions.”  That language is unambiguous: the court took the PSR’s facts as fact for the purposes of applying the guideline enhancement.  The court’s adoption of the PSR facts is a standard, *de novo* practice at sentencing.  The Fifth Amendment does not require the judge to personally verify every fact that a PSR reports; the PSR is generated by the Department of Justice and is treated as reliable guidance, not as evidence that must be corroborated in court.  See *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929‑30 (8th Cir. 2013) (a district court may adopt the PSR’s factual statements at sentencing without independent verification).  The adoption of PSR facts is a broad, discretionary act that is reviewed for *harmless error* on appeal, not for some “essentiality” error.

The question as posed—*What if, later, a new piece of evidence shows a conviction was actually for a misdemeanor?*—must be answered in light of that standard.  If a later discovery shows that one of the “felony” convictions cited in the PSR was in fact a misdemeanor, the error would affect the *Criminal‑History Category* calculation.  The appellate court would first assess whether the error was *material* and *prejudicial*.  If the misclassification would materially lower the guideline offense level (for example, dropping the defendant from Category VI to a lower category) and the sentence that *would* have followed that lower level would fall outside the range that the defendant ultimately received, the appellate court would likely order a new sentencing calculation.  If, however, the sentence that would have resulted from the corrected category would still fall within the range that the defendant was sent, the error would be considered harmless and the sentence would be affirmed.  Thus, the judge is not required to re‑verify each factual premise; the appellate court will remedy a demonstrated, material mis‑fact if that mis‑fact would be expected to change the outcome.

In short:

1. **Adoption is standard practice.**  A district court may take PSR facts at face value without independent verification; that is permissible and customary.  
2. **Later mis‑facts are addressed on appeal.**  If new evidence shows a prior conviction is in fact a misdemeanor, the appellate court will evaluate whether the error changes the relevant guideline and whether such a change likely altered the sentence.  If it did, a new sentence must be imposed; if not, the original sentence stands.

This is the correct procedural and substantive rule in *Cutbank* and the broader district‑court sentencing framework.
