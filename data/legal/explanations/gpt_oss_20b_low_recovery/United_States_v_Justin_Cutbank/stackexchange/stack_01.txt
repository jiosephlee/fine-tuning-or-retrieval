Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why was the prior possession of a sawed‑off Marlin considered 'intrinsic' evidence under Rule 404(b) in Cutbank?
Question:
I’m confused about the court’s application of 18 U.S.C. § 924(e) and the evidentiary ruling that the defendant’s prior possession of the sawed‑off Marlin was intrinsic evidence. The defendant argued that this violated Rule 404(b) because it was evidence of prior unlawful conduct. The court and the Ninth Circuit in United States v. Battle say it is ‘intrinsic’ when it helps prove ‘later possession of the same weapon.’ How exactly does this make the evidence admissible and what distinguishes intrinsic from extrinsic evidence in this context?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court’s ruling that the defendant’s prior possession of a sawed‑off Marlin was “intrinsic” evidence does not hinge on § 924(e) at all; it is a Rule 404(b) issue. Rule 404(b) bars evidence of a person’s prior unlawful conduct *unless* the evidence is “necessary to prove the validity of a charge, defense, or claim that is at issue in the case” or it is “directly relevant to an issue other than the character or reputation of the witness” and would *not* tend to “unlawfully prejudice the jury.”[1]

In *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), the court explained that evidence of *prior possession* of the *same* firearm “is intrinsically relevant to the subsequent possession charge” because it helps establish that the defendant owned or controlled the weapon in question. In *Cutbank*, the trial testimony—“I have seen ___ with the sawed‑off Marlin a handful of times before the night in question”—directly addressed the *present* charge of felon‑in‑possession. It was not simply a propensity statement about “violent or careless behaviour” but a factual claim that the defendant had continuous control of that particular weapon. That is why the district court said the evidence was “intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession of the firearm before the incident.”[2]

**Intrinsic vs. extrinsic**

* **Intrinsic evidence** is the testimony or physical evidence that the prosecution needs to prove one of the elements of the charged offense. It is allowed unless it is so prejudicial as to violate Rule 404(b) under the “unlawfully prejudicial” standard. In *Cutbank*, the prosecution used the claim to show that Cutbank had *already possessed* the sawed‑off Marlin, thus satisfying the “possession” element of the felon‑in‑possession statute.  

* **Extrinsic evidence** is testimony or evidence that is not directly tied to an element of the charged offense, such as prior acts that serve only to paint a picture of the defendant’s character or propensity. Under Rule 404(b), extrinsic conduct evidence is generally inadmissible unless it falls under an exception (e.g., impeachment of a witness). The defense correctly argued that the prior possession testimony was extrinsic, but the court found otherwise because the testimony was offered *to prove an element* of the charged crime, not to show the defendant’s general bad character.

**Why it is admissible**

1. **Relevance to an element of the charge** – The prosecution needed to show that Cutbank *owned or controlled* the sawed‑off Marlin at the time of the crime. Prior possession is the most direct path to establishing that ownership/control.  
2. **Not merely propensity** – The testimony was not telling the jury that Cutbank was “violent” or “has a tendency to misbehave.” It was saying, “he already had the weapon.”  
3. **No unjust prejudice** – The court applied the 403 balancing test and found that, while prejudice was possible, it was outweighed by the probative value of proving possession of the gun.  

Because the evidence met those requirements, it was not excluded as extrinsic propensity evidence. The court therefore held the evidence was *intrinsic* and admissible.

**Bottom line**

In *Cutbank*, the candidate for dismissal under Rule 404(b) was a *prior possession* claim that *directly proved a charge* (felon in possession of a firearm) and not *ostensible bad character* evidence. The Ninth Circuit in *Battle* corroborated that such evidence is intrinsically relevant because it establishes the defendant’s ownership or control of the weapon that is the subject of the current charge. Hence, the evidence was admissible and not barred as extrinsic conduct.
