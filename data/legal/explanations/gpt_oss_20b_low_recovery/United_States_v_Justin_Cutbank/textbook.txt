Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# I. Case Background and Procedural Posture

The court opinion hinges on a sequence of facts that unfolded on the night of November 19–20, 2020, and on the procedural steps that followed. This section recounts the core facts, the evidence that the prosecution relied upon, the jury’s findings, the application of the Armed Career Criminal Act and the United States Sentencing Guidelines, and the appellate petition submitted by defendant Justin Cutbank.

## Overview of the 2020 incident and the parties involved

Justin Cutbank, a longstanding felon with an extensive criminal record, allegedly had a history of violent behavior and drug use. On the night in question, Cutbank had been drinking and had used methamphetamine. After waking his girlfriend, D.F., he pulled a firearm—a Marlin .22‑caliber rifle with a sawed‑off barrel and stock—pointed it at her face, and forced her into a closet. While at gunpoint, he threatened her life, knocked her face with the gun, and allegedly forced her to rouse other occupants, including two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. He then fled with D.F.’s cell phone and the rifle. A second incident involved a man—later identified as Cutbank—who barricaded himself in a garage one‑half mile from D.F.’s apartment during a police encounter. He resisted arrest, bit an officer, seized the officers’ firearms, and was eventually apprehended. The police found D.F.’s phone, a butcher‑style knife, and the Marlin rifle along the suspected route cut between the two properties; DNA matched Cutbank to the weapon.

## Police investigation and evidence collection

The investigators had two primary bodies of evidence: testimonial and physical. Two victims were identified on the scene: D.F., the original girlfriend, and C.F., the housemate who also saw the weapon at the time of the first incident. D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off rifle “handful of times” before that night. C.F. corroborated that she had witnessed the gun “once or twice” prior to the incident. The private investigator who shot down D.F.’s cross‑examination notes that she described herself as “not familiar with guns” and that a sibling of hers died by a gun—statements that the defense sought to strike but were retained by the trial court. The law enforcement officers found a second weapon, a Marlin with a sawed‑off barrel, that matched DNA from Cutbank. The possession of this firearm was the central piece of evidence supporting the Armed Career Criminal Activity charge. The police report, useful for impeachment, was prepared by an external investigator and was kept out of the record until the defense sought to use it, at which point the trial judge denied the request to read portions of it into evidence.

## Trial proceedings and jury verdict

At trial, the prosecution sought to establish that Cutbank possessed the gun during the incident and had prior possession that indicated continuous possession. The defense motioned to exclude the testimonies of D.F. and C.F. regarding prior possession as “propensity” evidence under Rule 404(b). The district court agreed with the prosecution, deeming the evidence “intrinsic” because prior possession was directly relevant to proving current possession. The jury convicted Cutbank on all counts: felon in possession of a firearm under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), and for being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies, thereby making him subject to the ACCA enhanced penalties. The charge of possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense (the 924(e) enhancement) was also applied.

## Sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and advisory guidelines

The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) was produced by the U.S. Sentencing Commission’s regional system. It determined an offense level of 26 for the base offense (use of a weapon described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)), plus two additional levels for prior violence convictions, and further increases: a four‑level enhancement for “possession of a firearm in connection with another felony” (the gun was used during the domestic assault), a two‑level increase for reckless risk posed while fleeing from police, and a one‑level increase for an altered or obliterated serial number. The PSR placed Cutbank in Criminal History Category VI, yielding an Advisory Guidelines range of 292 to 365 months. The district court accepted the PSR’s factual findings and applied the four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. The trial judge ultimately sentenced Cutbank to 292 months—exactly the lower end of the advised range—conveying a sentence within policy limits.

## Appeal filing and the specific contentions raised by Cutbank

On October 7, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit heard Cutbank’s appeal. The defendant’s brief asserted that the district court erred in five key ways: (1) allowing the admission of D.F.’s and C.F.’s testimony about prior possession, (2) refusing to give the jury instructions precluding consideration of D.F.’s statements to police without her full testimony, (3) denying further objection to the prior‑possession testimony, (4) permitting D.F.’s mention of her sister’s death by gun as impeaching evidence, and (5) rejecting Cross‑examination regarding C.F.’s prior statements. Those claims failed on jurisdictional and evidentiary grounds; the brief provided no precedent to support them. Cutbank also contended that the district court had incorrectly applied the four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, adding that a failure to do so would bring the sentence below the advisory guidelines range and thereby violate § 3553(a). However, the court found no procedural error in the application of this enhancement and reasoned that the sentence, though within range, was reasonable under § 3553(a)’s factors. The appellate court therefore affirmed the district court’s decision in full.

# II. Evidentiary and Procedural Issues Presented by the Defendant

In this chapter we dissect the evidentiary and procedural arguments raised by the defendant in United States v. Justin Cutbank and explain the appellate court’s reasoning for finding that no reversible error occurred. The analysis follows the court’s order verbatim, adding only the doctrinal context necessary to understand each ruling.

---

## A. Rule 404(b) and Intrinsic Evidence of Prior Possession  

Cutbank contended that D.F.’s testimony that he had previously possessed a sawed‑off Marlin .22‑caliber rifle constituted inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The district court, aligned with the government’s position, held that the statements were *intrinsic* evidence—directly relevant to the charge of felon in possession of a firearm.

Rule 404(b) allows evidence of prior conduct when it is **intrinsic** to the alleged offense. The guiding principle is that such evidence is admissible so long as it helps prove the defendant’s state of mind or acts pertinent to the crime. The appellate court relied on *United States v. Battle* (8th Cir. 2014) to support this view, citing its reasoning that prior possession is a “handful of times” relevant to demonstrating ongoing control of the weapon. The court emphasized that intrinsic evidence may be admitted even if it could also be used to show propensity, so long as the heritage of the evidence is to prove a fact that is crucial to the offense—here, possession of the weapon at the time of the assault.

The defendant’s points were dismissed because they lacked a contrasting case, and the court found no misapplication of Rule 404(b). By treating D.F.’s testimony as intrinsic, the district court ruled the evidence admissible, and the appellate court agreed.

---

## B. Confrontation Clause and Jury Instructions Regarding Hearsay  

A second objection focused on the admissibility of D.F.’s statements to police and whether the court should have limited the jury’s consideration of those statements until D.F. testified in court. The contention raised two issues:

1. **Hearsay:** D.F.’s statements to the police could be considered hearsay if the court permitted them to be used as evidence of the defendant’s conduct.  
2. **Confrontation Clause:** The defendant’s argument was that the statements were “unconfrontable” because the declarant was absent and the statements were pre‑trial in nature.

The district court answered both questions as follows. First, it found that the statements were *not* introduced as *inner‑product* evidence; instead, they served a contextual function—explaining police officer actions and the circumstances of the encounter. Under *United States v. Bennett* (8th Cir. 2014), the court was not required to give a limiting instruction at the time of admission if there would be a subsequent, proper instruction at the close of the trial. The witness’s later testimony satisfied the *confrontation* requirement because she appeared in court and testified directly about the matter.

The appellate court noted that the district court’s instructions met the *Benches* standard: there was no substantive evidence of a violation of the Confrontation Clause and the jury was never given the opportunity to consider the statements as our evidence without the declarant’s presence. The court erred not only in labels but in the subtleties of the rule; the appellate court therefore found no reversible error.

---

## C. Admission of Prior Instances of Weapon Possession  

Relatable to Section A, the defendant also argued that the court should reject any testimony that he had *seen* the weapon “a handful of times” in the weeks preceding the incident. The district court denied the objection, emphasizing that the prior possession evidence *helped prove continuous possession*. The court also overruled C.F.’s testimony that she had observed the gun used during the incident “once or twice” before, because it again served to establish continuous possession, not as evidence of character.

The appellate court held that, in accordance with *Battle*, “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession” because it helps establish ownership or control. The court scored the same lineage of reasoning for both D.F. and C.F.—if the testimony did not add pro‑penalty bias but instead helped underpin a fact subject to the underlying offense, it could be admitted. Thus, the appellate court found the district court’s decision to permit the testimony correct.

---

## D. Striking of Additional Character Statements and Prejudice Analysis  

Cutbank objected to the court’s refusal to strike a statement made by D.F. in the form of “her sister was killed by a gun.” He argued that the statement was irrelevant, redundant, and, more importantly, inflammatory. The district court, however, upheld the statement because it was a defense of *inconsistent prior statements* during cross‑examination. When the defense asked why she had in some instances said she didn’t see the gun, D.F. explained that her discomfort with firearms was due to her sister’s death. Such a statement directly responded to the defense’s line of questioning, and the court proceeded under *Fed. R. Evidence 403*—prejudice was outweighed by relevance.

The appellate court treated the same reasoning in *Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn* (2008), where the court noted that before striking an inflammatory statement the judge must weigh more relevance than prejudice. The court applied the same balancing test and concluded that the statement was admissible since it was germane to an attempt to explain inconsistent prior testimony. No reversible error found.

---

## E. Cross‑Examination of Government Witnesses and Authentication Requirements  

The defendant sought to cross‑examine C.F. about prior statements made to a police investigator and to read portions of the resulting police report into evidence. The district court allowed questions regarding what C.F. said to the police but barred the introduction of the police report because it was not authored by C.F. and lacked proper foundation for authenticity.

Cutbank failed to argue this issue on appeal. Nevertheless, the appellate court reaffirmed the policy under *United States v. Cumbie* (5th Cir. 2022) that prior inconsistent statements are admissible for impeachment only when the evidence is authenticated. The court did not find the district court’s reliance on authenticity requirement erroneous, thereby affirming the dismissal of the police report.

---

## F. Harmless Error Doctrine and Cumulative Effect of Evidentiary Rulings  

After reviewing all the evidentiary rulings, the defense raised the standard of harmless error. The appellate court recalled *United States v. DeMarce* (8th Cir. 2009) and *United States v. Keys* (8th Cir. 2019) to emphasize that erroneous rulings must be *substantial* and “substantially sway the jury” to reverse a conviction. Simply put, a cumulative error must demonstrate that the defendant received a substantially unfair trial.

The court found that the evidence was *substantial* in its support of the convictions, and the add‑on by the district court did not create a “substantial prejudice” as defined under *DeMarce*. The adjudication surrounded a *robust basis of evidence*: a jury found Cutbank guilty, and the district court considered a range of evidence answering causal and effect questions. Accordingly, the appellate court concluded that no reversible error existed regarding the cumulative effect of the evidentiary rulings.

---

### Summary  

In summary, each of the defendant’s arguments—whether based on Rule 404(b), the Confrontation Clause, or broader evidentiary principles—were addressed thoroughly by the district court. The appellate court applied the proper precedent and found that the trial rulings were supported by established federal evidence rules and precedent. Because the court found the decisions both correct and harmless, it affirmed the conviction and sentence.

# III. Sentencing Law and the Application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the appellate court examined how the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) intersected in the determination of the defendant’s offense level, criminal‑history category, and final sentence.  The issues at the heart of the case were: (1) the statutory and procedural framework that governs ACCA sentencing enhancements; (2) the proper calculation of the offense level and criminal‑history category, especially with respect to the 4‑level “facilitated possession” enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B); and (3) the extent to which the district court’s computations complied with Supreme Court guidance on the core principle of § 3553(a).

## A. Overview of ACCA Enhancements and the Role of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

The ACCA enhances prison terms for the federal conviction of a “violent felony” or a “serious violent felony” after the defendant has had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies (18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1)(A)(i) and (ii)).  The statute itself does not specify an enhanced sentence; instead, it directs the sentencing court to apply the USSG.  The Act’s core purpose is to provide a uniform, predictable penalty—hence the reliance on the Guidelines.

Within the Guidelines, 2 K2.1(b)(6)(B) is a 4‑level “facilitated possession” enhancement.  It increases an offense level by four if the defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony, **regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained** (USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B); comment n. 14(C)).  The language is intentionally broad.  A “firearm” includes a gun, shotgun, rifle, or any weapon that could potentially fire a projectile, and a “felony” is a violation of any law that constitutes a felony for purposes of federal sentencing.  The phrase “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained” emphasizes that the presence of a separate but contemporaneous felony that involved a firearm suffices.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed the statutory interpretation of this enhancement.  In *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919 (8th Cir. 2013), the court reiterated that the enhancement applies when the defendant **used or possessed** a firearm *in connection with* another felony offense, no matter what the outcome of that felony: *“the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement applies if a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained.”*  Thus, the enhancement is factual, not procedural.  The question is whether the facts showing the use or possession of a firearm in tandem with a separate felony are present, not whether the defendant faced a charge for that felony.

In *Cutbank*, the PSR identified three prior felony convictions: a kidnapping, a robbery/assault, and a violent felonies involving firearms.  The district court noted that the Marlin rifle, which served as the defendant’s weapon of the robbery/assault, was used during the robbery.  The presence of the rifle during that felony satisfied the factual requirement for § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).  The prosecution did not need to demonstrate that the defendant was charged with a separate felony for possession of the rifle; the mere use of the rifle in the robbery was sufficient.

In sum, § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is a 4‑level enhancement applied when the defendant possessed or used a firearm in the course of committing another felony.  The defendant’s prior convictions provide the necessary “prior violent felony” trigger, and the PSR’s factual detail satisfies the “facilitated possession” requirement.

## B. Calculation of the Offense Level and Criminal‑History Category

The ACCA requires that the district court calculate the offense level according to the Guideline offense‑level table (USSG § 2C2.2) and use that level to determine the criminal‑history category (USSG § 4B2.1).  The calculation order is foundational: first determine the offense level, then apply the criminal‑history category algorithm.  The appellate court reviewed the PSR, which recommended an offense level of 26.  Two statutory aggravating factors raised the level from 26 to an overall level of 35:

1. **Modified Offense Base Level** – The gun was a sawed‑off Marlin rifle.  Under § 2C2.2(c)(4), the presence of a “sawed‑off” or “truncated” barrel or stock increases the base offense level by 0 in this case (the PSR’s calculation correctly used the 0‑level for a modified weapon).  However, § 2C2.2(c)(7) allows a **2‑level increase** for possession of a firearm that has a “serial number that has been altered or obliterated.”  The rifle’s serial number had been altered, so the PSR added 2 levels, raising the level to 28.

2. **Dangerous Factors** – The review of prior convictions, specifically the number and nature of violent felonies, moved the offender to **criminal‑history category VI**.  The presence of at least three prior violent felonies places a defendant in category VI.  That position pushes the advisory range upward; the PSR consequently set the range at 292–365 months.

3. **Four‑Level “Facilitated Possession” Enhancement** – Per § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the defendant’s possession of the rifle during the robbery/assault triggered a **4‑level increase**, from the previously computed level of 31 (after the 2‑level serial‑number adjustment) to 35.  The PSR’s total of 35 aligned with the district court’s calculations.

The district court used the PSR’s level‑25 baseline (after the gun‑modification adjustment) and added 2 levels for the altered serial, 2 for the reckless risk of death (the defendant’s fleeing and gun‑running), and 4 for the facilitated possession.  The additive process matched the statutory structure.  The court’s final offense level of 35 produced a guideline range of 292 to 365 months.  This equals the jurisdictional range for a category VI offender with an offense level of 35, confirming that the district court did not err in computing the offense level.

Although the town’s *USSG § 4B1.4(b)* states that the “greater offense level applies” when a judge chooses between competing enhancements, the district court simply applied the ACCA‑derived level and did not need to choose between conflicting statutory increases.  The court’s calculations adhered strictly to the statutory and Guideline framework.

## C. Application of the 4‑Level “Facilitated Possession” Enhancement

The appellate court scrutinized the defendant’s contention that the district court misapplied § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).  Specific concerns:

- **Did the enhancement apply?**  The admission is clear through the factual record: the defendant possessed and used the Marlin rifle during a separate felony (the robbery/assault).  The presence of the firearm at the felony scene satisfies the statutory requirement that the gun be “used or possessed” *in connection with another felony*.

- **Did the enhancement require a charge or conviction?**  The defendant was convicted of the robbery/assault, which provided a qualified felony.  Even if the defendant had not been charged for the robbery, the enhancement would still apply because the statutory language expressly allows it “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained.”  The presence of a conviction for the robbery strengthens the requirement, not negates it.

- **Was the 4‑level enhancement correct?**  Under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the increase is a fixed 4 levels.  The district court’s application of a 4‑level increase was fully supported by the Rules.  No intermediate factor or narrower interpretation can be advanced by the defendant.

- **Could a lower enhancement have applied?**  The defendant’s prior history and the fact that the gun was used during the felony satisfy the strictest application.  The district court was not required to consider a lesser enhancement; any alternative would be unjustified.

Thus, the appellate court concluded that the district court correctly applied the facilitated‑possession enhancement.  The enhancement is an objective factual finding that the gun was used during a felony; no discernible error occurred.

## D. Supreme Court Guidance on the Core Principle of § 3553(a) and Its Application to Guideline‑Based Sentencing

The Supreme Court’s most significant contribution to the analysis of ACCA sentencing comes from *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38 (2007).  The Court emphasized that a sentence must be “sufficient but not greater than necessary” to ensure the purposes of the federal sentencing system are met (the “core principle”).  *Gall* also held that the “admissibility” of a sentence is judged against a **guideline range** that is based on the most objective evidence available—but *Gall* did not require a mechanical recitation of § 3553(a) factors.  It merely demanded that the range be correctly calculated before the court considered other factors.

The appellate court noted that the district court’s calculation of the 292–365 month range was **provisional and objective**.  The court’s subsequent reference to the § 3553(a) factors (such as the defendant’s prior conduct, severity of offenses, personal history, ties to community, etc.) is part of the judge’s discretionary, fact‑based, and policy‑based assessment.  The core principle allows the judge to depart from the range if the § 3553(a) factors warrant it, but such a departure can only be justified if the judge shows that all transaction and balancing factors point to a lower or higher sentence.

Furthermore, the court cited *Rosales‑Mireles*, 585 U.S. 129 (2018), where the Court reiterated that a proper calculation of the Guidelines is a precondition for appellate review of a sentence’s substantive reasonableness.  A substantive appeal must first establish no significant procedural error in the calculation.  If there is no error, the appellate court **presumes** the sentence is within‑range and therefore “reasonable” unless there is a clear error in the judge’s application of § 3553(a) factors.  In *Cutbank*, the appellate court found no procedural error in the range calculation and, in the absence of a clear error in the judge’s balancing, affirmed the sentence.

## E. Review of the District Court’s Range Calculation and Compliance with § 3553(a) Factors

### 1. Provisional Range Calculation

The appellate court confirmed that the district court followed the correct procedure: it first used the PSR to calculate an offense level of 35, reflected the defendant’s criminal‑history category VI, and then applied the appropriate officer‑range.  The final range was 292–365 months, which is consistent with the USSG for a level 35, category VI offender.

### 2. Review of § 3553(a) Factors

The appellate court recognized that *Cutbank* had a formidable criminal history—over a dozen past convictions, including violent offenses involving firearms, assault of a police officer, and a kidnapping.  The judge’s memorandum included a section in which the judge “reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda” and “reviewed § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance.”  The appellate court noted that even if a judge does not itemize each factor or present a mechanical recitation, the record is sufficient for the court to conclude that the judge considered the factors.

The court reaffirmed that if a judge deems a sentence within the advisory range as presumptively “reasonable,” the appellate court must presume that the district court “actually considered” the § 3553(a) factors unless evidence indicates otherwise.  The appellate court found no editorial or factual evidence indicating the judge ignored any factor.

### 3. Non‑Deviation and Deference

Because the judge imposed a **within‑range sentence** (292 months), the decision reached a floor that the appellate court regards as the minimal assumption of reasonableness.  Under *Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, the court affirmed that the district court’s sentencing was sufficient, without requiring the appellate court to analyze each factor in depth.  The judge’s use of the 292‑month term represented the lowest end of the guideline range and, given the defendant’s violent history, is arguably the “sufficient but not greater than necessary” criterion attuned to the Federal Sentencing Scheme’s purposes.

### 4. Conclusion of the Appellate Review

The appellate court concluded that the district court made no procedural misstep in calculating the guideline range and had complied adequately with the core principle of § 3553(a).  The 292‑month sentence falls safely inside the 292–365 month guideline range and therefore satisfies the *Gall* and *Rosales‑Mireles* mandates.  Because there was no significant error or prejudice stemming from any evidentiary rulings, the appellate court affirmed the conviction and the sentence.

---

In sum, the judicial reasoning in *Cutbank* illustrates how the ACCA’s statutory framework, the Guideline’s structured offense‑level calculations, and the Supreme Court’s core principle coalesce to guide sentencing decisions.  The district court correctly applied the 4‑level “facilitated possession” enhancement, calculated the guideline range properly, and considered the § 3553(a) factors, yielding a sentence that was both within the advisory range and consistent with the sentencing scheme’s purposes.

# IV. The Court’s Reasoning and Final Holding

Throughout an appellate opinion the court’s task is twofold: first, to determine whether the trial court erred in admitting evidence that could have swayed the jury; and second, to decide whether the appellate court should accept or overturn the district court’s sentencing decision. In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit’s analysis follows a logical progression that ties the factual and evidentiary findings directly to the sentencing decision, ultimately affirming both the conviction and the 292‑month sentence.

## A. Integration of Factual and Evidentiary Findings

The appellate court’s assessment of evidentiary issues rests on a detailed synthesis of the trial record. It first addresses each of the defense’s objections—primer evidence regarding the sawed‑off Marlin rifle, limitations on hearsay, cross‑examination restrictions, and the admissibility of prior inconsistent statements. For each contention the court confirms that the district court’s ruling was grounded in a well‑established evidentiary principle:

1. **Intrinsic evidence on prior possession** – The court cites *United States v. Battle* to support the admissibility of D.F. and C.F.’s testimony that Cutbank had previously possessed or used the rifle. This testimony is classified as “intrinsic” because it is relevant to proving continuation of possession and is not prejudicial to the point of outweighing its probative value.

2. **Limiting instructions on hearsay and Confrontation** – Using *United States v. Bennett*, the court affirms that the district court need not give a separate instruction at the moment the hearsay evidence was admitted, as long as a proper overall instruction was given.

3. **Evidence regarding prior inconsistent statements** – The court references *United States v. Charboneau* and *Fed. R. Evidence 403* to indicate that the prosecution’s “toxic” statements (that the victim’s sister was killed by a gun) were permissible as they were elicited directly in response to defense cross‑examination and did not unreasonably prejudice the jury.

4. **Extraterritorial use of a police report for impeachment** – The court follows *United States v. Cumbie* to confirm that any extrinsic evidence used for impeachment must be authenticated, and notes that the district court properly required authentication before allowing the question.

In aggregate, the appellate court concludes that none of the evidentiary rulings “affected the outcome of the case” and that the cumulative admissibility of evidence was “harmless” under *United States v. DeMarce* and *United States v. Keys*. Thus, the conviction stands as it was decided by a jury that properly considered admissible evidence.

## B. Justification for affirming the appellate court’s discretion in sentencing within the guideline range

The next segment of the opinion tackles the sentencing issue. The defense’s argument rests upon the claim that the district court’s application of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4‑level enhancement “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that the sentence must be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary.” The court, however, isolates the issue. It observes:

* The district court correctly calculated the advisory guideline range based on the PSR’s offense level (35) and Criminal History Category (VI), arriving at a guideline range of 292–365 months.
* The 92‑month “extra” mandated under the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement was applied *after* the district court had already determined that the defendant’s prior violent felony convictions, discovered by the PSR, alone warranted a 34‑level offense (the ACJA enhancement). The four‑level "felony coincidence" enhancement was fully consistent with § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and was applied to a situational fact: the gun was used in a phone‑robbery setting “in connection with another felony offense.”
* The district court’s penalty of 292 months sits at the *lowest* end of the advisory range, leaving maximal presiding and parole discretion to the executive, hence satisfying the “no greater than necessary” requirement.

The court refers to *United States v. Dunn* and *United States v. Goodhouse* to underline its reluctance to overturn a sentencing decision that falls squarely within the guideline range and was reached after a comprehensive factual analysis. The decision to approve the sentence rests on the principle that an appellate court “receives a discretionary sentence that is reasonable in all material respects” when it sits within the guideline range, per *Gall v. United States* and *Rosales‑Mireles v. United States*.

## C. Clarification of the appellate standard for reviewing sentencing decisions

To preclude confusion about the appellate standard, the opinion repeats the core framework:

1. **First, the court determines that the district court did not err in calculating or selecting the guideline range.** If that procedural step fails, the entire sentence is void.
2. **Second, if the trial judge properly applied the guideline, the appellate court may only strike or alter the sentence if it establishes that a “substantial and unreasonable” departure from the guidelines, or “substantial prejudice” to the defendant, exists.** This is the standard articulated in *Rosales‑Mireles* and *United States v. Maluoth*.

The court stresses that the review does not entail a full pedigree of the sentencing analysis; it merely requires the record to contain evidence that the judge considered the § 3553(a) factors and that the sentence is within the range. Here those conditions are plainly satisfied. The district court provided a “close‑out” instruction that encompassed the voice of the judge’s deliberation; its absence of a “mechanical recitation” is unproblematic.

## D. Final conclusion and legal precedent cited

The opinion concludes with the assurance that none of the defense’s factual or procedural contentions succeeded. The appellate court affirms the conviction and sentence, the latter “upheld on appeal” because it lies within the advisory guideline range and reflects a reasonable exercise of the district court’s sentencing discretion.

The court cites, in turn, the following authorities to support its detailed analysis:

* *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014)
* *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887 (8th Cir. 2014)
* *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860 (8th Cir. 2010)
* *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907 (8th Cir. 2022)
* *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989 (8th Cir. 2009)
* *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982 (8th Cir. 2019)
* *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919 (8th Cir. 2013)
* *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38 (2007)
* *Rosales‑Mireles v. United States*, 585 U.S. 129 (2018)
* *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786 (8th Cir. 2023)
* *United States v. Maluoth*, 121 F.4th 1158 (8th Cir. 2024)

By referencing these precedents, the court demonstrates a systematic application of evidence law and sentencing doctrine: it confirms the admissibility of the defense’s objections, verifies the correctness of the guideline calculation, and reaffirms the sentence’s compliance with § 3553(a). The definitive holding—*the appeal is dismissed and the conviction and sentence are affirmed*—is the end product of a meticulously integrated factual, evidentiary, and sentencing analysis.
