Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Rule 404(b) and the “Intrinsic” Firearm Evidence Test: Why the Eighth Circuit’s Decision Matters

## Why the Cutbank Decision Matters

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit reaffirmed the “intrinsic evidence” doctrine that lets a defendant’s prior possession of a weapon be introduced to prove ownership or ongoing control in a later, unrelated case. The court’s majority, guided by *United States v. Battle* (774 F.3d 504, 2015), upheld the admission of D.F.’s testimony that the defendant had repeatedly seen him with a sawed‑off Marlin .22‑caliber rifle. One might assume that since the defendant was a repeat offender, the trial court could simply admit any evidence that showed the gun was “in his possession.” The Cutbank decision reveals that even in a case where a weapon is central to the charge, the evidentiary test is not a free pass; it is a nail‑cracking, rule‑based assessment of relevance versus unnecessary propitiousness.

The case matters for several reasons. First, it clarifies the scope of what can count as “intrinsic” evidence when the facts revolve around a weapon that has been previously used in violent conduct. Second, the decision celebrates the perverse tension within Rule 404(b) between the court’s prohibition on felony‑propensity evidence and the need to establish the weapon’s relationship to the defendant in a concrete manner. Third, the judgment implicitly signals how far the court is willing to extend the doctrine to accommodate “founder” evidence that might be peripheral to the alleged crime but still critical for proving possession. Finally, the case illustrates how a trial judge’s discretion under Rule 404(b) can be reviewed and ultimately upheld by an appellate court, treating the evidentiary ruling as a question of law rather than fact.

## The Intrinsic Evidence Test Revisited

Rule 404(b) forbids the use of “incriminating” evidence to establish a defendant’s propensity to commit the crime charged, unless the evidence is directly relevant to some other issue. In the gun‑possession context, this clash often plays out over whether “prior possession” evidence is allowed. Courts distinguish between “propitious” evidence—material only in a broader, prejudicial sense—and “intrinsic” evidence—material to a specific fact essential to the criminal prosecution (e.g., gun ownership, lawful possession, or control).

*Battle* is the pivotal controlling case when it comes to firearms. The Ninth Circuit held that, “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” That ruling created a tiered approach: if the prior possession evidence is directly tied to the asserted later possession, it is *intrinsic* and admissible; if it is merely suggestive of a propensity to commit violence, it is *propitious* and inadmissible.

The Eighth Circuit in *Cutbank* adopted the same reasoning, despite the judge’s terse opinion. The court emphasized that the sheriff’s evidence of the defendant’s prior possession with the same sawed‑off Marlin was, “directly relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession of the firearm before the incident.” The court granted the defendant’s pre‑trial motion to exclude the testimony, finding no conflict with Rule 404(b).

A more nuanced reading of *Battle* itself shows that the court deliberately focuses on *ownership or control*, not on mere “relationship” to the firearm. The “continuous possession” standard is required: the evidence must support the idea that the defendant had an ongoing interest in the weapon, not just that he had once touched it briefly. This “continuous possession” cutoff is why D.F.’s testimony that the defendant had seen the gun a “handful of times” was deemed admissible—those observations suggested ownership or at least a recurring connection that satisfied the “continuous possession” bar.

## How the “Intrinsic” Test Was Applied

Cutbank’s prosecution rested on a clear set of facts: the defendant, after a drunken binge and methamphetamine use, wielded a Marlin .22 with a sawed‑off barrel and a shortened stock, then forced the victim, D.F., into a closet, kept her at gunpoint for hours, and fled with her phone and the weapon. The gun was recovered with incriminating DNA. The confession—though punctuated by calls for the gang—pointed to a prior pattern of violence and weapon use.

Given that the same gun was pivotal to the jury’s determination of the felony‑in‑possession charge, the question was whether the defendants’ earlier encounters with the weapon were “intrinsic” or “propitious.” The court concluded that they were intrinsic because:

1. **Same Weapon, Same Context**: The Marlin was one
specifically‑altered weapon, and the plaintiff’s description matched the same one seized at the scene.

2. **Evidence of Ownership / Control**: D.F.'s testimony that she saw the defendant “handle” or “carry” the gun over multiple occasions supported the inference that the defendant had ongoing control of it.

3. **Continuous Possession**: The use of “handful of times” was taken to satisfy *continuous possession*—the weapon was not merely an item that belonged on the defendant’s property; it was a weapon that the defendant had displayed repeatedly over time.

The court carefully asserted that it was *not* this evidence’s potential to suggest a propensity to violence, but its role in establishing *ownership* that mattered.

In combination, these facts placed the evidence firmly inside the allowed realm. The court’s answer was a brief “yes” without a long, academic footnote—justifying the decision with two precedents: *Battle* and *United States v. Charboneau*, the latter clarifying the Confrontation Clause issue when testifying about the defendant’s statements.

## Where Rule 404(b) Still Says “No”

Nevertheless, the *Cutbank* ruling did not radicalize the Rule 404(b) doctrine. A defendant still cannot introduce general evidence that the gun belonged to him solely to imply a violent inclination absent a specific, credible link to ownership, control, or possession. For example, if the defendant had merely claimed at a random moment, “I use guns a lot” in some unrelated conversation, the court would still bar that. The criticaries of the “intrinsic” doctrine argue that prior possession evidence, when admitted in highly suggestive language, may tip evidence toward a “propitious” verdict, especially in the “shock and awe” of the modern criminal justice system.

**The real test lies in the jury’s willingness to hear, so the court must keep an eye out for the potential for prejudice.** If the testimony can be read outside the context of establishing control and is merely a flashy pop‑in of “I saw the guy touch a gun,” appellate law typically robs that evidence. In *Cutbank*, the appellate court remanded to a “harmless error” bar: because the defense argued no prejudice, the court upheld the ruling. Thus, in future cases, the presence of the same weapon and the defendant’s repeated, observable interactions with it are often sufficient for the evidence to be deemed intrinsically relevant.

## Implications for Future Gun‑Possession Cases

1. **Pre‑trial Motions**: Defense attorneys will continue to file pre‑trial motions under Rule 404(b) more aggressively. They will need to demonstrate the *continuous possession* standard, not just “he saw a gun.” The *Cutbank* precedent gives competitors concrete points: “the same weapon and evidence of ongoing control is admissible; mere claims are not.”

2. **Prosecution Strategy**: Prosecution attorneys can safely rely on eyewitness accounts of the defendant handling or having active contact with a specific firearm. The *Cutbank* ruling reaffirms the use of that evidence in cases where the prosecution’s case is significantly weapon‑centric.

3. **Jury Inquiries**: Trial judges must remind juries that the key here is ownership, not propensity. Jurors could think the defendant’s prior possession proves a violent inclination. The court’s decision underscores the need for instructions that emphasize relevance but undercut prejudice. This is one of the key challenges in balancing Rule 404(b) with relevance.

4. **Cross‑examination and the “Using Evidence to Counter Propensity”**: The appellate court’s assent to the trial judge’s ruling says that cross‑examination of witnesses about their prior statements regarding weapons is permissible provided the underlying evidence is intrinsic. The *Cutbank* ruling left open the administration of a “weights” analysis for any gun‑related identification and potentially opens the door for defendants to use victim testimony about prior weapon exposure. In practice, this means that witnesses who are sworn in after the fact may, if they have genuinely seen the defendant with the same weapon, be used to substantiate ownership without falling prey to the propensity exception.

5. **Sentencing and Further Jurisdictional Practice**: Though the *Cutbank* case was specifically a trial‑level admissibility decision, its ripple effects run into sentencing. Gun-possession cases tend to carry enhanced penalties, and the admissibility of prior possession evidence could provide sentencing courts with a firmer basis for establishing “the defendant had a weapon in a violent context before." Because the evidentiary ruling dovetails with the *Acquired Criminal History Category* (CHC) and the *Armed Career Criminal Act*, the permissible evidence provides a strong foundation for higher‐weight offenses or ever–tilting the sentencing guidelines.

## The Debate Should Continue

While *Cutbank* seems clear, the line between intrinsic and propitious evidence is markedly slippery. Federal courts continue to wrestle with how much the evidence should revolve around *owning*, *controlling*, or simply *having used* a gun. The *Cumbie* case (28 F.4th 907) hints at the necessity for claiming that *dispositions* (particularly unreliable or inadmissible extrinsic evidence for impeachment) are properly authenticated. Yet the *Cutbank* opinion remains an actionable checkpoint because it’s anchored in *Battle* and the concept of “continuous possession.”

The intrinsic test, therefore, remains a living doctrine. If future evidence swallows a guilty defendant’s own testimony for a weapon or an adjoining piece of evidence implicate an illegal impetus, the appellate focus will shift to whether that evidence is *propitious* and may lead to the prejudice doctrine in *United States v. DeMarce* or *United States v. Keys*. Jurisprudence across the courts will need to clarify how the *continuous possession* bar can survive a reversal on prejudice. Will a jury’s reasoned belief that the defendant “might have had a weapon” be deemed prejudicial? The answer remains uncertain.

## Conclusion

The 8th Circuit in *Cutbank* reasserted a gate that clarifies how to link prior gun possession to modern claims of unlawful possession. The ruling stands as a useful lighthouse for defense counsel foretelling Rule 404(b) objections and for prosecution lawyers who wish to use eyewitness testimony. By clarifying that a *handful of times* of the defendant handling a specific weapon is admissible, the court sets a clear contours for the court’s discretion. The Subtlety lies in ensuring that those demonstrations of possession cannot be read into a broader, prejudicial pattern; the evidence must remain about “ownership” not about “violence.” For anyone on the front lines of criminal proceedings, the *Cutbank* decision does indeed matter, not just for its merits, but for the door it opens for future case law to understand how our courts strike a delicate balance between the rights of the accused and the interests of justice.



# The 3553(a) Post‑Guideline Sentencing Debate: How the Eighth Circuit Reinforces the “Sufficient but Not Greater Than Necessary” Doctrine

## Why This Opinion Matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Justin Cutbank* is a textbook case in which the appellate court reaffirms the deference it owes to district courts when they calculate the sentencing guideline range, choose which § 3553(a) factors to consider, and ultimately impose a sentence within that range—no matter how aggravated the defendant’s record may appear. For those of us watching the erosive slant toward “maximum‑sentence‑first” approaches, this ruling is a welcome reminder that the post‑guideline landscape is still governed by a framework that requires a “sufficient but not greater than necessary” outcome. The court’s insistence that the defendant’s “within‑range” 292‑month term is correct, despite the protracted and violent nature of Cutbank’s offenses, shows the limits of appellate intervention in sentencing disputes.

## The Core Question: Was the 4‑Level Enhancement Proper?

The point of contention for Cutbank was whether the four‑level increase applied under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)—for “use or possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense”—unlawfully pushed the district court into the upper end of the Advisory Guidelines. The statute states that the enhancement “applies if a defendant used or possessed any firearm ... in connection … with another felony offense, regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The court accepted the PSR’s factual findings that Cutbank had indeed used a Marlin‑.22 to facilitate his escape from the house and later from police grounds, thus satisfying the factual prerequisite for the enhancement.

The appellate court’s review was strictly non‑deceptive: *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919 (8th Cir. 2013), invites the court to reverse only if “the district court acted with manifest error” or “the judge dramatically misapprehended the law.” By aligning with *Dunn*, the court underscored the idea that sentencing footnotes or marginally different arithmetic do not automatically trigger appellate reversal. This point is useful to practitioners who sometimes find themselves on the wrong side of a marginal guideline calculation.

## The Window of § 3553(a) Discretion

Having accepted the statutory calculation, the court turned to the post‑guideline discretion for sentencing. In the United States, the district judge may depart from the guideline range only if the sentence is “substantially unreasonable” or in violation of Section 3553(a), the “sufficient but not greater than necessary” standard. *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38 (2007), settled the big picture: the Eighth Circuit, like other circuits, treats a district court’s failure to calculate the guideline range correctly as a procedural error; a failure to consider the § 3553(a) factors as a potentially substantive mistake. Cutbank’s arguments that the judge simply cherry‑picked favorable factors (e.g., “relatively short 292‑month sentence”) were dismissed on the ground that the district court’s engagement with those factors was evident in the sentencing memorandum and PSR.

*Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786 (8th Cir. 2023), and *Masood*, 133 F.4th 799 (8th Cir. 2025), are the most recent cases that the court used to reinforce its position. Both decisions state that an appellate court must first confirm that the district court properly defined the guideline range and considered relevant § 3553(a) factors. Only after that can it adjudicate whether the final sentence was “substantially unreasonable.” In neither case was a “substantial reasonableness” bar met; the sentence was at the lower boundary of the 292‑to‑365‑month range and the court found no evidence that it was “more than necessary.”

## The “Sufficient but Not Greater Than Necessary” Compass

What immediately stands out is the court’s reliance on the *core principle* embedded in § 3553(a) that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary.” The court rejected Cutbank’s assertion that the 292‑month term “conflicts with the core principle of 3553(a) because it is somehow… too much.” The judge had, in its own sentencing memorandum, considered the extraordinary circumstances of the crime—a domestic abuse scenario, rape of a concubine, assault on a police officer, and multiple violent previous felonies—yet decided that the guideline lower bound was “sufficient” and that venturing above it would have been “unnecessary.” The appellate court took this at face value, a position common in post‑guideline cases that dismiss claims of “punitive overreach,” unless the evidence supports a contradictory narrative.

In other words, *Cutbank* reiterates the movement away from punitive discretion, restoring a more disciplined approach that mandates the blockade of “morbid curiosity” or “judicial grandstanding” arguments. It suggests that if a judge is willing to accept the lower end of the guideline range as sufficient, appellate courts will honor that judgment unless there is a clear violation of § 3553(a) or manifest procedural error.

## Do the 3553(a) Factors Have Any Bite?

The court’s “broad discretion” dictum is a double‑edged sword. On the one hand, it gives judges room to consider a defendant’s character, ties to the community, youth of the offender, or other mitigating evidence. On the other hand, the subjective nature of many § 3553(a) factors creates a domain where appellate courts stand back and let district courts perform the “sweeping last‑minute citation of the factors.” In *Cutbank*, the judge’s memorandum made it clear that a key factor was that the conviction involved a “violent felony” that had led to repeat victimization of the defendant’s girlfriend and her children. The appellate court’s response was almost non‑chalant: as long as the district court “clearly considered the factors,” its final decision stands.

In practice, this can allow judges to impose relatively severe sentences even when defendants plead “introspection” or “only a few months of imprisonment.” The bar of “substantial reasonableness” is difficult to cross; it requires clear evidence that the district judge “evidently misapplied the Sentencing Guidelines” or “willingly weighted the factors in a ‘neo‑levy’ fashion.” This is a high hurdle, well beyond the mere fact that a defendant has multiple prior convictions.

## Alleged Appellate Remedies: A Curt Review

The appellate court’s discretion to subsequently re‑evaluate the district court’s § 3553(a) analysis is limited. As noted in *DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989 (8th Cir. 2009), appellate courts rarely loiter over what amounts to a well‑scrutinized but “harmless error” in the evidentiary domain. Here, the court looks at whether *“the jury may have been substantially swayed”* by improperly admitted evidence—an amplifying factor that Cutbank could not prove. Similarly, the court recycles the state of the record: the district judge’s docket, sentencing memorandum, and PSR all suggest that the sentencing discretion was exercised in a “reasonable” way.

The *Corrections to Step 0* approach (Lotby, 2023) suggests that appellate courts focus on whether the court “misapplied the law” or “evidently calculated incorrectly.” The *Cutbank* decision illustrates that the court is pivoting away from mere policy critique of the sentence’s harshness: no level of dissent in the sentencing memorandum can automatically trigger a reversal. As such, the 3553(a) standard has been reaffirmed as an interpretive rather than a remedial bright line.

## The Broader Doctrinal Implications

### 3553(a) vs. Spirit of the Guidelines

The consistent theme of the *Cutbank* ruling is that the guidelines are advisory, not mandatory. When a judge respects the lower bound of the guideline range, the appellate court will not swoop in unless the judge “obviously misapplied the statutes.” This resonates with *Goodhouse*, where the court explained that if a district judge correctly applies the guidelines and acknowledges the § 3553(a) factors, the appellate court will merely make a “mere perusal” of the record and then affirm.

Practically speaking, this means that the guidelines do not diminish sentencing discretion. They merely provide a “common baseline” to which district courts can anchor their decisions. The appellate courts, in *Cutbank*, confirm that the baseline does not automatically inhibit the district court’s discretion to impose a “de facto maximum.” Like *Dunn* and *Gall*, the doctrine is that the appellate court looks at the “face of the record” for errors, not at any arguments brought after the fact.

### The Deference Standard Reinstated

The deference standard has been a recurring question in post‑guideline sentencing disputes. In *Masood*, the court noted that the judge’s “review of the PSR and sentencing memorandum” is adequate to evaluate the reasonableness of the judgment, reinforcing a deference stance. Thus, *Cutbank* stands as an affirmation that the deference standard remains the default rule; plaintiffs should only bring new evidence or show a “manifest” legal misinterpretation.

### Potential for Future Setback?

While *Cutbank* may delight defenders of sentencing discretion, it also foreshadows a future trend of stricter appellate review over procedural missteps rather than substantive sentencing arguments. If the Ninth Circuit or Seventh Circuit adopt a more rigorous review in the future, the *Cutbank* line may shift toward a more skeptical posture. Nevertheless, the core message is that the “sufficient but not greater than necessary” standard remains formidable.

## Practical Takeaways for Litigators

1. **Disclose the Entire Evidence Pack:** If you’re fooling around with the PSR and may want to challenge a district court’s application of an enhancement, the *Cutbank* decision reminds us that you must show that the judge made an “obvious” error or had a judicious error in recording facts. Merely pointing to a generous sentence is not enough.

2. **Leverage the Guideline Range:** The Eighth Circuit will not review a sentencing range that has been calculated correctly. Focus instead on query points like the “relevance" of an enhancement or whether the judge properly considered § 3553(a) factors.

3. **Use the “Substantially Unreasonable” Criterion Wisely:** “Substantial” is the touch‑stone. It is not enough to argue that the sentence seems “hyper‑punitive”; you have to demonstrate that the judge “demonstrably and irrationally” overrode the advisory guidelines.

4. **Prepare for the ‘Make‑it‑Clear’ Requirement:** The district judge must “clearly consider” the § 3553(a) factors. Prepare your memorandum to illustrate that you weighed each factor. The appellate court will not frown upon an omnibus “we did weigh” statement if you can back it up with concrete discussion.

5. **Mind the Post‑Trial Evidence Circle:** The *Cutbank* court reiterated that post‑trial evidence is a “harmless” error barring reversal. If you rely on later evidence for sentencing review, ensure it was admissible or that any evidence that was excluded or incorrectly admitted is demonstrably “deterrent to the outcome.”

## Conclusion

The *Cutbank* opinion is a light‑bulb moment for those following the evolution of post‑guideline sentencing. It underscores the remarkable stability of the “sufficient but not greater than necessary” standard. In practice, the appellate court’s refusal to rattle the guidelines even in a case involving a violent, multiple‑felony felon is reassuring for prosecutors, disquieting for defense attorneys, and intellectually satisfying for scholars.

While the Eighth Circuit is not at all showing itself eager to disrupt the established post‑guideline doctrine, its decision gives hope that the Supreme Court may soon revisit the inferno of the “maximum‑sentence‑first” trend. Until that happens, we can take **Cutbank** as a safe guardian of the principle that a sentence must match the magnitude of the wrongdoing and grasp the subtle dance between the guidelines and the *​3553(a)* standard that remains the bedrock of federal sentencing scholarship.



# A Inside Look at Evidentiary Abuses: Confrontation, Hearsay, and Striking Testimony in Federal Trials

## Why This Case Matters

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit upheld a potentially “harmless” jury verdict and a 292‑month sentence while quashing every one of the defendant’s evidentiary appeals. Where the drawing board is set on a federal firearm offense, the court’s rulings illustrate how the intersection of the Confrontation Clause, Rule 404(b) propensity concerns, and the district court’s broad discretionary power over “extrinsic” evidence continues to be a battleground for defense counsel. Practitioners will benefit from a close look at how the court weighed (or failed to weigh) D.F.’s statements to police, the testimony that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun, and the challenge to the admissibility of a police report. Each pivot is a reminder that, under the Constitution and the Rules, the defense’s job is not just to argue but to preserve precise arguments on appeal.

## The Confrontation Clause Revisited: Statements to Police

Cutbank’s first contentions were conveniently vague, but the core issue hinged on whether her statements to law‑enforcement officers could serve as admissible evidence, or if the prosecution needed to provide a limiting instruction to shield the jury from “propensity” arguments or “hearsay” concerns. Under the Sixth Amendment, the defendant has a right to confront testimonial evidence. The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Charboneau* clarified that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant appears personally to testify, even if their earlier statements were made to the police. In the Cutbank case, the district court found the rule’s two cornerstones—direct testimony in court and the offender’s opportunity to cross‑examine—were satisfied. The court ruled that the prior statements were *in situ* evidence that could be properly probed. 

The appellate court’s analysis reinforced that the government’s veracity argument is permissible: “If the statements establish context or are at least relevant, they can be introduced, and if the defendant is afforded the opportunity to cross‑examine, it satisfies the Confrontation Clause.” That said, the court’s blanket dismissal of Cutbank’s request for a *limiting instruction* demonstrates the court’s willingness to lean on the *Bennett* standard, which places the burden on the defense to argue that the evidence is “at issue” and that the jury should be warned that it is not. The defense’s failure to articulate a *“substantial prejudice”* argument here, coupled with the court’s insistence that the judge “need not instruct the jury regarding each item ... when the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial,” ultimately led to the brief’s erasure.  Practitioners should remember that a limiting instruction is not an absolute prerogative; it is only required when the evidence at issue could be confused with something that the statutes or laws would otherwise forbid.

## Propensity, Redaction, and the “Rough Draft” of Rule 404(b)

The trial court came away more cautious in extending the *Battle* principle to D.F.’s prior observations of the sawed‑off rifle. The court accepted the government’s call to treat the testifying witnesses’ memories as “intrinsic evidence” that might provide a continuous possession narrative. Cutbank on appeal, in a case study that echoed *Battle* 11 years later, noted that the evidence was “pre‑testimonies” that might be *propensity evidence*—a classic intersection of *Rule 404(b)*. Under that rule, the court must refuse to admit evidence that could only show a “tendency” to commit the crime at issue unless it also has a *primary purpose* to prove an element of the offense or is relevant to show “control” or “ownership,” as the *Battle* framework allows.  

The court’s choice to root the decision in *Battle* without asking for a *margin* test seemed to reflect doctrine that the *intrinsic* evidence can withstand a 404(b) challenge if it bears “relevance to ownership or control.” Still, Cutbank’s arguments lost ground because the record did not offer any additional nuance: the court plainly believed that such self‑contained statements should be perfectly permissible. The lack of an argument suggesting that the statements’ *propensity* aspect—“handful of times”—could either show that Cutbank had a “habit” of carrying a weapon or create an inference about future conduct, mattered here.  

In short, the court enacted a *“law of the case”* that placed a huge dam on the water of potential prejudice. For practitioners, the takeaway is that *intrinsic* evidence *does not automatically survive* a 404(b) challenge, nor is *“ownership”* a green light. A full, well‑structured argument that the evidence satisfies *Parker* and that the *risk of prejudice* is *material* versus *cardinal* should be front‑loaded early in the pre‑trial brief.

## The “Sister” Edge: Does a Personal Grief Testimony Count as Unfair Prejudice?

Cutbank’s filing suggested that the testimony that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun was “irrelevant” and “unwarranted” and should have been struck under Rule 403. The district court, citing Fed. R. Evidence § 403, ultimately upheld it, noting that the statement addressed prior inconsistencies after cross‑examination on the gun’s presence. The court held that the testimony was *“explanatory”* rather than *propensity*—the witness was explaining why she had acted suspiciously about the gun.  The court’s reasoning followed *Cumbie*, which clarified that any extrinsic evidence used for impeachment must be authenticated. Because the testimony under argument was *in‑court* “to rebut inconsistencies” rather than *outside* (i.e. “extrinsic”), the court felt it could apply a *“per se”* standard.  

From a doctrinal perspective, the court’s decision sits squarely on the principle that *Rule 403* does not disallow *explanation* but may strike *in‑court* or “extrinsic” evidence that is unreasonable or confusing. Here the court ELICITED a *"no prejudice" argument for the defense*—the *lawful* cross‑examination rendering the joke. Practitioners should not rely on the *"this is just explaining the suspicions"* line as a blanket exemption; it requires a *demonstrated link* between the testimony and the specific questioning, and an application of the *“unnecessary, confusing, or misleading”* criterion.  

Cutbank’s lack of a beyond‑reasonable doubt argument—claiming that the defense was to prove that "the controversy over the weight of the case fell below the constitutional threshold."—left the issue resolved in the default way the court always does when the assertion is “no prejudice.”  A future defense might consider a *“potential for cumulative prejudice”* argument, invoking *pagan/ideology* as a *material prejudice condition*. That approach would provide a more robust challenge than applying the *“explanation”* label alone.

## The Police Report: The “Extrinsic Evidence” Infiltration

The most intriguing portion of Cutbank’s appeal was the claim that the district court “thwarted” his effort to cross‑examine C.F. about police statements she had made to an investigator. The government’s use of a police report introduces the *extrinsic evidence* question: is the report admissible or must it be authenticated? The court, following *Cumbie*, held that the police report was *not* the subject of cross‑examination, so it fell under the umbrella of "impeachment by extrinsic evidence" and therefore needed authenticating foundations. The defense alleged that the court did not permit them to question *C.F.* about contradictions in the report, but the appeal missed that point. The court held that the *court’s refusal* was procedural, not erroneous, and also that the *refused* question did not establish *substantial prejudice*. 

For practitioners, this demonstrates that *extrinsic evidence*—be it a report, diary, or any outside source—must be treated with the same care as a *direct witness*’s statements. Courts are tending to look for an *“authenticity”* question in the *Cumbie* sense: the defense must prove that the extrinsic document is credible, that the source can be identified, and that the record contains *sufficient* information to do so.  The reversal of this approach would presumably hinge on a fact’s *materiality* and *probability of prejudice*—both not present in Cutbank.  

## Handy Takeaways for Practitioners

1. **Never Assume a “Non‑Propensity” Argument Is Irrelevant**  
   Whether the evidence is “intrinsic” or “extrinsic”, the defense must show *without exhaustive analysis* that the evidence satisfies the *Rules* and that any prejudice is marginal.  The “party‑based” decisions *Battle* and *Cumbie* illustrate a growing trend: If a witness provides *proper context*, the court tends to treat it as weldable, but the defense must still lift the *stone* by arguing the *potential for prejudice*.

2. **A Limiting Instruction Is Largely the Defense’s Responsibility**  
   The court’s failure to give a limiting instruction here is not necessarily an artifact of a “lack of justice.”  If a witness is likely to prejudice the jury, it is a “rule” action: the defense *must* argue it.  The same is true for Rule 404(b)-type concerns: hand‑written notes referencing a prior incident might count as “pre‑text”.

3. **Ground Your Evidence‑Rule Arguments in the *“Weapon‑Ownership”* Anthology**  
   Courts are generous when *ownership* or *continuous possession* readps to a *weapon* type—*Battle* is now a “stand‑alone” point.  If the evidence is *marginal*, you need a *“prejudice”* hypothesis: how would that marginal detail shift the jury from 77% conviction to 100%?  Write a *“Preliminary Prejudice”* paragraph.

4. **Authenticate Extrinsic Evidence Early**  
   No *“Courser”* exculpatory logs or police reports can fly in the court of law unless the defense *authenticates* them.  The best practice: file a *“verifiable record”* briefing on “authenticity” before the final ruling.  AV send a *list of sources* and *explain method*, if necessary, to pre‑empt the *“extrinsic”* objection.

5. **Document the *Sentencing Process* Early**  
   Contextual clues from the *“guidelines”* memo can be anachronistic in appellate ballots.  The Eighth Circuit here reaffirmed the *“certainty”* of being within the guidelines, then punished “pre‑sentencing arguments” as abstract.  A well‑documented *“argument trail”* shows you considered *3553(a)* factors, not that you should have restrictive or controlling *factors*.

## Concluding Thoughts

The *Cutbank* decision is, in many ways, the gate‑keeper’s final kiss of approval for conventional practice: literally police statements to a victim, “sister‑accident” numbers, and automatically tangled notes from a police department all stay in the courtroom.  The court’s affirmation underscores that the most effective defense strategy is to *preserve* weaknesses early.  If the defense manages to *build* a “prejudice hypothesis” that is *material* and *substantiated*, the appellate court will pause; otherwise the default law continues.  Just as the Fourth Amendment protects *reasonably expected* confrontation, the Fifth ensures *fully litigable justice*—the combined lines of *Rule 403*, *404(b)*, and *striking testimony* are the constitutional scaffolding we must routinely check before a jury swings the gavel.



# United States v. Cutbank: The Practical Implications of the Armed Career Criminal Act’s Felon‑in‑Possession Enhancements  

## Why this case matters  

When a felon in possession case is combined with the *Armed Career Criminal Act* (ACCA), the sentencing grid can turn a single offense into a five‑to‑seven‑year prison term. In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit tested how a four‑level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) should be applied when a defendant’s firearm is allegedly used “in connection with another felony” even though there is no separate charge or conviction for that other felony. The appellate decision confirms that the *Eighth* will continue to treat the “in connection” provision as a *strictly interpretive* question, not a factual one that invites the trial court’s discretion. For practitioners, this means two things: (1) the ACCA’s *use or possession* enhancement will almost always be added wholesale to the offense level once the factual basis is established, and (2) any attempt to challenge that addition on the grounds that it might “conflict with § 3553(a)” is unlikely to succeed unless the enhancement clearly exceeds the guideline range.

## The factual and procedural backdrop  

Cutbank’s indictment revolved around a domestic dispute that turned violent, culminating in the discovery of a sawed‑off Marlin .22 with an obliterated serial number. The jury found him guilty of felon‑in‑possession and of having at least three prior violent felonies, tipping him into the ACCA’s enhanced penalty zone. The district court adopted the PSR’s *offense level* of 35, with a defendant *Criminal History Category* (CHC) of VI, which translated into a guideline range of 292‑365 months. The key dispute—the appellate question—centered on the *four‑level* increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which the PSR asserted because the gun was used in the context of the domestic abuse offense.

## How the SC ruling applied the 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement  

The court reviewed the *text* of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) – “any firearm or ammunition used or possessed in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense.” The *exclusive* element is the “connection” between the firearm and another felony. Because the domestic assault was a *federal* crime (the gun was used on a domestic‑abuse charge), the connection was present. The PSR’s *factual statements* were deemed "sufficient" to confirm the connection, so the court did not abuse its discretion in accepting that fact and awarding the four‑level bump.

Importantly, the court did *not* treat the enhancement as a matter of defendant‑at‑trial error. Once the PSR packet shows a qualifying “connection,” the enhancement attaches automatically. The court therefore adopted the *higher* offense level (35 rather than 34, if the alternative acceleration had been considered). The *guideline range* remained 292‑365 months; the four‑level bump did not push the range beyond the allowable seven‑year extension that the ACCA intends. The appellate court thus affirmed the district court’s sentence of 292 months – the lower end of the range.

## What this means for “in connection” interpretation  

The decision reinforces that the phrase “in connection with another felony” is *strictly a statutory expression* that the court will apply per the text and existing precedent. In *Dunn* and *Battle*, the Eighth reaffirmed that ACCA enhancements are automatically added when the factual basis is present, even if the other felony has not been separately charged. By interpreting “in connection” in this way, the court preserves the *punitive purpose* of the ACCA while preventing the prosecution from inflating a sentence beyond what would be justified by the literal statute. That said, the court’s discussion—particularly the reference to *3553(a)*—underscores that an enhancement can still be contested if it yields an “unreasonable” sentence outside the guideline range. Here it did not.

## Implications for future ACCA cases  

1. **Adopt a proactive approach to the “connection” fact**: Defendants should insist on a factual baseline that the firearm was used during the other felony. In the *Cutbank* case, the PSR documented that the gun was involved in the domestic assault, so the enhancement was unchallenged. If a defendant’s defense can show that the firearm was *not* used in the other felony, they can attempt to have the enhancement struck.

2. **Expect the guideline range to absorb the enhancement**: The increase in offense level will often push a defendant into a higher CHC and a broader guideline range, but it rarely pushes the range “out of bounds.” If the enhancement pushes a defendant another level up in CHC, the penal range will expand accordingly, preserving the *Toxic Gray Zone* that the Department of Justice alleges to be unconstitutional.

3. **Beware of “reverse erasure”**: Some attorneys have argued that if a defendant’s sentence *exceeds* the guideline range *because* of a statutory enhancement, the penalty may be “unreasonable” under § 3553(a). *Cutbank* demonstrates that such a challenge must be tied to a clear procedural error or a violation of due process. Lacking that, the appellate court will likely affirm.

4. **Use of the PSR as a safeguard**: Because the PSR’s factual basis is considered *obligatory* for the sentencing court, we should ensure that PSR factual statements are as robust as possible, especially when the case hinges on a *connection* baton. If the PSR is thin, the defense may be able to argue that the enhancement should not apply.

## Bottom line for practitioners  

*United States v. Cutbank* is a textbook case confirming that the *Armed Career Criminal Act’s* 2‑K‑2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement is applied automatically when the factual record meets the statutory definition. The Eighth will uphold a sentence within guidelines, even if that sentence reflects the *maximum* guidance message that the statutory text intends. For defense counsel, focusing on eliminating the “connection” element may still be the most viable strategy to prevent or eradicate the four‑level bump. For prosecutors, ensuring that the PSR establishes a solid “used in connection” fact will guarantee the ACCA’s punitive impact is carried through to the final sentence. In the near future, as the Department of Justice and the courts grapple with the *Accredited* “Toxic Gray Zone” claims, *Cutbank* provides the procedural foothold that shows how the ACCA’s enhancements will be applied consistently, barring concrete constitutional challenges.
