Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 4: # 4. Segregability and the ‘Reasonable Steps’ Requirement

In the FOIA context, the statutory mandate that a federal agency “take [reasonable] steps” to segregate and release non‑exempt information is a procedural hurdle that sits beneath the substantive question of whether an exemption applies. The *Foad Farahi* case illustrates how the court applies this duty and why it ultimately held that no portion of the FBI file could be released without jeopardizing the agency’s investigations. The analysis pivots on three pillars: the statutory language itself, the doctrine of “inextricable intertwining,” and the procedural requirements for category‑by‑category segregation.

## 4.1 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii): Agency Obligation to Segregate

Section 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) directs agencies to “take reasonable steps to segregate and release non‑exempt information.” The statute does not define “reasonable,” but the courts have consistently read it as a standard that requires the agency to actively separate exempt material from any non‑exempt content that can be isolated without imposing a significant burden on the agency or jeopardizing exempt interests. Importantly, the obligation is two‑fold: first, the agency must determine what portions of a record are exempt; second, it must render any non‑exempt portions available to the requester.

In *Farahi*, the FBI complied with the procedural requirement in two distinct stages. Initially, after the agency’s 2019 summary‑judgment motion, it presented a declaration from Section Chief David Hardy that described the nature of its law‑enforcement investigations and asserted that the withheld records could not be reasonably segregated. The district court accepted Hardy’s declaration but ordered the FBI to update its assessment of pending investigations, underscoring the need for contemporaneous evidence. Subsequently, the FBI supplied a supplemental, ex‑parte declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender, clarifying that the investigations remained ongoing and elaborating on the reasons the documents could not be separated. The court found Bender’s information sufficiently detailed to satisfy the “reasonable steps” requirement, allowing it to deny the request for release.

Thus, the statute’s procedural duty was met not through an exhaustive line‑by‑line review—an impossible task given the size of the file—but through a series of credible, agency‑provided affidavits that explained why the exempt portions were inextricably linked to the non‑exempt material. The court held that the FBI had taken “reasonable steps” under the circumstances.

## 4.2 Doctrine of Inextricable Intertwining—Juarez, Mead, and Perioperative Precedents

A key concept in adjudicating FOIA segregation is that a record can be withheld entirely when the exempt and non‑exempt portions are “inextricably intertwined.” The D.C. Circuit has applied this principle to both criminal and national‑security investigations.

In **Juarez v. DOJ**, the court held that an agency may withhold a record in its entirety when a “reasonable specificity” in an affidavit demonstrates that the exempt portion “is inextricably intertwined” with the non‑exempt portion, making segregation impractical or harmful. **Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force** further clarified that segregation is impractical if a large amount of exempt information is peppered throughout the record such that extracting the non‑exempt content would not meaningfully reduce the risk of disclosure.

The **Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs.** decision amplified the *Mead* test by emphasizing that the focus of FOIA is information rather than mere words. Even if non‑exempt language appears line‑by‑line, if the overall context is so permeated by the exempt material that its removal would not provide meaningful relief to the requester, segregation is not required.

In *Farahi*, the FBI’s affidavits argued that the investigations were sufficiently broad—encompassing alleged ties to terrorist organizations, foreign combatants, and extremist networks—that key evidence was dispersed throughout the document. Consequently, the non‑exempt information was seen as part of a “larger investigative narrative” and therefore inseparable from the exempt portions. The court agreed that the FBI had met its burden by showing the inextricable intertwining, and therefore the agency could withhold the entire record.

## 4.3 The FBI’s Category‑by‑Category Justification and the Requirement of Distinct Categories

Under FOIA, when an agency withholds documents “on a category‑by‑category basis,” it must use categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category (**CREW v. DOJ**). This requirement prevents overly broad categorizations that would effectively release nothing.

The FBI in *Farahi* identified several categories of records: those relating to ongoing investigations of national security violations, those pertaining to suspected associations with designated terrorist entities, and others concerning background data on the subject. Farahi challenged the breadth of those categories, arguing that they were not sufficiently distinct and therefore failed to satisfy the FOIA requirement. However, the court noted that the FBI failed to provide a concrete justification for why each category was separate, and that Farahi had not offered a specific alternative. Because the court could not determine that any particular category represented a distinct, legally separable set of documents, it concluded that the FBI had not met the necessary condition for category‑based withholding.

Despite this objection, the court still upheld the denial. The rationale was that even if the categorization was imperfect, the *inextricable intertwining* doctrine and the overall affirmative declarations about the ongoing investigations were sufficient to meet the broader “reasonable steps” obligation. The procedural failure in presenting distinct categories did not render the entire withholding invalid, because the court already found the agency’s substantive exemption claim to be valid.

## 4.4 The Court’s Evaluation of Bender’s Supplementary Ex‑parte Argument

The most significant addition to the FBI’s justification was the ex‑parte declaration from Acting Section Chief Bender. This affidavit served two purposes: it confirmed that the investigations remained ongoing as of March 2023, and it supplied additional narrations of why the FBI could not segregate the exempt material.

The district court examined Bender’s assertions in light of Hardy’s earlier affidavits and the legal requirement that an agency may rely on *ex‑parte* statements when no alternative exists. The court recognized that FOIA plaintiffs are typically on the losing side when a declaration is offered ex‑parte, yet it was bound by the statutory standard that a non‑exempt body may provide such evidence to satisfy the “reasonable steps” requirement if the material is otherwise unspoolable.

In its analysis, the court noted that Bender’s declaration simply reiterated earlier statements rather than adding new factual detail. Nevertheless, because the earlier affidavits had already established pendency and the nature of the investigation, Bender’s added confirmation was deemed sufficient. The court also explained that it did not need to disclose the substance of the ex‑parte declaration on the public record; the affidavit’s contents were deemed non‑public even under FOIA, yet admissible as evidence in a non‑disclosure context. This approach aligns with precedent that allows ex‑parte affidavits to be used to decide segregation issues without disclosure of the very material the but.

Therefore, Bender’s supplement did not change the court’s finding; it merely reinforced the agency’s burden‑carrying obligations.

## 4.5 Determining ‘Meaningful’ vs. ‘Random’ Non‑Exempt Data in Large Volumes

Finally, the court faced the practical question of how much non‑exempt data exists within a 10‑plus thousand page file. The standard in *Mead* and *Perioperative* suggests that if non‑exempt statements are so rare or so randomly disposed that extracting them would provide no “meaningful” information to the requester, segregation is unnecessary. The court acknowledged that identifying a handful of non‑exempt statements scattered throughout a voluminous file does not necessarily provide the requester with public‑interest information; such statements might be anonymized, contain only procedural routine, or be logically dependant on the exempt context.

In *Farahi*, the FBI argued that the non‑exempt material was minimal, but the district court found that a random, sparse presence of non‑exempt lines did not satisfy the “reasonable steps” criterion because the overall narrative remained tied to the investigation. The court emphasized that the FOIA “focus is information, not simply words”, and that the real concern is whether the release of the non‑exempt material would jeopardize the underlying law‑enforcement activity.

The outcome was a denial of the request, holding that the FBI had efficiently applied its segregation obligations by providing ample justification for why the exempt material could not be separated from the rest of the file.
