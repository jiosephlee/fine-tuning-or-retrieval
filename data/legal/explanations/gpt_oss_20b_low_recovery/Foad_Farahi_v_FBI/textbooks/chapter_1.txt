Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # 1. Contextualizing the Case: Facts, Parties, and Procedural History  

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the plaintiff, Foad Farahi, an Iranian national, seized a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) claim as a keystone in a long‑running struggle with the federal government over alleged ties to terrorism and immigration enforcement. This chapter sketches the factual back‑story of Farahi’s immigration and FBI investigation, tracks the FOIA request and the agency’s oscillating compliance, and traces the procedural milestones that culminated in the Ninth‑Circuit decision.

## 1.1. Partisan Facts: Farahi’s Immigration, Asylum, and Alleged Terrorist Ties  

Farahi entered the United States in 1993, a year after Iranian sanctions were intensified. By 2002, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) applied for removal proceedings against him on the basis of an overstayed visa. In the course of these proceedings he pursue several forms of relief—an initial asylum claim, a claim of withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and finally a voluntary departure—each of which prompted DHS to produce records detailing the claims and the government’s counter‑arguments.  

The crux of the dispute is the FBI’s assertions that Farahi had, over a period of years, engaged in activities “intersecting with violations of national security and international terrorism.” The FBI presented both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who allegedly “documented Farahi’s connections to high‑level terrorists.” These allegations appeared in *Farahi v. U.S. Attorney General*, 2024 WL 483601 (11th Cir. 2024), where the agent’s testimony played a pivotal role in advancing the government’s case. Though the appeals court’s decision in that case is not the focus of the current opinion, the material is relevant because the same FBI records are the subject of Farahi’s FOIA request.

In 2024, Farahi misrepresented that his FBI file contained specific allegations that he was connected to untenable terrorist organizations, including the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, Khaled Sheikh Mohammed, Al Qaeda members, and the convicted terrorist Jose Padilla, as well as alleged ties to charities that purportedly financed terrorism. These claims, though not independently verified in the *an eye of the public*, were the basis for his FOIA request.

## 1.2. FBI’s FOIA Response and Initial Review  

Farahi filed a FOIA request in the summer of 2015, seeking his entire FBI file, which the agency discovered spanned roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Under FOIA, the FBI is obliged to provide all responsive records unless an exemption applies. The agency, after a prolonged internal review, complied partially: it produced a limited, filtered set of documents and withheld the bulk, citing statutory exemptions. The withheld portion was large—most of the file—and the request was never fulfilled before the statutory 90‑day response deadline lapsed, prompting Farahi to file suit.

During litigation, the FBI filed a series of *disclosures* and *declarations* in support of summary‑judgment defenses. The first declaration, from Section Chief David Hardy, was public. It asserted that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” and that their disclosure could reasonably interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings—i.e., the FBI’s arguments for Exemption 7(A).  
Simultaneously, the FBI submitted an ex‑parte, sealed declaration from Hardy that gave more detail concerning the nature of the investigations. Farahi’s counsel filed no opposition to Hardy’s motions, and the district court granted the summary‑judgment motion in October 2020, finding the FBI’s basis for exemption timely.

## 1.3. Summary‑Judgment Motions and the District Court’s Orders (Oct 2020, Nov 2022, Oct 2023)  

The timeline unfolded as follows:

| Date | Event | Outcome | Notes |
|------|-------|---------|-------|
| **Oct 2020** | District court reviews FBI’s first summary‑judgment motion (containing Hardy’s public and ex‑parte assertions) | Grants summary judgment | Concludes records are exempt under 7(A) and that they are not reasonably segregable |
| **Nov 2022** | District court considers renewed summary‑judgment motion after defendant revised its declaration | Denies motion but “without prejudice”; orders FBI to update assessment of pendency of enforcement proceedings | District court notes the elapsed time and wants to confirm whether investigations remain ongoing; no specific remedial order is articulated in the public record |
| **Oct 2023** | FBI submits updated declarations from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender, both public (confirmation that investigations remain ongoing) and ex‑parte (detailing ongoing investigations and segregability RULS) | District court grants summary judgment | Finds that the FBI adequately updated the pendency claim and explained why the records cannot be reasonably segregated |

Thus, after 13 months of litigation, the district court reversed its earlier “grant” and “deny” positions, ultimately finding in favor of the FBI and confirming the application of Exemption 7(A).

## 1.4. The Role of Hardy and Bender Declarations (Public versus Ex‑Paré)  

The distinction between public and ex‑parte declarations is at the heart of FOIA litigation. Public declarations are admissible on the record; ex‑parte declarations are sealed and only available to the parties. The FDA’s objective in de‑classifying the information is to preserve national‑security or law‑enforcement interests while still allowing a court to examine the validity of an exemption claim.  

- **Hardy’s Public Declaration (Oct 2020)** indicated that the FBI’s records pertained to national‑security investigations and implied that they were exempt.  The commission also indicated that the FBI’s investigation was still active at that point.  
- **Hardy’s Ex‑Parte Declaration** (also filed in Oct 2020) offered greater detail about the investigation, but being sealed, it is visible only to the parties.  
- **Bender’s Public Declaration (Oct 2023)** largely reiterated that the investigations were still ongoing, effectively “refreshing” the pendency claim in light of the time elapsed.  
- **Bender’s Ex‑Parte Declaration** provided the detailed justification for the nonsplit nature of the records, which the court uses to evaluate the *segregability* analysis.  

Because the Ninth Circuit accepts that the court can rely on ex‑parte information that the district court showed as “reasonably specific,” the declarations from Hardy and Bender were central to the court’s determination that the records were exempt under FOIA and that no meaningful non‑exempt material could be segregated and released.

## 1.5. Appeal Filing and the Core Issues for Review  

Farahi’s appeal, filed in November 2023, challenged two core issues:

1. **Whether the FBI’s assertion that the records were compiled for “law‑enforcement purposes” was supported by *sufficient connecting evidence*** given the breadth of the alleged investigations.
2. **Whether the records’ withholding was justified under Exemption 7(A)** in light of the reported passage of time and the lack of explicit detail in the Bender declaration stating that the investigations were indeed still pending or reasonably anticipated.

In addition, Farahi contested the *segregability* analysis, arguing that the FBI had not differentiated its categories adequately to allow a court to assess which portions were exempt and which were not.

The district court’s record did not support the ex‑parte files, but the Ninth Circuit determined that the court’s succinct statements—allowing de facto reliance on Bender’s declaration to update the pendency claim—were sufficient, given the *government’s* burden to show that the records were indeed exempt under FOIA. The appellate court upheld the lower court’s grant of summary judgment and, in so doing, affirmed that the FBI had a non‑abusive basis to withhold Farahi’s file under Exemption 7(A).
