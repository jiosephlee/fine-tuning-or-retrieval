Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What is the exact definition of a 'law‑enforcement purpose' under FOIA Exemption 7(A), and how does that apply to an FBI file that includes both investigative notes and immigration‑related documents?
Question:
The opinion states that the FBI ‘carried its burden to show that Farahi’s file was compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.’  However, the file contains not only investigation notes but also immigration‑related documents and commentary from an immigration hearing.  How do courts discriminate between records that are truly ‘compiled for law‑enforcement purposes’ and those that are merely administrative or immigration‑related?  Does the presence of any law‑enforcement–related content automatically render the entire file subject to Exemption 7(A)?
Answer:
**Answer to “What is the exact definition of a “law‑enforcement purpose” under FOIA Exemption 7(A), and how does that apply to an FBI file that includes both investigative notes and immigration‑related documents?”**

---

### 1.  The statutory standard

FOIA Exemption 7(A) protects records that

> **(i)** *were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes*, and  
> **(ii)** their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are *pending or reasonably anticipated*.

(The statutory text is in § 552(b)(7)(A).) The *agency* bears the burden of proving both prongs.  A court must accept the agency’s determination of prong (i) if it is *reasonable*; this is the deference described in *Campbell* and *Clemente*.

### 2.  What “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” means

The Court in *Foad Farahi v. FBI* reiterated the two‑part test first articulated in *Campbell*:

1. **A rational nexus between the investigation and the agency’s law‑enforcement duties.**  
2. **A connection between “an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.”**

These requirements are *not* limited to substantive criminal investigations; they apply to any gathering of evidence that could form the basis of a law‑enforcement proceeding, including investigations that may later give rise to civil or immigration proceedings.  The Court explicitly accepted **“investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism”** as falling under the agency’s law‑enforcement duties.

### 3.  How the Court applied the test to Farahi’s file

#### (a)  Proof of a law‑enforcement nexus

The FBI’s Hardy and Bender declarations explained that Farahi’s file was part of an ongoing **counter‑terrorism investigation**.  The Court found that the declarations furnished a “reasonable” description of:

* The necessity of the investigation to **intercept potential security threats**;  
* The presence of *world‑wide* terrorism‑related allegations (e.g., ties to Al‑Qaeda, the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, and other terrorist organizations); and  
* The fact that the FBI is itself a **law‑enforcement agency**.

Because the FBI is charged with maintaining national security, the Court concluded that the *nexus* requirement was satisfied.  See *U.S. v. LoBue*, 678 F.3d 146, 153 (D.C. Cir. 2012) (law‑enforcement agency → deference).  The presence of immigration‑related documents does **not** automatically defeat the claim: the Court treated the entire file as **compiled in furtherance of a law‑enforcement investigation** that could give rise to federal prosecution or other enforcement actions.

#### (b)  Pendency or reasonableness of enforcement proceedings

The Court required that the investigation be **pending or reasonably anticipated**.  The Hardy declarations (public and ex parte) documented that the FBI’s investigation was *ongoing* and that evidence was still being collected for a *possible future criminal case*.  The Bender declaration merely confirmed that “nothing had changed” ‑ an update rather than a new assertion of pendency.  Since the Court had already accepted the first declaration sequence, the second declaration was sufficient to establish ongoing proceedings.

(If the investigation had been conclusively closed, the “reasonably anticipated” prong would fail; see *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 59–60.)

#### (c)  Segregability

The Court also found that the FBI had met its burden to show that the exempt portion was not *reasonably segregable*.  The FBI’s justification that investigative notes and immigration documents were **intertwined** met the standard in *Mead* and *Perioperative Services*, where the Court held that “interspersed” non‑exempt material is insufficiently segregable when it cannot be separated without destroying the investigative context.

### 4.  Does any law‑enforcement content automatically protect the whole file?

No.  Exemption 7(A) applies only to the **portion of the record that was compiled for the law‑enforcement purpose**.  If a file contains **both** exempt and non‑exempt material, the agency must segregate and release the latter.  The Court cautions that the agency may withhold the entire file only if the exempt portion is **inextricably intertwined** with non‑exempt material, as was the case here.  Thus, the presence of an immigration‑related note *does not* negate the exemption; it is the overall context that matters.

### 5.  Bottom line for Farahi

The Court affirmed that, under the statutory framework and the FOIA precedent, the FBI met its burden that:

* the file was **compiled for law‑enforcement purposes** (counter‑terrorism investigation), and  
* its disclosure could *reasonably* be expected to **interfere** with *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings (an ongoing investigation continuing to gather evidence for a potential federal prosecution).

The file’s mixed content (investigative notes plus immigration documents) was deemed “inextricably intertwined” with the exempt investigative portion, so complete withholding was deemed appropriate.  Had the immigration documents been entirely separate from the investigative material, the FBI would have been required to segregate and disclose them.

In sum, a record is covered by Exemption 7(A) only when:

1. **A rational link** exists between the data gathering and the agency’s law‑enforcement duties; and  
2. The disclosure *could* reasonably compromise *current or foreseeable* enforcement proceedings.

The court found both present in the Farahi file, and the mixed nature of the documents did not automatically bring the whole file within Exemption 7(A) unless the agency could show that the exempt material was inseparable from the non‑exempt portion.

### How do courts determine whether an investigation is still 'pending or reasonably anticipated,' especially when time has elapsed since the original FOIA request?
Question:
In this case, the Bender declaration was brief and essentially reiterated that the investigation remains ongoing.  Yet Farahi argues that the time lapse casts doubt on the continuation of enforcement proceedings.  What factual evidence must the government provide to satisfy the standard that proceedings are still pending or reasonably anticipated?  Are ongoing investigations automatically considered pending, or is a more detailed, time‑specific analysis required?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI* the court reiterated the standard for FOIA Exemption 7(A): a record may be withheld only if (1) it was *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* and (2) its disclosure could *reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated”* (Id. at 6‑7).  The key issue here is how a court determines that second prong—i.e., what facts the government must supply to show that the proceedings still exist.

---

### 1.  The “pending or reasonably anticipated” test

The courts have consistently held that the *exclusion* of a record is a question of law that must be decided de novo, not merely on a *deference* footing (see *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361; *CREW*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096).  The government bears the burden of showing that a *specific* enforcement proceeding is still pending or that a *prospective* proceeding is reasonably likely (CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096; *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 59).

Critically, the court in *CREW* warned that a *vague* assertion that an investigation “continues” is not enough: “the vague nature of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice to support the invocation of Exemption 7(A)” (CREW, 746 F.3d at 1098).  The citations that *CREW* used, however, were not absolute; courts have allowed a reasonably concise statement to suffice if it is part of a broader factual matrix that establishes the existence of an ongoing investigation.

---

### 2.  What factual evidence is required?

The *Farahi* opinion, following *CREW* and *Juarez*, set out the following **prudential evidentiary standard**:

| Element | What the government must show | How the court treated it in *Farahi* |
|---------|------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Existence of a law‑enforcement investigation** | A clear, factual statement that an investigation of the applicant’s alleged wrongdoing is underway.  The statement may be made by an agent or senior official and does not require exhaustive detail. | The Hardy declaration (public, then ex‑parte) provided such a statement, attesting that the FBI’s investigation was “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism.”  This was deemed adequate (Id. at 50). |
| **Continuity of that investigation** | Evidence that the investigation has not been terminated and that it is still collecting evidence for a potential future prosecution.  Narrow detail is not required, but the statement must be accompanied by context that shows continuity (e.g., updates on case status, ongoing interviews, procurement of new evidence). | The Bender declaration, though brief, was treated as an *update* confirming that the investigation “remains ongoing.”  The court reasoned that the declaration was not meant to restart the claims of pendency but to verify that nothing had changed since the earlier Hardy affidavits (Id. at 131). |
| **Likelihood of a future proceeding** | An indication that the evidence gathered is sufficient to anticipate a prospective criminal case.  The government may simply state that the case is *“concrete”* or that the investigator has a “reasonable expectation” of bringing charges. | The court accepted the Hardy ex‑parte declaration’s description of the investigation’s breadth (ties to foreign terrorist organizations, etc.) as sufficient to conclude that a “concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding” persisted (Id. at 131‑132). |
| **Potential interference** | Demonstration that the release of the information could jeopardize the investigation (e.g., by tipping off suspects or revealing investigative techniques).  The court requires only a *reasonable* expectation of interference, not a *certainty* of damage. | The Hardy declaration explained that disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere” by “tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation.”  The court accepted this as sufficient (Id. at 49). |

Thus, the *government need not provide a detailed, time‑specific chronology*; a reasonably specific attestation of ongoing investigative activity that is part of a larger factual context suffices.  That context can be established in a prior affidavit (Hardy) and then updated (Bender).

---

### 3.  Are ongoing investigations automatically considered “pending”?

No.  An ongoing investigation must be *also* shown to be *reasonably likely* to result in a future prosecutorial proceeding.  The court in *Juarez* emphasized that “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies” (Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59).  If an investigation has “frozen” or if no prosecutorial prospect remains, the pendency argument fails.  In *Farahi*, the government proved that the investigation was still active and that a future case was realistic, thus satisfying the *pending or reasonably anticipated* language.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for a FOIA requester

If you are challenging an Exemption 7(A) claim:

1. **Look for a *preceding* affidavit or declaration that establishes the existence of an ongoing investigation.**  A later update can be seen merely as a confirmation, not a new claim of pendency.
2. **Confirm that the claim of pendency is *part of a continuous factual narrative***.  Courts will not read an isolated, one‑sentence statement as proof that a proceeding is pending; they require that it be integrated with earlier evidence that demonstrates the investigation’s continuity and possible prosecution.
3. **Seek evidence of an anticipated prosecution** (e.g., evidence‑collection status, interviewer statements, or case‑forecasting).  A “reasonable expectation” is adequate, but it should be more than an abstract assertion.

If you cannot find any such contextual evidence—if the record‑keeping officer merely states “the investigation is ongoing” without any backing details—the court will likely deem the Exemption 7(A) claim insufficient and require disclosure of the non‑exempt portions.

---

**In summary:** *Foad Farahi* illustrates that courts accept a reasonably concise affirmation of an ongoing investigation—provided it is set within a broader factual context that shows that the investigation remains in play and that a future prosecution is reasonably likely.  Ongoing investigations are not automatically “pending”; they must be *continuously substantiated* and *connected* to a prospective prosecution for Exemption 7(A) to survive.

### When does the FOIA ‘reasonable segregability’ standard allow for partial release of an otherwise exempt record?
Question:
The court concluded that no portion of the 10,750 pages and 80 CDs was reasonably segregable because the exempt material was “inextricably intertwined.”  What specific factors does a court look for to assess segregability?  Must the exempt content completely dominate each page, or can a single paragraph or line within a document be isolated?  How does the court balance the need to preserve law‑enforcement evidence against the public’s right to access non‑exempt information?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* the court clarified the “reasonable segregability” standard that governs whether an otherwise exempt aggregate can be partially released.  The standard is applied after the agency has already satisfied the burden of showing that an exemption applies.  When that burden is met, the court must determine whether the agency can meaningfully isolate the non‑exempt portions of the record so that they can be turned over “without jeopardizing the legitimate investigative or law‑enforcement interest in the remaining material.”  U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); *Farahi*, 60 F.4th at 134.

### 1.  What the court looks for

1. **Extent of intermingling**  
   The court examines how the exempt portions are woven into the rest of the record.  If the exempt material “is inextricably intertwined” – that is, it is not confined to a distinct page, document, or data set but is *pervasive* throughout the material – the record is deemed *not reasonably segregable* (*Farahi*, 60 F.4th at 139‑40).  The language is not limited to a single page; it encompasses the entire volume as a whole.

2. **Distribution of exempt vs. non‑exempt content**  
   The court considers whether there are *meaningful* blocks of non‑exempt material that can be separated from the exempt content *without* removing anything that would impair the purpose of the interference defense.  As the court noted, “a small amount of non‐exempt material randomly interspersed line‑by‑line throughout the document” is unlikely to be *reasonably segregable* (*Farahi*, 60 F.4th at 140).  The standard requires that the exempt content not dominate the record so fully that any remaining non‑exempt portions are, for all practical purposes, embedded in the exempt material.

3. **Agency’s justification**  
   The agency must provide a *detailed justification* for why the record cannot be segregated.  The justification must treat each category of withholding separately and explain why the exempt portions are so tightly bound to the non‑exempt material that removal would “would (sic) be the assistant to produce the remaining material” (*Farahi*, 60 F.4th at 139).  The court relies on the agency’s affidavits to make this assessment (*Farahi*, 60 F.4th at 139‑40).

### 2.  Does exempt content have to dominate each page?

No.  The court’s language requires that the exempt material be “inextricably intertwined” *with the exempt portions,” essentially meaning that the exempt and non‑exempt portions are so entangled across the entire record that you cannot isolate one from the other.  The fact that a single paragraph, line, or even a few words might be non‑exempt does not, on its own, make the record segregable.  What matters is the **overall pattern** of intermingling throughout the requested volume.  *Farahi* explained that when a “small amount of non‑exempt material is randomly interspersed line‑by‑line throughout the document, that material is not likely to be “reasonably segregable.”*  Thus an isolated paragraph does not automatically allow a partial release.

### 3.  Balancing evidence preservation and public access

The court balances the need to preserve law‑enforcement evidence against the public’s FOIA right by:

1. **Deference to the agency’s assessment** – The agency’s determination that the record is compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, and that its release would reasonably interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings, is entitled to “deference” (see *Campbell*, *CNSS*).  

2. **Strict scrutiny of segregation** – Even with that deference, the agency must still show that it has taken “reasonable steps” to segregate non‑exempt information.  If it cannot do so because the exempt material is folded throughout the record, the court will deny a partial release.  The agency cannot rely on a vague or terse statement such as “the investigation remains ongoing”; it must provide “sufficient detail” to demonstrate that the exempt portions cannot be isolated (*Farahi*, 60 F.4th at 139‑40).

3. **Both interests must be weighed** – The court acknowledges that *“the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.”*  Still, if segregating the non‑exempt parts would undermine the investigative integrity of the remaining record—or if the exempt portions effectively erode any meaningful information—the public interest in disclosure diminishes.  The court ultimately found that, in this case, the FBI’s justification that the exempt material was “inextricably intertwined” satisfied the court’s segregation test; thus, no portion of the record was deemed reasonably segregable.

---

**Bottom line:** Under *Farahi*, a record is **not** reasonably segregable when exempt documents and information are woven throughout the entire volume so that any attempt to carve out non‑exempt parts would leave those parts so incomplete as to be useless. A single line or paragraph of non‑exempt content does not automatically trigger a partial release; the overall degree of intermingling and the agency’s detailed explanation determine the outcome. The court applies a deference‑leaning standard for the agency’s determination of an exemption but maintains a stringent segregation test to protect law‑enforcement interests while still honoring the FOIA’s openness mandate.

### What is the scope of deference given to agencies when invoking FOIA Exemption 7(A), especially in national‑security contexts?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly cites deference to the FBI’s determination that a record is compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.  How far does that deference extend to the agency’s assertion that information could ‘reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings’?  Are agencies allowed to rely on broad, high‑level statements (e.g., ‘investigations remain ongoing’) without more granular evidence, or must they provide detailed factual corroboration?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit re‑affirmed the well‑established deference rule that applies when an agency invokes FOIA Exemption 7(A). The court’s reasoning shows precisely how that deference operates, what it requires of the agency, and where the line is drawn between a “high‑level” assertion of ongoing investigations and the detailed factual corroboration that a plaintiff may still seek.

---

### 1.  The scope of deference

A FOIA **exemption claim is made by the agency, not by the claimant**.  
Under § 552(a)(4)(B), the agency bears the entire burden to show that a statutory exemption applies. The court therefore “reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo” (*Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th at 361) and “deems the government’s determination entitled to deference” (*Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d at 32).

The deference is **qualified**:  
* The agency must make a *reasonable* and *nuanced* assertion that the records are “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and that their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1096).  
* The agency’s statement is not simply an empty “investigations are ongoing”; the court requires **an evidential basis** that the investigation still exists and that the owed risk to law‑enforcement proceedings remains.

In *Farahi*, the court found that the FBI’s Hardy declaration “provided considerable detail about the nature of that investigation” (*J.A.* at 49). That detail satisfies the “reasonable basis” standard. The later Bender declaration, though terse, was treated as an **update** rather than a *stand‑alone* assertion and therefore did not erode the agency’s earlier evidentiary support.

---

### 2.  What counts as “reasonable evidence”

The court’s language in *Farahi* makes it clear that "reasonable evidence" may be **high‑level, but it must be specific enough to satisfy the court’s inquiry**:

* **Existence of a pending proceeding** – the agency may simply attest to the *existence* of ongoing investigations (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1099).  
* **Potential interference** – the agency must show *how* disclosure could interfere, e.g., by “tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation” (*Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 59).  
* **Inextricable link to law‑enforcement duties** – the agency’s declaration must link the requested records to a *specific* law‑enforcement activity (the FBI’s counterterrorism investigation in this case) (*Campbell*, 164 F.3d at 32).

The court expressly stated that the Bender declaration “confirm[ed] that the investigation remained ongoing” but did not require that declaration alone to meet the burden. Instead, it was the *cumulative* evidence—including the Hardy declarations and the FBI’s own public statements about Farahi’s alleged ties to terrorist organizations—that sufficed.

---

### 3.  Are broad, high‑level statements enough?

No.  While agencies may not need a *word‑by‑word transcript* of proceedings, they **cannot** rely on an empty “we are still investigating” statement without backup. The court in *Farahi* emphasized that “the *vague nature* of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may *not* suffice” (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1098). The agency must **anchor the claim** with either:

1. **A documented, specific, or at least highly detailed description** of the investigative activity; or  
2. **A credible recommendation** from an agent or supervisor that the investigation is not yet closed, coupled with any available internal commentary on why disclosure would interfere.

The Bender declaration, by itself, would likely have been deemed *conclusory* and insufficient. The court held that, in this case, it functioned as a *confirmation* of pre‑existing evidence, not a *new, standalone* justification.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for future FOIA plaintiffs

| Issue | Requirement | Practical Take‑away |
|-------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Evidence of pending proceedings** | A verifiable, specific assertion of ongoing law‑enforcement activity | A concise statement that investigations are “ongoing” is not enough; supplement with dates, names, or descriptions of the investigation’s scope. |
| **Potential interference** | Demonstrate *how* release could harm the proceeding (e.g., risk of suspect tampering, coercion, or tactical advantage) | Explain the mechanism; a general claim of “could interfere” without explanation is weak. |
| **Segregability** | Provide a *categorical* secret‑portion designation and explain why it cannot be separated from non‑exempt detail | Use clear “categories” and, if needed, provide a logical scheme that separates exempt and non‑exempt material. |
| **Deference** | Respect the agency’s reasonable determination, but insist on a *reasonably specific* basis | A request for the agency’s “reaffirmation” can be satisfied only if the agency documents new facts that maintain the pendency of the proceeding. |

The *Farahi* decision therefore clarifies that agencies must move beyond a generic “open investigation” statement. They need to supply a **reasonable, detail‑laden, and credible basis** for both pendency and interference. Plaintiffs may challenge the agency’s claims if the evidence is *conclusory* or *inconsistent* with known facts, but a *frank* yet *well‑grounded* assertion will generally earn deference under Exemption 7(A).

### What are the requirements for category‑by‑category withholding under FOIA, and why did the court find Farahi’s challenge to the categories ‘unavailing’?
Question:
Farahi objected that the FBI used overbroad categories without explanation.  The court said he forfeited that argument because he offered no specific reasoning for overbreadth.  What standard does a plaintiff need to meet to succeed in a category‑by‑category withholding challenge?  Must the agency justify each category?  How does the court determine whether a category is wide enough to conceal exempt material?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under FOIA, a plaintiff must show that the agency’s category‑by‑category withholding is *not* warranted.  The plaintiff must (1) identify the specific categories the agency claims are exempt and (2) explain why each category is over‑broad, i.e., why the material covered by the category is not actually subject to the exemption that the agency cites.  If the plaintiff fails to *provide a reasoned, category‑specific argument*, the court will deem the challenge “unavailing” and dismiss it (see *Khine v. DHS*, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019); *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, 2025 U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit).

**How the court applies the standard**

1. **Deference to the agency’s determination of law‑enforcement purpose.**  
   Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court gives deference to its assertion that the file was compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” (cf. *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119; *Cambridge v. USA*, 164 F.3d 20, 32).  The agency need not explain why it appears that way; the deference pure‑law requirement is satisfied.

2. **Proving “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings.**  
   *CREW* (746 F.3d 1082, 1096) and *Juarez* (518 F.3d 54, 61) hold that the agency only needs to attest to the *existence* of a pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding.  The arguments that the declaration was *conclusory* are outweighed by the richer context supplied in earlier Hardy affidavits and by the FBI’s public statements about a continuing counterterrorism investigation.

3. **Category‐by‑category withholding** – *CREW* establishes the procedural bar: the agency must use categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category.  The plaintiff must point to those categories and explain *exactly* why they are over‑broad.  Farahi failed to do that.  He simply alleged that “the FBI used overbroad categories” without telling the court what the categories were or why the exemption did not apply to the material included in them.  The court therefore concluded that his fight over category breadth was “unavailing” and dismissed it (*Khine*, 987‑995).

4. **Segregability** – even if the exemption applies, the agency must show that the exempt portion is **not reasonably segregable** from the non‑exempt material.  *Juarez* says a court may rely on the agency’s declaration if it explains why exemption‑protected portions *cannot be separately extracted* without impairing the agency’s interests.  The FBI supplied a “detailed justification” in Bender’s ex‑parte declaration that the exempt material was intertwined with non‑exempt material.  Farahi’s brief objection that “some non‑exempt words might be released” was dismissed; the court held that a small amount of scattered non‑exempt data does not satisfy the “reasonable segregability” requirement.

**Bottom line**

A FOIA plaintiff who wants to defeat an agency’s category‑by‑category withholding must:

* Itemize the specific categories the agency has identified,
* Show why the exemption the agency cites does not apply to *each* category (i.e., explain the overbreadth),
* And, if the exemption is claimed, demonstrate that at least some portion of the record is *reasonably segregable*.

If the plaintiff does not identify the categories and explain their overbreadth, the court will consider the challenge “unavailing” and refuse to give it oral argument, as the court did for Farahi.

### What is the procedural value of ex‑parte affidavits in FOIA cases, and can they be challenged on the public record?
Question:
The FBI’s update was an ex‑parte affidavit, and the court relied on it heavily, yet Farahi could not contest it on the public record.  Under what circumstances is it permissible for a court to accept an ex‑parte declaration without a hearing?  When can a plaintiff raise objections to the adequacy or sufficiency of an ex‑parte declaration?  How does this balance the need to protect sensitive information with the FOIA’s transparency purpose?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In FOIA disputes a court may rely on an ex‑parte declaration *without a hearing* when the agency, acting in deference to its own expertise, shows that an exemption applies and, crucially, that the confidential material cannot be reasonably segregated. The plaintiff can still challenge the sufficiency of the declaration, but only after the agency has made a *public* showing of the same facts. Ex‑parte affidavits are accepted when the material is so sensitive that disclosure would itself undermine the very enforcement proceeding that the exemption protects. The balance is struck by the “reasonable‑steps” requirement to segregate non‑exempt information and by the “deference to the executive” standard: the court must give the agency the benefit of the doubt unless the affidavit is conclusory or the agency crashes on the segregability test.

---

### 1.  When can a court accept an ex‑parte affidavit without a hearing?

The *principle* is that a FOIA de‑summation is **de novo review** (see *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press* v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350).  The only circumstance that permits an ex‑parte declaration to be the sole basis for a decision is **when the agency cannot provide that information on the public record** without itself revealing the very material it seeks to keep secret. The opinion in *Farahi* cites two primary authorities:

1. **Exemption 7(A) requires a real threat that disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably‐anticipated law‑enforcement proceedings** (see *Crews* v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082).  
2. **The executive branch’s decisions on enforcement status are entitled to a fair measure of deference** (see *Clemente*, *Campbell*, *CNSS*). ▷Thus the court is permitted to “rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated” (*Juarez* v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54).

In practice, the court looks for:

* A **public declaration** that establishes the *flourishing* of a law‑enforcement investigation and its relevance to the FOIA‑requested record.  
* An **ex‑parte, sealed declaration** that supplies the **necessary detail** (e.g., names of targets, scope, and status of an investigation) sufficient to support the exemption claim.  

If a plaintiff has no means of contesting the exemption on the public record, the court typically will not hold a hearing. As the *Farahi* opinion notes, “the court did not need to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration” when *Farahi* had not opposed the agency’s certificate.

---

### 2.  How can a plaintiff raise objections to an ex‑parte declaration?

The plaintiff’s options are limited, but not denied:

| Step | Action | Timing | Basis |
|------|--------|--------|-------|
| **A. Oppose the declaration in the same pleading** | File an objection or *motion to dismiss* the agency’s exempt claim. | Before the court grants summary judgment. | The plaintiff cannot “challenge it on the public record” only if it has not been filed or recorded; otherwise the court has already decided. |
| **B. Amended pleadings** | After an agency’s initial declaration, the plaintiff can submit an *amended complaint* or *motion* setting out factual grounds that rebut the exemption. | Anytime up to the jurisdictional deadline for the case. | The plaintiff may provide evidence that the investigation is no longer pending or that the material is reasonably segregable. |
| **C. Dependent judgment hearing** | If the court attaches a *certificate of the need for a hearing* to the decision, the plaintiff may request a hearing on the same record at that time. | After the decision but before the judgment becomes final. | The court may deem it appropriate to let the plaintiff present oral argument. |
| **D. Post‑judgment motion** | File a *motion for reconsideration* or *motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict* if new evidence shows the exemption was incorrectly applied. | After the judgment is entered. | The plaintiff can argue that the agency has failed the “reasonable‑steps” test for segregation. |

In *Farahi*, the plaintiff was denied the opportunity to contest the *Bender* ex‑parte declaration because the agency’s initial affidavits already satisfied the court’s deference threshold. The court reinforced that the plaintiff “cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them” unless they had contested the *public* showing of the same facts.

---

### 3.  Public‑record objections and the segregation requirement

**Segregability** is the linchpin of the balance between transparency and confidentiality. Under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) the agency must “take reasonable steps to segregate and release non‑exempt information.” (*Farahi* v. FBI, 8 F.Ct. 834 (D.C. Cir. 2025)).

*Farahi* demonstrates the court’s deference to a **reasonably‑specific** ex‑parte declaration when it cannot isolate non‑exempt portions. The federal court found the FBI’s explanation “adequately explained why the protected material was not reasonably segregable” (see *Farahi* 139). Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court is especially willing to accept the agency’s in‑depth knowledge of the investigative context.

If a plaintiff thinks the agency’s categorization is “overbroad” or “inaccurate,” it must have matched evidence in the public record to contest it. *Farahi* notes that the plaintiff “forfeited his arguments on this score” by failing to present a viable challenge to the categories in the public pleadings (*Khine* v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959). Therefore, the “public record” requirement is not a procedural limitation; it is a substantive filter that requires the plaintiff to articulately identify how each claimed exemption applies to each factual subset.

---

### 4.  Key take‑aways for a law student

1. **Ex‑parte affidavits are permissible** when a government agency can demonstrate, in detail (public or sealed per the FOIA rules), that an exemption applies *and* that the evidence cannot be reasonably segregated.  
2. **Plaintiffs may challenge the affidavit** only after the agency has furnished a *public* presentation of the same facts. If the agency’s initial pleading satisfied the court, the plaintiff has no further avenue to contest it on the public record unless it file an opposing pleading.  
3. **The balance** is struck by the *reasonable‑steps* segregation duty. The court will only enforce an exemption if the agency can show that attempting to isolate non‑exempt material would itself compromise the investigation.  
4. **Deference to the executive**—particularly in national‑security contexts—means that courts will rarely overturn an agency’s finding of “pending or reasonably anticipated” litigation or “inextricably intertwined” records.

> *In short, an ex‑parte affidavit can be the fulcrum of a FOIA decision, but only because the record‑keeping agency has demonstrated that disclosure would itself endanger the enforcement proceeding it seeks to protect.*

### How does the FOIA exemption framework handle the interplay between Exemption 7(A) and the requirement to release ‘reasonably segregable’ portions?
Question:
The opinion notes that the government must disclose any reasonably segregable portion of a record.  But if Exemption 7(A) applies to the entire record, does that automatically preclude the release of non‑exempt material even if it is interleaved?  What are the precedents for partial disclosure when part of a record is exempt but part is not?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes.  Exemption 7(A) can shield an entire record, and when a record is exempt the agency is still required to *attempt* to separate and disclose any parts that are **reasonably segregable**.  If the exempt portions are so intertwined with non‑exempt material that the agency can’t in a practical sense separate the two, the entire record may be withheld.  The *Farahi* opinion confirms that the FBI may withhold the whole file because the exempt evidence is “inextricably intertwined” with unexempt data, and because no “meaningful” non‑exempt portions can be isolated without compromising the agency’s interests.

---

### 1.  The statutory framework

FOIA § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and § 552(b) require that *“any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided…after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”*  In other words, the exemption does **not** grant an agency a blanket right to keep an entire record.  The agency must determine whether the exempt and non‑exempt parts can be separated.  If they cannot, the record may be withheld in its entirety.

FOIA § 552(b)(7)(A) protects records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” that “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”  The court repeatedly stresses that the agency bears the burden of showing this exemption applies and that the disclosure would endanger pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions.

---

### 2.  *Farahi* on the interplay

The *Farahi* opinion turns directly to the segregability requirement in § V:

> “Even if a FOIA request encompasses protected records, the agency still must take ‘reasonable steps’ to ‘segregate and release nonexempt information.’”  
> …  
> “The district court explained that the FBI had provided a ‘detailed justification’ for why the protected records were **not reasonably segregable** … and found that this submission satisfied the agency’s burden.”  
> “If a small amount of non‑exempt material is randomly ‘interspersed line‑by‑line throughout the document,’ that material is not likely to be ‘reasonably segregable.’”

Thus the court reinforces the principle that **interleaving** exempt and non‑exempt text can defeat segregation.  It cites *Mead Data Center* and *Perioperative Services* to show that text that is “randomly interspersed” is not reasonably segregable.

---

### 3.  Precedent for partial disclosure

In the “interleaved” context, two cases in the same circuit are most relevant:

| Case | Holding | Key point |
|------|---------|-----------|
| **Juarez v. DOJ**, 518 F.3d 54 (2008) | The agency may withhold an entire record when the exempt portion is “inextricably intertwined” with non‑exempt portions. | Exemption triggers a “no‑segregation” bar if intermingling is too tight. |
| **Mead Data Cent. v. U.S. Dept. of Air Force**, 566 F.2d 242 (1977) | The agency need not disclose non‑exempt material that is “randomly line‑by‑line interspersed.” | Lines of non‑exempt text that are spread through exempt pages cannot be segregated. |

*Creat-then*—the *Farahi* court expressly adopts *Juarez* and *Mead* in its analysis (see the discussion in Part V).  Thus, the *Farahi* opinion does not “invent” a new rule; it re‑asserts the already established doctrine that **the interplay of 7(A) and segregation is a factual question** adjudicated on the record.  When the exemption covers the whole file and a “detailed justification” shows that the exempt bits are inseparable from the rest, full withholding is permissible.

---

### 4.  Practical implications for a FOIA request

1. **Agency’s burden** – The agency must (i) show that the file is exempt and (ii) explain why any exempt portions cannot be segregated.  
2. **Plaintiff’s burden** – The requester must argue that:
   - The agency’s claim of intermixing is overly broad or incorrect; or  
   - There is a “meaningful” segment that can be isolated without violating the exemption.
3. **Court’s review** – The court looks at the evidence (affidavits, declarations, document analysis).  It will give deference to the agency’s expertise but will trigger a *strict* segregation test when the file is interleaved.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

- **Exemption 7(A) does not, by itself, bar the release of non‑exempt material.**  
- **The agency is required to segregate and release any reasonably segregable portions.**  
- **If the exempt and non‑exempt parts are so entangled that segregation is not practically achievable, the entire record may be withheld.**  
- The *Farahi* opinion exemplifies this doctrine: the FBI’s detailed declaration that its exempt records are “inextricably intertwined” with the rest satisfied the segregation requirement, allowing the court to uphold the total withholding.

**Hence, partial disclosure is permitted only where the agency can demonstrate segregation is possible; where the std test fails, the whole record can be exempted, as in *Farahi*.**

### What is the impact of the passage of time on the plain‑text “likely interference” analysis under Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The court cited CREW that a vague assertion of ongoing investigations might not suffice, yet ended up accepting a brief declaration.  How does the passage of time influence the factual assessment of whether disclosure could interfere with future enforcement?  Are there specific statutory or case‑law thresholds for determining when an investigation is considered ‘overdue’ or ‘to be concluded’?
Answer:
**Answer**

The passage of time matters only insofar as it changes the factual backdrop that the government must establish to satisfy Exemption 7(A) (b)(1).  The statute itself does not carve out a “time‑based” limit on the exemption; it merely requires that the withheld records **</strong>could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are currently pending or reasonably anticipated** (§ 552(b)(7)(A)(B)).  The “likely interference” test is therefore a function of the *scope* of the investigation at the moment that the FOIA request is made, not the number of months or years that have elapsed since the investigation began.  The court in *Farahi* made clear that the relevant question is whether an investigation still exists, not whether it was “concluded” decades ago.

---

### 1.  When does an investigation become “no longer pending or reasonably anticipated”?

*The only factual standard the Court requires is the existence of evidence that an enforcement action is still going on or could start in the foreseeable future.*  
- In *CREW*, the Court explained that a mere assertion that an investigation is “ongoing” may be insufficient unless the agency can show that the investigation *continues to gather evidence that could be used in a future criminal case* (CREW, 746 F.3d at 1098).  
- The Court further clarified that “ongoing criminal investigations count as *proceedings* for these purposes” (CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097–98).  
- A statement that the investigation “remains ongoing” is therefore viewed as a *snapshot* of the current state of affairs, and it is the agency’s responsibility to keep that snapshot up to date.

In *Farahi* the Court found that the Hardy declarations, supplemented by Bender’s update, provided exactly that snapshot: the FBI attested to the **continuing** nature of an interrogation‑centered, national‑security‑related inquiry.  The Court did not require the government to demonstrate that the investigation **had not yet produced a chargeable case** or that it was “overdue”; it merely required that the investigation **was current** and that the release of evidence could “reasonably be expected to interfere” with the inquiry at that point in time.

---

### 2.  How does the court assess “reasonable expectation” of interference?

The Court reiterated the “tipping‑off” theory upon which Exemption 7(A) rests: a release could jeopardize an investigation by **alerting suspects or targets to the existence, scope, or strategy of the inquiry** (CREW, 746 F.3d at 1099; *CNSS*, 331 F.3d at 929).  That theory does not hinge on a particular date; it pivots on current investigative tactics and the likelihood that the material would alter the trajectories of ongoing law‑enforcement actions.

If an investigation has “been *overdue*” and no longer will conduct new investigative work (for example, the case is closed, a complaint has been filed, or the suspect has been prosecuted), the expectation of interference collapses.  The case law does not impose a statutory “time‑threshold”—for example, six months, a year, or a decade—after which a pending investigation automatically ceases to be protected.

---

### 3.  What are the implications for FOIA practice?

Because Exemption 7(A) is judged on a **point‑in‑time** basis, agencies must:

1. **Periodically review** the status of investigations that were flagged in the past and provide an updated factual basis when a FOIA request is made.  
2. **Document the scope and stage** of the investigation (e.g., “we are still collecting interview evidence, and the suspect has not yet been charged”).  
3. **Confirm** that the investigation **could reasonably be expected** to be influenced by the release (e.g., the suspect could be warned about the contents of the files, or key evidence could be lost).

In *Farahi* the district court required the FBI to submit an updated *Bender* declaration after the initial motion died.  The Court saw that update as sufficient to show that the investigation remained “ongoing.”  Had the FBI failed to confirm that the investigation was still active—if, for instance, the investigation had been closed in 2018—the court would have found the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement unsatisfied.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

• **No statutory cut‑off**: Exemption 7(A) does not specify a time limit after which a pending investigation ceases to qualify.  
• **Factual snapshot**: The court looks to the state of the investigation at the **time of the FOIA request** (or the time of the agency’s declaration).  An assertion that an inquiry “remains ongoing” is sufficient if it is supported by other evidence that the investigation is still gathering evidence or that it could result in a future prosecution.  
• **Reasonable expectation of interference**: The key determinative factor is whether the release of the information could “reasonably be expected to interfere” with the *current* investigation—most often through a tipping‑off.  

Thus, the passage of time affects Exemption 7(A) only insofar as it may change the factual reality of whether an investigation is still ongoing.  If it is still active, the exemption remains in force; once the investigation is finished or no longer anticipatable, the exemption no longer applies.

### How do FOIA courts distinguish between ‘law‑enforcement duties’ and ‘security‑related duties’ when applying Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The opinion references that the FBI’s determination was entitled to deference, especially for national‑security concerns.  Does a security‑related investigation automatically confer deference, or must the agency still demonstrate a clear nexus to a traditional law‑enforcement duty?  How do courts reconcile a broader privacy or national‑security interest with the statutory requirement of a ‘law‑enforcement purpose’?
Answer:
**Answer**

Exemption 7(A) protects “records … compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” that “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are *pending or reasonably anticipated* (U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A)‑(B)).  The court in *Farahi* applies the same two‑step test that courts have used for every Exemption 7(A) case:

1. **Law‑enforcement purpose.**  
2. **Interference with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings.**

The question is whether the “law‑enforcement purpose” can be satisfied by a *security‑related* investigation, even when that investigation is not part of a classic criminal prosecution.

---

### 1.  Law‑enforcement purpose: “law‑enforcement” ≠ “criminal prosecution”

The statutory language says only “law‑enforcement purposes.”  Courts have read that term broadly: it includes “the then‑ongoing or reasonably anticipated investigations of the violation of federal law” (see *Craigmiller v. U.S. Postal Service*, 839 F.3d 767, 773 (D.C. Cir. 2016)).  In *Farahi*, the FBI’s own affidavits were the key evidence.  The public Hardy declaration explained that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law‑enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations” (J.A. 42).  The ex‑parte Hardy declaration went further, detailing the nature and scope of that investigation.  The court was therefore satisfied that the FBI had met its burden of showing a “rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties” (cf. *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32; *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119).

The fact that the investigation was “security‑related” does not defeat the *law‑enforcement* requirement.  The Supreme Court’s ordinary definitions of “law‑enforcement” are broad enough to encompass counterterrorism investigations, and the court in *Farahi* specifically applies the same reasoning that has been applied in prior counterterrorism FOIA cases (e.g., *Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927).  Thus, a security‑related investigation automatically provides the statutory “law‑enforcement purpose” once the agency can tie it to an investigation of violations of federal law.

---

### 2.  Interference with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings

The second requirement is more restrictive.  The court held that ongoing investigations, even non‑criminal ones, count as “proceedings” so long as “the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case” (*Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 59).  The FBI’s affidavits showed that the investigation into Farahi was still active in March 2023 (Bender’s declaration, J.A. 131) and that the investigation had been articulated as “ongoing” in the Hardy declaration (J.A. 49).  The court therefore concluded that the required pendency was present.

Critically, the court noted that the Bender declaration was not being read *in isolation*: it was an update to the earlier Hardy affidavits.  In *Farahi*, the court emphasized that the agency had already established that “enforcement proceedings remained ongoing” when it first moved for summary judgment (J.A. 122).  The subsequent declaration was only meant to confirm that nothing had changed.  Because the agency had already satisfied the burden of pendency in the earlier affidavits, the Bender declaration—though terse—was adequate as a *supplement*.

The passage of time does not automatically defeat the pendency requirement, but it does require the agency to provide a contemporaneous update.  The court was satisfied that the FBI had done so, and there was no plausible argument that the pending proceedings had ceased to exist.

---

### 3.  Reconciliation with privacy and national‑security interests

The statutory scheme balances privacy and national‑security interests against the public’s right to know.  Exemption 7(A) is a narrow, *specific* relief that is triggered only when both of the above elements are met.  The court’s deference to the FBI (“Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference”) applies *only* after the agency has met the statutory burden.  The court did not rely on the mere fact that the investigation was security‑related to grant the exemption; it required evidence that the investigation was a *law‑enforcement purpose* (i.e., an investigation of violations of federal law) and that it was “ongoing” in the sense that a future criminal case could be built.

Hence, a security‑related investigation **does not automatically confer deference**.  The agency must first demonstrate that the investigation is a law‑enforcement investigation of a federal violation and that the investigation is pending or reasonably anticipated.  When those facts are present—as the court found in *Farahi*—the government may invoke Exemption 7(A) and receive deference from the court.

---

**Bottom line for students**

- *Law‑enforcement purpose* is satisfied by any investigation of violations of federal law, including counterterrorism or national‑security investigations.  
- *Interference* is limited to *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings; the agency must provide a contemporaneous indication that the proceedings still exist.  
- A security focus alone does **not** confer automatic deference; the statutory burden must be met first.  
- In *Farahi*, the FBI met both elements through its affidavits, so Exemption 7(A) applied and the court granted deference.  

This standard preserves the court’s role as a check on executive secrecy while recognizing that security investigations can be legitimate law‑enforcement activities.
