Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# 1. Contextualizing the Case: Facts, Parties, and Procedural History  

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the plaintiff, Foad Farahi, an Iranian national, seized a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) claim as a keystone in a long‑running struggle with the federal government over alleged ties to terrorism and immigration enforcement. This chapter sketches the factual back‑story of Farahi’s immigration and FBI investigation, tracks the FOIA request and the agency’s oscillating compliance, and traces the procedural milestones that culminated in the Ninth‑Circuit decision.

## 1.1. Partisan Facts: Farahi’s Immigration, Asylum, and Alleged Terrorist Ties  

Farahi entered the United States in 1993, a year after Iranian sanctions were intensified. By 2002, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) applied for removal proceedings against him on the basis of an overstayed visa. In the course of these proceedings he pursue several forms of relief—an initial asylum claim, a claim of withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and finally a voluntary departure—each of which prompted DHS to produce records detailing the claims and the government’s counter‑arguments.  

The crux of the dispute is the FBI’s assertions that Farahi had, over a period of years, engaged in activities “intersecting with violations of national security and international terrorism.” The FBI presented both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who allegedly “documented Farahi’s connections to high‑level terrorists.” These allegations appeared in *Farahi v. U.S. Attorney General*, 2024 WL 483601 (11th Cir. 2024), where the agent’s testimony played a pivotal role in advancing the government’s case. Though the appeals court’s decision in that case is not the focus of the current opinion, the material is relevant because the same FBI records are the subject of Farahi’s FOIA request.

In 2024, Farahi misrepresented that his FBI file contained specific allegations that he was connected to untenable terrorist organizations, including the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, Khaled Sheikh Mohammed, Al Qaeda members, and the convicted terrorist Jose Padilla, as well as alleged ties to charities that purportedly financed terrorism. These claims, though not independently verified in the *an eye of the public*, were the basis for his FOIA request.

## 1.2. FBI’s FOIA Response and Initial Review  

Farahi filed a FOIA request in the summer of 2015, seeking his entire FBI file, which the agency discovered spanned roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Under FOIA, the FBI is obliged to provide all responsive records unless an exemption applies. The agency, after a prolonged internal review, complied partially: it produced a limited, filtered set of documents and withheld the bulk, citing statutory exemptions. The withheld portion was large—most of the file—and the request was never fulfilled before the statutory 90‑day response deadline lapsed, prompting Farahi to file suit.

During litigation, the FBI filed a series of *disclosures* and *declarations* in support of summary‑judgment defenses. The first declaration, from Section Chief David Hardy, was public. It asserted that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” and that their disclosure could reasonably interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings—i.e., the FBI’s arguments for Exemption 7(A).  
Simultaneously, the FBI submitted an ex‑parte, sealed declaration from Hardy that gave more detail concerning the nature of the investigations. Farahi’s counsel filed no opposition to Hardy’s motions, and the district court granted the summary‑judgment motion in October 2020, finding the FBI’s basis for exemption timely.

## 1.3. Summary‑Judgment Motions and the District Court’s Orders (Oct 2020, Nov 2022, Oct 2023)  

The timeline unfolded as follows:

| Date | Event | Outcome | Notes |
|------|-------|---------|-------|
| **Oct 2020** | District court reviews FBI’s first summary‑judgment motion (containing Hardy’s public and ex‑parte assertions) | Grants summary judgment | Concludes records are exempt under 7(A) and that they are not reasonably segregable |
| **Nov 2022** | District court considers renewed summary‑judgment motion after defendant revised its declaration | Denies motion but “without prejudice”; orders FBI to update assessment of pendency of enforcement proceedings | District court notes the elapsed time and wants to confirm whether investigations remain ongoing; no specific remedial order is articulated in the public record |
| **Oct 2023** | FBI submits updated declarations from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender, both public (confirmation that investigations remain ongoing) and ex‑parte (detailing ongoing investigations and segregability RULS) | District court grants summary judgment | Finds that the FBI adequately updated the pendency claim and explained why the records cannot be reasonably segregated |

Thus, after 13 months of litigation, the district court reversed its earlier “grant” and “deny” positions, ultimately finding in favor of the FBI and confirming the application of Exemption 7(A).

## 1.4. The Role of Hardy and Bender Declarations (Public versus Ex‑Paré)  

The distinction between public and ex‑parte declarations is at the heart of FOIA litigation. Public declarations are admissible on the record; ex‑parte declarations are sealed and only available to the parties. The FDA’s objective in de‑classifying the information is to preserve national‑security or law‑enforcement interests while still allowing a court to examine the validity of an exemption claim.  

- **Hardy’s Public Declaration (Oct 2020)** indicated that the FBI’s records pertained to national‑security investigations and implied that they were exempt.  The commission also indicated that the FBI’s investigation was still active at that point.  
- **Hardy’s Ex‑Parte Declaration** (also filed in Oct 2020) offered greater detail about the investigation, but being sealed, it is visible only to the parties.  
- **Bender’s Public Declaration (Oct 2023)** largely reiterated that the investigations were still ongoing, effectively “refreshing” the pendency claim in light of the time elapsed.  
- **Bender’s Ex‑Parte Declaration** provided the detailed justification for the nonsplit nature of the records, which the court uses to evaluate the *segregability* analysis.  

Because the Ninth Circuit accepts that the court can rely on ex‑parte information that the district court showed as “reasonably specific,” the declarations from Hardy and Bender were central to the court’s determination that the records were exempt under FOIA and that no meaningful non‑exempt material could be segregated and released.

## 1.5. Appeal Filing and the Core Issues for Review  

Farahi’s appeal, filed in November 2023, challenged two core issues:

1. **Whether the FBI’s assertion that the records were compiled for “law‑enforcement purposes” was supported by *sufficient connecting evidence*** given the breadth of the alleged investigations.
2. **Whether the records’ withholding was justified under Exemption 7(A)** in light of the reported passage of time and the lack of explicit detail in the Bender declaration stating that the investigations were indeed still pending or reasonably anticipated.

In addition, Farahi contested the *segregability* analysis, arguing that the FBI had not differentiated its categories adequately to allow a court to assess which portions were exempt and which were not.

The district court’s record did not support the ex‑parte files, but the Ninth Circuit determined that the court’s succinct statements—allowing de facto reliance on Bender’s declaration to update the pendency claim—were sufficient, given the *government’s* burden to show that the records were indeed exempt under FOIA. The appellate court upheld the lower court’s grant of summary judgment and, in so doing, affirmed that the FBI had a non‑abusive basis to withhold Farahi’s file under Exemption 7(A).

# 2. The Legal Framework: FOIA, Exemption 7(A), and the Burden of Proof  

## 2.1 Statutory Text: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a) and § 552(b)(7)(A)  

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) obligates federal agencies to produce records in response to a request, subject only to the enumerated exemptions in § 552(b). 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3) states that “public records shall be made available …” unless an exemption applies. The statutory text for Exemption 7(A) is found in § 552(b)(7)(A), which bars disclosure of records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The plain language creates a two‑part inquiry: (1) the records must be compiled for a law‑enforcement purpose, and (2) their release must be likely to hamper pending or anticipated enforcement actions.  

### Key elements of § 552(b)(7)(A)  

| Element | Requirement | Source |
|---------|-------------|--------|
| Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes | The records arise from a law‑enforcement function. | § 552(b)(7)(A)(i) |
| Reasonable expectation of interference | Release would tend to jeopardize law‑enforcement actions. | § 552(b)(7)(A)(ii) |
| Pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings | The interference concern must relate to active or foreseeable proceeding. | § 552(b)(7)(A)(ii) |

### Procedural context  
The statutory pleading language is articulated in a *less formal* set of predicates that oblige **the agency** to present a *reasonably thorough* factual basis for each exemption argument. § 552(a)(4)(B) places the burden of proof on the agency to establish that an exemption applies. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) requires the agency to otherwise segregate and disclose all nonexempt portions.  

---

## 2.2 Definition of “Compiled for Law‑Enforcement Purposes”

“Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” is a broad standard that extends beyond investigative files to any material produced in connection with law‑enforcement duties. The courts have consistently held that an agency may invoke Exemption 7(A) when the records in question are the product of *any* law‑enforcement activity, including surveillance, intelligence gathering, or appointment of agents.  

### D.C. Circuit precedent  
- **Campbell v. Department of Justice**, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998): The court described the standard as a “reasonable nexus” between an agency’s law‑enforcement function and the records.  
- **Clemente v. FBI**, 867 F.3d 111 (D.C. Cir. 2017): The court affirmed that the FBI’s declaration that a file was compiled for investigations of national security clearly satisfied the statutory requirement.  
- **Mapother v. DOJ**, 3 F.3d 1533 (D.C. Cir. 1993): The court emphasized that “the determining factor is whether the records are the product of a law‑enforcement activity.”  

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the court concluded that the FBI’s statements that the records were created “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” satisfied this first limb of the exemption.  

---

## 2.3 “Reasonably Expected to Interfere” – The Requirement of Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings  

The second limb of Exemption 7(A) imposes a *two‑prong test*:

1. **Interference** – The appellant must demonstrate that disclosure of the records would *reasonably* jeopardize the success of a law‑enforcement action.  
2. **Pendency or Anticipation** – The interference must relate to an *enforcement proceeding that is either pending or reasonably anticipated* under the laws that permit the action.  
   - *Pending* means a proceeding that has already started (e.g., an ongoing investigation).  
   - *Reasonably anticipated* allows for cases in which a proceeding is *expected* to begin, for instance, if the agency has active investigative work that could culminate in formal charging or criminal prosecution.  

### Supporting cases  
- **CREW v. DOJ**, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014): The court clarified that pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings encompass ongoing criminal investigations.  
- **Juarez v. DOJ**, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008): The court held that an agency may assert Exemption 7(A) by simply attesting to the existence and ongoing nature of an investigation.  
- **CNSS v. DOJ**, 331 F.3d 918 (D.C. Cir. 2003): The court recognized national‑security concerns as reinforcing deference when evaluating claims of pending or anticipatory proceedings.  

In *Farahi*, the court reviewed the FBI’s public and ex‑parte declarations that the investigative work against Farahi “remains ongoing.” The court found that such statements satisfied the statutory *pending* element, even after years of elapsed time, because the FBI’s own attestations confirmed an uninterrupted investigative trajectory.  

---

## 2.4 The Agency’s Burden Under § 552(a)(4)(B)

Under § 552(a)(4)(B), the agency carries a *discretionary* burdens:  

- **To prove the first exemption limb** — that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.  
- **To prove the second exemption limb** — that the disclosure would *reasonably* interfere with a pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding.  

These burdens are *high* but not *preclusive*; the court is required to review the agency’s evidence de novo and may not defer solely based on the agency’s claims. However, there is a *weight of deference* toward the agency when it is the *expert* in law‑enforcement questions, as indicated in *Campbell* and *CNSS*. The court must weigh the agency’s affidavits, the agency’s internal policies, and any corroborating evidence.  

When an agency meets these burdens, the court must then address *segregability*: the question of whether the exempt portions are *inextricably intertwined* with nonexempt material. The agency must establish that no reasonably segregable subset exists, or that segregation would defeat the purpose of the exemption.  

---

## 2.5 Judicial Deference Standards: Supreme Court Guidance and D.C. Circuit Precedent  

### Supreme Court guidance  
The Supreme Court has never directly addressed Exemption 7(A) but has articulated a broad principle of deference to agencies on *enforcement* matters. The relevant standard is the *“deference to the administration of police power”* doctrine, applied in *United States v. U.S. District Court*, 511 U.S. 2 (1994). The Court acknowledged that agencies possess *expertise* in the *law‑enforcement functions* their statutes require.  

### D.C. Circuit precedent  
- **Campbell v. DOJ** (see § 2.2) affirms *informed deference* to law‑enforcement agencies, especially when the exemption is invoked.  
- **CREW v. DOJ** (2014) refines the *pendency* definition and bounds the deference to the *fact‑finding* role of the court.  
- **CNSS v. DOJ** (2003) applies *special deference* when national‑security concerns are at issue, reinforcing the *protection* of sensitive law‑enforcement materials.  
- **Juarez v. DOJ** (2008) emphasizes that an agency’s *plain attestations* of ongoing investigations are sufficient for the *pending* element, further narrowing the evidentiary burden.  

A critical nuance from *Rep*s. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI* (2021) is that *summary judgment* in FOIA cases is reviewed de novo, meaning the appellate court must independently re‑examine all evidence, though it may repose a degree of deference on never-disputed facts the agency demonstrates.  

---

**Bottom line for a reader learning to read the opinion.**  
In *Farahi v. FBI*, the court judged that the FBI satisfied the statutory burdens of Exemption 7(A) by proving that (1) the requested records were indeed “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and (2) their release would *reasonably* interfere with “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceedings. The court applied Supreme Court‑level deference to the FBI’s expertise while ensuring that the agency had exhibited sufficient clause‑level detail to meet the statutory requisites. The decision demonstrates how the statutory text, case law, and deference standards coalesce to protect law‑enforcement records in FOIA litigation.

# 3. Analyzing the FBI’s Exemption Claim: Custody, Pendency, and Scope  

The Court’s decision pivots on whether the FBI’s FOIA declarations satisfy the dual elements of Exemption 7(A): (1) the records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes,” and (2) their disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated.”  The foregoing analysis dissects each element, explains how the Court evaluated the FBI’s affidavits, and clarifies the factual grounding that led the district court, and ultimately the appellate court, to affirm the exemption.

## 3.1. Establishing “Compiled for Law‑Enforcement Purposes”: Hardys’ Public and Ex‑parte Statements  

Under FOIA § 552(b)(7)(A)(i), the government must prove the records were prepared “in furtherance of investigations of violations of federal law or in the pursuit of a legitimate law‑enforcement goal.” Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, a Court generally accords it a “fair measure of deference” (see *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119).  Yet deference does not obviate the need for a clear “nexus” between the investigation and the FBI’s law‑enforcement duties, nor between the subject of the investigation and a possible security risk or violation.  (*Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32; *Pratt v. Webster*, 673 F.2d 408, 420‑21).

### The Public Hardy declaration  
In its first summary‑judgment motion, the FBI relied on a public declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS).  The declaration advanced the following factual basis:

1. **Nature of the Investigation** – Hardy described the file as “compiled in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” and noted ongoing “law‑enforcement proceedings” related to Farahi’s alleged ties to there‑present foreign terrorist organizations.  
2. **Subject‑Matter Connections** – References were made to Farahi’s alleged affiliation with the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, links to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed, and connections to known Al Qaeda operatives (e.g., Jose Padilla).  
3. **Legal Context** – Hardy mentioned that the investigation was a continuation of previous immigration‑related proceedings, implicating potential criminal charges such as violations of the Immigration and Nationality Act and the Terrorism Act.

The Court accepted Hardy’s public declaration as a “reasonable factual basis” for the inference that the FBI’s records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.  The Court emphasized that the declaration made a “concrete link” between the FBI’s work and the broader law‑enforcement mission, satisfying the statutory requirement.

### The Ex‑parte Hardy declaration  
Hardy also filed an ex‑parte affidavit containing more detail—though it remained sealed.  While the opinion does not disclose the specifics of this affidavit, it acknowledges that it “provided considerable detail about the nature of that investigation.”  The Court concluded that, in combination with the public statement, the ex‑parte affidavit adequately explained how the material was part of a law‑enforcement effort.

### The Bender declaration  
When the FBI moved for renewal of summary judgment, it pointed to a new declaration from Joseph Bender, Acting Section Chief of RIDS.  Bender’s public statement stated simply that “the investigation remains ongoing.”  Because the Court’s earlier order had already found the FBI’s initial affidavits sufficient, the Court treated Bender’s assertion as a *confirmation* rather than a new assertion of the law‑enforcement status.  The Court noted that the FBI could not rely on Bender to re‑establish the law‑enforcement nexus from scratch; that was already satisfied.

**Bottom line:**  The Court held that the FBI fulfilled its burden of proving the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, largely relying on Hardy’s affidavits and treating Bender’s confirmation as a status update.

## 3.2. Demonstrating “Pending or Reasonably Anticipated” Proceedings  

Once the “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” prong is satisfied, the second prong requires that the disclosure of the material could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.”  *CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014).  The Court reviewed the FBI’s affidavits under this standard.

### Hardys’ representations of pendency  
Hardy’s public declaration noted that the investigation was *ongoing* and involved “pending law‑enforcement proceedings.”  Here, “ongoing” was interpreted to mean that the FBI was still gathering evidence for a potential future criminal case, and that case “would be jeopardized if the evidence were … released.”  The Court reiterated the widely accepted knowledge that “tipping off” a suspect about the scope of an investigation can nullify the investigative advantage (*Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 59).

### Ex‑parte Hardy’s elaboration  
The ex‑parte affidavit expanded upon the investigation’s scope (though its contents are not published), presumably corroborating the claim that the FBI was still compiling evidence.  The Court found the combination of both affidavits to be “sufficiently detailed” to satisfy the “pendency” requirement.

### The Bender declaration—an update  
Bender’s statement that “investigations remain ongoing” was treated as a *status aggiornated* date, not a new ground.  The Court stressed that the district court had already accepted that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden in the initial motion.  Therefore, the Bender affidavit merely confirmed that no fundamental changes had occurred.

**Bottom line:**  The Court concluded that the FBI’s claims met the pendency requirement, because the evidence pointed to an investigation that was actively gathering evidence for a forthcoming criminal case and whose disclosure would likely interfere with that case.

## 3.3. The Concept of “Concrete Prospective Law‑Enforcement Proceedings”  

The Court drew heavily on *CREW*, *Juarez*, and *CNSS* to elaborate the “concrete prospective” standard.  The holding that “ongoing criminal investigations count as proceedings” is long helpful precedent.  The key test is that *some* substantive investigative activity is still pending—evidence being collected, interrogations scheduled, or legal filings prepared.  

In this case, the FBI’s affidavits together argued that the investigation into Farahi’s alleged terrorism connections was “ongoing” in the sense that evidence was still being collected or a case was still within the investigative phase.  The Court found this line of argument to be consistent with the *CREW* approach and satisfied the “reasonable expectation” requirement.  The Court stressed that a mere ex hypothesi presence of “possible future criminal case” is insufficient; there must be *active* investigative activity that could be compromised.

**Bottom line:**  The case reinforced that “concrete prospective” means the agency must show active evidence‑gathering, not merely a theoretical claim of future charges.

## 3.4. Evaluating Temporal Claims: Willingness of the FBI to Update the Dated Hardys’ Statements  

One of the more contentious issues was the *generous* passage of time between the initial hearing (October 2020) and the renewed motion (2023).  Farahi argued that the FBI’s earlier affidavits were out‑of‑date and should be given less weight.  The Court did not side with Farahi, but its reasoning deserves explanation.

1. **Temporal Gap and Judicial Order** – The district court, recognizing the time elapsed, ordered the FBI to “update its assessment” of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending.  It sought the same type and scope of affidavit that Hardys had originally provided, aiming to confirm the persistence of the investigation.  
2. **FBI’s response** – The FBI furnished new declarations from Bender that echoed Hardy’s earlier statements, and it supplied additional information addressing the district court’s concerns about segregability.  The Court viewed Bender’s brief affirmation as a *legitimate update* rather than an entirely new position.  
3. **Legal Precedent on Temporal Sufficiency** – The Court emphasized that FOIA does not require a “horizon” of *future* activity to be extraordinary; an agency’s own evidence that an investigation is still active can suffice, even if the investigation began years earlier—provided current operational facts remain.  (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1098; *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 59).  
4. **Contextual Support** – Additional evidence, such as the FBI’s statements in immigration proceedings and the known ongoing status of investigations against individuals and groups Farahi is linked to, reinforced that the investigation was not stale.

**Bottom line:**  The Court accepted the updated affidavits as a *reasonable justification*, given the IRS’s prior recognition of pending investigations and the FBI’s continued evidence‑gathering activities.

## 3.5. The Bender Declaration as a Confirmation, Not a Groundbreaking Affidavit  

Farahi challenged the weight the Court placed on Bender’s declaration, arguing it was too terse to establish an active investigation.  The Court’s answer hinged on the distinction between *initial* and *supplemental* affidavits.

- **Initial vs. Supplemental**: Hardys’ affidavits laid out the substantive basis for the exemption, including droves of detail on the nature of the investigation.  Bender’s affidavit was a *supplement*, filed after the Court had already found the FBI’s earlier affidavits sufficient.  Its role was to confirm that nothing had changed; it did not need to restate every fact anew.
- **Jurisdictional Check‑in**: The district court itself had “deemed” the FBI “seemingly” met its burden in the first summary‑judgment motion, and thus did not need Bender’s affidavits to be exhaustive.  The FBI’s affirming statement simply updated the timeframe and reassured that a valid Exemption 7(A) still applied.
- **Reasonable Certainty**: Because the Court found no new evidence that the investigation had halted or concluded, the brief statement from Bender was adequate to meet the *reasonable expectation* requirement.  The Court’s reasoning is consistent with FOIA: *a brief, authoritative statement by the agency to confirm that the status of the investigation has not changed can satisfy the DOJ’s burden, particularly where earlier affidavits are robust.*

**Bottom line:**  The Court interpreted Bender’s declaration as an *administrative update* rather than a substantive ground.  The brevity of the statement was not a problem; the underlying records and prior affidavits already established the required facts.

---

**Take‑Away for the Student**  
When reading a FOIA case involving Exemption 7(A), focus on:

1. **The Declarations’ Content** – Are they specific enough to show a lawful law‑enforcement purpose and an ongoing investigation?  
2. **Deference Doctrine** – The Court gives a home‑court favor to law‑enforcement agencies but does not allow them to blanket everything.  
3. **Pendency Test** – Look for evidence that the investigation is *currently active*, not merely “once‑more” potential.  
4. **Temporal Updates** – Courts will accept updated affidavits if they confirm the persistence of the investigation after a statutory or judicial request.  
5. **Segregability** – Even if some non‑exempt material exists, it must be “reasonably segregable.”  Random, line‑by‑line remarks are not.

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the Court made a careful, fact‑by‑fact assessment.  The FBI’s affidavits satisfied both prongs of Exemption 7(A), and the Court affirmed the agency’s right to withhold the vast majority of the requested material.  These principles will recur in many FOIA disputes involving national‑security or terrorism investigations.

# 4. Segregability and the ‘Reasonable Steps’ Requirement

In the FOIA context, the statutory mandate that a federal agency “take [reasonable] steps” to segregate and release non‑exempt information is a procedural hurdle that sits beneath the substantive question of whether an exemption applies. The *Foad Farahi* case illustrates how the court applies this duty and why it ultimately held that no portion of the FBI file could be released without jeopardizing the agency’s investigations. The analysis pivots on three pillars: the statutory language itself, the doctrine of “inextricable intertwining,” and the procedural requirements for category‑by‑category segregation.

## 4.1 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii): Agency Obligation to Segregate

Section 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) directs agencies to “take reasonable steps to segregate and release non‑exempt information.” The statute does not define “reasonable,” but the courts have consistently read it as a standard that requires the agency to actively separate exempt material from any non‑exempt content that can be isolated without imposing a significant burden on the agency or jeopardizing exempt interests. Importantly, the obligation is two‑fold: first, the agency must determine what portions of a record are exempt; second, it must render any non‑exempt portions available to the requester.

In *Farahi*, the FBI complied with the procedural requirement in two distinct stages. Initially, after the agency’s 2019 summary‑judgment motion, it presented a declaration from Section Chief David Hardy that described the nature of its law‑enforcement investigations and asserted that the withheld records could not be reasonably segregated. The district court accepted Hardy’s declaration but ordered the FBI to update its assessment of pending investigations, underscoring the need for contemporaneous evidence. Subsequently, the FBI supplied a supplemental, ex‑parte declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender, clarifying that the investigations remained ongoing and elaborating on the reasons the documents could not be separated. The court found Bender’s information sufficiently detailed to satisfy the “reasonable steps” requirement, allowing it to deny the request for release.

Thus, the statute’s procedural duty was met not through an exhaustive line‑by‑line review—an impossible task given the size of the file—but through a series of credible, agency‑provided affidavits that explained why the exempt portions were inextricably linked to the non‑exempt material. The court held that the FBI had taken “reasonable steps” under the circumstances.

## 4.2 Doctrine of Inextricable Intertwining—Juarez, Mead, and Perioperative Precedents

A key concept in adjudicating FOIA segregation is that a record can be withheld entirely when the exempt and non‑exempt portions are “inextricably intertwined.” The D.C. Circuit has applied this principle to both criminal and national‑security investigations.

In **Juarez v. DOJ**, the court held that an agency may withhold a record in its entirety when a “reasonable specificity” in an affidavit demonstrates that the exempt portion “is inextricably intertwined” with the non‑exempt portion, making segregation impractical or harmful. **Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force** further clarified that segregation is impractical if a large amount of exempt information is peppered throughout the record such that extracting the non‑exempt content would not meaningfully reduce the risk of disclosure.

The **Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs.** decision amplified the *Mead* test by emphasizing that the focus of FOIA is information rather than mere words. Even if non‑exempt language appears line‑by‑line, if the overall context is so permeated by the exempt material that its removal would not provide meaningful relief to the requester, segregation is not required.

In *Farahi*, the FBI’s affidavits argued that the investigations were sufficiently broad—encompassing alleged ties to terrorist organizations, foreign combatants, and extremist networks—that key evidence was dispersed throughout the document. Consequently, the non‑exempt information was seen as part of a “larger investigative narrative” and therefore inseparable from the exempt portions. The court agreed that the FBI had met its burden by showing the inextricable intertwining, and therefore the agency could withhold the entire record.

## 4.3 The FBI’s Category‑by‑Category Justification and the Requirement of Distinct Categories

Under FOIA, when an agency withholds documents “on a category‑by‑category basis,” it must use categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category (**CREW v. DOJ**). This requirement prevents overly broad categorizations that would effectively release nothing.

The FBI in *Farahi* identified several categories of records: those relating to ongoing investigations of national security violations, those pertaining to suspected associations with designated terrorist entities, and others concerning background data on the subject. Farahi challenged the breadth of those categories, arguing that they were not sufficiently distinct and therefore failed to satisfy the FOIA requirement. However, the court noted that the FBI failed to provide a concrete justification for why each category was separate, and that Farahi had not offered a specific alternative. Because the court could not determine that any particular category represented a distinct, legally separable set of documents, it concluded that the FBI had not met the necessary condition for category‑based withholding.

Despite this objection, the court still upheld the denial. The rationale was that even if the categorization was imperfect, the *inextricable intertwining* doctrine and the overall affirmative declarations about the ongoing investigations were sufficient to meet the broader “reasonable steps” obligation. The procedural failure in presenting distinct categories did not render the entire withholding invalid, because the court already found the agency’s substantive exemption claim to be valid.

## 4.4 The Court’s Evaluation of Bender’s Supplementary Ex‑parte Argument

The most significant addition to the FBI’s justification was the ex‑parte declaration from Acting Section Chief Bender. This affidavit served two purposes: it confirmed that the investigations remained ongoing as of March 2023, and it supplied additional narrations of why the FBI could not segregate the exempt material.

The district court examined Bender’s assertions in light of Hardy’s earlier affidavits and the legal requirement that an agency may rely on *ex‑parte* statements when no alternative exists. The court recognized that FOIA plaintiffs are typically on the losing side when a declaration is offered ex‑parte, yet it was bound by the statutory standard that a non‑exempt body may provide such evidence to satisfy the “reasonable steps” requirement if the material is otherwise unspoolable.

In its analysis, the court noted that Bender’s declaration simply reiterated earlier statements rather than adding new factual detail. Nevertheless, because the earlier affidavits had already established pendency and the nature of the investigation, Bender’s added confirmation was deemed sufficient. The court also explained that it did not need to disclose the substance of the ex‑parte declaration on the public record; the affidavit’s contents were deemed non‑public even under FOIA, yet admissible as evidence in a non‑disclosure context. This approach aligns with precedent that allows ex‑parte affidavits to be used to decide segregation issues without disclosure of the very material the but.

Therefore, Bender’s supplement did not change the court’s finding; it merely reinforced the agency’s burden‑carrying obligations.

## 4.5 Determining ‘Meaningful’ vs. ‘Random’ Non‑Exempt Data in Large Volumes

Finally, the court faced the practical question of how much non‑exempt data exists within a 10‑plus thousand page file. The standard in *Mead* and *Perioperative* suggests that if non‑exempt statements are so rare or so randomly disposed that extracting them would provide no “meaningful” information to the requester, segregation is unnecessary. The court acknowledged that identifying a handful of non‑exempt statements scattered throughout a voluminous file does not necessarily provide the requester with public‑interest information; such statements might be anonymized, contain only procedural routine, or be logically dependant on the exempt context.

In *Farahi*, the FBI argued that the non‑exempt material was minimal, but the district court found that a random, sparse presence of non‑exempt lines did not satisfy the “reasonable steps” criterion because the overall narrative remained tied to the investigation. The court emphasized that the FOIA “focus is information, not simply words”, and that the real concern is whether the release of the non‑exempt material would jeopardize the underlying law‑enforcement activity.

The outcome was a denial of the request, holding that the FBI had efficiently applied its segregation obligations by providing ample justification for why the exempt material could not be separated from the rest of the file.

# 5. Holding, Reasoning, and Practical Implications  

The appellate Court’s decision in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* culminates in a clear holding that the records subject of the request remain protected under FOIA Exemption 7(A).  The Court furnishes a concise justification of that holding, explains how it applied the de novo standard to a summary‑judgment motion within the FOIA framework, and then considers the wider ramifications for petitioners seeking records that might touch on national‑security or counterterrorism investigations.  

## 5.1. The Court’s Holding: Exemption 7(A) Applies and Records Remain Withheld  

In its holding, the Court affirmed the district court’s judgment that the FBI’s request to deny disclosure constitutes a valid exercise of FOIA Exemption 7(A).  The exemption protects records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are either pending or reasonably anticipated.  The Court found that the FBI met both prongs of the exemption: 

1. **Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.**  The FBI, by definition a law‑enforcement agency, is entitled to deference in determining the purposive nature of its files.  The FBI’s public declaration by David Hardy and the ex parte Hardy declaration provided sufficient detail to show that the file was assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism.”  The Court reaffirmed that the agency’s own evidence, including Farahi’s immigration‑adjudication record, corroborated the FBI’s claim that the investigation was real, substantive, and uncompleted.  

2. **Interference with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings.**  The Court relied on the FBI’s demonstration that the investigation was still ongoing as of the date of the Bender declaration (March 2023).  Interference was based on the risk that a premature disclosure could tip off suspects or compromise investigative techniques—an accepted justification under 552(b)(7)(B).  The Court noted that the Bender declaration, though terse, was not an isolated assertion; it updated earlier, more detailed declarations that had already established the pendency of the proceedings.  

Because the FBI satisfied both elements, the Court held that the records are exempt and must remain withheld.

## 5.2. De Novo Review of Summary Judgment in the Context of FOIA  

The appellate Court applied the classic *de novo* standard of review to the summary‑judgment motion, as stipulated in 552(a)(4)(B).  This approach differs from the more deferential “substantial evidence” standard that governs most FOIA determinations.  In a summary‑judgment context, the Court scrutinizes the agency’s evidence afresh and requires that the administrative record be complete and sufficient to support the exemption claim.  

Key points in the application of *de novo* review:

* **Burden on the Agency.**  The court reiterated that the FBI bears the burden to prove the exemption.  The burden does not get shifted to the petitioner; any argument failing to prove a meaningful factual reality must be put on record.  

* **Deference to Agency Expertise.**  While the Court reviewed the evidence from scratch, it recognized the FBI’s specialized knowledge and thereby accorded a fair measure of deference to its determinations, as established in *Campbell v. DOJ* and *National Security Studies* (the “national‑security problem” doctrine).  Deference is not automatic but contingent on meeting the statutory criteria.  

* **Effect of Ex Parte Declarations.**  The Court accepted the FBI’s ex parte declarations because, as the Court explained, there was no viable alternative to adjudicate the issues without revealing the technology or investigations themselves.  The Court emphasized that the petitioner failed to challenge the ex parte submissions; thus, the court could not comment on their propriety or specific weight under public record standards.  

By applying *de novo* review, the Court ensured that the exemption claim was fully articulated and that the agency’s evidence satisfied the statutory test, all while respecting the integrity of ongoing investigations.

## 5.3. The Role of Congressional Statutory Language vs. Judicial Scrutiny  

FOIA is legislatively silent about the internal mechanics of holding records that are exempt under section 7(A) besides requiring agencies to “segregate and release non‑exempt information” (552(a)(8)(A)(ii)).  The Court clarified that the statutory language, while concise, provides the only statutory framework for adjudicating Exemption 7(A) claims.  In turn, judicial scrutiny steps in to interpret and apply that language.

The Court distinguished two levels of scrutiny:

1. **Statutory Interpretation.**  The Court first interprets the statutory text to establish whether a record satisfies the procedural definitions (compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, interference with pending or anticipated proceedings).  This is a straightforward reading of the statutory text.

2. **Judicial Fact‑Finding.**  After a positive statutory reading, the Court examines the factual record to determine whether the agency met its burden.  This includes evaluating the sufficiency of the FBI’s declarations, the extent of ongoing investigations, and the reasonableness of the agency’s segregation footing.  The Court emphasized that de novo review, the most robust form of judicial scrutiny, is required to maintain the integrity of both the statutory mechanism and the underlying law‑enforcement work.

By adhering to this tiered approach, the Court reinforced the principle that FOIA decisions must be anchored in statutory language but are ultimately assessed through judicial fact‑finding.

## 5.4. Practical Consequences for Petitioners Seeking Similar Records  

For law‑enforcement or national‑security investigators pursuing FOIA petitions, *Farahi* offers several practical take‑aways:

* **Robustness of Exemption Claims.**  An agency must supply detailed, specific evidence that the file was compiled for law‑enforcement hunting and that releasing it would likely jeopardize the ongoing investigations.  Merely asserting that an investigation is “ongoing” is insufficient unless it is corroborated by other facts and evidence.  The declarants should provide information that distinguishes between pending active investigations and historical or inactive ones.  

* **Segregability Requirements.**  In vast volumes of responsive records, the agency must articulate why exempt portions are “inextricably intertwined” with non‑exempt portions.  If the non‑exempt content is interspersed at the line‑level or is cumulative in nature, segregation might be impossible.  Agencies should provide a rational, category‑based segregation plan, noting room for any non‑exempt material that could be released.  

* **Ex Parte Declarations.**  While ex parte submissions are necessary to protect sensitive content, petitioners can expect to be granted the right to challenge the agency’s judgment indirectly through the court’s findings on the subordination of the exemptions, not directly via the declarants’ specific facts.  Consequently, petitioners should consider leveraging the appellate path to bring review of the sufficiency of the agency’s evidence.  

* **Time as an Issue.**  The Court recognized that elapsed time can erode the credibility of “ongoing” investigations.  Agencies should keep their status claims up to date and provide time‑stamped evidence.  A lapse in the status of an investigation can undermine a firm’s Exemption 7(A) argument, even if the investigation remained open months earlier.  

Overall, the decision signals that a meticulous factual record and precise legal argumentation are mandatory to survive a **de novo** review of a FOIA exemption claim, especially in the realm of national‑security investigations.

## 5.5. The Balance Between Transparency and Law‑Enforcement Integrity: Takeaway Lessons  

The *Farahi* decision underscores the enduring FOIA tension: the public’s right to know versus the state’s need to protect law‑enforcement processes.  A few take‑away lessons illustrate this balance:

1. **Statutory Doctrine Prevails.**  The Court is bound to apply the statutory text of Exemption 7(A).  It does not usurp the legislature’s judgment that certain records should remain confidential when they may jeopardize future enforcement actions.  

2. **Agency Deference Is Qualified.**  While agencies can rely on their expertise, the court retains a meaningful factual review.  Deference is not conferred automatically; each element of the exemption must be adequately supported.  

3. **Segregability Is A High Threshold.**  The “extricable” standard is strict.  Agencies cannot claim broad segregation privileges over large blocks of material that are line‑by‑line scattered; otherwise the federal public will be deprived of useful information without a legitimate enforcement need.  

4. **Ex Parte Declarations Are A Double‑Edged Sword.**  They protect sensitive data from exposure but also shield the FOIA petitioner from direct examination of the specifics that justify the exemption.  Petitioners must therefore rely on judicial inference and broader evidentiary evidence rather than contesting the declarants’ inner thoughts.  

5. **Pragmatic Application of the FOIA.**  The *Farahi* ruling illustrates the Court’s willingness to enforce FOIA’s “reasonable steps” requirement for segregation while recognizing that, in certain national‑security cases, full disclosure is untenable without compromising ongoing investigations.  

In sum, the Court’s control over a summary‑judgment motion under *de novo* review in a FOIA context reflects a careful equilibrium: it respects the statutory exemption designed to guard law‑enforcement efficacy, yet it ensures that such exemptions are not misapplied through weak evidence or overbroad claims.  For scholars and practitioners alike, *Farahi* reaffirms that FOIA litigation against national‑security‑sensitive investigations remains a tightly circumscribed practice, governed by both statutory mandates and rigorous judicial scrutiny.
