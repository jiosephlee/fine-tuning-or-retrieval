Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What is the procedural value of ex‑parte affidavits in FOIA cases, and can they be challenged on the public record?
Question:
The FBI’s update was an ex‑parte affidavit, and the court relied on it heavily, yet Farahi could not contest it on the public record.  Under what circumstances is it permissible for a court to accept an ex‑parte declaration without a hearing?  When can a plaintiff raise objections to the adequacy or sufficiency of an ex‑parte declaration?  How does this balance the need to protect sensitive information with the FOIA’s transparency purpose?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In FOIA disputes a court may rely on an ex‑parte declaration *without a hearing* when the agency, acting in deference to its own expertise, shows that an exemption applies and, crucially, that the confidential material cannot be reasonably segregated. The plaintiff can still challenge the sufficiency of the declaration, but only after the agency has made a *public* showing of the same facts. Ex‑parte affidavits are accepted when the material is so sensitive that disclosure would itself undermine the very enforcement proceeding that the exemption protects. The balance is struck by the “reasonable‑steps” requirement to segregate non‑exempt information and by the “deference to the executive” standard: the court must give the agency the benefit of the doubt unless the affidavit is conclusory or the agency crashes on the segregability test.

---

### 1.  When can a court accept an ex‑parte affidavit without a hearing?

The *principle* is that a FOIA de‑summation is **de novo review** (see *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press* v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350).  The only circumstance that permits an ex‑parte declaration to be the sole basis for a decision is **when the agency cannot provide that information on the public record** without itself revealing the very material it seeks to keep secret. The opinion in *Farahi* cites two primary authorities:

1. **Exemption 7(A) requires a real threat that disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably‐anticipated law‑enforcement proceedings** (see *Crews* v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082).  
2. **The executive branch’s decisions on enforcement status are entitled to a fair measure of deference** (see *Clemente*, *Campbell*, *CNSS*). ▷Thus the court is permitted to “rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated” (*Juarez* v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54).

In practice, the court looks for:

* A **public declaration** that establishes the *flourishing* of a law‑enforcement investigation and its relevance to the FOIA‑requested record.  
* An **ex‑parte, sealed declaration** that supplies the **necessary detail** (e.g., names of targets, scope, and status of an investigation) sufficient to support the exemption claim.  

If a plaintiff has no means of contesting the exemption on the public record, the court typically will not hold a hearing. As the *Farahi* opinion notes, “the court did not need to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration” when *Farahi* had not opposed the agency’s certificate.

---

### 2.  How can a plaintiff raise objections to an ex‑parte declaration?

The plaintiff’s options are limited, but not denied:

| Step | Action | Timing | Basis |
|------|--------|--------|-------|
| **A. Oppose the declaration in the same pleading** | File an objection or *motion to dismiss* the agency’s exempt claim. | Before the court grants summary judgment. | The plaintiff cannot “challenge it on the public record” only if it has not been filed or recorded; otherwise the court has already decided. |
| **B. Amended pleadings** | After an agency’s initial declaration, the plaintiff can submit an *amended complaint* or *motion* setting out factual grounds that rebut the exemption. | Anytime up to the jurisdictional deadline for the case. | The plaintiff may provide evidence that the investigation is no longer pending or that the material is reasonably segregable. |
| **C. Dependent judgment hearing** | If the court attaches a *certificate of the need for a hearing* to the decision, the plaintiff may request a hearing on the same record at that time. | After the decision but before the judgment becomes final. | The court may deem it appropriate to let the plaintiff present oral argument. |
| **D. Post‑judgment motion** | File a *motion for reconsideration* or *motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict* if new evidence shows the exemption was incorrectly applied. | After the judgment is entered. | The plaintiff can argue that the agency has failed the “reasonable‑steps” test for segregation. |

In *Farahi*, the plaintiff was denied the opportunity to contest the *Bender* ex‑parte declaration because the agency’s initial affidavits already satisfied the court’s deference threshold. The court reinforced that the plaintiff “cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them” unless they had contested the *public* showing of the same facts.

---

### 3.  Public‑record objections and the segregation requirement

**Segregability** is the linchpin of the balance between transparency and confidentiality. Under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) the agency must “take reasonable steps to segregate and release non‑exempt information.” (*Farahi* v. FBI, 8 F.Ct. 834 (D.C. Cir. 2025)).

*Farahi* demonstrates the court’s deference to a **reasonably‑specific** ex‑parte declaration when it cannot isolate non‑exempt portions. The federal court found the FBI’s explanation “adequately explained why the protected material was not reasonably segregable” (see *Farahi* 139). Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court is especially willing to accept the agency’s in‑depth knowledge of the investigative context.

If a plaintiff thinks the agency’s categorization is “overbroad” or “inaccurate,” it must have matched evidence in the public record to contest it. *Farahi* notes that the plaintiff “forfeited his arguments on this score” by failing to present a viable challenge to the categories in the public pleadings (*Khine* v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959). Therefore, the “public record” requirement is not a procedural limitation; it is a substantive filter that requires the plaintiff to articulately identify how each claimed exemption applies to each factual subset.

---

### 4.  Key take‑aways for a law student

1. **Ex‑parte affidavits are permissible** when a government agency can demonstrate, in detail (public or sealed per the FOIA rules), that an exemption applies *and* that the evidence cannot be reasonably segregated.  
2. **Plaintiffs may challenge the affidavit** only after the agency has furnished a *public* presentation of the same facts. If the agency’s initial pleading satisfied the court, the plaintiff has no further avenue to contest it on the public record unless it file an opposing pleading.  
3. **The balance** is struck by the *reasonable‑steps* segregation duty. The court will only enforce an exemption if the agency can show that attempting to isolate non‑exempt material would itself compromise the investigation.  
4. **Deference to the executive**—particularly in national‑security contexts—means that courts will rarely overturn an agency’s finding of “pending or reasonably anticipated” litigation or “inextricably intertwined” records.

> *In short, an ex‑parte affidavit can be the fulcrum of a FOIA decision, but only because the record‑keeping agency has demonstrated that disclosure would itself endanger the enforcement proceeding it seeks to protect.*
