Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What is the scope of deference given to agencies when invoking FOIA Exemption 7(A), especially in national‑security contexts?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly cites deference to the FBI’s determination that a record is compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.  How far does that deference extend to the agency’s assertion that information could ‘reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings’?  Are agencies allowed to rely on broad, high‑level statements (e.g., ‘investigations remain ongoing’) without more granular evidence, or must they provide detailed factual corroboration?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit re‑affirmed the well‑established deference rule that applies when an agency invokes FOIA Exemption 7(A). The court’s reasoning shows precisely how that deference operates, what it requires of the agency, and where the line is drawn between a “high‑level” assertion of ongoing investigations and the detailed factual corroboration that a plaintiff may still seek.

---

### 1.  The scope of deference

A FOIA **exemption claim is made by the agency, not by the claimant**.  
Under § 552(a)(4)(B), the agency bears the entire burden to show that a statutory exemption applies. The court therefore “reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo” (*Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th at 361) and “deems the government’s determination entitled to deference” (*Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d at 32).

The deference is **qualified**:  
* The agency must make a *reasonable* and *nuanced* assertion that the records are “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and that their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1096).  
* The agency’s statement is not simply an empty “investigations are ongoing”; the court requires **an evidential basis** that the investigation still exists and that the owed risk to law‑enforcement proceedings remains.

In *Farahi*, the court found that the FBI’s Hardy declaration “provided considerable detail about the nature of that investigation” (*J.A.* at 49). That detail satisfies the “reasonable basis” standard. The later Bender declaration, though terse, was treated as an **update** rather than a *stand‑alone* assertion and therefore did not erode the agency’s earlier evidentiary support.

---

### 2.  What counts as “reasonable evidence”

The court’s language in *Farahi* makes it clear that "reasonable evidence" may be **high‑level, but it must be specific enough to satisfy the court’s inquiry**:

* **Existence of a pending proceeding** – the agency may simply attest to the *existence* of ongoing investigations (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1099).  
* **Potential interference** – the agency must show *how* disclosure could interfere, e.g., by “tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation” (*Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 59).  
* **Inextricable link to law‑enforcement duties** – the agency’s declaration must link the requested records to a *specific* law‑enforcement activity (the FBI’s counterterrorism investigation in this case) (*Campbell*, 164 F.3d at 32).

The court expressly stated that the Bender declaration “confirm[ed] that the investigation remained ongoing” but did not require that declaration alone to meet the burden. Instead, it was the *cumulative* evidence—including the Hardy declarations and the FBI’s own public statements about Farahi’s alleged ties to terrorist organizations—that sufficed.

---

### 3.  Are broad, high‑level statements enough?

No.  While agencies may not need a *word‑by‑word transcript* of proceedings, they **cannot** rely on an empty “we are still investigating” statement without backup. The court in *Farahi* emphasized that “the *vague nature* of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may *not* suffice” (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1098). The agency must **anchor the claim** with either:

1. **A documented, specific, or at least highly detailed description** of the investigative activity; or  
2. **A credible recommendation** from an agent or supervisor that the investigation is not yet closed, coupled with any available internal commentary on why disclosure would interfere.

The Bender declaration, by itself, would likely have been deemed *conclusory* and insufficient. The court held that, in this case, it functioned as a *confirmation* of pre‑existing evidence, not a *new, standalone* justification.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for future FOIA plaintiffs

| Issue | Requirement | Practical Take‑away |
|-------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Evidence of pending proceedings** | A verifiable, specific assertion of ongoing law‑enforcement activity | A concise statement that investigations are “ongoing” is not enough; supplement with dates, names, or descriptions of the investigation’s scope. |
| **Potential interference** | Demonstrate *how* release could harm the proceeding (e.g., risk of suspect tampering, coercion, or tactical advantage) | Explain the mechanism; a general claim of “could interfere” without explanation is weak. |
| **Segregability** | Provide a *categorical* secret‑portion designation and explain why it cannot be separated from non‑exempt detail | Use clear “categories” and, if needed, provide a logical scheme that separates exempt and non‑exempt material. |
| **Deference** | Respect the agency’s reasonable determination, but insist on a *reasonably specific* basis | A request for the agency’s “reaffirmation” can be satisfied only if the agency documents new facts that maintain the pendency of the proceeding. |

The *Farahi* decision therefore clarifies that agencies must move beyond a generic “open investigation” statement. They need to supply a **reasonable, detail‑laden, and credible basis** for both pendency and interference. Plaintiffs may challenge the agency’s claims if the evidence is *conclusory* or *inconsistent* with known facts, but a *frank* yet *well‑grounded* assertion will generally earn deference under Exemption 7(A).
