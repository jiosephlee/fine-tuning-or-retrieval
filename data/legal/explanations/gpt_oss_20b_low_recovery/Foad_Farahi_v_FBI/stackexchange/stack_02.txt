Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How do courts determine whether an investigation is still 'pending or reasonably anticipated,' especially when time has elapsed since the original FOIA request?
Question:
In this case, the Bender declaration was brief and essentially reiterated that the investigation remains ongoing.  Yet Farahi argues that the time lapse casts doubt on the continuation of enforcement proceedings.  What factual evidence must the government provide to satisfy the standard that proceedings are still pending or reasonably anticipated?  Are ongoing investigations automatically considered pending, or is a more detailed, time‑specific analysis required?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI* the court reiterated the standard for FOIA Exemption 7(A): a record may be withheld only if (1) it was *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* and (2) its disclosure could *reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated”* (Id. at 6‑7).  The key issue here is how a court determines that second prong—i.e., what facts the government must supply to show that the proceedings still exist.

---

### 1.  The “pending or reasonably anticipated” test

The courts have consistently held that the *exclusion* of a record is a question of law that must be decided de novo, not merely on a *deference* footing (see *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361; *CREW*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096).  The government bears the burden of showing that a *specific* enforcement proceeding is still pending or that a *prospective* proceeding is reasonably likely (CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096; *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 59).

Critically, the court in *CREW* warned that a *vague* assertion that an investigation “continues” is not enough: “the vague nature of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice to support the invocation of Exemption 7(A)” (CREW, 746 F.3d at 1098).  The citations that *CREW* used, however, were not absolute; courts have allowed a reasonably concise statement to suffice if it is part of a broader factual matrix that establishes the existence of an ongoing investigation.

---

### 2.  What factual evidence is required?

The *Farahi* opinion, following *CREW* and *Juarez*, set out the following **prudential evidentiary standard**:

| Element | What the government must show | How the court treated it in *Farahi* |
|---------|------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Existence of a law‑enforcement investigation** | A clear, factual statement that an investigation of the applicant’s alleged wrongdoing is underway.  The statement may be made by an agent or senior official and does not require exhaustive detail. | The Hardy declaration (public, then ex‑parte) provided such a statement, attesting that the FBI’s investigation was “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism.”  This was deemed adequate (Id. at 50). |
| **Continuity of that investigation** | Evidence that the investigation has not been terminated and that it is still collecting evidence for a potential future prosecution.  Narrow detail is not required, but the statement must be accompanied by context that shows continuity (e.g., updates on case status, ongoing interviews, procurement of new evidence). | The Bender declaration, though brief, was treated as an *update* confirming that the investigation “remains ongoing.”  The court reasoned that the declaration was not meant to restart the claims of pendency but to verify that nothing had changed since the earlier Hardy affidavits (Id. at 131). |
| **Likelihood of a future proceeding** | An indication that the evidence gathered is sufficient to anticipate a prospective criminal case.  The government may simply state that the case is *“concrete”* or that the investigator has a “reasonable expectation” of bringing charges. | The court accepted the Hardy ex‑parte declaration’s description of the investigation’s breadth (ties to foreign terrorist organizations, etc.) as sufficient to conclude that a “concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding” persisted (Id. at 131‑132). |
| **Potential interference** | Demonstration that the release of the information could jeopardize the investigation (e.g., by tipping off suspects or revealing investigative techniques).  The court requires only a *reasonable* expectation of interference, not a *certainty* of damage. | The Hardy declaration explained that disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere” by “tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation.”  The court accepted this as sufficient (Id. at 49). |

Thus, the *government need not provide a detailed, time‑specific chronology*; a reasonably specific attestation of ongoing investigative activity that is part of a larger factual context suffices.  That context can be established in a prior affidavit (Hardy) and then updated (Bender).

---

### 3.  Are ongoing investigations automatically considered “pending”?

No.  An ongoing investigation must be *also* shown to be *reasonably likely* to result in a future prosecutorial proceeding.  The court in *Juarez* emphasized that “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies” (Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59).  If an investigation has “frozen” or if no prosecutorial prospect remains, the pendency argument fails.  In *Farahi*, the government proved that the investigation was still active and that a future case was realistic, thus satisfying the *pending or reasonably anticipated* language.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for a FOIA requester

If you are challenging an Exemption 7(A) claim:

1. **Look for a *preceding* affidavit or declaration that establishes the existence of an ongoing investigation.**  A later update can be seen merely as a confirmation, not a new claim of pendency.
2. **Confirm that the claim of pendency is *part of a continuous factual narrative***.  Courts will not read an isolated, one‑sentence statement as proof that a proceeding is pending; they require that it be integrated with earlier evidence that demonstrates the investigation’s continuity and possible prosecution.
3. **Seek evidence of an anticipated prosecution** (e.g., evidence‑collection status, interviewer statements, or case‑forecasting).  A “reasonable expectation” is adequate, but it should be more than an abstract assertion.

If you cannot find any such contextual evidence—if the record‑keeping officer merely states “the investigation is ongoing” without any backing details—the court will likely deem the Exemption 7(A) claim insufficient and require disclosure of the non‑exempt portions.

---

**In summary:** *Foad Farahi* illustrates that courts accept a reasonably concise affirmation of an ongoing investigation—provided it is set within a broader factual context that shows that the investigation remains in play and that a future prosecution is reasonably likely.  Ongoing investigations are not automatically “pending”; they must be *continuously substantiated* and *connected* to a prospective prosecution for Exemption 7(A) to survive.
