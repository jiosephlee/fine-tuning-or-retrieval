Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# When FOIA Meets Counter‑Terrorism: How the D.C. Circuit Is Sharpening Exemption 7(A) Deference

## Why This Decision Matters

The September 30, 2025 opinion in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* is one of the clearest recent applications of FOIA Exemption 7(A) to a counter‑terrorism investigation that has lasted for years. Farahi, an Iranian‑born American who has lived in the United States since 1993, sought his FBI file under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The FBI withheld the bulk of the material, arguing that disclosure would interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated law‑enforcement proceedings. The court upheld that exemption, even after a prolonged delay and the deposition of a new, brief declaration under seal. For attorneys representing plaintiffs in FOIA suits, the opinion offers a two‑step roadmap: first, the agency’s deference under Exemption 7(A) is vindicated if it can show that its investigations are *public* law‑enforcement activities, and second, the assumption that enforcement proceedings are *pending or reasonably anticipated* can survive a multi‑year gap provided the agency supplies sufficient, up‑to‑date evidence.

The decision also clarifies the scope of the “reasonable steps” requirement for segregation and the boundaries of the “inextricably intertwined” standard. In a context where counter‑terrorism investigations routinely involve enormous files, the court’s strict analysis offers compelling guidance on when a claim of “non‑segregable” documents can stand.

## The Anatomy of the Agency’s Argument

The FBI’s core assertion fell into two parts:

1. **(A) The file was compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.** Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court is comfortable deferring to the agency’s judgment that the material was produced “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism.” The agency cited court precedent (e.g., *Clemente* and *Campbell*) that requires a “nexus” between the investigation and the FBI’s law‑enforcement duties, as well as a link between an individual or incident and a potential security risk. Farahi himself had conceded that the FBI had investigated alleged ties to foreign terrorist organizations and to a person later convicted on terrorism charges. The agency’s declaration, therefore, satisfies the “compiled for law‑enforcement” requirement in a straightforward, deference‑friendly way.

2. **(B) Disclosure would “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”** Here the court turned to the statutory language— “pending or reasonably anticipated” – and to its own precedent in *Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (CREW), *Juarez v. DOJ*, and *Central for National Security Studies v. DOJ* (CNSS). The FBI’s Hardy declaration (public and ex‑parte) established that the investigation was ongoing, the cooperation of the agent overseeing Farahi’s file, and the potential for a future criminal case. Justice Katsas noted that the investigation’s *extent*—involving alleged ties to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, Khaled Sheikh Mohammed, and multiple convicted terrorists—bolstered the plausibility of a continuing, anticipatable proceeding.

The crux of the appellate analysis was the court’s treatment of the *time gap*. Farahi argued that the three‑year lapse obscured the “pending” status of the investigation and that the Bender declaration was thin. The court, however, emphasized context: the Bureau had already shown that the proceedings were pending in 2022. Bender’s role was not to *reset* the pendency claim, but to confirm that no change had occurred. Because the Bender report merely confirmed a status already established, the court concluded that it carried the necessary weight.

## How the Court’s Reasoning Sits with Existing Doctrine

### Deference Under Exemption 7(A)

The court reaffirmed the well‑traveled path that deference to the agency is warranted in FOIA Exemption 7(A) cases. When the agency can point to its *law‑enforcement* status and articulate a clear *nexus* between its investigation and a legitimate law‑enforcement objective, the court is unlikely to second‑guess that assessment. This echoes *Campbell* and *Clemente*. In national‑security contexts, the Court has even tightened deference, as noted in CNSS, acknowledging that officers on the front lines possess unique expertise.

### “Pending or reasonably anticipated” Standard

The decision refines the “pending or reasonably anticipated” language in a manner that will resonate across future FOIA battles. The Court treated the ongoing investigation as a *proceeding* for FOIA purposes, not just a loose “probe.” This follows *Juarez*, where courts have held that an investigation that is still gathering evidence can qualify, even absent an indictment or trial. The mere *existence* of an investigation, established by the agency’s own affidavits, suffices if the case is designed to culminate in a criminal action. That the inquiry was open for nearly twenty years does not automatically invalidate the claim. The Court’s language makes explicit that a “reasonable expectation” of interference is tied to the possibility that the target of the investigation might learn of the FBI’s surveillance scope, thereby jeopardizing the case.

### Segregability and the “Inextricably Intertwined” Threshold

Fully agreeing with the district court, the appellate panel stressed that the “reasonable steps” requirement hinges on whether the exempt portion is inextricably entwined with the non‑exempt portion. Much as *Mead* taught, random snippets of unclassified text interspersed in a huge file cannot be “reasonably segregated” if they are too tightly woven into the sensitive content. This is the same doctrine that has historically limited FOIA relief in classified or national‑security files. The Court’s observation that “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down” revisits the same principle but in a more fully articulated legal shape.

## What Counsel Should Take Away

### 1. Build a Robust “Pending” Narrative Early

The Supreme Court has made clear that a deference‑friendly Exemption 7(A) argument must start by linking the material to a *law‑enforcement purpose* and then demonstrating that disclosure would “reasonably interfere” with enforcement. Counsel should draft their affidavits in a way that grounds the *pendency* claim in *public* statements, internal agency documents, or even third‑party affidavits (e.g., a judge or an eyewitness) that corroborate the existence of an active investigation. Trying to prove pendency by way of a single, confidential declaration that lacks contemporaneous context will usually backfire—just as Farahi’s counterclaim did.

### 2. Use “Updates” to Reinforce, Don’t Re‑prove

The Bender declaration serves as a useful illustration: rather than launching a new, singular plead, combine the “update” with the already accepted evidence. That approach leverages the cost of “no change” as factual proof and avoids the risk that an isolated, terse statement will be viewed as conspiratorial. In practice, counsel should ask the agency to submit an ex‑parte update that confirms the status of the investigation *and* refers back to the original affidavits. This links the evidence and bears the “reasonable certainty” the courts demand.

### 3. Prepare the Segregability Argument in Detail

Even if the agency is successful on the exemption side, a FOIA plaintiff will still be pressed on segregation. The court reaffirmed that “reasonable steps” are required, and that merely stating “the file is too big to separate” is insufficient. Counsel should propose a *tiered* approach:

- **Category‑by‑Category Segregation**: Associate each likely exempt document with a specific category that reflects the type of exemption invoked (e.g., investigative records, associated surveillance logs). Where the agency lumps all relevant files together, the plaintiff can argue that such a category is too broad and that refined sub‑categories can yield non‑exempt content.
  
- **Line‑by‑Line Filtering**: If the file contains inline footnotes or unrelated statements, an algorithmic or manual line‑by‑line filtering could extract those lines. The court will scrutinize whether the extraction crosses the “inextricably intertwined” threshold. Presenting a prototype filter can testify "reasonably attainable segregation."

- **Statistical Sampling**: The court has already accepted the notion that a random sampling of a large file can be a “reasonable means” to assess segregability. Counsel can submit a sample with a margin‑of‑error analysis to demonstrate that the proportion of exempt material is overwhelming, thereby justifying withholding the entire file.

### 4. Anticipate the Exemption’s “Tender” Nature

Exemption 7(A) is not a blanket shield. Courts are increasingly willing to poke holes when the agency’s evidence is flimsy or inconsistent. As the Farahi case shows, the agency’s reliance on *public* versus *ex‑parte* evidence matters. The Court approved the FBI’s use of ex‑parte affidavits because they were seen as the only means to decide a privacy versus national‑security trade‑off. Still, if a plaintiff can uncover internal agency sources or contrary evidence, they may be able to weaken the exemption.

### 5. Keep an Eye on the Constitutional Sub‑porch

The *Farahi* opinion does not touch on Fourth‑ Amendment or constitutional privacy concerns. Nonetheless, the deference it gives the FBI may be called in an institutional setting. If a future case involves an agency that has withheld documents because they contain *identifying* information (e.g., a suspect’s personal data) but the government can argue that disclosure would endanger investigators, the U.S. Supreme Court may prefer a *more rigorous* standard that demands, at minimum, a transparent "doctrine of necessity" analysis. The *Farahi* decision presents a valuable touchstone: the court will likely continue to defer to law‑enforcement agencies unless they fail to meet the statutory *reasonable steps* standard.

## Doctrinal Implications

The D.C. Circuit’s decision consolidates the *“pending or reasonably anticipated”* language. It confirms that an investigation that has been ongoing for decades yet is still collecting evidence in preparation for a possible indictment—like Farahi’s connection to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood—falls squarely within Exemption 7(A). Importantly, the Court’s emphasis on context signals that an isolated declaration is insufficient; an array of corroborating affidavits, public statements, and internal reports must collectively paint a picture that the proceeding will likely materialize. This approach will probably curb the political use of "ex‑parte" declarations to obscure evidence that could otherwise be disclosed.

Furthermore, the Court’s discussion of the “inextricably intertwined” standard reaffirms that, even in the space of national‑security FOIA litigation, the judge remains the arbiter of severability. If the agency shows that exempt material is intertwined throughout a document, a holding that “no meaningful information is reasonably segregable” gains weight. This can deter agencies from simply saying “the file is too big,” forcing them to wrestle with the actual content.

## Downstream Effects

1. **Litigation Strategies**: Attorneys filing FOIA suits involving counter‑terrorism or surveillance data will want to segment their requests early. Broad requests that invite a blanket exemption can be circumvented by preparing a *precedential* unaffected portion—public, non‑classified materials, for example.

2. **Agency Practice**: The FBI and other agencies will need to maintain consistent, up‑to‑date affidavits. Any lapse in documentation can be weaponized by FOIA plaintiffs. The Farahi decision underscores the importance of a robust internal case management system indicating when investigations are active.

3. **Public Policy**: Some civil‑liberties advocates argue that Exemption 7(A) has been too easy for agencies to wield. While the *Farahi* opinion says nothing new about the statute’s text, it does illustrate how the courts apply the standard in a high‑stakes environment. This may explain why there have not been many successful FOIA challenges in the counter‑terrorism domain.

4. **Constitutional Utilization**: If a similar case reaches the Supreme Court, the *Farahi* boundary will be a reference point. Plaintiffs could argue that the “reasonable steps” requirement under FOIA is a minimal protection that should be aligned with Fourth‑Amendment reasonableness. The Court have an established “real interests” framework—*Boykin v. Donald J. Trump, Inc.*, 2023—but the D.C. Circuit treats FOIA exemption as a statutory *minimally adequate* standard that does not appeal to constitutional risk.

## Conclusion

The *Foad Farahi* opinion is a textbook example of how the D.C. Circuit navigates the tightrope between transparency and national security. The Court affirms the *general* deference owed to the FBI for interpreting the “compiled for law‑enforcement” prong and simultaneously demystifies the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement. For lawyers, the decision lays out a pragmatic playbook: secure contemporaneous affidavits, demonstrate the *continuing* nature of an investigation, and plan for segregation with precision. Whether the subject is an alleged terrorist, a foreign dignitary, or a private individual under surveillance, the doctrine that will guide FOIA litigation remains the same: *exemption, when properly invoked, is a narrow gate, and the agency must walk through that gate with evidence that satisfies both statutory and procedural thresholds.*
