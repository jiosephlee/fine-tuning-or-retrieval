Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Time Is of the Essence? Assessing “Pending” or “Reasonably Anticipated” Enforcement Proceedings After Years of Delay

## Why This Case Matters

The Federal Bureau of Investigation’s refusal to hand over the bulk of Foad Farahi’s file raises a question at the heart of the Freedom‑of‑Information Act (FOIA): how much time can elapse before a law‑enforcement investigation ceases to qualify for Exemption 7(A)’s blanket protection? When the DOJ or FBI is allowed to withhold the *entire* contents of a file because it believes disclosure could interfere with *pending* or *reasonably anticipated* proceedings, the public’s right to see those records hangs in the balance. If the standard for “pending” is too porous, privacy‑savvy agencies win; if it’s too strict, the public’s watchdog role weakens. In *Foad Farahi v. FBI* the D.C. Circuit grappled exactly with that balance, affording the agency room to use an ex‑parte declaration to confirm that investigations had not changed after a three‑year interval.

Farahi’s FOIA request wrapped up in a mass of 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Years of delays stretched between the initial submission, government reviews, and the ultimate denial of the request. The central issue: does a three‑year lapse in time *automatically* break the “pendency” of an investigation, or can an agency reasonably assert that the case remains “ongoing” so that Exemption 7(A) still applies? The answer has ripple effects—from how the Department of Homeland Security negotiates de‑classifications in immigration contexts, to how intelligence agencies weigh the tension between secrecy and accountability in counter‑terrorism work.

## The Court’s Approach: Straight‑Line Deference and a Pragmatic Timeline

Judge Katsas’s opinion follows the standard testing ground in FOIA law—a de‑novo review of the agency’s burden test, with deference toward a specialized law‑enforcement agency. The court particularly leans on two earlier D.C. Circuit decisions: *Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Washington v. DOJ* (“CREW”) and *Mapother v. DOJ*. CREW teaches that EX 7(A) requires *ongoing enforcement proceedings*: “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court acknowledged CREW’s caution that a vaguely asserted “ongoing” status could be insufficient, but ultimately treated the FBI’s declarations as a coherent narrative that the investigations persisted through 2023.

Katsas ordered the FBI to produce a supplemental declaration after the district court deemed its initial one “seemingly” sufficient but possibly stale. The agency complied in 2022 by providing a *public* declaration by Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender, confirming that the investigations “remained ongoing.” Importantly, the court took the *public* and *ex‑parte* (sealed) declarations together as evidence that the investigation had not degenerated into a dormant file. The court emphasized that these declarations were *updates* rather than *restarts*: the FBI had already proven pendency in earlier submissions.

When the court had to decide on the *segregability* issue, it gave weight to the Bender filing – which, though terse, was supplemented by earlier, more detailed affidavits. The court noted that a single–size document that is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material can be withheld entirely. The decision was therefore light‑handed on segregation, focusing on whether any *meaningful* non‑exempt portion could be isolated. The court found none.

## A Critical Assessment of the Timing Analysis

The crux of Farahi’s argument rests on the *time* element. CREW warned that an assertion of “ongoing” status might be “conclusory” and lacking in facts. Katsas largely sidestepped that concern, relying on the chain of prior affidavits and the mystery “nothing had materially changed” from 2019 to 2023. While the court correctly notes that once an investigation is underway, it can be *reasonably* anticipated to progress, it offers scant guidance on *how many years* can elapse before an initial claim is considered stale.

This is an intentional structural choice. The FOIA inquiry is not a statute that defines “pending” with a numeric cap. Thus, a court must recognize that the *nature* of a particular investigation, its complexity, the legal framework governing it, and the historical clerical practice all factor into what “pending” means. Katsas’s reliance on the FBI’s institutional knowledge—“the investigators developing the case, the gathering of evidence” —suggests a more *fact‑driven* rather than *time‑driven* test. The assumption is that there is a continuous chain of event that keeps the case alive, even if no formal charges are filed today.

One might argue that the 3‑year interval allowed for a **“reasonable anticipation”** horizon. The court noted that as long as the investigation was still gathering evidence and had the potential to culminate in a criminal case, Exemption 7(A) applies. By that logic, a transcript of an advisory meeting with a suspect in 2022 could still prevent opponents from gleaning evidence, depending on the sensitivity of the information. This approach offers flexibility but invites litigation in later cases where the “ongoing” flag sits on a dust‑bowl of information dating back a decade. In such contexts, courts may have to reconcile the FOIA’s openness with the *tenets of due process* that provide an interested party with a chance to see evidence.

## Do We Agree With the Court?

Yes, but only if the standard is *not* a hard time‑limit. The court’s deference to the FBI and its insistence on a holistic review, integrating earlier affidavits with current updates, sits in line with the principle that agencies should not be overwhelmed by an all‑or‑nothing FOIA refusal. Farahi’s own NTA (non‑disclosure agreement) in the immigration context shows that the FBI had been studying his background for *nearly three decades*. The central question is whether that study **is** a *pending enforcement proceeding* in the sense of the statute, and the court leans that it is.

The court did not throw a definitive answer to “how long can an investigation remain pending” out of the legal pipeline. That remains an open issue, ripe for a *higher* court to resolve. For now, the D.C. Circuit’s approach is to presume an investigative thread continues as long as the agency has not expressed a categorical termination, especially when the content is so *deeply* tied to national security.

## Doctrinal Implications

1. **Deference to Agencies**: The decision reinforces the *statutory deference* afforded to the FBI under § 552(a)(4)(B). It suggests that a FOIA claimant must provide detailed evidence of termination or decision to close an investigation, rather than rely on the passage of years alone.

2. **Segregability**: The ruling affirms that *not every* FOIA request that yields a “mixed bag” of data can be satisfied by piecemeal release if the exempt portions are *inextricably intertwined* with the non‑exempt portions. The assumption that a small amount of “interspersed” data can’t be segregated draws attention to the *semantic* challenge of FOIA’s *information* focus, re‑affirming Mead’s principle that the record’s *meaning* matters over literal word‑level “clips.”

3. **Temporal Edge Cases and the “Reasonably Anticipated” Clause**: The case keeps the door open to a *fact‑based* test rather than a *time‑based* cutoff. That could prompt future FOIA scholars to examine whether *historic investigation timelines* should be standard or whether the *enforcement proceedings* truly take the shape of the legal context (e.g., a serial offender intelligence gathering program versus a singular homicide investigation).

## Potential Downstream Effects

- **Intelligence Community**: In the intelligence arena, investigators have a longer shelf‑life to groom evidence. The Farahi decision could embolden agencies to maintain internal “pending” flags for longer, knowing FOIA courts will be hesitant to fracture the chain of evidence just because a battery of materials has been stored for years.

- **Immigration Enforcement**: The decision dovetails with the DOJ’s preference for maintaining extensive FBI files on alleged terrorists even when the immigration process is stalled by appeals or administrative delay. Law‑enforcement agencies might therefore keep their applicant files “open” for a longer period than previously anticipated, citing the FOIA’s “pending” criterion.

- **Litigation Strategies**: Plaintiffs will be encouraged to seek *robust* evidence that their “pending” status has been *terminated*. Courts may require a *public* declaration that the investigation was officially closed in a timeframe as specific as “within the last fiscal year” to defeat an Exemption 7(A) claim.

- **Policy Recommendations**: The lack of an explicit timeframe points to a governance gap. A middle‑ground could be a *statutory* *“no more than five years”* policy that preserves the *threshold* for “reasonable anticipation” without unduly limiting the investigative capacity of agencies.

## Final Thought

Farahi’s FOIA battle underscores the tension between national security’s need to retain long‑term investigative records and the public’s right to post‑humous accountability. The D.C. Circuit’s decision affirms that what matters is *whether an investigation is actively gathering evidence*, not the ticking of a calendar. Until Congress does something different, the courts will likely continue to defer to agencies’ reasoned “ongoing” statements, as long as those statements rest on a solid evidentiary footing. The question, then, is not whether a three‑year lapse is enough to extinguish pending status, but whether the agency can convincingly show that its investigations remain *intrinsically alive**. In practice, that seems to be the standard far from the legislative bench.
