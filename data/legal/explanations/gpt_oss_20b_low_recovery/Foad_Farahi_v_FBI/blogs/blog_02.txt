Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Segregability at the Crosshairs: The Fine Line Between “Reasonably Segregable” and “Inextricable” Under FOIA

## Why the Farahi Case Matters

A good FOIA complaint rarely ends with a simple “Open–Closed” decision. More often the tension lies in the *segregability* question: can a government withdraw a bulk of documents as exempt, only to peel off a handful of non‑exempt nuggets for release? In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit reaffirmed the federal government’s power to withhold “reasonably uncut” records under Exemption 7(A), but it also clarified how agencies must justify that excise. The case is a sharp reminder that entire swaths of motivation, “intractable” mixing, and ex‑parte affidavits can conspire to defeat a FOIA seeker’s best hopes.

At first glance the fact pattern is straightforward: Farahi, an Iranian national subject to removal proceedings, sought his FBI file on potential terrorism ties. The FBI stopped short of disclosing 10 750 pages and 80 CDs, claiming Exemption 7(A) because its investigative activity was “ongoing.” The district court, drawing on the agency’s affidavits, found that the exempt portion was not reasonably segregable. The appeals court affirmed.  What hangs in the balance is how the court applied the *Mead* and *Perioperative* segregation doctrines, and how much weight it gave to the FBI’s ex‑parte statements.

The takeaway? Court‑ready FOIA litigants must read the *Mead* test not as a stream of technicalities, but as a barometer of the agency’s actual practice. They must also anticipate that the part of the record that mediates between "exempt" and "non‑exempt" is often buried under the very material the agency refuses to expose.

## The “Mead” Foundation: Exemption Magnets

In *Mead Data Cent., Inc.*, the D.C. Circuit laid out a pragmatic “puzzle‑piece” test: a record can be withheld in part if the segregated and withheld pieces are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt portions. The court emphasized the everyday reality that documents often contain a patchwork of public, privileged, and secret information. The test can be as simple as “Are the exempt and non‑exempt words literally interspersed?” or as sophisticated as “Does separating a paragraph from a report ruin the context that justifies the exemption?”

Later decide that the FBI’s request–citizenness which presumably included a massive stack of interrogation transcripts, surveillance logs, and written statements is exactly the sort of dense “interleaving” that *Mead* fears. The court was comfortable that the FBI’s categorical withdrawals were not “overly broad,” because each category—such as “investigative notes,” “interrogation transcripts,” “surveillance footage”—aligned with the nature of the content, and the real‑world evidence indicated that the categories failed to neatly separate exempt from non‑exempt content.

In practice, *Mead* is a hurdle of lower degree: if the record contains a line or paragraph that can be removed without stripping it of meaning, the agency can’t just shrug and tuck the whole thing away. The limiting factor is the *conferral of context.* If the word “Kalim” appears on a document that, on its own, might not reveal any incriminating evidence, but the surrounding material names literal threats or scrolling logs, the government can claim that any proliferation of that word must stay together. This approach tends to favor the agency when the record is essentially “one big spreadsheet” of raw data.

## The “Perioperative” Shift: Vagueness and Vagueness

In *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs*, the court expanded the *Mead* framework. It recorded that, in some cases, the fiscal or statutory justification for an agency’s decision should be the same that one would give in a *categorical withdrawal*, but *“reasonable judgment”* about the **extent** of the exempt material mattered more specifically. The “vagueness” clause was a key pivot. The court said it is unlikely that a *“small amount of non‑exempt information” is randomly “interspersed line‑by‑line” throughout the document* would survive a *reasonable segregation* test.

Farahi’s attorneys tried to point to that detail: if one can conceive of a single paragraph of a largely classified email, could that paragraph be pulled loose? The court denied that attack on the basis that the FBI’s affidavit was more than a simple “yes/no.” It noted that even a “small” line of non‑exempt content that is embedded near a large chunk of the sensitive material may not be “reasonably segregable” because removing it would leave a hole that would defeat the secrecy argument. In short: *Perioperative* tightened the bounds on the *Mead* test by rooting the decision in the **practicalities** of segregating material *before* the court has to peek at it.

The effect is clear: the lawyer must provide *more granular* evidence of segregation—like a *token excerpt* of the supposedly non‑exempt portion—to meet *Perioperative*’s heightened scrutiny. In *Farahi*, the DOJ’s affidavits promised to locate and segregate “confidential” entries, but the court found the justification naively vague.

## Ex‑Parte Affidavits: A Double‑Edged Sword

FOIA is notorious for allowing agencies to hold back information by renouncing to disclose documents that could tip off their investigations. In *Fredericksburg* and the *Katsas* decision‑maker, the burden for courts is to find the *right* level of “reasonable specificity” in the agency’s assertions. The lower courts have accepted that the *FBI* can provide *ex‑parte* filings for the *sake* of preserving investigatory leads. But the *ex‑parte affidavits* are usually kept from public scrutiny, meaning the court’s eye is unblinded for the recorder’s direction.

In *Farahi*, the court floated the “Supplemental Bender Declaration” – an ex‑parte supplement that simply affirmed “ongoing investigations.” The dissenters argued that a single climactic line is not enough to find an investigative pendency under 7(A). The majority thought otherwise: the government had previously already provided a detailed *Hardy* declaration showing an ongoing investigation of terrorism. Thus the Bender exce⟂ sent an “update” rather than a *new* story. In effect, the court passed “realist deference” to the agency.

The caution to litigants is this: ex‑parte affidavits are not a fallback for the bar to litigate; they are a premium. As a FOIA veteran argues, a good case may—if you’re lucky—achieve segregation *without* the need for an ex‑parte statutory justification. That requires heads‑up on why the agencies are leaving “non‑exempt” elements because they could uncover forms (e.g., inconsistencies) that undermine the investigative narrative. If the lawyer believes the *ex‑parte* is a red flag, bring it to court’s light in remedial form—for instance, request the agency to produce a *sample of cut‑back* documents to prove their claim that no meaningful content remains.

## “Open, Closed, or Partial” in Practice

Electricity is about voltage. FOIA claims revolve around the more nuanced notion of *“borrowed context.”* That concept is most vividly displayed in *Mead* and *Perioperative*. In *Mead*, a partially extraneous text can remain attached if its omission would ultimately lose context for the entire case. Think of a logbook: a single line “beginning at 1:45 AM” is stripped, but its absence breaks a chain of evidence for the agency’s claim. The *Perioperative* doctrine says: the textual “has to be an “independent segment” that can be scratched off like gum from a photo.

What does that look like in practice? A plaintiff might take the entire 10 750‑page incident file, isolate a *section* that mentions “patient names” or “public addresses”, cross‑check that the agency’s “copied lines” are not actually “cross‑referenced to a security dossier.” If friction exists—i.e., the line can’t be taken in isolation because the surrounding page includes a call sign of a secretive operation—then the agency is white‑labeled. But if the line says “Name: John Doe; Address: 123 Main; Date: 6/4/2009" and no other part of the document references John Doe elsewhere, the plaintiff could argue segregation approved’s identity claim.

In *Farahi*, plaintiff offered a generic category (“non‑interview records”) but the court saw no verifiable sample. Had the plaintiff filed an excerpt such as “Personal Statement: I was a tall man…”, the agency might have been forced to segregate an additional paragraph that potentially contradicted its characterization, strengthening the FOIA defense.

## The Take‑Home: What Litigants Need to Do

- **Sample‑Mandatory**: If you suspect the agency’s categorical withdrawal is too broad, demand that they produce a non‑exempt excerpt the exact same way it appears in the larger file. Without that, *Perioperative* grants the government the mantle of “inextricability.”

- **Mind the Ex‑Para**: Ex‑parte affidavits are a “black‑box” incentive. If the government depends on them heavily, you must challenge their sufficiency. Ask for *rational specificity*: Not just “the investigation was ongoing.” Seek “specific incident(s) under investigation” and “responsible detectives.” If their answer remains a generic “ongoing,” you might push for a *public* counterpart and a court‑prescribed “segregation testing.”

- **Time Stamp is a Weapon**: Exemption 7(A)’s “pending or reasonably anticipated” hinge on more than a dated letter. If the agency’s last affidavit goes back 2 years, a FOIA judge might creep under the *CREW* “vague” doctrine. Bring fresh evidence: e.g., mention that a protest or grant is still pending, that indictments are being drawn, or that a witness is on the witness list.

- **Future‑Proofing**: In a world of Electronic Records, it is rare that a record contains pure “public trophies” and “secret crimes.” In all likelihood, a library is an *inextricable* bundle. The best defense is to move early, request an *intermediate* request for only the *public portions* of a file. If the government shows that *any* part of the record is potentially usable, you can force it to segregate a smaller low‑stakes subset.

## Do Not Forget the Governing Doctrine

The *Mead* and *Perioperative* tests are not empty sins. They reflect a back‑and‑forth between statutory freedom of information and the practicalities of investigative secrecy. In *Farahi*, the FCDC's majority recognized that the first principle—agency deference—is vital; the second principle—conciseness of determination—should not be forgotten. The court’s final swing of the gavel was to carefully weigh the FBI’s internal tracking versus the FOIA plaintiff’s rights *and* to consciously grant the agency a broad "inextricably intertwined" predicate in the final analysis. 

What does that mean for the future? Those who attempt to claw away a record under § 552(b)(7)(A) will likely find that the line between compliance and violation is narrower when the agency presents a *categorical* withdrawal. The statutory guard rails set by *Mead* and *Perioperative* become more than an academic brain‑teaser: they are a litmus test for how FOIA will continue to shine the light or the shadows on our nation’s law‑enforcement processes.
