Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

The dispute in *Santos v. Kimmel* centers on a series of license‑to‑use agreements made through the online performance‑request platform Cameo and the subsequent use of the resulting videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (JKL).  The issue arose when former congressman George Santos requested that Kimmel produce short personalized videos for fans.  Kimmel fulfilled the requests, each time creating a short clip that repeated Santos’s own words and slogans, and Kimmel then aired those clips on JKL as part of a segment titled “Will Santos Say It?”  The segment was intended as a comedic commentary on Santos’s public persona and the “absurd” statements he had made while in office.

## Facts of the Cameo Transaction and Kimmel’s Use of the Videos

Santos signed up for a Cameo account and posted a request asking Kimmel to make a “short video” that would “spread positivity and hope” using phrases such as “I can’t believe it’s true.”  Kimmel:

1.  Agreed to the request and filmed a five‑minute video in which he echoed these phrases.
2.  Delivered the video to Santos via the Cameo platform on the same day it was filmed.
3.  Re‑uploaded the video to JKL as a segment of the “Will Santos Say It?” parody series.  The segment included a brief introduction in which Kimmel noted that “Santos has promised to say absurd things for money,” before playing the full clip of Santos’s own words.

Santos claimed that Kimmel had used the videos without permission and that the use had deprived him of the commercial benefits of his own speech.  He also alleged that Kimmel had made false statements that constituted fraud.  Santos objected that the Cameo Terms of Service (ToS) granted Kimmel some contractual rights that protected him from lawsuits that Kimmel later filed against him.  Santos brought a federal copyright infringement claim and state‑law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.

## Parties

| Party | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **George Santos** | Former congressman, plaintiff, claimant of copyright ownership and route for state‑law claims. |
| **Jimmy Kimmel** | Defendant, host of *JKL*, creator of the videotaped content. |
| **American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (ABC)** | Defending party that owns the JKL platform, and the immediate broadcast venue for Kimmel’s segments. |
| **The Walt Disney Company** | Parent company of ABC, also named in the complaint because the broadcast was ultimately performed under its corporate umbrella. |

The suit was filed in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (SDNY), where the district judge was the Honorable Cote.

## District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) Dismissal and Grounds

The SDNY judge dismissed the complaint in its entirety under Rule 12(b)(6) – that is, the pleading stage – after a review of the complaint, posted exhibits, and cited registrations.  The dismissal rested on two pillars:

1. **Copyright Infringement – Fair Use**  
   The court applied the four‑factor test of § 107.  It found that Kimmel’s use was *transformative* because the videos were employed for “commentary, criticism, and parody.”  The court also concluded that the purpose factor, the nature of the original works (publicly spoken statements), the amount used (complete videos), and the market effect were all consistent with a fair‑use defense.  Thus, the court held that the copyright claims were barred at the pleading level.

2. **State‑Law Claims – Preemption**  
   The court rejected the breach‑of‑contract and fraudulent‑inducement claims.  For the contract claims, the court said that the claims were “preempted by the Copyright Act” because they hinged on alleged wrongful attribution of ownership of the copyrighted material.  For the fraud claim, the court found that the complaint did not allege a specific out‑of‑pocket loss, a requirement for a New York fraud action.  Consequently, the state‑law claims failed to state a claim for relief.

Because the judge abandoned the entire complaint, there was no trial, and the case advanced directly to the appellate court.

## Appellate Review Standards: De Novo and Permissible Supplemental Evidence

The Second Circuit reviewed the dismissal *de novo* in accordance with *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010).  The appellate court looked only at the facts alleged in the complaint, the exhibits actually on record, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.  It may also take judicial notice of certain facts such as the existence of a copyright registration, which must be hosted in government records.

The court reiterated that it may still consider arguments that were not raised in the complaint if they are linked to facts that are.  However, it could not discuss new substantive issues or try to re‑evaluate the court’s conclusions on the merits beyond the scope of the pleadings.  The appeal was limited to whether the district court’s dismissal was legally sound under the standards above.

# 2. Copyright Infringement and the Fair‑Use Defense

The district court’s dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims rested on an application of the four‑factor fair‑use framework. In this chapter we examine how the Second Circuit reviewed that conclusion, why the court found the use “transformative,” and how it treated the defendant’s bad‑faith accusations.

## Statutory Framework: U.S.C. § 107 and the Four Fair‑Use Factors

Section 107 of the Copyright Act permits a court to refuse liability for an infringement “in cases of a non‑commercial or a commercial use that can be justified as a fair use” under the following factors:

1. **Purpose and character of the use** – Whether the use is commercial or non‑commercial, and whether the new work is transformative.
2. **Nature of the copyrighted work** – The more creative the original, the stronger the copyright protection.
3. **Amount and substantiality** – The quantity and quality of the portion used relative to the whole.
4. **Effect on the market** – Whether the new work usurps the market or would harm the original’s commercial value.

The test is *non‑exclusive*; courts weigh the factors together, not against one another. The Second Circuit, following the Supreme Court’s precedent in *Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive*, reiterates that fair use is flexible and fact‑specific. A court may dismiss an infringement action under Rule 12(b)(6) if the fair‑use defense “is so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” (*TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*).

## Analysis of Purpose/Character: Transformative Use Principles and Prior Cases

### The Transformative Standard

In *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts v. Goldsmith*, the court held that the purpose and character factor “focus[es] chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative, i.e., whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” The Second Circuit treats transformation as the yardstick for “purpose and character” because once a use is truly transformative, the remaining factors become almost inevitable.

### Applying Transformation to the Kimmel Segments

Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segments aired the videos Santos had made for him on Cameo, but the segments were edited, inserted into a broader satirical context, and accompanied by the host’s commentary. The court concluded that a reasonable observer would perceive this as a “submenu of humor, criticism, and mockery” rather than a direct copy or faithful reproduction. Even though Kimmel “instigated” the creation of the videos, the defendant was a *designer* of a *new* purpose—commentary on Santos’s willingness to perform absurd acts for money. The court rejected Santos’s claim that the purpose of the videos was the same as that of the original work, emphasizing that “the subjective intent of the user does not determine the purpose of the use” (*Andy Warhol*).

### The “Coffee‑House” Criterion

The court alluded to the “coffee‑house” test from *Encyclopedia of Ethics* as a heuristic for transformation: an excerpt that was “publicly available and used for allowable purposes in a coffee house or other public venue." While the court did not cite the article by name, it pointed to the idea that brief, publicly shared snippets for commentary fall within fair use. Kimmel’s broadcasts meet this criterion because the videos were publicly aired on national television and utilized in a broader context of public commentary.

### TCA Television and the “Designer” Exception

*TCA Television v. McCollum* held that a show that “designed” its own material is not impacted by a pre‑existing work if the new creation is markedly different. The Second Circuit reaffirmed that Kimmel’s role as a “designer” of the new segment created an entirely different purpose, thereby supporting the transformation finding.

## Nature of the Work, Amount Used, and Market Impact Factors as Applied to the Videos

### Nature of the Original Work

The recordings were short, informal videos produced by a former congressman. The Second Circuit treated them as “creative works” but noted that their functional, performative nature made the *nature* factor neutral in favor of fair use.

### Amount and Substantiality

The court considered that the videos were “published on the same day they were created” (copyright registration evidence), suggesting that they were short and non‑substantial. Even if the entire video was used, the court noted that the portion integrated into the televised segment was proportionate to the overall material and served a new purpose.

### Market Impact

The defendant’s use was deemed “non‑substantive” with respect to Santos’s market. Because the videos were broadcast publicly as satire, they did not serve as a commercial substitute for the original content. The court found no evidence that the segments undermined Santos’s market for the videos. The fourth factor therefore weighed in favor of fair use, echoing *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music*’s observation that “criticism or parody rarely competes with the market for the original.” 

## Bad‑Faith and “Good‑Faith” Considerations: Harper & Row, Swatch, Authors Guild

### Harpers & Row and Good‑Faith

The plaintiff argued that Kimmel’s false claims about the authenticity of the videos demonstrated bad faith, thus nullifying fair use. *Harper & Row v. Nation* stated that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but the Second Circuit pointed out that the Supreme Court later distanced this statement from its binding authority. Without independent precedent establishing a *prima facie* link between bad faith and a total defeat of fair use, the court declined to consider the defendants’ alleged deceit an obstacle to the fair‑use analysis.

### Swatch and the Absence of “Commercial Supplant”

In *Swatch Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg*, the court held that lack of commercial coercion or market supplant implied a non‑commercial intent. The fact that Kimmel had no financial incentive to produce the videos, beyond the narrative of satire, reinforced the court’s view that the use was not motivated by a desire to devalue Santos’s original works.

### Authors Guild and Commentary

*Authors Guild v. Google* clarified that criticism and commentary are core fixtures of fair use. The court applied this precedent to the Kimmel segments, treating them as legitimate commentary rather than an act of commercial exploitation.

## Conclusion: Fair Use Bars Infringement at the Pleading Stage

By scrutinizing each factor—understanding that transformation is the linchpin—the Second Circuit found that Santos’s copyright claim had failed even before a motion to dismiss. The defendant’s use of the videos in a comedic, critical context was transformative, the original works were not replaced or commercialized, and no market harm was identified. Thus, the district court properly dismissed the infringement claim under Rule 12(b)(6), and the Second Circuit affirmed.

# 3. State‑Law Claims: Breach and Fraudulent Inducement

The Court denied Santos’ state‑law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.  In this chapter we trace the Court’s reasoning on the contractual claims—how Illinois and New York law treat third‑party benefits and implied‑in‑fact agreement—and then examine the fraudulent‑inducement claim, focusing on New York’s requirement that a plaintiff allege an actual out‑of‑pocket loss.  The analysis is confined to the facts, procedural posture, and holdings contained in the opinion.

## 3.1 Breach of Contract and Breach of Implied Contract

Santos alleged that Kimmel and ABC breached the Cameo Terms of Service by using videos Santos produced on the platform and airing them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*.  The Timed Crowds situation demands that the court consider whether the Terms of Service extend liability to a non‑party such as Santos.  The Court applied the Illinois doctrine that **non‑contracting third parties enjoy no benefit unless the contract expressly provides otherwise**.  This doctrine is grounded in *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.*, 68 N.E.3d 964 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016), where the court emphasized a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties” and required an *express declaration* indicating that a third party may enforce a provision.

Santos failed to deliver that declaration.  His complaint identified no terms that, even implicitly, applied to him or any other non‑party.  The Terms of Service do contain third‑party‑beneficiary language in other contexts, but those include clear express statements of intent.  Because Santos did not allege *any* express provision applying to him, the Illinois test precludes enforcement.

Even if the parties had agreed to a contract that bound Santos, *Caniglia v. Ch. Trib.* requires the complaint to allege **essential terms** of that contract.  A complaint that merely states a vague claim of breach is insufficient.  *Rocky Point Props.* (NY) extends this idea: to establish an implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law, the plaintiff must plead **demonstrable assent**—the “meeting of minds”—on the specific provisions that the conduct allegedly violated.  Santos’ pleading did not provide that level of detail.  Thus the court dismissed the breach‑of‑contract and breach‑of‑implied‑contract claims on pleading‑stage grounds without addressing the merits.

The Court specifically noted that Santos’ claims “fail to state a claim for relief under state law.”  The absence of an expressed contractual obligation for a third party and the lack of alleged assent meant that the Court had no basis to proceed with the substantive analysis of breach.

## 3.2 Fraudulent Inducement: Loss Requirement

The second state‑law claim that survived was fraudulent inducement.  Under New York law, to bring a claim for fraudulent inducement the plaintiff must allege: (1) a misrepresentation known to be false, (2) intent to induce reliance, (3) reliance by the plaintiff, and (4) **an actual out‑of‑pocket loss** *or* some other tangible detriment that is causally connected to the misrepresentation.  See *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996).  The court found that Santos did not address the fourth element.  His complaint did not describe any financial or other loss directly tied to the alleged fraud.  The absence of such a loss allegation renders the claim defective.

The Court cited *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill*, 29 N.Y.3d 137 (2017), as an example of a case where the court dismissed the claim for the same reason.  Because the requirement to plead an actual loss is *fundamental* to a fraudulent‑inducement claim, Santos’ failure to do so meant the claim could not survive a 12(b)(6) dismissal.  

In sum, the Court saw no grounds to hold the defendants liable for breach of contract—whether express or implied—because the contract never extended to Santos, and the fraudulent‑inducement claim failed to allege the statutory loss element.  Consequently, all state‑law claims were dismissed, and the Court affirmed the District Court’s ruling.

# 4. Integration of Analysis and Holding

The appellate court’s task in this case was twofold: first, to decide whether the pleadings contained an arguable claim for copyright infringement; second, to determine whether the state‑law claims that Santos alleged could be sustained. The opinion’s structure mirrors this dual objective. At the outset, the court performs a de novo review of the dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) and gives judicial notice of the factual basis needed to evaluate fair use. It then follows a logical sequence—geared by the four statutory factors of fair use—to decide that the Division’s denial was correct. Finally, it affirms the district court’s dismissal of the state‑law claims on the basis that they either were preempted or failed to state a claim. The following sections explain how each of these elements is brought together.

## De Novo Review and Permissible Judicial Notice of Copyright Registrations

Under the Second Circuit precedent of *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, the appellate court may review a district‑court ruling under Rule 12(b)(6) de novo. The court re‑examines “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” Consequently, the lower court’s factual findings are not binding; the appellate court can reach different conclusions based on what is plausibly alleged. This standard is particularly important in copyright cases, where the pleadings themselves often contain the core data needed to assess fair use.

Santos’ complaint presented the copyright registrations of the six cameo videos he created for Kimmel. *Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.* and *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum* authorize the court to take judicial notice of those registrations, as they are items published in the Copyright Office’s registry. By doing so, the appellate court confirms that the videos constituted published, copyrighted works. These facts enable the court to address the four statutory factors without needing a formal expert or additional evidence. The court’s reliance on judicial notice clarifies that the appellate panel can, within the confines of the complaint, assess fair use entirely de novo.

## Logical Flow from Facts to Fair‑Use Determination

Once the factual ground is established, the opinion applies the four-metric framework of 17 U.S.C. § 107. The first factor—purpose and character—receives the most substantive analysis. The court treats the purpose question as a matter of transformativity, a term the Second Circuit uses to capture whether a new work merely supersedes the original or adds new value. Citing *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith* and *Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive*, the court explains that the judge’s assessment should be grounded in actual observable impact, not the plaintiff’s hope or subjective intent. The complaint’s own language, which frames the cameo videos as “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” illustrates that the videos are a commentary on Santos rather than his attempt to produce a commercially-derived substitute. Because Kimmel’s use was clearly “insignificant” (the first factor awardee), the court finds it strongly suggestive of transformative use.

The second factor—the nature of the copyrighted work—is less contentious. The videos were creative and expressive, which the fair‑use doctrine traditionally weighs against infringement. Yet, as the opinion notes, the weight of this factor is secondary when balanced against a transformative purpose.

The third factor—amount and substantiality—focuses on the core content of the videos. The court discusses how, despite their publication on the same day, the plaintiff’s complaint clarifies that no portion of the videos was copied without permission. The analysis acknowledges that fair use protects the plaintiff’s use of the entire work, not merely a selective excerpt, but that the entire work was transformed into a new context (satirical commentary). Thus the factor does not weigh heavily against the court’s conclusion.

The fourth factor examines effect on market value. Crucially, *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc.* is cited to emphasize that if a secondary use does not serve as a competing substitute for the original, it cannot be considered a market injury. The court affirms that the cameo videos were not marketed to the same audience the original videos might have been, and that Santos’ own complaint provides no evidence that the use adversely affected the market for the original works. Therefore, even this factor fails to defeat the fair‑use defense.

The court brings these analytical strands together to decide that, even at the pleading stage, fair use applies. It avoids any “rubber‑stamp” on the dismissal and demonstrates how the application of the statutory framework aligned with the details of the case.

## Reaffirmation of District Court’s Dismissal and Conclusion of Appeal

Having concluded that fair use bars the copyright claims, the opinion moves to the state‑law claims. It begins with the breach of contract and breach of implied contract allegations. Here, the court invokes *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.* to support the doctrine that a third party cannot rely on a contract that does not expressly bestow rights upon them. Coupled with *Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Group*, the court finds that Santos neither named an express declaration that the Terms of Service applied to him nor alleged essential terms that would create an implied contract. Hence those claims fail to state a claim under New York law.

The fraudulent‑inducement claim is dismissed on the ground that Santos did not allege any out‑of‑pocket loss, a requirement for a New York fraud claim (see *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*). The court’s strict adherence to the pleading requirements, consistent with the Court’s earlier decision to dismiss the claims for failure to state, leads it to uphold the district court’s ruling.

By tying these disparate analyses into a coherent narrative—first the statutory barrier of fair use, then the procedural non‑merit of the state‑law claims—the court arrives at a single, streamlined conclusion: the district court’s dismissal was proper, and the appeal is denied. The final paragraph, therefore, offers a concise, affirmative summary that “for the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court,” cementing the appellate court’s integration of the factual, statutory, and procedural strands.
