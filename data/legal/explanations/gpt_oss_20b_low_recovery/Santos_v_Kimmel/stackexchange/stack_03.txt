Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### On what grounds are Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claims deemed preempted by the Copyright Act?
Question:
The district court dismissed Santos’s first two state‑law claims claiming they were preempted by the Copyright Act. The appellate court says it does not need to resolve the issue because the claims fail to state a claim. Yet I’m unsure which preemption doctrine—section 202(a) or 302(a)—is at issue and why the court treats it as essentially a deferral. Could someone explain the preemption analysis that applies to contract claims arising from alleged copyright infringement?
Answer:
**Answer**

Santos’s two state‑law breach‑of‑contract claims were dismissed for *preemption* under **U.S.C. § 202(a)**, not § 302(a).  
The court’s reasoning can be distilled as follows:

| § | What it preempts | What the court said |
|---|-----------------|---------------------|
| **302(a)** | State‑law claims that *simply* address the result of infringement (e.g., damages for infringement or a “copyright‑infringement” claim). | The court did **not** invoke this provision. The claims were not “copyright‑infringement” claims; they were *contractual* claims. |
| **202(a)** | State‑law claims that *deal with the very act of reproduction, distribution, or public performance* of a copyrighted work, **or with any issues directly related to a copyright**. | “We first address Santos’s direct breach‑of‑contract claim under the Cameo Terms of Service. ... The District Court dismissed the first two state‑law claims on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act.” The court then considered the policy of § 202(a) and concluded that a claim “dealing with the use of a copyrighted work … is *directly related* to a copyrighted work” and is therefore preempted.

---

### Why § 202(a) applies to contract claims

§ 202(a) reads:

> “No state law shall amend or affect the rights, duties, or liabilities … created by the Copyright Act or the rights that are the product of a common law right to a copyright.”

The court identifies Santos’s claims as “breach of contract” for the *undisclosed, presumably copyrighted material* that Kimmel used (the user‑generated videos). The court is dealing with the *actual use* of the copyrighted material in a defamatory, public‑broadcast context. Because the contract was centered on the *distribution* of a copyrighted work, the court treats the claim as “directly related” to copyright law.

The policy behind § 202(a) is that courts should not have to decide whether a particular use would constitute infringement under the Act *and* still allow a state‑law suit for the same conduct. This eliminates duplicated litigation and preserves the finality of copyright decisions.

---

### Why the court calls the preemption a “deferral”

The opinion states:

> “We need not resolve this issue, *because Santos’s state‑law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law.*”

Thus, the preemption claim is **not the basis of the judgment**. The court uses the preemption analysis only to *justify* the dismissal that results from a failure‑to‑state‑claim objection. In other words, the court’s dismissal hinges on substantive pleading deficiencies—not on the merits of § 202(a). The preemption argument is “deferral” because the court does not actually decide whether the claims would be preempted if they were properly pleaded.

---

### Bottom line for a student

1. **The claims are contract claims, not copyright‑infringement claims**.  
2. **Section 202(a) preempts any state‑law claim that deals directly with the use of a copyrighted work** (or any issue *directly related* to copyright). The court correctly applied § 202(a).  
3. **The court never actually lit the preemption; it simply noted that the claims, if pleaded, would be preempted.** The real reason for dismissal was the failure to plead the essential terms of the alleged contract.  

So, when you see a court dismiss a contract‑based claim for preemption, first check whether the contract allegation concerns the *use* of a copyrighted work. If it does, § 202(a) is likely the doctrine at issue. § 302(a) comes into play only when the claim is a *direct copyright‑infringement* claim, which is not the case here.
