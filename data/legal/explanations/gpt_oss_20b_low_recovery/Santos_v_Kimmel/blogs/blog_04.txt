Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Preemption, Fair Use, and State‑Law Claims: Why the Second Circuit Kept the Copyright Act King

## Why This Case Matters

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit confronted a twice‑delicate legal tightrope: a former congressman, George Santos, sued late‑night host Jimmy Kimmel and the Walt Disney Company for “copyright infringement” after Kimmel aired custom‑made video messages Santos had produced for Cameo subscribers. The district court dismissed Santos’s claims, both on the grounds that fair use barred any copyright infringement claim and that his state‑law contract and fraud claims failed to state a cause of action. The appeals court, who was the one to lock in that reasoning, reaffirmed the dismissal with a decisive nod to the primacy of federal copyright law in this kind of commercial dispute.

This case is a prismatic test of three doctrines that frequently collide in the digital‑media age: (1) the *fair‑use* defense that lets commentators, critics, and parodists flourish; (2) *copyright preemption*, the idea that the Copyright Act can top state‑law remedies; and (3) the traditional *state‑law contract* framework that provides remedies for breach, fraud, and implied‑in‑fact agreements. By confirming that fair use can defeat a copyright claim *even at the pleading stage* and that state‑law claims can be preended by the Copyright Act only where the claims are fanned up, the Second Circuit reaffirmed the hierarchy that keeps the federal copyright ceiling high in market‑oriented disputes. In doing so, it wrought a clear doctrinal signal: federal copyright law will remain the primary shield against “co‑optation” of creative works by those who wish to repackage them under a new banner, even when the plaintiff seeks both federal and state remedy.

## The Fair‑Use Armature – Rehashing the Four‑Factor Dance

The appellate court’s language in the brief is nothing new. But the case pushes the four‑factor test into an arena where the “purpose and character” of a use is clearly transitional: Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segment was a parody and a satire. The court points out that Santos’s own complaint, citing his inclusive tone of “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” made clear a difference between a “self‑styled” moral purpose and a “commercially motivated” use.  This focus aligns with *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* and the Supreme Court’s own acknowledgment that fair‑use determination turns on “what a reasonable observer thinks” rather than the subjective purpose of the user. The Second Circuit leans heavily on these precedents, finding that the transformation—Kimmel using Santos’s intimate videos to make a mocking commentary—is enough to tilt the scales.

In the *fourth factor* discussion, the court treats the absence of market harm as a categorical rebuff to the “potential market impact” argument. This is key: Santos’s complaint did not allege that Kimmel, by publishing the videos, deprived Santos of what a licensing market might have been. The court notes that this silliness is routinely dismissed in parody cases. Not new material, per se, but a *new idea and commentary*—the necessary quality to carve out a fair use shield. In short, the fair‑use armature works as the court expected, and it remains a key engine in protecting second‑tier work that comments on or critiques the original.

## Preemption Fever: Where the Copyright Act Shields the Market

The case’s second core pillar is the doctrine of preemption. In *Melendez v. Sirius XM*, the Second Circuit had already carved out a panel that “copyright infringement claims are not automatically preempted by the Copyright Act” when the underlying claims are qualitatively different. Santos’s claim for breach of contract under the Cameo Terms of Service, and the contractual claims for fraudulent inducement, closely resemble *ordinary contract law* rather than copyright infringement. However, the court found that the claims were *not* founded on the same contractual relationship that the Copyright Act would preempt; the complaint never identified an *express* contractual agreement between Santos and the parties. Without a contract that the parties created for each other, the preemption argument crumbles. 

The appellate court’s decision aligns with the principle that preemption applies *only* when a state‑law claim *depends upon the copyright itself*. In other words, a “breach of the moral or contractual obligations under the Copyright Act” can be preempted, but *ordinary* breach of a *non‑copyright contract* cannot. The court’s stance also reflects the older analysis in *Territory v. Gucci Apparel* (example, not actual case). It shields the uniform application of federal protection while not denying that a non‑copyright contract can be enforced at the state level, albeit subject to statutory limitations. As the court consciously rests on this logic, it provides a useful map for future litigants: if you can prove that the underlying claim is *intent‑to‑infringe*, then preemption is a sure‑fire.

## The Rise, and Limits, of the State‑Law Contract Defense

With the preemption bar notch out of the way, the court still rejuts Santos’s state‑law claims—both breach of contract and fraudulent inducement. Its failure rests on *failure to state* a claim. For a breach of contract claim to survive the pleading stage, the plaintiff must articulate sufficiently clear “essential terms” that constitute a contract (see *Rocky Point Props.*). Santos did not, and the court also found he was no party to the Cameo terms, which a typical third‑party contract prevention doctrine (the “well‑known presumption against benefits to non‑parties”) would hold by default. 

Turning to fraudulent inducement, the court required Santos to allege an *actual loss* at the pleading stage (a standard from *Lama Holding Co.*) and found none. The federal courts have historically framed fraud as an “actual victim” hypothesis in the absence of clear damages, which in many cases leaves the claim in limbo until after discovery or evidence is laid out. In this scenario, the lack of tangible out‑of‑pocket loss, coupled with a simple *commentary and parody* model, effectively wipes out the fraud claim.

In both cases, the ruling indicates that the courts are not willing to use state law to tack on an additional remedy that is redundant or unnecessary because the federal copyright framework already covers the matter. The importance of the *failure to state* motion here is that it underscores how the state‑law system can index itself directly to federal copyright protection yet still maintain its own requirements and fences.


## Doctrinal Ramifications and Future Lines of Inquiry

*Inherit Error with the Copyright Act:*  The court’s decision confirms the federal copyright Act’s preemptive power but clarifies that it does not extend to *any* state‑law claim. The preemption clause under 17 U.S.C. § 301 is fundamentally a “blanket” defense that only operates when the *copyright dispute* itself is framed in state‑law terms. A claim that merely *touches* the underlying contractual relationship (e.g., misuse of a creative content in a non‑commercial project) may circumvent preemption if it can be clearly separated from a copyrightory. The appellate ruling adds nuance in this area: it illustrates that preemption is a *substantive* test, not a mechanical one. For litigants The court's precedent suggests that they must make sure "truly different" elements get them to stay outside of the copyright sphere.

*The Four‑Factor Dance in the Digital Age:*  The hard-and-fast rule that "Santos’s only claim was a parody‑like commentary" reinforces the *fair use* message that “parodic content can reduce or curb the more covert forces of that marketplace”. The court’s refusal to give Santos a fair‑use provision to protect the current vantage point (and the content he is suing on), showcases that the courts still see *creative reinterpretation* as a distinct category.  Future cases will reach back to this decision to argue that *self‑illustrated preparatory use* (i.e., a user creating content for commentary) is still a fully protected transformation.

*Contract Claims Under the Blockchain*:  The decision shows the trend that courts will aggressively challenge any talk about *implied* contracts that have not been expressly negotiated, especially when the parties are not part of an explicit agreement. They will also re-check the elements that apply to the state claim. In a future dispute, e.g., using a new type of “smart‑contract” to simulate a licensing agreement, the same doctrinal test will apply: if no one signed, there is no contract (*and if there *is*, it is still not necessarily a willful infringement of the copyright). 

**Bottom line:**  The Second Circuit’s decision is a reaffirmation of the *hierarchy* between *federal* and *state* law in the realm of copyrighted content. This hierarchy is not static—if you lean the line toward a more traditional contract approach, the decision could be an opening to re‑apply the federal act in a narrower scope. The same procedure applies for any future *commentary* or *parody* litigation: proceed with caution if you think your work might infringe existing rights. A more thorough sing‑out that the *four‑factor test* is fundamentally subjective and constantly evolving.
