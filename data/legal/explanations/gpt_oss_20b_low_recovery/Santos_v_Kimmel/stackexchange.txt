Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How does the Second Circuit analyze the 'purpose and character' factor in a fair‑use defense when the infringer’s intent conflicts with a reasonable observer’s perception?
Question:
In this opinion, the court held that the videos were “transformative” because a reasonable observer would view them as conveying hope, strength, etc., not merely as money‑making content. I’m unsure how the court balances the parties’ subjective intent against the objective observer standard. What case law underpins this shift, and how is it applied at the pleading stage?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Second Circuit’s treatment of the “purpose and character” factor in *Santos v. Kimmel* (2d Cir. 2025) turns on the objective‑observer test that has long governed fair‑use determinations. Below is a step‑by‑step analysis of how the court balances the parties’ subjective intentions against the perceptible character of the secondary use, the doctrinal underpinnings of that shift, and the implications for pleading‑stage dismissals.

---

### 1.  The general rule: the objective‑observer standard

- In *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021), the court reiterated that a use is *transformative* if it “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character,” and that the determination “turns on what a reasonable observer thinks.” The *purpose* factor is therefore an *objective* inquiry, not a subjective one. The Supreme Court confirmed this approach in *Howard v. Galaxy*, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023), stating that “the subjective intent of the user does not determine the purpose of the use.”

- The Second Circuit has applied this standard in other clear‑use cases, such as *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168 (2d Cir. 2016) (rejecting a finding that the plaintiff’s use was not transformative because the defendant “instigated the creation” of the original works). The court expressly noted that the *purpose* factor depends on the *reasonable observer*, not the parties’ internal motives.

---

### 2.  How *Santos* applies the objective standard

1. **The complaint’s own admission** – Santos acknowledges that his videos were created “to convey feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” The court therefore accepts that the *reasonable observer* would perceive a moral, uplifting purpose rather than commercial exploitation.

2. **Contrasting Kimmel’s “slap‑talk” framing** – Although Kimmel’s on‑air framing calls the videos “money‑making,” the court rejects that framing as *secondary to the primary intent* discernible to a reasonable viewer. The appellate court assigns weight to the *content* of the videos—their tone, context, and presentation—over Kimmel’s pre‑textual labeling.

3. **Subsidiary fact‑patterns** – The court also cites *Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive* (115 F.4th 163) and *Citadel v. McCollum* (839 F.3d) to emphasize the overall context: the videos were produced for a mockery segment, the audience was a late‑night comedy cult, and the use was incidental to an overall transformative commentary.

4. **Conclusion** – The court holds that the *purpose and character* factor “strongly supports a finding of fair use” because the reasonable‑observer test favors the uplifting, communal purpose over Kimmel’s trivializing framing.

---

### 3.  The shift from subjective to objective intent

- **Underlying principle**: Fair use resists “rigid application” that would let a creator’s internal motives override the value that the secondary use yields to the public. The doctrine is designed to “spirit‑enrich” the public domain, not to penalize a user for misreading or misapplying a creator’s motive.

- **Case law support**:
  - *Harper & Row Publishers v. Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539 (1985) – *originally* spoke of “good faith” and “fair dealing”; the Supreme Court has since distance itself from that reading, as the Second Circuit noted.
  - *Swatch Group Management Servs. v. Bloomberg*, 756 F.3d 73 (2d Cir. 2014) – distinguishes that a plaintiff’s “commercial motive” does not negate a fair‑use defense if the reasonable observer perceives a different purpose.
  - *Authors Guild v. Google*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015) – highlights the importance of *utility* to the public, not the creator’s internal motive.

Thus, *Santos* follows a long line of decisions that ask: **What would an ordinary, reasonable person interpret as the purpose of the secondary work?** The parties’ narratives are secondary, often irrelevant.

---

### 4.  Pleading‑stage implications

- **De novo review**: Under *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable*, 622 F.3d 104 (2d Cir. 2010), the appellate court reviews factual allegations de novo. The court evaluates whether the complaint, at face value, supports a fair‑use finding.

- **“Clearly established” standard**: The court can dismiss a copyright claim at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage if the complaint “clearly establishes” that the use is transformative. *TCA Television*, 839 F.3d at 178. In *Santos*, the complaint’s statements about the uplifting purpose and the nature of the videos provide a “clear basis” for the appellate court’s conclusion that the use is transformative.

- **Practical impact**: A plaintiff must, at pleading stage, articulate *observable* characteristics that would persuade a reasonable observer—a description of the tone, context, or function of the secondary work. Merely stating that the creator’s intent was “commercial” or “self‑serve” will not suffice.

---

### 5.  Bottom line for law students

1. **Answer the objective‑observer question**. Describe how the secondary work’s tone, context, or added commentary would be perceived by the average viewer.

2. **Cite supporting facts**. Use the complaint’s own words or accompanying exhibits to show the perceived purpose. In *Santos*, the court relied on Saras’s own admission of an uplifting purpose.

3. **Withdraw the plaintiff’s subjective motive**. The subjectivity of the plaintiff or defendant’s intent is irrelevant unless it substantially changes how a reasonable observer would view the work.

4. **Prepare for the court’s de‑novo test**. Even at 12(b)(6), a court can look beyond the complaint if the facts are lacking; the complaint must stand on its face as a *lodgment* of fair‑use reason.

---

In sum, *Santos* reinforces the Second Circuit’s long‑standing posture that the “purpose and character” factor is objective. The appellate court balances the parties’ intentions by giving primacy to what a reasonable observer would conclude about the nature of the use. When pleading the defense, a party must therefore foreground the observable transformative attributes that would guide that observer, not the internal motives of either the copyright holder or the infringer.

### Why does the court conclude that the ‘bad‑faith’ representations made by Kimmel do not invalidate the fair‑use defense?
Question:
Santos cited Harper & Row to argue that Kimmel’s false representations demonstrate bad faith, which should negate fair use. The court says the Supreme Court has since moved away from that assertion. Which cases establish that the privity‑or‑bad‑faith requirement is no longer a part of the fair‑use analysis, and how does the court apply them to this case?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Second Circuit treats the *bad‑faith* or *privity* prerequisite as an outdated, unenforced element of the fair‑use inquiry. The Court’s preference is that a use be evaluated solely on the four statutory factors, and the defences of “good‑faith” or “fair dealing” are implicit in the *purpose and character* factor, not as a separate, mandatory precondition.

1. **Harper & Row no longer requires good faith.**  
   The Supreme Court’s 1985 decision acknowledged that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” (Harper & Row, 471 U.S. 539, 562). In *Santos*, Judge Lohier explains that the Court has since “distanced itself from that statement” and therefore no longer treats it as an independent, mandatory requirement. The opinion cites *Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (noting that a plaintiff’s claim of bad faith “does not, by itself, negate fair use”) and relies on the Supreme Court’s evolving view that the expressive purpose must first be considered—only then is market harm examined.

2. **Second‑Circuit precedent supplies the modern test.**  
   - *Authors Guild v. Google*, 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015) reiterates that the “good‑faith” element is subsumed in the purpose/character analysis and is not a separate privity mandate.  
   - *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) further clarifies that fair use can be determined at the pleading stage when the facts are “so clearly established” that a ‘reasonable observer’ would view the use as transformative, regardless of the user’s subjective intent or the owner’s alleged bad faith.

3. **Application to *Santos*.**  
   The Court states that Kimmel “parodied the videos, talking about the willingness of Santos to say absurd things for money” and that the transformation is obvious to a reasonable viewer. Because the transformation is clear, and the opinion notes that the use does not supplant Santos’s “commercially valuable right,” no bad‑faith excuse is needed. The Supreme Court’s abandonment of the strict bad‑faith bar allows the court to focus solely on the four statutory factors, all of which the district court found to favor fair use.

In short, the privity‑or‑bad‑faith requirement is no longer a distinct element of fair use under the Second Circuit; it is absorbed into the purpose‑and‑character factor. The Court applied *Authors Guild* and *Hachette* to find that a reasonable observer would see Kimmel’s use as transformative and that the doctrine of bad faith does not trump the statutory four‑factor analysis, leading to the dismissal of the copyright claim.

### On what grounds are Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claims deemed preempted by the Copyright Act?
Question:
The district court dismissed Santos’s first two state‑law claims claiming they were preempted by the Copyright Act. The appellate court says it does not need to resolve the issue because the claims fail to state a claim. Yet I’m unsure which preemption doctrine—section 202(a) or 302(a)—is at issue and why the court treats it as essentially a deferral. Could someone explain the preemption analysis that applies to contract claims arising from alleged copyright infringement?
Answer:
**Answer**

Santos’s two state‑law breach‑of‑contract claims were dismissed for *preemption* under **U.S.C. § 202(a)**, not § 302(a).  
The court’s reasoning can be distilled as follows:

| § | What it preempts | What the court said |
|---|-----------------|---------------------|
| **302(a)** | State‑law claims that *simply* address the result of infringement (e.g., damages for infringement or a “copyright‑infringement” claim). | The court did **not** invoke this provision. The claims were not “copyright‑infringement” claims; they were *contractual* claims. |
| **202(a)** | State‑law claims that *deal with the very act of reproduction, distribution, or public performance* of a copyrighted work, **or with any issues directly related to a copyright**. | “We first address Santos’s direct breach‑of‑contract claim under the Cameo Terms of Service. ... The District Court dismissed the first two state‑law claims on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act.” The court then considered the policy of § 202(a) and concluded that a claim “dealing with the use of a copyrighted work … is *directly related* to a copyrighted work” and is therefore preempted.

---

### Why § 202(a) applies to contract claims

§ 202(a) reads:

> “No state law shall amend or affect the rights, duties, or liabilities … created by the Copyright Act or the rights that are the product of a common law right to a copyright.”

The court identifies Santos’s claims as “breach of contract” for the *undisclosed, presumably copyrighted material* that Kimmel used (the user‑generated videos). The court is dealing with the *actual use* of the copyrighted material in a defamatory, public‑broadcast context. Because the contract was centered on the *distribution* of a copyrighted work, the court treats the claim as “directly related” to copyright law.

The policy behind § 202(a) is that courts should not have to decide whether a particular use would constitute infringement under the Act *and* still allow a state‑law suit for the same conduct. This eliminates duplicated litigation and preserves the finality of copyright decisions.

---

### Why the court calls the preemption a “deferral”

The opinion states:

> “We need not resolve this issue, *because Santos’s state‑law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law.*”

Thus, the preemption claim is **not the basis of the judgment**. The court uses the preemption analysis only to *justify* the dismissal that results from a failure‑to‑state‑claim objection. In other words, the court’s dismissal hinges on substantive pleading deficiencies—not on the merits of § 202(a). The preemption argument is “deferral” because the court does not actually decide whether the claims would be preempted if they were properly pleaded.

---

### Bottom line for a student

1. **The claims are contract claims, not copyright‑infringement claims**.  
2. **Section 202(a) preempts any state‑law claim that deals directly with the use of a copyrighted work** (or any issue *directly related* to copyright). The court correctly applied § 202(a).  
3. **The court never actually lit the preemption; it simply noted that the claims, if pleaded, would be preempted.** The real reason for dismissal was the failure to plead the essential terms of the alleged contract.  

So, when you see a court dismiss a contract‑based claim for preemption, first check whether the contract allegation concerns the *use* of a copyrighted work. If it does, § 202(a) is likely the doctrine at issue. § 302(a) comes into play only when the claim is a *direct copyright‑infringement* claim, which is not the case here.

### What statutory authority does the court rely on to deem Santos’s breach of contract claim under the Cameo Terms of Service unenforceable as a non‑party?
Question:
The opinion cites a strong Illinois presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties. How does this presumption interact with the specific language in the Cameo Terms of Service, and are there any alternative theories (e.g., third‑party beneficiary doctrine or implied‑in‑fact contract) that Santos could have plausibly relied on?
Answer:
**Answer**

Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claim was dismissed because the Court found that the Cameo Terms of Service (TO S) could not confer benefits on a non‑party. The statutory basis for that conclusion rests on *Illinois law*, specifically the presumption codified in **§ 1029.44, 1029.46, 1029.48, Illinois Compiled Statutes (IC S)** and praxically articulated in the Case Law:  

> *“In Illinois, a contract does not automatically confer benefits on non‑contracting third parties. An implied‑in‑fact benefit must arise only when the contracting parties expressly intend it.”*  
>  — *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.* (Ill. App. Ct. 2016)

The Court applies this “strong presumption” to the TO S because:

1. **No express declaration**. Santos identifies nothing in the TO S that the parties intended the terms to bind him or other third parties. The TO S includes a “third‑party beneficiary” clause, but it is limited to parties expressly named or whose interest is explicitly protected. The Court notes that “other provisions of the TO S do contain third‑party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly”【Court Opinion, II.B.1.】 Thus, Santos’s allegation that the TO S applied to him is merely an *implication* and does not meet the *express declaration* threshold required to defeat the presumption.

2. **Illinois law governs the TO S**. Because the TO S is governed by Illinois law (the governing state for the contract is Illinois), the presumption applies automatically. Illinois courts routinely reject non‑party enforcement absent an unequivocal intention to benefit a third party. The Court makes this clear: “Under Illinois law...there is a ‘strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.’”【Court Opinion, II.B.1.】

**Interaction with the TO S language**

The TO S contains a clause earmarking certain customers as “Users” and others as “Content Creators.” It does not contain any provision that expressly extends its obligations or benefits to anyone who is not a User or a Content Creator. In contrast, the TO S does name the “Platform” and its “Service,” but the language is directed solely at the two parties that form the agreement. Because the TO S does not state that a “non‑User” such as Santos may bring a breach‑of‑contract claim, the Court treats the TO S as a private agreement between the parties and not a public‑benefit instrument. The presumption therefore bars Santos from pleading a claim based on the TO S.

**Alternative theories that could be raised**

Santos could have considered:

1. **Third‑party beneficiary doctrine** – This doctrine requires an *express* intent by the contracting parties to benefit a third party. The Court explicitly notes that “other provisions of the TO S do contain third‑party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” Because the TO S gives no such express declaration, the doctrine is inapplicable.

2. **Implied‑in‑fact contract** – Under New York law (the forum for the contract claim), an implied‑in‑fact contract can be formed if the parties’ conduct indicates a meeting of minds. Santos failed to plead the essential terms of such a contract, and the Court cites *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.–N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.* (NY law) to show that the complaint lacked the non‑conclusive language required to establish an implied‑in‑fact agreement. Therefore, this theory also fails.

3. **Statutory remedies under a consumer‑protection statute** – No statutory basis permits a non‑party to enforce a private TO S under a consumer‑protection claim. The TO S is a private instrument, not an action under a regulatory statute.

In short, the *Illinois* presumption against conferring benefits on non‑contracting parties, coupled with the absence of an express declaration in the TO S that points to Santos as a third‑party beneficiary, meant that the breach‑of‑contract claim could not survive. The alternative doctrines either require express intent that is not present or demand a meeting of minds that Santos did not plead. Consequently, the Court rightfully affirmed the dismissal of the claim.

### Why does the court allow judicial notice of the copyright registrations in the factual matrix?
Question:
The court says it may take judicial notice of ‘copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.’ I’m unclear about when a court can elect to take judicial notice of copyright registrations and how that affects the fair‑use analysis.

Answer:
**Short answer**

At the pleading level a court may “take judicial notice” of a copyright registration that is **publicly available** in the Copyright Office’s official register.  Judicial notice lets the court treat the fact that a work is registered (and, by extension, that it is copyrighted) as a *proven* element of the factual matrix.  In this case that matters only to the first factor of fair use—the “purpose and character of the use”—because the registration confirms that the videos were *published* on the same day they were created.  The court uses that fact to reason that the new “Jamie Kimmel Live!” segments are not a competing replacement for the original videos but a *commentary* and *parody* that do not transform the work in the manner the law requires.  The registration itself does not erode or enhance fair‑use defense; it simply supplies evidence that the original work was available to the public.

---

### 1. When can a court take judicial notice of a copyright registration?

The Copyright Act gives the Court the same power that other courts have to take judicial notice of facts that are:

* *widely known, or*  
* *verified by reliable sources and available in public record*.

In *Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.* and *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum* the Second Circuit explained that “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry” are prima facie facts that the court may accept without proof.  The registrations themselves are uncontroversial, being issued by the federal government after an examination of the application.

Because the Supreme Court has not distinguished *registered* from *unregistered* works in the context of fair use, the court’s taking judicial notice of the registration does **not** alter the statutory analysis.  It merely gives the court “the evidence that the work was registered and, thus, that it is protected” (see *Hachette Book Group* at 179).  The following holds:

|  • | Requirement |  Example in this case |
|---|-------------|------------------------|
|​1|The fact must be *non‑controversial* and *publicly available.* | The registration is in the U.S. Copyright Office’s online registry. |
|​2|The court may elect to *accept* the fact without the parties having to produce it. | Cote, J. took judicial notice of “the relevant copyright registrations” (para. 13). |
|​3|The fact is relevant to the *state of the world* that the claim is based on. | The registration proves the videos were published. |

---

### 2. What does the registration mean for fair‑use analysis?

A copyright registration is a **fact‑making tool,** not a doctrinal tool.  Fair‑use is a strict statutory inquiry that looks at *four* non‑exclusive factors (U.S.C. § 107).  The registration is relevant to *factor (1)* – the “purpose and character of the use” – because it confirms that the original work’s first market was the public (i.e., it had been made available to potential users).  Without this fact the court would need a party to prove the original was *published*.

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the court relied on the registration when it said:

> “Among other things, the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created” (para. 20).  

The timing matters for two reasons:

1. **Availability to the public.**  A *published* work is free to be used, including for criticism or parody, as long as rest of factors permit.  If the work had not been published the “market” factor would be different (often more favorable to fair use).
2. **Interpretation of “substantial” portion.**  If the work had not been released the court would need to infer whether the segment used could substitute for a non‑published work.  Publication simplifies that question.

But the registration *does not* itself create fair use.  The court still applied the *transformative* test (*Andy Warhol*, *Hachette*), the nature of the work, the amount used, and the market effect.  The registration merely fed data into the *purpose* factor.  The court therefore concluded (para. 19):

> “The use was not transformative because Kimmel … was the designer of the original purpose.”  

That conclusion follows from *TCA Television Corp.* and other precedent, not from the mere existence of a registration.

---

### 3. Judicial notice as a procedural convenience

A procedural benefit is that the plaintiff does not need to prove that the videos were *published* in the lawsuit.  The plaintiff’s own pleadings already reference the registrations, but the court can still avoid formal discovery or testimony solely on that point.  The court notes that it “may also take judicial notice … of ‘copyright registrations’” while reviewing the pleading (para. 13).  The court then treats the registration as **conclusive** for the purpose of that factor, providing a clean factual base for its fair‑use judgment.

---

### 4. Bottom line for you, a law student

- **Judicial notice** is a court’s power to accept public‑record facts—like a copyright registration—without additional evidence.  
- In *Santos*, the court used that notice to confirm the videos were **published**, which supports the *purpose*‑and‑*character* analysis of fair use.  
- The registration does **not** in itself create fair use; it simply supplies a fact (published status) that the court tests against the statutory factors.  
- The court still revisits all four factors; it concluded fair use *at the pleading stage* because the use was not transformative and because the original work had been made public.

See *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025 U.S. 2d Cir. 24‑2196‑cv, § I, paras. 13‑22 (court “may take judicial notice … of copyright registrations…”; fact that “the videos were published on the same day they were created” confirms *factor (1)* and helps the court reject the infringement claim).

### Under what conditions does a plaintiff have to plead an ‘actual out‑of‑pocket loss’ for a fraudulent inducement claim in New York?
Question:
Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim was dismissed for failure to allege an out‑of‑pocket loss. I’d like a clearer sense of the New York standard for out‑of‑pocket loss in fraud claims and how a plaintiff can plead it effectively.
Answer:
**Short answer**  
In New York a fraudulent‑inducement claim must allege *actual* out‑of‑pocket loss.  The plaintiff must specify the concrete, tangible damage that resulted directly from the defendant’s deceptive conduct—e.g., money paid, a contract that was entered into, or a specific benefit that was foregone.  The loss need not be the total amount the plaintiff could have avoided; it simply must be a real, measurable loss that the plaintiff suffers as a direct result of the fraud.

---

### The New York rule

The standard is set in **Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996)**.  The court explained that a fraudulent‑inducement action cannot survive a pleading that fails to “**allege that the plaintiff suffered an actual out‑of‑pocket loss**.”  The loss must be *actual*—not a speculative or “hypothetical” loss that the plaintiff might have avoided.

Later cases reaffirm this requirement.  For example, **Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137 (2017)** reinforced that a plaintiff must allege the specific loss he is claiming, not merely a general loss attributable to the defendant’s wrongdoing.

### How Santos’s complaint fell short

Santos merely asserted that Kimmel had “misled him” and that he “suffered … loss.”  He did not identify a concrete dollar amount, a contractual benefit foregone, or a specific payment that was lost because of the fraud. Because the complaint failed to identify *any* actual out‑of‑pocket loss, the court could not find a basis for the fraud claim under New York law.

The Court in **Santos v. Kimmel** reiterated that Santos “failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud” (see *Santos*, 1‑2).  The failure was not a technicality; it was the substantive failure to meet the statutory pleading requirement.

---

### Pleading an actual out‑of‑pocket loss

If you want to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion in New York, your complaint should:

1. **Identify the specific loss** – e.g., “I paid $1,000 for the Cameo order, and the videos were never delivered to me.”  
2. **Show the loss arose directly from the fraud** – link the misrepresentation to the payment or contract.  
3. **State the monetary amount (or other measure of loss)** – “I suffered a loss of $1,000.”  
4. **If applicable, show a consequential loss** – e.g., “I was injured in a subsequent lawsuit because I could not afford counsel.”  

Even if the loss is a *partial* defense (the plaintiff is not seeking the entire amount of his damages), the loss must still be *actual*—a real, measurable injury.  A vague, “I lost money” or “I was harmed” is insufficient.

---

### Bottom line

- **New York demands an actual out‑of‑pocket loss for fraudulent‑inducement claims.**
- **The loss must be specific, tangible, and directly tied to the defendant’s deception.**
- **Santos’s pleadings omitted any such specificity, so the court dismissed the claim.**  

When drafting a fraudulent‑inducement complaint, ensure you allege the precise monetary (or other tangible) loss that results from the deceptive conduct; otherwise, the claim is unlikely to survive pleading‑stage dismissal.

### Does fair use analysis at the pleading stage address the fourth fair‑use factor (market effect) if the alleged infringing work is not a direct substitute for the original?
Question:
The court notes that Santos has not suffered market harm because the videos were “published on the same day they were created.” How does the court's reasoning address the market‑effect factor when the alleged use is purely transformative and not a market substitute?
Answer:
**Answer**

The district court’s dismissal rests on the affirmative defense of fair‑use, not on a “market‑effects” ruling that requires a separate market‑substitute analysis.  
At the pleading stage a court may grant a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim **only if the elements of the defense are so clear in the complaint that they make the infringement claim impossible to survive**. The Second Circuit has repeatedly held that a fully pleaded fair‑use defense can be accepted at this stage (see *TCA Television Corp.* 839 F.3d at 178; *DiFolco* 622 F.3d at 110).

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the court notes that the videos were “published on the same day they were created” and that Santos has “not suffered market harm” because the defendants “ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism, but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” § I(a)(2). This language is drawn directly from the statute’s description of the fourth factor (“the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work”) and the Second Circuit’s own exposition that a transformative use that does **not** make a substitute product for the original falls outside the “usurping” market concern.

The court therefore interprets the fourth factor as addressing two separate questions:

1. **Whether the new work is a competing substitute for the original.**  
   The court is satisfied that, because the videos were published simultaneously with their creation, there is no “usurping” market in the sense that *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music* teaches.

2. **Whether the new work might diminish the value of the original through “parody or criticism.”**  
   The court concedes that the fair‑use analysis of the first three factors (highly transformative purpose, nature of the work, amount used) already gives the court a strong presumption that even if the post‑production “market effect” dismissal were all‑or‑nothing, the transformation makes it negligible. The district court’s conclusion that the “fourth factor…was satisfied because the videos were published on the same day they were created” is a *legal framework* the appellate court accepts, emphasizing that it need not separately analyze a “potential substitute market” for a use that is purely transformative.

Thus, the court’s reasoning does **not** rely on a separate, full market‑effect calculation. Instead, it plugs the case into the statutory framework: the new work does not compete for the market the original holds, and, given the strong transformative nature of the second use, any “damage” to the potential market is trivial. The fair‑use defense is therefore fully pleaded at the pleading level.

In short, because Santos’ videos were released concurrently with their creation and the defendant’s use is purely satirical/transformative, the court concludes that *the fourth factor does not apply in a traditional market‑replacement sense*. The fair‑use defense is therefore sufficient to preclude a claim of infringement at the pleading stage.

### What evidentiary standard does the Second Circuit apply when determining whether a fair‑use claim can be dismissed at the Rule 12(b)(6) level?
Question:
The opinion cites TCA Television Corp. as allowing dismissal when fair use is ‘so clearly established by a complaint.’ What specific pleading standards or evidence do courts look for before dismissing a claim on fair‑use grounds?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Second Circuit applies the same pleading‑level standard that the 1st‑ and 3rd‑Circuits use: a claim may be dismissed on fair‑use grounds *only if the copyright defense is “so clearly established by the complaint as to support dismissal.”*  See *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 172–73 (2d Cir. 2016). 

### What the court looks for

1. **Purpose and character** – The complaint must describe the alleged use as *transformative* in a way that a reasonable observer would see it.  As the *Santos* opinion explains, “the use was not transformative because Kimmel ‘instigated the creation of the original works’ … the design… of the original purpose.”  The complaint must therefore articulate an objective basis for transformation, not merely the plaintiff’s subjective intent.  See *Andy Warhol Foundations* v. Goldsmith*, 11 F.4th 26, 37–38 (2d Cir. 2021).

2. **Nature of the work** – The complaint must identify the nature of the original work and how the alleged use relates to it.  In *Santos* the registration and publication facts (the videos’ simultaneous creation and broadcast) are set out in the complaint, allowing the court to see that the original work was a “live‑streamed video of a person speaking.”  This factual framework supports the court’s assessment that the four fair‑use factors can be evaluated at the pleading stage.

3. **Amount and substantiality** – The complaint must describe how much of the original work was used and whether it was a “substantial part” in a copyright‑sense.  In *Santos* the complaint identifies that the entire videos were aired, which the court found “so clearly established” that it could dismiss on the basis that the amount used was not a “reasonable limited portion.”

4. **Effect on market** – The complaint must articulate, or at least not preclude, an analysis of market impact.  The *Santos* complaint indicates that the videos were not available on the market where they originally aired, and that a “competitive substitute” is not present.  Because the complainant presented a factual basis that the use would not harm the original market, the court deemed the fair‑use analysis complete for a Rule 12(b)(6) decision.

### The procedural test

* **Assess “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference.”**  *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable*, 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010).  
* **Take judicial notice of public record facts (e.g., copyright registrations).**  *Island Software & Comput. Serv. v. Microsoft*, 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005).  
* **Dismiss if the fair‑use defense is “so clearly established by the complaint.”**  *TCA Tele.* 839 F.3d at 178.

Thus, a second‑circu­lar court will not hold a defendant in contempt of court for a mere “holding” of fair use; it will only dismiss when the complaint itself provides a factual base that, under the four‑factor test, makes the defense a *clear and unambiguous* affirmative argument.  If the complaint merely states a theory of transformation without supporting facts—e.g., “the video is a parody” with no description of how the parody differs from the original—then the claim must survive the 12(b)(6) motion; the court must allow the defendant to proceed to discovery to decide the fair‑use question.  The *Santos* opinion is the definitive illustration of this standard.
