Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 2: # 2. Copyright Infringement and the Fair‑Use Defense

The district court’s dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims rested on an application of the four‑factor fair‑use framework. In this chapter we examine how the Second Circuit reviewed that conclusion, why the court found the use “transformative,” and how it treated the defendant’s bad‑faith accusations.

## Statutory Framework: U.S.C. § 107 and the Four Fair‑Use Factors

Section 107 of the Copyright Act permits a court to refuse liability for an infringement “in cases of a non‑commercial or a commercial use that can be justified as a fair use” under the following factors:

1. **Purpose and character of the use** – Whether the use is commercial or non‑commercial, and whether the new work is transformative.
2. **Nature of the copyrighted work** – The more creative the original, the stronger the copyright protection.
3. **Amount and substantiality** – The quantity and quality of the portion used relative to the whole.
4. **Effect on the market** – Whether the new work usurps the market or would harm the original’s commercial value.

The test is *non‑exclusive*; courts weigh the factors together, not against one another. The Second Circuit, following the Supreme Court’s precedent in *Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive*, reiterates that fair use is flexible and fact‑specific. A court may dismiss an infringement action under Rule 12(b)(6) if the fair‑use defense “is so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” (*TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*).

## Analysis of Purpose/Character: Transformative Use Principles and Prior Cases

### The Transformative Standard

In *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts v. Goldsmith*, the court held that the purpose and character factor “focus[es] chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative, i.e., whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” The Second Circuit treats transformation as the yardstick for “purpose and character” because once a use is truly transformative, the remaining factors become almost inevitable.

### Applying Transformation to the Kimmel Segments

Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segments aired the videos Santos had made for him on Cameo, but the segments were edited, inserted into a broader satirical context, and accompanied by the host’s commentary. The court concluded that a reasonable observer would perceive this as a “submenu of humor, criticism, and mockery” rather than a direct copy or faithful reproduction. Even though Kimmel “instigated” the creation of the videos, the defendant was a *designer* of a *new* purpose—commentary on Santos’s willingness to perform absurd acts for money. The court rejected Santos’s claim that the purpose of the videos was the same as that of the original work, emphasizing that “the subjective intent of the user does not determine the purpose of the use” (*Andy Warhol*).

### The “Coffee‑House” Criterion

The court alluded to the “coffee‑house” test from *Encyclopedia of Ethics* as a heuristic for transformation: an excerpt that was “publicly available and used for allowable purposes in a coffee house or other public venue." While the court did not cite the article by name, it pointed to the idea that brief, publicly shared snippets for commentary fall within fair use. Kimmel’s broadcasts meet this criterion because the videos were publicly aired on national television and utilized in a broader context of public commentary.

### TCA Television and the “Designer” Exception

*TCA Television v. McCollum* held that a show that “designed” its own material is not impacted by a pre‑existing work if the new creation is markedly different. The Second Circuit reaffirmed that Kimmel’s role as a “designer” of the new segment created an entirely different purpose, thereby supporting the transformation finding.

## Nature of the Work, Amount Used, and Market Impact Factors as Applied to the Videos

### Nature of the Original Work

The recordings were short, informal videos produced by a former congressman. The Second Circuit treated them as “creative works” but noted that their functional, performative nature made the *nature* factor neutral in favor of fair use.

### Amount and Substantiality

The court considered that the videos were “published on the same day they were created” (copyright registration evidence), suggesting that they were short and non‑substantial. Even if the entire video was used, the court noted that the portion integrated into the televised segment was proportionate to the overall material and served a new purpose.

### Market Impact

The defendant’s use was deemed “non‑substantive” with respect to Santos’s market. Because the videos were broadcast publicly as satire, they did not serve as a commercial substitute for the original content. The court found no evidence that the segments undermined Santos’s market for the videos. The fourth factor therefore weighed in favor of fair use, echoing *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music*’s observation that “criticism or parody rarely competes with the market for the original.” 

## Bad‑Faith and “Good‑Faith” Considerations: Harper & Row, Swatch, Authors Guild

### Harpers & Row and Good‑Faith

The plaintiff argued that Kimmel’s false claims about the authenticity of the videos demonstrated bad faith, thus nullifying fair use. *Harper & Row v. Nation* stated that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but the Second Circuit pointed out that the Supreme Court later distanced this statement from its binding authority. Without independent precedent establishing a *prima facie* link between bad faith and a total defeat of fair use, the court declined to consider the defendants’ alleged deceit an obstacle to the fair‑use analysis.

### Swatch and the Absence of “Commercial Supplant”

In *Swatch Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg*, the court held that lack of commercial coercion or market supplant implied a non‑commercial intent. The fact that Kimmel had no financial incentive to produce the videos, beyond the narrative of satire, reinforced the court’s view that the use was not motivated by a desire to devalue Santos’s original works.

### Authors Guild and Commentary

*Authors Guild v. Google* clarified that criticism and commentary are core fixtures of fair use. The court applied this precedent to the Kimmel segments, treating them as legitimate commentary rather than an act of commercial exploitation.

## Conclusion: Fair Use Bars Infringement at the Pleading Stage

By scrutinizing each factor—understanding that transformation is the linchpin—the Second Circuit found that Santos’s copyright claim had failed even before a motion to dismiss. The defendant’s use of the videos in a comedic, critical context was transformative, the original works were not replaced or commercialized, and no market harm was identified. Thus, the district court properly dismissed the infringement claim under Rule 12(b)(6), and the Second Circuit affirmed.
