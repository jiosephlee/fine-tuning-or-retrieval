Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # 3. State‑Law Claims: Breach and Fraudulent Inducement

The Court denied Santos’ state‑law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.  In this chapter we trace the Court’s reasoning on the contractual claims—how Illinois and New York law treat third‑party benefits and implied‑in‑fact agreement—and then examine the fraudulent‑inducement claim, focusing on New York’s requirement that a plaintiff allege an actual out‑of‑pocket loss.  The analysis is confined to the facts, procedural posture, and holdings contained in the opinion.

## 3.1 Breach of Contract and Breach of Implied Contract

Santos alleged that Kimmel and ABC breached the Cameo Terms of Service by using videos Santos produced on the platform and airing them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*.  The Timed Crowds situation demands that the court consider whether the Terms of Service extend liability to a non‑party such as Santos.  The Court applied the Illinois doctrine that **non‑contracting third parties enjoy no benefit unless the contract expressly provides otherwise**.  This doctrine is grounded in *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.*, 68 N.E.3d 964 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016), where the court emphasized a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties” and required an *express declaration* indicating that a third party may enforce a provision.

Santos failed to deliver that declaration.  His complaint identified no terms that, even implicitly, applied to him or any other non‑party.  The Terms of Service do contain third‑party‑beneficiary language in other contexts, but those include clear express statements of intent.  Because Santos did not allege *any* express provision applying to him, the Illinois test precludes enforcement.

Even if the parties had agreed to a contract that bound Santos, *Caniglia v. Ch. Trib.* requires the complaint to allege **essential terms** of that contract.  A complaint that merely states a vague claim of breach is insufficient.  *Rocky Point Props.* (NY) extends this idea: to establish an implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law, the plaintiff must plead **demonstrable assent**—the “meeting of minds”—on the specific provisions that the conduct allegedly violated.  Santos’ pleading did not provide that level of detail.  Thus the court dismissed the breach‑of‑contract and breach‑of‑implied‑contract claims on pleading‑stage grounds without addressing the merits.

The Court specifically noted that Santos’ claims “fail to state a claim for relief under state law.”  The absence of an expressed contractual obligation for a third party and the lack of alleged assent meant that the Court had no basis to proceed with the substantive analysis of breach.

## 3.2 Fraudulent Inducement: Loss Requirement

The second state‑law claim that survived was fraudulent inducement.  Under New York law, to bring a claim for fraudulent inducement the plaintiff must allege: (1) a misrepresentation known to be false, (2) intent to induce reliance, (3) reliance by the plaintiff, and (4) **an actual out‑of‑pocket loss** *or* some other tangible detriment that is causally connected to the misrepresentation.  See *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996).  The court found that Santos did not address the fourth element.  His complaint did not describe any financial or other loss directly tied to the alleged fraud.  The absence of such a loss allegation renders the claim defective.

The Court cited *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill*, 29 N.Y.3d 137 (2017), as an example of a case where the court dismissed the claim for the same reason.  Because the requirement to plead an actual loss is *fundamental* to a fraudulent‑inducement claim, Santos’ failure to do so meant the claim could not survive a 12(b)(6) dismissal.  

In sum, the Court saw no grounds to hold the defendants liable for breach of contract—whether express or implied—because the contract never extended to Santos, and the fraudulent‑inducement claim failed to allege the statutory loss element.  Consequently, all state‑law claims were dismissed, and the Court affirmed the District Court’s ruling.
