Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

The dispute in *Santos v. Kimmel* centers on a series of license‑to‑use agreements made through the online performance‑request platform Cameo and the subsequent use of the resulting videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (JKL).  The issue arose when former congressman George Santos requested that Kimmel produce short personalized videos for fans.  Kimmel fulfilled the requests, each time creating a short clip that repeated Santos’s own words and slogans, and Kimmel then aired those clips on JKL as part of a segment titled “Will Santos Say It?”  The segment was intended as a comedic commentary on Santos’s public persona and the “absurd” statements he had made while in office.

## Facts of the Cameo Transaction and Kimmel’s Use of the Videos

Santos signed up for a Cameo account and posted a request asking Kimmel to make a “short video” that would “spread positivity and hope” using phrases such as “I can’t believe it’s true.”  Kimmel:

1.  Agreed to the request and filmed a five‑minute video in which he echoed these phrases.
2.  Delivered the video to Santos via the Cameo platform on the same day it was filmed.
3.  Re‑uploaded the video to JKL as a segment of the “Will Santos Say It?” parody series.  The segment included a brief introduction in which Kimmel noted that “Santos has promised to say absurd things for money,” before playing the full clip of Santos’s own words.

Santos claimed that Kimmel had used the videos without permission and that the use had deprived him of the commercial benefits of his own speech.  He also alleged that Kimmel had made false statements that constituted fraud.  Santos objected that the Cameo Terms of Service (ToS) granted Kimmel some contractual rights that protected him from lawsuits that Kimmel later filed against him.  Santos brought a federal copyright infringement claim and state‑law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.

## Parties

| Party | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **George Santos** | Former congressman, plaintiff, claimant of copyright ownership and route for state‑law claims. |
| **Jimmy Kimmel** | Defendant, host of *JKL*, creator of the videotaped content. |
| **American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (ABC)** | Defending party that owns the JKL platform, and the immediate broadcast venue for Kimmel’s segments. |
| **The Walt Disney Company** | Parent company of ABC, also named in the complaint because the broadcast was ultimately performed under its corporate umbrella. |

The suit was filed in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (SDNY), where the district judge was the Honorable Cote.

## District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) Dismissal and Grounds

The SDNY judge dismissed the complaint in its entirety under Rule 12(b)(6) – that is, the pleading stage – after a review of the complaint, posted exhibits, and cited registrations.  The dismissal rested on two pillars:

1. **Copyright Infringement – Fair Use**  
   The court applied the four‑factor test of § 107.  It found that Kimmel’s use was *transformative* because the videos were employed for “commentary, criticism, and parody.”  The court also concluded that the purpose factor, the nature of the original works (publicly spoken statements), the amount used (complete videos), and the market effect were all consistent with a fair‑use defense.  Thus, the court held that the copyright claims were barred at the pleading level.

2. **State‑Law Claims – Preemption**  
   The court rejected the breach‑of‑contract and fraudulent‑inducement claims.  For the contract claims, the court said that the claims were “preempted by the Copyright Act” because they hinged on alleged wrongful attribution of ownership of the copyrighted material.  For the fraud claim, the court found that the complaint did not allege a specific out‑of‑pocket loss, a requirement for a New York fraud action.  Consequently, the state‑law claims failed to state a claim for relief.

Because the judge abandoned the entire complaint, there was no trial, and the case advanced directly to the appellate court.

## Appellate Review Standards: De Novo and Permissible Supplemental Evidence

The Second Circuit reviewed the dismissal *de novo* in accordance with *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010).  The appellate court looked only at the facts alleged in the complaint, the exhibits actually on record, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.  It may also take judicial notice of certain facts such as the existence of a copyright registration, which must be hosted in government records.

The court reiterated that it may still consider arguments that were not raised in the complaint if they are linked to facts that are.  However, it could not discuss new substantive issues or try to re‑evaluate the court’s conclusions on the merits beyond the scope of the pleadings.  The appeal was limited to whether the district court’s dismissal was legally sound under the standards above.
