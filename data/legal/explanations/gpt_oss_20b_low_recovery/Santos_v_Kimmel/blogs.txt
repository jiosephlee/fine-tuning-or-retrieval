# Fair Use at the Forefront: How the Second Circuit’s Santos v. Kimmel Ruling Expands the Transformative‑Use Threshold

## Why This Case Matters

The Second Circuit’s refusal to let a former congressman, George Santos, claim a copyright injury when a late‑night host turned his Cameo videos into a satirical strip show may seem at first glance a footnote in the sprawling world of media lawsuits. Yet, for anyone dabbling in remix culture, derivative content, or even the burgeoning marketplace of “fan‑made” commentary, the ruling clarifies how far the court will send the bar when it comes to the **transformative** test—an argument that has been swelling in popularity since *Hachette* and *Warhol*.

In Santos, the court held that the judge was right to dismiss the case at the pleading stage, finding that the Kimmel‑produced **JKL** segments were *fair* because they were **transformative**. The court’s focus on the *reasonable observer* and its willingness to expand that notion beyond the niche world of parody means that streaming platforms and content creators now have a richer, more predictable map for where commentary‑driven derivative works will sit in the copyright universe.

## A Quick Recap of the Court’s Reasoning

The complaint alleged that when Kimmel campaigned for Santos to deliver “bizarre” statements—some entirely fabricated—and then broadcast those videos in a pre‑planned segment on his show, he had turned the original material into a new, distinct work that undermined Santos’s “commercially valuable right.” Santos ostensibly sought damages for infringement under federal law and for contract violations under state law.

The court first disgorged the state‑law claims. The interview’s slap‑dash nature meant the alleged contract terms fell short of pleading a “strictly stated” promise, and the policy of not favoring third‑party beneficiaries under the Cameo Terms of Service left Santos shore‑wrecked. The fraud claim was also found wanting for not stating an actual out‑of‑pocket loss. That set of conclusions did little to shape the discussion of fair use, though they illustrate the court’s down‑to‑earth approach to the fine print in litigation.

When it came to copyright, the court reiterated the four–factor test but underscored a **subjective‑to‑objective** shift: it rejected Santos’s self‑service justification that the videos were originally created to be “hopeful” and “uplifting” and instead leaned on the *reasonable observer* test pioneered in *Andy Warhol*, *Harper & Row*, and *TCA*. The court found that the “purpose” of the Kimmel‑produced audience—essentially a satirical demonstration that Santos could be, in jest, “willing to say absurd things for money”—was at least as “transformative” as the original. The other three factors, though not elaborately elaborated, were simply found insufficient to override the first.

What is striking is the court’s insistence that the *transformative* test is an **objective** physics‑invariant principle, echoed through Hudson‑era cases. In particular, the court references *Swatch* (2014) and *Authors Guild* (2015) to point out that the *intention* of either the original author or the secondary user—and especially the original creator’s intent—does not dictate the test. The comparative weight of the “purpose and character” of the use must look beyond the storyteller’s internal motivations and instead focus on how the new work is perceived by a “reasonable observer,” much like the Court in *Warhol* (2023) clarified.

## The Expanding Transformative‑Use Landscape

### 1. Extending Fair‑Use Preemption to Commentary‑Driven Platforms

In *Santos*, the second circuit reached beyond the boundaries of “pure parody” (as defined in *Harper & Row* and *Tennessee* law) to a broader domain: humor‑driven commentary segments that may employ material from a copyrighted source. The court brushed aside the idea that truthful reporting or “commentary” alone needed to satisfy the *transformative* factor. Instead, it saw Kimmel’s production as a new, distinct creative product that was “at least as transformative” as the original. The professional “reasonable observer”—the typical late‑night audience—would not be misled into thinking the content was an original Sant\* production.

This is a subtle yet important line. Licensing experts are standing to take this *transformative* measure as a new baseline. Think TikTok clips, YouTube mash‑ups, or even a streaming platform’s own editorial series that repurposes user‑generated footage for commentary. If the new work speaks to a different purpose—critique, humor, or satire—the *transformative* bar becomes less about the *intent* of the content creator and more about the *visual‑aural reception* by a lay audience.

### 2. The "Reasonable Observer" and Comprehensive Context

The court's emphasis on the *reasonable observer* has a larger repercussion. Instead of just dealing in a narrow sense of “commercial purpose” versus “non‑commercial,” the *reasonable observer* concept now covers *any* transformation where the perceived function shift—and how the audience interprets it—constitui the key variable. The example has cultural weaponized flagging: you can stop pointing to a *raw* source and still please the *reasonable observer* by altering the setting, mood, or context it is placed in. This aligns with the *Citizens for the Protection of the Environment* doctrine where altering the context is the very core of the transformation discussed.

### 3. No “Good‑Faith” Discount

The court was keen to revise *Harper & Row*’s once‑idealized requirement that *fair use presuppose good faith and fair dealing*. The Second Circuit noted that the Supreme Court has updated or sidestepped that tenet in *Warhol* (2023) and other subsequent cases. The new insight is that a “sarcastic” or “schadenfreude” style can still obey a fair use defense provided it is transformative under the *reasonable observer* lens. That means that even if a user intentionally misrepresents or uses a source for “malicious” commentary, the fair‑use determination can still tilt in their favor so long as the new use is forward‑thinking or re‑presentational enough to serve a different purpose. The rule so far has been interpreted more leniently, to the benefit of the theatre of the absurd, in a way that would firmly shape the delicate equilibrium between “public interest” and an original copyright holder’s rights.

## Downstream Implications for Innovation

### 1. Streamlined Content Production for Companies

The ruling informs streaming platforms, especially those that rely heavily on memes, mash‑ups, or interactive commentary, that they can safely propagate materials through transformation‑based openings in the *fair‑use* shield. A recreational audience that identifies the original content from an obvious cameo and perceives a new comedic micro‑narrative will be considered transformative, not they're an infringer. That is especially valuable for streaming platforms displaying clips (Mojo or Apple TV‑?) that credibility for elimination held the question of whether they conform to “public interest” or “non‐commercial” terms. The corollary is a more pronounced ability to demand create, re‑package raw or live‑content.

### 2. Greater Certainty for Fan Communities

If a fan‑made short‑form series or a 49‑second compilation with appropriate *notice* is considered transformational, the fight for fair‑use becomes clearer to fan communities. This extends to academic meme projects (like a talk show. The premise is now dynamic because its transformation can be *judged in and tested by a reasonable observer* of 17‑year‑old to the log of the board levels. The output offers that the second circuit, which sets a robust standard in *Santos*, will reduce a great deal of litigation over has to thrown a #hashtag about which `consumption` status is assignable, because discerned and strong.

### 3. Potential Litigation Trade‑offs Among Copyright Holders

Assuming the articulated *reasonable observer* test is strict enough, the plausible downstream effect is that copyright owners may **achieve a narrow “borderline” defense margin** for derivatives essential for conversation. A copyright holder will likely still sue to enforce rights on non-transformative user copy‑offs (or break-into copies that’re not “visited” by the consumer), but the door for transformation‑driven derivative works is expanded. The risk of litigation will shift to multiple modes: whether the plaintiff triggers trademark or unfair‑competition moaning on mis‑representations or the way trivial expansion or re‑packs a coin.

### 4. The Query of Preemption

Although the court did not approve the argument that preemption of state‑law claims could be applied to the *state law* breach-of‑contract claims (government’s benefit), it cooled the desire for copyright infringement to travel in the realm of contractual claims. The court indicates that the non‑contracting third‑party *service* depends on best enforcement. The line between the permissible *copyright* problem and the *preempted state* problem remains subtle, but it’s cleaning up a handful of unintentional contractual injustiquities.

## Conclusion

*Santos v. Kimmel* doesn’t abolish fair use; it simply clarifies one of its controlling factors: *transformative* usage. The court’s decision to look past the subjective intentions of the original or other parties and to anchor fair use in how a reasonable observer perceives a piece empowers more creative novelty while also protecting rights. It keeps the two main axes—intent and context—separate enough to protect a reasonable‑observation requirement.

For third‑party platforms, the verdict creates robust footing for “content cap,” though its impact will still produce a market for advice. For creators of derivative content on streaming platforms and in mainstream economies, the decision will open the door to broader experiments of commentary‑driven stuff. The transformation bar remains a razor‑sharp instrument, and in *Santos*, it’s provided a more precise test—one that is both necessary for fairness and tolerant of innovation. The decision is a reminder that the law remains a living machine that will always require both creators and publishers to interpret the legality of their creative outputs in a constantly evolving middle ground.



# Contractual Pitfalls in the Digital Age: Why Non‑Contracting Third Parties Can’t Hop on a Platform’s Terms of Service  

## Why Santos‑v‑Kimmel Matters  
In the sprawling comedy‑copyright cross‑fire that dropped in the Second Circuit in October 2025, former congressman George Santos tried to seize a late‑night host for using his cameo‑made videos on a national broadcast. The parties fought over whether “Will Santos Say It?” was a fair use parody or a trespass‑to‑copyright, but the real legal fireworks emerged from the *state‑law* claims that Santos planted in his complaint—breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement—based on the terms of service (ToS) of the Cameo platform.

A de‑novo dismissal of those claims underscores a doctrine that has become increasingly relevant as influencers, meme‑bakers, and other content creators make deals on the shoulders of digital platforms. The court’s reasoning spells out, in plain terms, why a platform’s ToS rarely extends to non‑signatories and what that means for anyone who tries to piggy‑back on a contract that only the platform and its direct users entered into.

## The Court’s Core Holding  
In short: **State‑law contract claims that Serra’s analog child‑compliant litigation is based on a digital platform’s ToS fail to state a cause of action because 1) the parties never entered into an agreement that covers the alleged breach and 2) there is a strong presumption that a franchise‑type contract does *not* benefit non‑signatories**. The Second Circuit explicitly pointed to Illinois law for the Terms of Service and New‑York law for the implied‑contract argument, but the reasoning is readily extrapolated to other jurisdictions that share similar principles.

## Third‑Party Beneficiary Doctrine and the “Strong Presumption”  
The civil‑law doctrine of third‑party beneficiaries exists to delineate who actually holds the contractual teeth. Under Illinois, a non‑signatory can only enforce a contract if the contract expressly designates them as a beneficiary or if it’s so apparent that the parties intended that the beneficiary be bound. The court cited *Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp.*, which explains that “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” In Santos’s case, there was no such declaration. He was not a party to the Cameo terms, nor did the platform’s language or the “feeling of a meeting of minds” indicate that Kimmel and Santos had entered into an agreement that made Santos a beneficiary.

For New‑York law, the court referenced *Rocky Point Props.*, noting that an implied‑in‑fact contract requires the essential terms of the parties’ agreement to be clear. Santos’ complaint merely listed the fact that the crew had “supplied” the videos and that the platform's ToS had some stipulations—it never spelled out what the parties intended to be bound by the contract or specifically assigned duties that would affect a third party. The court found the claim under the “burden of the initial pleading” turned over to the defendant’s legal team.

## What This Means for Influencers and Content‑Sharing Platforms  
1. **Contracts Are Promissory, Not Mandatory.** If you are a content creator with a brand partnership announced by an influencer via a platform, you’re in a position analogous to Santos: you have not signed the contract between the influencer and the platform. The platform can enforce its ToS against the influencer, but there is no legal mechanism for the brand to complain about the influencer’s breach of the platform’s terms.  

2. **Third‑Party Beneficiaries Must Be Explicit.** Platform policies that talk about “consultants” or “third‑party partners” must craft language that explicitly recognizes those parties as beneficiaries or imposes obligations that extend beyond the two direct parties. Absent that language, any attempt to sue a non‑signatory for breach of the ToS is likely to fail.  

3. **The “Meeting of Minds” Sledstone.** An implied contract doctrine, often invoked in “silent” or “evidential” correspondence, still requires more than a resumé‑style email thread. Courts will look for *essential terms*—obligation, consideration, or a specific promise that a third party would enforce. If it is merely that “Kimmel’s content is of low quality,” that is insufficient.  

4. **Digital Platforms and Creative Economy Dynamics.** In an ecosystem where a platform’s terms govern a flurry of ancillary creators, there is a candled path for “sub‑sub‑contractors” to try to pose as beneficiaries if they stand to suffer in the marketplace. This decision puts an important boundary—platform ToS can be joined through a *direct* relationship, not a *derivative* one.  

## A Case Study in Content‑Leveraging  
Consider a social media influencer who signs a contract with a brand while simultaneously using a platform’s ToS that obligates them to deliver “original content” to the brand. The brand refuses delivery, claiming the influencer’s underlying contract with the platform forced the influencer to create generic mash‑ups that are no longer original. Under this court’s logic, the brand cannot sue the influencer for breach of the platform contract; they can only sue for breach of the brand contract itself. The platform contract holds up against the influencer, but not the brand.  

This is precisely the boundary the Second Circuit set, leaving the brand with no recourse other than the contract they negotiated directly with the influencer.

## Potential Downstream Effects  
1. **Contract Drafting Revisions.** Platforms will likely revisit their terms to expressly carve out certain beneficiary roles or to limit third‑party enforcement. Some may create companion agreements that allow third parties to opt‑in.  
2. **Guidelines for Brands vs. Influencers.** Brands should be mindful when signing agreements that depend on a platform’s policy, ensuring that any “third‑party obligations” are codified somewhere the brand can invoke directly.  
3. **Legal Research for Influencers.** Agencies that manage creators need to counsel clients that they cannot rely on platform ToS to defeat third‑party claims unless the ToS is expressly written to extend.  
4. **Marketplace Transparency.** If platform ToS are obscure, a brand could find itself out of luck. Transparent, signed, and disseminated agreements will be the industry’s new virtue.

## Analytical Perspective  
The court’s reliance on the Illinois “strong presumption” and the New‑York analysis may seem jurisdictional, but they crystallize a long‑standing principle: *contracts are made between consenting parties.* Even a “boilerplate” ToS is no more binding on an outsider than a business list of terms that did not determine a mutual agreement. The Second Circuit’s balancing outline also implicitly reminds us that the purpose of a contract clause is to satisfy an *actual* contractual need—a need that cannot arise for a party who did not sign into the equation.

There is a lingering concern, though, that platforms could advantageously circumvent this doctrine by crafting “program participation agreements” that involve an omnibus statement recognizing a broad group of beneficiaries. The onus would then shift to brands and creators to wire‑tap the fine print. That means greater diligence for all parties in the content chain.

## Bottom Line  
“Santos v. Kimmel” sends a clear message: *Placing a contract clause in a platform’s ToS is not an invisible safety net for everyone who witnesses the end result.* For content creators, brands, and the platform itself, it underscores the need for explicit agreements that define who is bound and who may sue. As the digital economy keeps churning out new biotech and get‑creative ways to monetize influence, the rules that keep the chain unbroken—explicit, signed, and consistently enforced contracts—remain the most reliable safeguard.



# Fraudulent Inducement Without Loss: The New York Standard and the Limits of Allegations in Platform‑Related Disputes

## Why This Case Matters

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit held that a plaintiff’s fraud claim can be dismissed for failure to plead an “out‑of‑pocket loss” under New York law, even when a seemingly substantial contractual misrepresentation underlies the dispute. The decision is a rare, yet impactful illustration of how statutory standards for fraud interact with the particularities of modern online marketplaces. As platforms like Cameo, Patreon, and TikTok become the venue for contract formation, the *Santos* ruling signals that courts will scrutinize meticulously whether a non‑contracting user has suffered a concrete, recoverable loss before allowing fraud remedies to flourish.

## The Court’s Analytical Path

### The New York “Out‑of‑Pocket Loss” Doctrine

New York law requires a fraud plaintiff to allege a “had‑to‑pay” loss that vanishes when the defrauded party is refunded or replaced. The Supreme Court of New York and the Court of Appeals have repeatedly emphasized that mere transaction costs, opportunity costs, or speculative damages do not satisfy the requirement. *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* (1996) insists that a plaintiff must demonstrate that an actual, identifiable loss incurred “out of pocket.” *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.* (2017) further clarifies that loss must be “real, present, and specific.”

Santos framed his fraud claim around the fact that Jimmy Kimmel & ABC had violated Cameo’s Terms of Service and breached a presumed non‑contractual relationship. He argued that he was “enticed” to perform the videos based on Kimmel’s lure, implying that the subsequent airing of the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* deprived him of future earnings. However, the Second Circuit found that Santos failed to and could not plausibly allege an out‑of‑pocket loss—a loss that would vanish upon refund or replacement. The court pointed out that the complaint did not specify any actual loss, such as a suspended *Cameo* credit, a reduced fee, or any other concrete detriment. Hence, the fraud claim was barred from pleading.

### Tying Platform‑Specific Claims to General Fraud Standards

Santos relied on an alleged breach of "Cameo Terms of Service" as a vehicle to allege fraud. This approach is noteworthy because the Terms of Service, governed by Illinois law, create an implicit contract involving the user. The court’s analysis underscored that, in the absence of an express declaration that the Terms of Service applied to Santos, there was no basis to treat the interaction as a contract. Moreover, the *Cameo* context does not override New York’s fraud standard: the court’s reasoning was grounded in New York law regardless of the contractual domicile of the platform agreement.

## What the Court Sought to Do (and Why It Fell Short)

Santos’s complaint suggested that he suffered a loss from the “cost of the performance” he undertook under false pretenses. Yet, the complaint omitted any statement that an actual fee was paid, that the performance required a material out‑of‑pocket expense, or that the resulting broadcast harmed his earnings in a specified, calculable way. The court concluded that a plaintiff cannot simply allege that the defendant “is liable for misrepresentations” without showing a tangible loss. This outcome is consistent with established New York rulebook: that the fraud insurer’s obligation to show a loss is foundational to any recovery.

## Implications for Future Platform Litigation

### 1. The “Real and Specific” Loss Requirement Is Not Permissible “Mishap” Proof

The *Santos* decision reaffirms that if a plaintiff is attempting to use a platform’s terms or the nature of a digital agreement to allege fraud (or even breach of contract), they must craft a complaint that pinpoints a concrete loss. Courts will not allow a plaintiff to “arm‑chair” a misrepresentation claim simply because the second party published a video or resold an item. The grievance must detail the financial or tangible cost borne by the plaintiff. In practice, this means that any New York‑based or New York‑plaintiff platform dispute must have a robust factual basis for loss—such as a reimbursement record, an actual withheld fee, or a documented shift in revenue streams.

### 2. The Court’s Emphasis on “Non‑Contractual” Claims Is a Red Flag for Platform Users

Santos treated his claim as a “non‑contracting” relationship with the Cameo platform, assuming a sort of implied third‑party benefit. The court decisively rejected that narrative, underscoring Illinois law’s presumption against non‑contracting third‑party benefits. This doctrine is likely to apply more broadly to online marketplaces where users rarely sign explicit contracts or where contract terms are implicit in user agreements. Plaintiffs who wish to invoke breach of contract or fraudulent inducement must therefore either prove an explicit contract (or a clear, enforceable expectation) or fall back on a different remedy, such as tort-based claims for defamation or verbal agreements.

### 3. Defendants May Argue, and Likely Will, That No Loss Has Occurred in Similar Cases

Given the *Santos* precedent, defendants will be better positioned to argue that a plaintiff’s alleged loss is intangible, speculative, or not “out of pocket.” If they can demonstrate that the plaintiff committed no monetary error or that any damages were purely “qualitative” (e.g., loss of reputation), then a fraud claim will likely be at a disadvantage. For instance, if a content creator alleges that their videos were misappropriated on a platform and that it violated moral rights, the defendant can argue that the plaintiff has not shown a concrete financial loss to satisfy the New York standard.

### 4. The “Platform Agreement” Preemption Tactic Becomes Riskier

Santos’s attempt to invoke preemption of state law by the Copyright Act is textbook for eminent judicial deference to federal law. The court declined to analyze the preemption issue because the factual pleading failed. But it left open the possibility that, under different circumstances, a state‑law contract claim might indeed be preempted by federal copyright or intellectual‑property statutes. This tension will likely magnify in platforms where user‑generated content is both a commercial product and a platform for artistic expression.

## A Critical View of the Court’s Reasoning

While the court’s adherence to New York’s fraud standard is legally sound, it invites a cautionary pause for plaintiffs in other jurisdictions where the “out‑of‑pocket loss” bar is less strict. The *Santos* ruling calls into question the applicability of a purely fact‑based approach when the “loss” could arguably be measured in future earnings or reputational damage—particularly in the gig economy. A more flexible approach might preserve an essential check on fraud claims in digital economies, where actual out‑of‑pocket losses are often hard to quantify.

Additionally, the court did not squarely address the interplay between the “fair use” defense and fraud. By deeming the copyright claims fail at the pleading stage, the court sidestepped an opportunity to elaborate on whether a platform's use of user content, even if considered fair, might nonetheless constitute fraud if the user was misled about the downstream dissemination of her content. That balancing act remains open for future litigation.

## Looking Ahead: Practical Takeaways for Legal Practitioners

1. **Spell Out Losses Early** – In pleadings that rely on platform-based allegations, provide documentary evidence of fees paid, refunds, or any actual financial shortfall. Courts will not “invent” a loss out of thin air.
2. **Shift to State‑Law Claims Where Appropriate** – If a platform’s user agreement is ambiguous, plaintiffs might better focus on state‑law claims such as breach of contract or torts (e.g., defamation) that require less stringent loss requirements.
3. **Avoid “Non‑Contracting” Tribulations** – Do not rely on an implied non‑contractual relationship with a platform unless the user agreement expressly provides for third‑party benefit.
4. **Prepare for Preemption Analysis** – When dealing with IP litigation in platform contexts, be ready to address whether federal copyright law might preempt the state‑law claim.

## Conclusion

*Santos v. Kimmel* is an instructive example of how a traditional tort principle—fraud’s requirement for an out‑of‑pocket loss—survives scrutiny even in the modern context of online contracts and digital content. The case circumscribes the remedial utility of fraud claims in platform disputes, underscoring the need for plaintiffs to anchor their allegations in concrete, measurable losses. As online marketplaces continue to blur the lines between contractual and non‑contractual relationships, courts will inevitably revisit these doctrines, perhaps refining the loss requirement or tightening the definition of “non‑contracting” claims. For now, the *Santos* ruling remains a sober reminder that digital negligence and misrepresentation are not a free pass to a recovery; the law still demands an evidentiary scaffold.



# Preemption, Fair Use, and State‑Law Claims: Why the Second Circuit Kept the Copyright Act King

## Why This Case Matters

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit confronted a twice‑delicate legal tightrope: a former congressman, George Santos, sued late‑night host Jimmy Kimmel and the Walt Disney Company for “copyright infringement” after Kimmel aired custom‑made video messages Santos had produced for Cameo subscribers. The district court dismissed Santos’s claims, both on the grounds that fair use barred any copyright infringement claim and that his state‑law contract and fraud claims failed to state a cause of action. The appeals court, who was the one to lock in that reasoning, reaffirmed the dismissal with a decisive nod to the primacy of federal copyright law in this kind of commercial dispute.

This case is a prismatic test of three doctrines that frequently collide in the digital‑media age: (1) the *fair‑use* defense that lets commentators, critics, and parodists flourish; (2) *copyright preemption*, the idea that the Copyright Act can top state‑law remedies; and (3) the traditional *state‑law contract* framework that provides remedies for breach, fraud, and implied‑in‑fact agreements. By confirming that fair use can defeat a copyright claim *even at the pleading stage* and that state‑law claims can be preended by the Copyright Act only where the claims are fanned up, the Second Circuit reaffirmed the hierarchy that keeps the federal copyright ceiling high in market‑oriented disputes. In doing so, it wrought a clear doctrinal signal: federal copyright law will remain the primary shield against “co‑optation” of creative works by those who wish to repackage them under a new banner, even when the plaintiff seeks both federal and state remedy.

## The Fair‑Use Armature – Rehashing the Four‑Factor Dance

The appellate court’s language in the brief is nothing new. But the case pushes the four‑factor test into an arena where the “purpose and character” of a use is clearly transitional: Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segment was a parody and a satire. The court points out that Santos’s own complaint, citing his inclusive tone of “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” made clear a difference between a “self‑styled” moral purpose and a “commercially motivated” use.  This focus aligns with *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* and the Supreme Court’s own acknowledgment that fair‑use determination turns on “what a reasonable observer thinks” rather than the subjective purpose of the user. The Second Circuit leans heavily on these precedents, finding that the transformation—Kimmel using Santos’s intimate videos to make a mocking commentary—is enough to tilt the scales.

In the *fourth factor* discussion, the court treats the absence of market harm as a categorical rebuff to the “potential market impact” argument. This is key: Santos’s complaint did not allege that Kimmel, by publishing the videos, deprived Santos of what a licensing market might have been. The court notes that this silliness is routinely dismissed in parody cases. Not new material, per se, but a *new idea and commentary*—the necessary quality to carve out a fair use shield. In short, the fair‑use armature works as the court expected, and it remains a key engine in protecting second‑tier work that comments on or critiques the original.

## Preemption Fever: Where the Copyright Act Shields the Market

The case’s second core pillar is the doctrine of preemption. In *Melendez v. Sirius XM*, the Second Circuit had already carved out a panel that “copyright infringement claims are not automatically preempted by the Copyright Act” when the underlying claims are qualitatively different. Santos’s claim for breach of contract under the Cameo Terms of Service, and the contractual claims for fraudulent inducement, closely resemble *ordinary contract law* rather than copyright infringement. However, the court found that the claims were *not* founded on the same contractual relationship that the Copyright Act would preempt; the complaint never identified an *express* contractual agreement between Santos and the parties. Without a contract that the parties created for each other, the preemption argument crumbles. 

The appellate court’s decision aligns with the principle that preemption applies *only* when a state‑law claim *depends upon the copyright itself*. In other words, a “breach of the moral or contractual obligations under the Copyright Act” can be preempted, but *ordinary* breach of a *non‑copyright contract* cannot. The court’s stance also reflects the older analysis in *Territory v. Gucci Apparel* (example, not actual case). It shields the uniform application of federal protection while not denying that a non‑copyright contract can be enforced at the state level, albeit subject to statutory limitations. As the court consciously rests on this logic, it provides a useful map for future litigants: if you can prove that the underlying claim is *intent‑to‑infringe*, then preemption is a sure‑fire.

## The Rise, and Limits, of the State‑Law Contract Defense

With the preemption bar notch out of the way, the court still rejuts Santos’s state‑law claims—both breach of contract and fraudulent inducement. Its failure rests on *failure to state* a claim. For a breach of contract claim to survive the pleading stage, the plaintiff must articulate sufficiently clear “essential terms” that constitute a contract (see *Rocky Point Props.*). Santos did not, and the court also found he was no party to the Cameo terms, which a typical third‑party contract prevention doctrine (the “well‑known presumption against benefits to non‑parties”) would hold by default. 

Turning to fraudulent inducement, the court required Santos to allege an *actual loss* at the pleading stage (a standard from *Lama Holding Co.*) and found none. The federal courts have historically framed fraud as an “actual victim” hypothesis in the absence of clear damages, which in many cases leaves the claim in limbo until after discovery or evidence is laid out. In this scenario, the lack of tangible out‑of‑pocket loss, coupled with a simple *commentary and parody* model, effectively wipes out the fraud claim.

In both cases, the ruling indicates that the courts are not willing to use state law to tack on an additional remedy that is redundant or unnecessary because the federal copyright framework already covers the matter. The importance of the *failure to state* motion here is that it underscores how the state‑law system can index itself directly to federal copyright protection yet still maintain its own requirements and fences.


## Doctrinal Ramifications and Future Lines of Inquiry

*Inherit Error with the Copyright Act:*  The court’s decision confirms the federal copyright Act’s preemptive power but clarifies that it does not extend to *any* state‑law claim. The preemption clause under 17 U.S.C. § 301 is fundamentally a “blanket” defense that only operates when the *copyright dispute* itself is framed in state‑law terms. A claim that merely *touches* the underlying contractual relationship (e.g., misuse of a creative content in a non‑commercial project) may circumvent preemption if it can be clearly separated from a copyrightory. The appellate ruling adds nuance in this area: it illustrates that preemption is a *substantive* test, not a mechanical one. For litigants The court's precedent suggests that they must make sure "truly different" elements get them to stay outside of the copyright sphere.

*The Four‑Factor Dance in the Digital Age:*  The hard-and-fast rule that "Santos’s only claim was a parody‑like commentary" reinforces the *fair use* message that “parodic content can reduce or curb the more covert forces of that marketplace”. The court’s refusal to give Santos a fair‑use provision to protect the current vantage point (and the content he is suing on), showcases that the courts still see *creative reinterpretation* as a distinct category.  Future cases will reach back to this decision to argue that *self‑illustrated preparatory use* (i.e., a user creating content for commentary) is still a fully protected transformation.

*Contract Claims Under the Blockchain*:  The decision shows the trend that courts will aggressively challenge any talk about *implied* contracts that have not been expressly negotiated, especially when the parties are not part of an explicit agreement. They will also re-check the elements that apply to the state claim.
