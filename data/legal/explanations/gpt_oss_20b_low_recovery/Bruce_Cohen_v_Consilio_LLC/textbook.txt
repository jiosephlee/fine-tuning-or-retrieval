Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# I. Background and Procedural History

The court’s opinion is anchored in the factual and procedural chronology that led the parties to the district court’s summary‑judgment decision. Understanding the parties’ relationship, the specific employment arrangement, the employer’s policy change, the timing of the lawsuit, the post‑filing settlement, and the agreement that the court decide all claims before discovery is essential for grasping the appellate analysis that follows.

## Parties

Bruce Cohen is a licensed attorney who, in 2018, joined Consilio, LLC, a company that contracts with legal‑service clients to provide document‑review work. Consilio’s Minnesota offices employ a mixture of attorneys and paralegals to read, index, and analyze discovery documents. Cohen’s role was that of an hourly, licensed‑attorney document reviewer. Consilio’s corporate structure is that of a limited liability company, and its employment relationship with Cohen is governed by the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA).

## Employment Facts

Cohen began working in 2018 as an hourly employee whose hourly rate was set by the Minnesota bar licensing regulations. His duties involved reviewing large volumes of legal documents for the firm’s clients, and he was paid at the rate established for licensed attorneys doing document review. On the basis of his status, Cohen was covered by Minnesota’s wage‑and‑hour statutes, which apply to hourly licensed attorneys. Consilio’s policy at the time of Cohen’s hire allowed for a “premium” overtime rate for hours worked beyond normal business hours.

## Premium Overtime Policy Change

In August 2019, Consilio sent an email to Cohen and other Minnesota‑based hourly licensed‑attorney reviewers. The email announced the institution of a new premium‑overtime policy effective that month. The change removed the premium overtime rate; instead, all hours worked—including overtime—would be compensated at the employee’s base pay rate. The message did not contain any statutory language or official policy manual; it functioned as a unilateral change to the employment terms. This change was perceived by Cohen and the other reviewers as a reduction of compensation above the statutory minimum.

## Filing of Suit

In August 2020, Cohen filed suit against Consilio in federal court, bringing claims on behalf of himself and a putative class of similarly situated employees. He alleged that the 2019 policy change violated several Minnesota wage‑and‑hour statutes: the MPWA (including §§ 181.101 and 181.171), the MFLSA (including §§ 177.25 and 177.27), and the MWTA (including §§ 181.032(f), 181.171, and 177.27). The proposed remedies were wage restitution (overtime), liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief requiring the revocation of the policy.

## Settlement Stipulations

Prior to the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling, the parties reached a partial settlement. Consilio agreed to pay Cohen and the other Minnesota licensed‑attorney reviewers a sum of $256,010.01, which the parties agreed would cover all outstanding overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA claim. Cohen’s share of that payment was $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. The settlement contained a stipulation that both parties acknowledged that Cohen had been paid for all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed. The parties could not agree on whether penalties were payable to Cohen; they remained in dispute over: $172,080 in MPWA average‑daily‑wage penalties, $42,000 in MFLSA penalties, and $42,000 in MWTA penalties.

## Agreement on Summary‑Judgment Timing

Both parties convened in a post‑settlement conference and mutually agreed that the court should hear and decide all remaining claims—MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA—on the merits before any additional discovery or litigation moved forward. They understood that a grant of summary judgment in full would resolve the case entirely. This agreement, embedded in the settlement stipulation, directly influenced the district court’s procedural posture: the court was instructed to grant summary judgment on the claims before the parties had a full slate of evidence.

## District Court Ruling

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. The court concluded that (1) Consilio’s policy change was not actionable under the MPWA because it had not been enforced by the Minnesota Commissioner, thereby precluding penalties, (2) the MFLSA claim was moot after the settlement payment was made, and (3) the MWTA claim was dismissed due to lack of statutory penalties. The district court entered judgment for Consilio and allowed the case to be fully resolved.

Bruce Cohen filed an appeal at the Eighth Circuit on October 6, 2025, seeking reversal of the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims and challenging the determination of mootness and standing with respect to penalties and attorney’s fees. This chapter establishes the factual and procedural context that the appellate court must review before applying the law to the specific statutory claims.

# II. The MPWA Claim: Statutory Penalties and Commissioner Authority

This chapter explains the court’s analysis of Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) as it applies to Cohen’s claim for average‑daily‑wage penalties. It follows the path the court took—from the statutory text to the interpretive principles, to the procedural mechanics of commissioner‑driven penalties—and shows why the court held that only the Commissioner may seek such penalties and consequently found Cohen’s claim barred.

## Statutory Prose: MPWA §181.101(a) and §181.171(i) – Employer Liability, Commissioner Role

Section 181.171(i) establishes the MPWA’s remedial framework: when an employer “is found to have violated” a wage‑payment provision, it is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” and the aggrieved party may recover “civil penalties or damages … provided for in the section violated.” The statute further refers to § 181.101(a) as the conduit for these penalties. Section 181.101(a) is the operative text for this portion of the MPWA. It specifies that an “employer who is found to have violated” the wage‑payment provisions must “pay any wages, commissions, or other payment due to an employee” and, if payment is not made, allows the Claimant to “serve a demand for payment of the wages” on behalf of an employee. Following the demand, the Commissioner may, **if payment is not made within ten days**, “charge and collect the wages earned … and a penalty.” The section ends by requiring that any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.

Two elements are critical:

1. **The Commissioner, not the employee, may invoke the penalty provision.** The statute obliges the Commissioner to act only after a statutory demand is served, and only the Commissioner may thereafter collect a penalty.
2. **The penalty is payable to the employee.** The mandate that any collec­ted funds be paid to the employee guarantees that the employee’s receipt of the penalty is contingent on the Commissioner’s action.

The MPWA does not contain any parallel mechanism that allows an employee to unilaterally seek a penalty. Because § 181.171 passes the power to the Commissioner for discretionary enforcement, any claim for penalties brought directly by an employee is barred by the statute’s structure.

## Plain‑Language Interpretation of “Penalties” and Legislative Intent

The court applied the plain‑language rule, which treats statutory words as conveying their ordinary, ordinary‐lance meaning when the language is clear and unambiguous. In *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, the Minnesota Supreme Court held that “when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” The court noted that the MPWA’s coverage of penalties is explicit: the Commissioner is the sole actor authorized to seek them. No statutory wording suggests an employee’s authority to do so. Therefore, the plain‑language rule supports the conclusion that the legislature intended only the Commissioner to enforce these penalties.

Additionally, the court considered the legislative practice in analogous statutes. *Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch.* recognized that the statutory framework for penalties here is miniature to the general scheme of employer penalties, reinforcing the notion that the Legislature’s design—through the Commissioner—was purposefully restrictive.

## Commissioner’s Procedural Power: Demand for Payment, Ten‑Day Rule, Collection of Wages and Penalty

The court mapped the Commissioner’s procedure as follows:

1. **Demand for Payment.** The Commissioner issues a demand on behalf of an employee for unpaid wages or commissions. The demand is the statutory trigger for the potential penalty.
2. **Ten‑Day Window.** If the employer fails to pay within ten days of the demand, the Commissioner can collect the wages owed, plus a penalty.
3. **Collection and Distribution.** Any amount collected by the Commissioner is to be paid directly to the employee. The statutory language makes no provision for the employee to recover a penalty through a civil action separate from the Commissioner’s enforcement.

Cohen’s allegation that the statute authorizes an employee to sue for average‑daily‑wage penalties violates this chain. The MPWA never furnishes the employee with an independent or parallel avenue to invoke the penalty provision; instead, all enforcement is funneled through the Commissioner. The court therefore found that Cohen’s claim was not supported by the statutory text.

## Legislative Context: Statutes §181.13(a) and §181.14 – Authority for Employees to Recover Penalties in Other Contexts

To illustrate the Legislature’s capacity to grant employees penalty rights, the court cited §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14. These provisions create a scenario where a **dismissed** or **resigned** employee, upon paying a demand, may recover unpaid wages or commissions *and* “may charge and collect a penalty.” The custody of the penalty‑enabling language in these statutes demonstrates that the Legislature could, had it wished, have provided an employee‑initiated penalty scheme under the MPWA. However, because the MPWA’s wording is different—specifically attributing penalty authority to the Commissioner—it reveals that the Legislature purposely limited penalty authority to the Commissioner under the MPWA. Thus, the employee-initiated approach is not implied by the MPWA.

## Courthypical Cases: Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., Hull v. ConvergeOne

The court drew from three decisions that ground its reasoning:

1. **Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.** (plain‑language rule) – affirmation that statutory clarity should be respected.
2. **Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch.** – acknowledgement that the statute’s design is conclusive and that the Commissioner holds exclusive penalty authority.
3. **Hull v. ConvergeOne** – confirms that the Commissioner’s collection of penalties is intended to be passed to the employee; the court emphasized that the employer cannot retain the penalty, and thus the employer’s liability is limited to the wages earned, not a penalty.

These cases confirm the structural pattern of the MPWA: employer liability is met through wage payment (and possibly liquidated damages); civil penalties are a discretionary power of the Commissioner, not an employee claim.

## Court’s Conclusion: Penalties Unavailable to Cohen; District Court Error Not Found

The court concluded that Cohen could not seek average‑daily‑wage penalties because the MPWA expressly provides that the Commissioner, not the employee, is empowered to levy such penalties. Cohen’s claim relied on a statutory reading that the MPWA authorizes employee‑initiated penalties, which the court found was unsupported by the statutory language, the plain‑language rule, and the broader legislative framework. Therefore, the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio on the MPWA penalty claim was correct: Cohen could not, by law, sue for those penalties.

The court affirmed the district court’s ruling on the MPWA claim while vacating only the portion of the judgment that addressed Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim, which the court later found potentially moot. This partial affirmation shows that the court’s reasoning was tightly linked to the statutory requirements of § 181.101 and § 181.171.

## Implications for Employee‑Initiated Penalty Claims

Employees seeking MPWA penalties must rely on the Commissioner’s enforcement mechanism. An employee cannot file a direct civil action for penalties under § 181.101; instead, the proper procedure is to invoke the statutory demand on behalf of the employee, allowing the Commissioner to act. The court’s decision underscores that the MPWA is a *statistical* penalty scheme, not an *employee*‑directed remedy. Future litigation will likely involve challenges to the Commissioner’s authority or to the application of the ten‑day rule, rather than to the existence of employee‑initiated penalties.

# III. The MFLSA Claim: Standing, Mootness, and Federal Jurisdiction

## Summary‑Judgment Overview: De Novo Review and Karlen

When a court grants a summary‑judgment motion, the nonmoving party may file a brief and the moving party may file a reply. The appellate court reviews the district court’s decision “de novo,” meaning it looks at the evidence and inferences anew, as if no prior ruling had been made. This standard requires the appellate court to consider the record in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. The Ninth‑Circuit precedent that most directly governs this analysis is *Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc.*, 766 F.3d 863 (8th Cir. 2014). *Karlen* affirms that *when reviewing a district court’s summary‑judgment ruling, the appellate court must view the evidence “in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party,” then decide whether the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law*. The court likewise notes that the appellate court’s review is premised on the same standard that applies to the district court. For *Placzek v. Mayo Clinic* and *Green Plains Otter Tail*, the court reiterated that the same “no genuine issue of material fact” test governs de‑novo review.

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the district court granted summary judgment on all three claims. The appellate court therefore reviewed that order under the *Karlen* standard. The substantive remaining issue concerned the MFLSA statutory penalty claim. The court had to decide whether, in light of the evidence, the MFLSA penalties were “materially disputed” and whether the opinion should have denied summary judgment on that claim.

## MFLSA Penalties: Statutory Basis and Distinction from MPWA Penalties

The Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) authorizes employers who infringe on certain pay provisions to owe “liquidated damages” and, in some circumstances, statutory penalties. *Section 177.27* of the MFLSA contains a statute‑of‑limitations provision and a list of penalty provisions. The statute makes clear that penalties, if awarded, are payable to an employee *but* in some contexts the penalty provisions make it explicit that the employee “is entitled to a civil penalty” after the complaint is filed. The details differ from the MPWA, where the penalty for a wage‑payment violation is limited to penalties payable only to a commissioner, who may collect and distribute the penalties to employees. The MFLSA is a “civil‑action” statute that empowers an employee to sue an employer for unpaid wages, liquidated damages, and penalties *directly*. The court notes that this naming distinction matters when it applies the *Friends of the Earth* standing doctrine.

In *Cohen*, the plaintiff argued that because he had been paid all overdue overtime wages and liquidated damages, the only remaining reversible claim was the statutory penalties authorized by the MFLSA. The district court suppressed the MFLSA penalty claim, finding no dispute over such penalties. The appellate court agreed that the MFLSA provisions authorize employees to seek penalties; however, the court declined to find that the district court had erred entirely because the *standing* and *mootness* issues were unresolved.

## Standing Doctrine under *Friends of the Earth* and *Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n*

Under *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff can sue for civil penalties under an environmental statute even if a government agency cannot sue to recover those penalties. *Friends* specifically addressed the *Clean Water Act* and recognized that a private plaintiff could still recover civil penalties when the agency was barred from doing so. The court underscored that a plaintiff must demonstrate a *personal interest* at the outset of the litigation *for the relief he seeks*. The *Davis v. Federal Election Commission* analysis (554 U.S. 724) clarified that *standing* is a continuous requirement—once a plaintiff’s *personal interest* is lost, the claim becomes moot.

Cohen’s standing for MFLSA penalties depends on whether he claims personal injury from the alleged violation. The statute itself does not require a plaintiff to suffer physical injury; more pertinently, it requires a plaintiff to suffer a *financial loss* in the form of unpaid wages. Because Cohen has already been paid all “overtime wages” and “liquidated damages” claimed under the MFLSA, Cohen’s *personal financial interest* in the penalty claim remains uncertain. If the policy that gave rise to the penalty claim (the premium overtime policy) has been rescinded, Cohen can no longer argue that the injury or potential injury that motivated the penalty claim is continued. The court held that the *Friends* standing requirement demands that a plaintiff’s *financial injury* be *present* and not merely *potential*.

In *Cohen*, the appellate court concluded that the plaintiff had not sufficiently demonstrated that he retains a continuing personal interest in the penalties, and that the district court’s refusal to address the standing question left the MFLSA penalty claim in analytical limbo. The court, therefore, vacated the summary‑judgment ruling that had dismissed the MFLSA penalty claim.

## Mootness Analysis: Policy Persistence and the Continuing Interest

The *Friends* decision also established that a statutory penalty claim can become moot if the alleged violation is “clearly no longer present” or “cannot reasonably be expected to recur.” The court cited *Friends* assurances that *“the ultimate question is whether the alleged violation exists”* and aligned with *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283 (1982), which noted that a voluntary termination of the challenged activity does not automatically hobble a court’s jurisdiction. The distinction lies in whether the termination is “absolutely clear” that the violation will not recur.

Here, the parties agreed that Consilio had stopped paying overtime premiums and had compensated Cohen for all wages and liquidated damages. However, the court noted that the *policy that allegedly violated the MFLSA*—the “new premium overtime policy” in August 2019—was still technically in effect until a policy mute. The contract to pay “base salary” for all hours had been implemented in August 2020, but whether the policy had *absolutely* ceased remained in dispute. With the policy possibly still in place, the MFLSA penalty claim was not yet moot. The appellate court, however, concluded that the existing evidence was insufficient to determine whether the policy had truly ended, leaving the judgment on that point uncertain.

Accordingly, the court addressed the *moistness doctrine* by stating that the *continued existence of the punitive conduct* is a *necessary element* of a valid MFLSA penalty claim. The court therefore vacated the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on the MFLSA penalty claim to allow the district court to further evaluate whether the policy has been rescinded and whether Cohen retains a personal interest.

## Jurisdiction Concerns: Federal Courts Cannot Retain Cases Lacking a Continuing Interest

The Supreme Court has made clear that *federal courts lack standing to maintain jurisdiction over cases in which there is no continuing interest*—i.e., when a plaintiff's personal stake has been obliterated. In *Friends*, the Court noted that *“the jurisdiction ends as soon as the plaintiff's interested fails to exist”*. The appellate court echoed that *“it is proper to dismiss a claim that lacks a continuing interest”* and that *“the federal court is not licensed to keep a case that has lost the elements it was licensed to decide.”*

Applying that principle to *Cohen*, the appellate court found that the MFLSA penalty claim’s viability was inextricably tied to whether Cohen had a *continuing interest* in obtaining penalties. The fact that Consilio had already paid Cohen for overtime wages and liquidated damages did not eliminate the potential for a penalty claim if the unlawful policy remained. The court held, therefore, that a *mootness‑related lack of continuing interest* could arise only if the policy had been rescinded. Because the record did not resolve that point, the appellate court could not dismiss the claim outright but could not affirm it either. The court thus decided to vacate the district court’s decision to grant summary judgment in the MFLSA claim and remand for further proceedings.

## Court’s Action: Vacate Summary Judgment and Remand for Mootness Determination

The appellate court’s final order carried the following key actions:
1. The district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on the MPWA and MWTA claims was affirmed, because those claims were either barred by statute or lacked standing/authority.
2. The district court’s summary‑judgment order on the MFLSA penalty claim was vacated because the claim’s standing and mootness had not been adequately addressed.
3. The appellate court remanded the case for the district court to *first* determine whether Consilio’s punitive policy is still in force and whether Cohen retains a continuing personal interest in the penalty claim. The remand explicitly requires the district court to resolve the mootness/standing question before any further decision on the MFLSA penalties can be made.

Thus, the episode illustrates that federal courts will not affirm or affirm by default a penalty claim that is essentially rendered moot by the cessation of the wrongful conduct, or denied it hastily if the plaintiff’s standing is uncertain. The appellate court preserved the decision **only** when it had collected adequate evidence and legal analysis, and it remanded the remaining unresolved distributed roles.

In sum, *Cohen* underscores the necessity of satisfying the **standing** and **moistness** requirements under *Friends* when enforcing statutory penalties, and it delineates how a federal court must act when those requirements collide with procedural judgment.”

# IV. The MWTA Claim: Notice Requirements and Lack of Penalty Authority  

## MWTA statutory framework: §181.032(f), (d)(4) – notice of policy changes  

The Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), codified at Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f) and (d)(4), imposes an employer–specific duty to provide *notice of a change in overtime policy* to employees. Section 181.032(f) states that an employer **must** notify employees of any *substantial* alteration of its wage‑payment method, whereas subsection (d)(4) expands the duty to “require” that *notice be given* for changes affecting overtime. The statute, however, stops short of specifying a *penalty* that an employee may pursue for non‑compliance. Indeed, unlike the MPWA and the MFLSA, the MWTA contains only remedial provisions for unpaid wages and liquidated damages; it does not create *civil penalties* that are enforceable by private litigants.

Thus, while the MWTA requires employers to give notice of policy changes, the statutory language does not authorize employers to be subject to a civil penalty if they fail to do so. The primary remedy under the MWTA has historically been a claim for unpaid wages, not a punitive penalty.

## Cohen’s alleged violations: failure to provide required notice  

Cohen argued that Consilio’s August 2019 policy, which eliminated overtime premium pay and clarified that all hours would be paid at base rate, was a *substantial* change in overtime policy. He further contended that Consilio did not provide the required notice stipulated by § 181.032(f) or (d)(4) to employees, thereby violating the MWTA. Accordingly, he sought civil penalties for the alleged statutory violation.

Because Consilio had already settled with Cohen on unpaid wages and liquidated damages, the core issue was whether the *absence* of notice constituted a *penalizable*, non‑compensatory violation capable of yielding civil penalties.

## Plaintiff’s arguments: claim of negligence and penalties  

In his complaint, Cohen framed the MWTA claim as a *negligence* action—an abuse of its liability framework alleging that Consilio’s failure to comply with the notice requirement harmed him. In doing so, he also sought *civil penalties* that the MWTA in itself does not provide. His argument hinged on the notion that a failure to provide notice justified punitive damages, even though the statutes merely prescribe no‑fault wage‑related remedies.

The complaint did not present any statutory authority or case law specifically allowing a private plaintiff to recover civil penalties under the MWTA. Moreover, Cohen failed to articulate why court‑approved remedial clauses—limited to unpaid wages and liquidated damages—should be interpreted to include punitive penalties.

## Court’s limited discussion: no statutory penalty authority identified  

The district court acknowledged the existence of the MWTA’s notice requirement but did not elaborate on the statutory remedy beyond its complainer. Importantly, the court **did not** find any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under the MWTA, and it did not rely on extraneous authority to infer such a penalty. The brief, therefore, contains *only* a superficial reference to the MWTA’s notice obligation with no supporting analysis of potential penalties.

Because the statutory language fails to prescribe any penalty, the district court’s dismissal of the claim was grounded in the premise that the court had *no* statutory basis to entertain a penalty request. It therefore steered the discussion toward the plaintiff’s *lack of argument*: Cohen did not present any justification or authority supporting his claim for penalties under the MWTA.

## Application of waiver and dismissal precedent: Milligan v. City of Red Oak  

The court used *the doctrine of waiver* to justify dismissal, citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000). In Milligan, the Ninth Circuit held that a plaintiff who *“did not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority”* was effectively *waiving* the claim. The court emphasized that the party’s brief was silent on the critical issue of statutory penalty authority and that the district court could not, in the absence of any argument, alter the statutory framework.

Because Cohen’s brief contained no cogent statutory or case‑law argument to extend the MWTA’s notice requirement to include civil penalties, the court held that Cohen had *waived* that claim.  Milligan is invoked to show that a party may not simply insert an unsubstantiated claim into a brief and expect the court to treat it as viable; the court must rely on documented authority or persuasive argumentation.

Therefore, the district court’s ruling to dismiss was affirmed. The court and the appellant agree that no statutory authority exists under the MWTA to sustain a claim for penalties. Because the plaintiff had not argued for such penalties, the claim was deemed waived, and the court appropriately dismissed it.

# V. Injunctive Relief and Procedural Economy

The appellate court’s decision to leave Cohen’s injunctive‑relief claim unanswered was the result of a strict application of procedural rules rather than a substantive denial of his request.  Below we unpack the facts of the claim, the procedural misstep that led to its dismissal, and the appellate rule that governed the court’s treatment of the issue.

## Cohen’s Original Injunctive Relief Request: Rescind 2019 Policy

When Cohen filed his lawsuit in August 2020, he identified three avenues of relief: (1) unpaid overtime wages and liquidated damages, (2) statutory penalties, and (3) an injunctive order that required Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy.  The 2019 policy, communicated to employees by email in July 2019, eliminated overtime premium pay and stipulated that all hours would be paid at base rate.  Cohen’s complaint alleged that this policy violated Minnesota’s wage‑law statutes and demanded that the court “issue an affirmative injunction directing Consilio to cease the illegal payroll practices.”

This request was a direct irritant to Consilio’s business model; it would require the company to reinstate overtime premiums for a sizable segment of its workforce, a cost the company expressly promised to abandon.  By seeking a court‑mandated change to a policy that had already been in effect for more than a year, Cohen aimed to enforce the statutory rule that employees are entitled to overtime compensation at a rate equal to the base wage plus one‑and‑a‑halftimes.

## Later Change of Relief Sought: Withdrawal of Policy in Summary‑Judgment Brief

Shortly before the district court heard the summary‑judgment motions, the two parties negotiated a settlement.  Consilio agreed to pay Cohen the full amount that Cohen claimed was owed under the MFLSA and the MPWA, and the parties entered a stipulation confirming that “Cohen had been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit.”  The settlement did not touch the injunctive‑relief component, but the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.”

Cohen’s counsel subsequently introduced a new argument in the summary‑judgment briefing—a “withdrawal of the 2019 policy.”  The brief, which appeared as a supplemental motion, did not re‑file a complaint asking for the same injunctive relief that was originally pleaded.  Instead, it simply asked the court to acknowledge that the 2019 policy “has been withdrawn” and that any residual policy changes would be moot.  Because the injunctive claim was no longer the subject of a properly amending action, the appellate court treated the request as an oralally introduced, “new” claim that did not meet the procedural threshold for appellate review.

## Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a): Amendment, New Claims, and the Appellate Rule

Rule 15(a) governs a party’s right to amend pleadings.  It allows a party to file an amendment in a timely manner—or to “add claims, defenses, or parties” that “result in the admission of a material change” to the case.  However, a new claim introduced without a concomitant amendment to the original pleading—whether via amendment or a new complaint—constitutes a procedural error that the court can overlook only if the court deems the claim “patently unfair” under 28 U.S.C. § 1297(c).

In *InfoDeli, LLC* (2025) the 8th Circuit made clear that a request for a new type of relief—particularly an injunctive order that was not pleaded at the start of the action—must arise from an amending complaint or a new complaint, not an opposition brief.  The court held that “a petitioner who introduces a novel claim or a different type of relief without filing a correctly punctuated amendment or new complaint is effectively losing the race to do so.  The pleadings are the one true source of claimable rights, and the appellate court is bound by what the district court had jurisdiction to adjudicate.”

Consequently, Cohen’s brief did not satisfy the procedural requirements for an appellate review of the injunctive relief claim.  The court did not have jurisdiction to decide a claim that was never properly brought in the original complaint or any annexed amendment.

## Attorney’s Commentary on Procedural Economy: InfoDeli, LLC Case Support

The dissenting opinion in *InfoDeli, LLC* emphasized the importance of procedural economy.  The court’s decision to “clamp a new claim that was not properly pleaded” protects the parties from an endless cycle of pleadings and motions at the appellate level, which would erode the finality of judgments and squander judicial resources.  The dissent argued that proper procedural conduct—filing an amending complaint within the statutory period—would have let the appellate court examine whether the injunctive request had merit.  Nonetheless, the court expressed respect for the principle that “things practiced in a lawsuit are best practiced when they are well‑begun,” thereby underscoring that new claims presented only at the appellate level are not admissible unless the parties were “properly offered” the opportunity to bring them earlier in the litigation.

From Cohen’s perspective, the absence of an amending complaint meant that the injunction was a *post‑hoc* change in the nature of the relief sought—moving from an injunction to rescind the policy to a withdrawal that effectively no longer demanded injunctive action.  The appellate court deemed this change insufficient for review because the parties had not formally amended the complaint to reflect the revised relief request.

## Court’s Decision: Issue Not Yet Ready, Not Considered on Appeal

Given these procedural facts, the appellate court held that it could not address Cohen’s injunctive‑relief claim.  In its opinion the court noted that the claim “was not properly pleaded in either the original complaint or a subsequent amendment; it was introduced only in the summarily‑judgment briefing, which does not confer appellate jurisdiction.”  Consequently, the argument was omitted from the appellate analysis, and the district court’s order to dismiss the claim was affirmed.

### Recap

1. **Original Pleadings** – Cohen showed injunctive relief (rescinding the 2019 policy) as a claim in the complaint.
2. **Settlement & Brief** – The parties settled the wage and damages aspects; Cohen introduced a withdrawal of the policy in a brief, not an amended complaint.
3. **Rule 15(a)** – Only an amendment or new complaint may introduce new claims; briefs alone are insufficient.
4. **InfoDeli, LLC** – The appellate court cited *InfoDeli* citing the need for procedural economy and proper pleadings.
5. **Outcome** – The appellate court declined to review the injunctive claim, affirming the district court’s dismissal for failure to amend properly.

By understanding the interplay between the original pleading, the later settlement, and the procedural rules governing amendment and new claims, students can see why the appellate court made its decision and how procedural choices shape the ultimate fate of a claim.

# VI. Overall Holding, Remand and Significance

The Court’s opinion resolves the case in a mixed fashion: it leaves in place the district court’s judgment on the MPWA claim and the dismissal of the MWTA claim, vacates and remands the MFLSA penalty claim and the associated district‑court order, and directs the lower court to address standing and mootness issues that remain unresolved. This chapter unpacks the Court’s reasoning for each outcome and examines what the decision signals to employers and litigants engaging in wage‑law litigation in Minnesota.

## A.  The Court’s Holding on the MPWA Claim

The Court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment on Consilio’s MPWA claim by finding that the statute expressly limits the enforcement of civil penalties to the Minnesota Commissioner. Section 181.101(a) requires the Commissioner to serve a demand for unpaid wages and, if the employer fails to pay, to impose a penalty. Because the statute does not provide a private‑party right to recover such penalties, Consilio’s motion for summary judgment was proper. The Court emphasized that statutory language that is “plain and unambiguous” must be given effect, citing *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.* and *Burkstrand v. Burkstrand*. Thus, Cohen could not recover “average daily wage penalties” under § 181.101, nor could he invoke the statutory right to penalizes his employer.

This holding clarifies that Minnesota employers who reset overtime policies must be wary that employees cannot, for themselves, recover statutory penalties arising from MPWA violations. Enforcement, and any attendant penalties, rest with the Commissioner’s office. The practical consequence is that employers need not fear being sued for penalties simply for violating MPWA provisions; only the Commissioner can levy such penalties.

## B.  The Court’s Holding on the MWTA Claim

The Court affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA claim. The Court found that the claim was not adequately pleaded: Cohen could not point to any statutory authority that would allow civil penalties under § 181.032(f) or § 181.032(d)(4). The Court reiterated that a claim must be supported by argument or legal authority; without such support the claim is effectively waived. By dismissing the MWTA claim, the Court underscored that employers are immune from statutory penalties for failure to provide notice of overtime policy changes unless the statute explicitly furnishes a private‑party right to such penalties.

## C.  The Court’s Vacatur and Remand of the MFLSA Penalty Claim

The House of a MFLSA penalty claim was vacated because the Court found that its status as a viable claim depended upon issues of standing and mootness that were unresolved. While the statute does provide for statutory penalties under § 177.27, those penalties are payable to the Commissioner, not directly to the aggrieved employee. Under *Friends of the Earth*, a plaintiff must demonstrate a continued, personal interest in obtaining penalties. Had Consilio’s overtime policy been discontinued, the claim could be moot. The Court therefore remanded the case for the district court to decide whether the MFLSA penalty claim survives a possible mootness challenge and whether the Court retains jurisdiction to decide a claim that otherwise has no direct benefit to the plaintiff.

On remand, the district court must first determine if Cohen retains a “continuing interest” in the enforcement of the alleged violations. If the policy is still in force, the claim may be viable; if not, the claim may be deemed moot. Simultaneously, the District Court must resolve its jurisdiction over a claim that would, upon enforcement, pay a penalty to the Commissioner. This intersects with *Friends of the Earth* and *Town of Chester* concerns regarding standing and mootness: the plaintiff has no tangible stake in the penalty payment.

## D.  Implications for Employers

1. **Commissioner‑only Penalties**: Employers can safely conclude that statutory penalties under Minnesota’s wage‑law statutes will be administered by the Commissioner. Employees cannot file separate penalty claims to recover funds directly.

2. **Threat of Litigation Retained to the Commissioner**: Employers should focus on compliance to avoid triggering Commissioner enforcement and penalties. The threat of public enforcement, however, may still serve as a deterrent.

3. **Policy Changes Must Be Consistent with Statute**: A premature or inadequately communicated change to overtime policy does not automatically create a statutory penalty claim by employees. Consilio’s experience suggests that employers that enforce a change will not be subject to employee‑initiated penalties under the wage‑law statutes.

4. **Planning for Litigation**: If employees attempt to seek penalties, courts will scrutinize the statutory language and limit enforcement to the Commissioner. Employers may reduce litigation risk by ensuring that internal policies are consistent with the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA provisions and that any changes are communicated in line with statutory requirements.

## E.  Strategic Lessons for Future Litigation

1. **Clarify the Statutory Penalty Mechanism**: When litigating wage‑law issues, parties must identify the precise statutory mechanism governing penalties. Federal courts will not infer a private‑party right where the statute explicitly delegates enforcement to an agency or commissioner.

2. **Address Standing and Mootness Early**: Claims that could be rendered non‑enforceable if wrongful conduct ceased must be litigated before the conduct ends or before an employer’s policy changes. Otherwise, a dismissal or remand can occur, as happened with Cohen's MFLSA penalty claim.

3. **Focus on Concrete Remedies**: Employees must articulate in their pleading the concrete benefit they seek (e.g., overtime wages, liquidated damages) rather than an abstract penalty. Courts gave priority to employee claims for direct benefits and were more likely to dismiss claims for penalties that were payable to a government office.

4. **Avoid Alternative Relief Claims After the Fact**: Cohen’s attempt to secure injunctive relief after the policy had already been changed illustrates that courts interpret pleadings literally. Employers should anticipate and actively respond to employee signals of potential injunctive claims if they want to defend against them.

5. **Use Settlement and Stipulations Wisely**: The parties’ settlement stipulating payment of all overtime wages and liquidated damages was decisive in limiting the viability of future parity claims. Employers can consider similar agreements to resolve single‑issue disputes early and avoid protracted litigation.

## F.  Broader Impact on Wage‑Law Litigation in Minnesota

The Court’s decision provides a clear boundary for the enforcement of civil penalties under Minnesota wage law: only the Commissioner may enforce penalties, and employees must litigate for direct monetary relief. This eliminates a potential avenue for employees to seek punitive redress against employers outside of public enforcement mechanisms. Consequently, Minnesota employers can manage wage‑law complaints more confidently, provided they comply with statutory wage‑payment obligations and maintain accurate records of payments and communications. The decision also signals that courts will closely scrutinize the standing and mootness of penalty claims, especially where the penalties accrue to an agency rather than to the plaintiff. Future litigation will likely see a more precise focus on enforceable wage claims and a lesser emphasis on broad penalty claims, leading to a more predictable legal environment for both employers and employees in wage‑law matters.
