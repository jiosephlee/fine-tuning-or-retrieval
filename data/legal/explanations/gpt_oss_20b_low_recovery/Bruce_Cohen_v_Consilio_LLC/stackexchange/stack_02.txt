Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does a Minnesota employee have standing to recover civil penalties under the MPWA when the penalties must be paid to the Commissioner, not the employee?
Question:
The court concluded that only the Commissioner can enforce the daily‑average‑wage penalty under § 181.101(a) of the MPWA, and the employee cannot collect the penalty. How does this affect the employee’s standing? Is the employee allowed to sue for those penalties at all, or is standing limited to the wage and liquidated‑damage components? What precedent supports limiting an employee’s claim to the wage portion only?
Answer:
**Answer**

No. Because § 181.101(a) gives the Commissioner, not the employee, the **right to recover daily‑average‑wage penalties**, a Minnesota employee cannot sue to recover those penalties. The only claims an employee may prosecute against an employer under the MPWA are for unpaid wages (and, where the statute allows, for liquidated damages).  

**Why the court said so**

1. **Statutory text.**  
   § 181.101(a) states that a *payment demand* must be *served by the Commissioner* and that *“if payment of wages is not made within ten days, the Commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned … and a penalty.”*  The statute goes on to say that *“any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.”*  Thus the language expressly limits the power to impose a penalty to the Commissioner.  (See *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015).)

2. **No statutory authority for employees to seek penalties.**  
   The MPWA’s injury‑relief clause (§ 181.171) states that an employee may bring an action *“for redress”* and that, where the employer is found liable, the employer is “liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”  But because § 181.101(a) wires the penalty‑imposing power exclusively to the Commissioner, the *aggrieved party* that the statute refers to is the Commissioner.  

3. **The court applied the same reasoning.**  
   In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the court declared that “only the Commissioner may seek daily average‑wage penalties… and any money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.”  The court affirmed the district court’s summary judgment on the MPWA claim, holding that an employee cannot recover penalties under § 181.101(a).  The court did not address the MFLSA or MWTA penalties because they fall under different statutes.

**Effect on standing**

*Standing* is a question of *who may sue for a particular remedy*.  The court’s holding is precisely that an employee may not sue for the MPWA’s daily‑average‑wage penalty: the statutory right to recover such a penalty is vested in the Commissioner, not in the employee.  Therefore the employee lacks standing to bring an action for those penalties.  The employee can, however, bring an action for unpaid wages and for liquidated damages, which the MPWA expressly protects the employee against.

**Supporting precedent**

- *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) – the court upheld the text that only the Commissioner may impose the penalty.  
- *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.*, 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) – the district court reiterated that the Commissioner alone may enforce the penalty.  
- *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) – the court noted that the legislature “knows how” to give an employee the right to recover penalties, but it must do so by means of language that expressly does that. The MPWA does not, so the employee cannot sue.

**Bottom line**

An employee’s *standing* to recover the MPWA’s daily‑average‑wage penalty is nil. The employee may sue for unpaid wages and liquidated damages, but not for the statutory penalty, which must be sought by the Commissioner under § 181.101(a).
