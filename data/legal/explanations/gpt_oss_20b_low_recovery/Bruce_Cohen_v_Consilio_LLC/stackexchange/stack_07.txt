Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why did the court hold that the parties’ stipulation that Cohen had been paid did not preclude a penalty claim under the MFLSA?
Question:
Even after a stipulation that Cohen was paid all wages and liquidated damages, the court left open the possibility of a penalty claim. Under what reasoning can a penalty claim survive a stipulation that all compensatory damages have been paid? Does the MFLSA penal clause operate independently of the compensation clause?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court allowed a penalty claim to survive because the MFLSA’s statutory penalty is *not* a form of compensatory damages that can be satisfied by a payment to the employee.  The MFLSA expressly separates “overtime wages and liquidated damages” from the “statutory penalty” that is payable to the Commissioner, and the statute does not say that the payment of wages and liquidated damages extinguishes the right to the penalty.  

In **Cohen v. Consilio** the parties stipulated that Cohen had been “paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and that he had also been “paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled” under § 177.27 of the MFLSA.  The court recognized that the parties agreed that the § 177.27 penalty would be **paid to the Commissioner, not to the employee**.  Because a statutory penalty is a *separate* remedy, its payment does not satisfy the employee’s claim for it; it merely satisfies the potential payment that would be made to the state if the court found a violation.

The court’s reasoning is articulated in paragraph B of the opinion.  It notes that a plaintiff “must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought” (Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., 581 U.S. 433) and that a statutory penalty is an *independent claim* that can be pursued even after a payment of wages and liquidated damages has been made.  The court therefore held that Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim was not moot and could still be adjudicated, provided that Consilio’s overtime policy was still in effect or could reasonably be expected to recur.

The MFLSA penal clause operates independently of the compensation clause for two reasons:

1. **Statutory Text.**  § 177.27 specifically provides that “an employee may recover… liquidated damages, **and** an additional amount equal to twice the unpaid overtime wages as a *statutory penalty*.”  The penalty is a *separate statutory remedy*, not a component of liquidated damages.

2. **Remedy Allocation.**  The opinion explains that “any statutory penalties imposed—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen.”  Thus, payment of the liquidated damages does not satisfy the statutory penalty claim; the penalty remains an outstanding claim until the court actually awards it or it is paid to the Commissioner.

Because the MFLSA penalty is a distinct remedy, a stipulation that Cohen had been paid wages and liquidated damages does not preclude a penalty claim.  The court therefore vacated the district court’s judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remanded, allowing the penalty issue to be addressed separately from the payment of wages and liquidated damages.  This reflects a standard approach that a statutory penalty remains recoverable as long as the underlying conduct could still occur (or is likely to recur) and the plaintiff retains a continuing interest.
