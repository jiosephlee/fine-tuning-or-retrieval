Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 4: # IV. The MWTA Claim: Notice Requirements and Lack of Penalty Authority  

## MWTA statutory framework: §181.032(f), (d)(4) – notice of policy changes  

The Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), codified at Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f) and (d)(4), imposes an employer–specific duty to provide *notice of a change in overtime policy* to employees. Section 181.032(f) states that an employer **must** notify employees of any *substantial* alteration of its wage‑payment method, whereas subsection (d)(4) expands the duty to “require” that *notice be given* for changes affecting overtime. The statute, however, stops short of specifying a *penalty* that an employee may pursue for non‑compliance. Indeed, unlike the MPWA and the MFLSA, the MWTA contains only remedial provisions for unpaid wages and liquidated damages; it does not create *civil penalties* that are enforceable by private litigants.

Thus, while the MWTA requires employers to give notice of policy changes, the statutory language does not authorize employers to be subject to a civil penalty if they fail to do so. The primary remedy under the MWTA has historically been a claim for unpaid wages, not a punitive penalty.

## Cohen’s alleged violations: failure to provide required notice  

Cohen argued that Consilio’s August 2019 policy, which eliminated overtime premium pay and clarified that all hours would be paid at base rate, was a *substantial* change in overtime policy. He further contended that Consilio did not provide the required notice stipulated by § 181.032(f) or (d)(4) to employees, thereby violating the MWTA. Accordingly, he sought civil penalties for the alleged statutory violation.

Because Consilio had already settled with Cohen on unpaid wages and liquidated damages, the core issue was whether the *absence* of notice constituted a *penalizable*, non‑compensatory violation capable of yielding civil penalties.

## Plaintiff’s arguments: claim of negligence and penalties  

In his complaint, Cohen framed the MWTA claim as a *negligence* action—an abuse of its liability framework alleging that Consilio’s failure to comply with the notice requirement harmed him. In doing so, he also sought *civil penalties* that the MWTA in itself does not provide. His argument hinged on the notion that a failure to provide notice justified punitive damages, even though the statutes merely prescribe no‑fault wage‑related remedies.

The complaint did not present any statutory authority or case law specifically allowing a private plaintiff to recover civil penalties under the MWTA. Moreover, Cohen failed to articulate why court‑approved remedial clauses—limited to unpaid wages and liquidated damages—should be interpreted to include punitive penalties.

## Court’s limited discussion: no statutory penalty authority identified  

The district court acknowledged the existence of the MWTA’s notice requirement but did not elaborate on the statutory remedy beyond its complainer. Importantly, the court **did not** find any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under the MWTA, and it did not rely on extraneous authority to infer such a penalty. The brief, therefore, contains *only* a superficial reference to the MWTA’s notice obligation with no supporting analysis of potential penalties.

Because the statutory language fails to prescribe any penalty, the district court’s dismissal of the claim was grounded in the premise that the court had *no* statutory basis to entertain a penalty request. It therefore steered the discussion toward the plaintiff’s *lack of argument*: Cohen did not present any justification or authority supporting his claim for penalties under the MWTA.

## Application of waiver and dismissal precedent: Milligan v. City of Red Oak  

The court used *the doctrine of waiver* to justify dismissal, citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000). In Milligan, the Ninth Circuit held that a plaintiff who *“did not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority”* was effectively *waiving* the claim. The court emphasized that the party’s brief was silent on the critical issue of statutory penalty authority and that the district court could not, in the absence of any argument, alter the statutory framework.

Because Cohen’s brief contained no cogent statutory or case‑law argument to extend the MWTA’s notice requirement to include civil penalties, the court held that Cohen had *waived* that claim.  Milligan is invoked to show that a party may not simply insert an unsubstantiated claim into a brief and expect the court to treat it as viable; the court must rely on documented authority or persuasive argumentation.

Therefore, the district court’s ruling to dismiss was affirmed. The court and the appellant agree that no statutory authority exists under the MWTA to sustain a claim for penalties. Because the plaintiff had not argued for such penalties, the claim was deemed waived, and the court appropriately dismissed it.
