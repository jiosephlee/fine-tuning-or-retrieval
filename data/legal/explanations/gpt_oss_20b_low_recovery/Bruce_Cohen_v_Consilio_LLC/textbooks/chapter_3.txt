Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 3: # III. The MFLSA Claim: Standing, Mootness, and Federal Jurisdiction

## Summary‑Judgment Overview: De Novo Review and Karlen

When a court grants a summary‑judgment motion, the nonmoving party may file a brief and the moving party may file a reply. The appellate court reviews the district court’s decision “de novo,” meaning it looks at the evidence and inferences anew, as if no prior ruling had been made. This standard requires the appellate court to consider the record in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. The Ninth‑Circuit precedent that most directly governs this analysis is *Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc.*, 766 F.3d 863 (8th Cir. 2014). *Karlen* affirms that *when reviewing a district court’s summary‑judgment ruling, the appellate court must view the evidence “in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party,” then decide whether the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law*. The court likewise notes that the appellate court’s review is premised on the same standard that applies to the district court. For *Placzek v. Mayo Clinic* and *Green Plains Otter Tail*, the court reiterated that the same “no genuine issue of material fact” test governs de‑novo review.

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the district court granted summary judgment on all three claims. The appellate court therefore reviewed that order under the *Karlen* standard. The substantive remaining issue concerned the MFLSA statutory penalty claim. The court had to decide whether, in light of the evidence, the MFLSA penalties were “materially disputed” and whether the opinion should have denied summary judgment on that claim.

## MFLSA Penalties: Statutory Basis and Distinction from MPWA Penalties

The Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) authorizes employers who infringe on certain pay provisions to owe “liquidated damages” and, in some circumstances, statutory penalties. *Section 177.27* of the MFLSA contains a statute‑of‑limitations provision and a list of penalty provisions. The statute makes clear that penalties, if awarded, are payable to an employee *but* in some contexts the penalty provisions make it explicit that the employee “is entitled to a civil penalty” after the complaint is filed. The details differ from the MPWA, where the penalty for a wage‑payment violation is limited to penalties payable only to a commissioner, who may collect and distribute the penalties to employees. The MFLSA is a “civil‑action” statute that empowers an employee to sue an employer for unpaid wages, liquidated damages, and penalties *directly*. The court notes that this naming distinction matters when it applies the *Friends of the Earth* standing doctrine.

In *Cohen*, the plaintiff argued that because he had been paid all overdue overtime wages and liquidated damages, the only remaining reversible claim was the statutory penalties authorized by the MFLSA. The district court suppressed the MFLSA penalty claim, finding no dispute over such penalties. The appellate court agreed that the MFLSA provisions authorize employees to seek penalties; however, the court declined to find that the district court had erred entirely because the *standing* and *mootness* issues were unresolved.

## Standing Doctrine under *Friends of the Earth* and *Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n*

Under *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff can sue for civil penalties under an environmental statute even if a government agency cannot sue to recover those penalties. *Friends* specifically addressed the *Clean Water Act* and recognized that a private plaintiff could still recover civil penalties when the agency was barred from doing so. The court underscored that a plaintiff must demonstrate a *personal interest* at the outset of the litigation *for the relief he seeks*. The *Davis v. Federal Election Commission* analysis (554 U.S. 724) clarified that *standing* is a continuous requirement—once a plaintiff’s *personal interest* is lost, the claim becomes moot.

Cohen’s standing for MFLSA penalties depends on whether he claims personal injury from the alleged violation. The statute itself does not require a plaintiff to suffer physical injury; more pertinently, it requires a plaintiff to suffer a *financial loss* in the form of unpaid wages. Because Cohen has already been paid all “overtime wages” and “liquidated damages” claimed under the MFLSA, Cohen’s *personal financial interest* in the penalty claim remains uncertain. If the policy that gave rise to the penalty claim (the premium overtime policy) has been rescinded, Cohen can no longer argue that the injury or potential injury that motivated the penalty claim is continued. The court held that the *Friends* standing requirement demands that a plaintiff’s *financial injury* be *present* and not merely *potential*.

In *Cohen*, the appellate court concluded that the plaintiff had not sufficiently demonstrated that he retains a continuing personal interest in the penalties, and that the district court’s refusal to address the standing question left the MFLSA penalty claim in analytical limbo. The court, therefore, vacated the summary‑judgment ruling that had dismissed the MFLSA penalty claim.

## Mootness Analysis: Policy Persistence and the Continuing Interest

The *Friends* decision also established that a statutory penalty claim can become moot if the alleged violation is “clearly no longer present” or “cannot reasonably be expected to recur.” The court cited *Friends* assurances that *“the ultimate question is whether the alleged violation exists”* and aligned with *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283 (1982), which noted that a voluntary termination of the challenged activity does not automatically hobble a court’s jurisdiction. The distinction lies in whether the termination is “absolutely clear” that the violation will not recur.

Here, the parties agreed that Consilio had stopped paying overtime premiums and had compensated Cohen for all wages and liquidated damages. However, the court noted that the *policy that allegedly violated the MFLSA*—the “new premium overtime policy” in August 2019—was still technically in effect until a policy mute. The contract to pay “base salary” for all hours had been implemented in August 2020, but whether the policy had *absolutely* ceased remained in dispute. With the policy possibly still in place, the MFLSA penalty claim was not yet moot. The appellate court, however, concluded that the existing evidence was insufficient to determine whether the policy had truly ended, leaving the judgment on that point uncertain.

Accordingly, the court addressed the *moistness doctrine* by stating that the *continued existence of the punitive conduct* is a *necessary element* of a valid MFLSA penalty claim. The court therefore vacated the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on the MFLSA penalty claim to allow the district court to further evaluate whether the policy has been rescinded and whether Cohen retains a personal interest.

## Jurisdiction Concerns: Federal Courts Cannot Retain Cases Lacking a Continuing Interest

The Supreme Court has made clear that *federal courts lack standing to maintain jurisdiction over cases in which there is no continuing interest*—i.e., when a plaintiff's personal stake has been obliterated. In *Friends*, the Court noted that *“the jurisdiction ends as soon as the plaintiff's interested fails to exist”*. The appellate court echoed that *“it is proper to dismiss a claim that lacks a continuing interest”* and that *“the federal court is not licensed to keep a case that has lost the elements it was licensed to decide.”*

Applying that principle to *Cohen*, the appellate court found that the MFLSA penalty claim’s viability was inextricably tied to whether Cohen had a *continuing interest* in obtaining penalties. The fact that Consilio had already paid Cohen for overtime wages and liquidated damages did not eliminate the potential for a penalty claim if the unlawful policy remained. The court held, therefore, that a *mootness‑related lack of continuing interest* could arise only if the policy had been rescinded. Because the record did not resolve that point, the appellate court could not dismiss the claim outright but could not affirm it either. The court thus decided to vacate the district court’s decision to grant summary judgment in the MFLSA claim and remand for further proceedings.

## Court’s Action: Vacate Summary Judgment and Remand for Mootness Determination

The appellate court’s final order carried the following key actions:
1. The district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on the MPWA and MWTA claims was affirmed, because those claims were either barred by statute or lacked standing/authority.
2. The district court’s summary‑judgment order on the MFLSA penalty claim was vacated because the claim’s standing and mootness had not been adequately addressed.
3. The appellate court remanded the case for the district court to *first* determine whether Consilio’s punitive policy is still in force and whether Cohen retains a continuing personal interest in the penalty claim. The remand explicitly requires the district court to resolve the mootness/standing question before any further decision on the MFLSA penalties can be made.

Thus, the episode illustrates that federal courts will not affirm or affirm by default a penalty claim that is essentially rendered moot by the cessation of the wrongful conduct, or denied it hastily if the plaintiff’s standing is uncertain. The appellate court preserved the decision **only** when it had collected adequate evidence and legal analysis, and it remanded the remaining unresolved distributed roles.

In sum, *Cohen* underscores the necessity of satisfying the **standing** and **moistness** requirements under *Friends* when enforcing statutory penalties, and it delineates how a federal court must act when those requirements collide with procedural judgment.”
