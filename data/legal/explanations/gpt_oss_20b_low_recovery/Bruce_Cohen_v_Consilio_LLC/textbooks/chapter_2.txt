Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # II. The MPWA Claim: Statutory Penalties and Commissioner Authority

This chapter explains the court’s analysis of Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) as it applies to Cohen’s claim for average‑daily‑wage penalties. It follows the path the court took—from the statutory text to the interpretive principles, to the procedural mechanics of commissioner‑driven penalties—and shows why the court held that only the Commissioner may seek such penalties and consequently found Cohen’s claim barred.

## Statutory Prose: MPWA §181.101(a) and §181.171(i) – Employer Liability, Commissioner Role

Section 181.171(i) establishes the MPWA’s remedial framework: when an employer “is found to have violated” a wage‑payment provision, it is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” and the aggrieved party may recover “civil penalties or damages … provided for in the section violated.” The statute further refers to § 181.101(a) as the conduit for these penalties. Section 181.101(a) is the operative text for this portion of the MPWA. It specifies that an “employer who is found to have violated” the wage‑payment provisions must “pay any wages, commissions, or other payment due to an employee” and, if payment is not made, allows the Claimant to “serve a demand for payment of the wages” on behalf of an employee. Following the demand, the Commissioner may, **if payment is not made within ten days**, “charge and collect the wages earned … and a penalty.” The section ends by requiring that any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.

Two elements are critical:

1. **The Commissioner, not the employee, may invoke the penalty provision.** The statute obliges the Commissioner to act only after a statutory demand is served, and only the Commissioner may thereafter collect a penalty.
2. **The penalty is payable to the employee.** The mandate that any collec­ted funds be paid to the employee guarantees that the employee’s receipt of the penalty is contingent on the Commissioner’s action.

The MPWA does not contain any parallel mechanism that allows an employee to unilaterally seek a penalty. Because § 181.171 passes the power to the Commissioner for discretionary enforcement, any claim for penalties brought directly by an employee is barred by the statute’s structure.

## Plain‑Language Interpretation of “Penalties” and Legislative Intent

The court applied the plain‑language rule, which treats statutory words as conveying their ordinary, ordinary‐lance meaning when the language is clear and unambiguous. In *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, the Minnesota Supreme Court held that “when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” The court noted that the MPWA’s coverage of penalties is explicit: the Commissioner is the sole actor authorized to seek them. No statutory wording suggests an employee’s authority to do so. Therefore, the plain‑language rule supports the conclusion that the legislature intended only the Commissioner to enforce these penalties.

Additionally, the court considered the legislative practice in analogous statutes. *Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch.* recognized that the statutory framework for penalties here is miniature to the general scheme of employer penalties, reinforcing the notion that the Legislature’s design—through the Commissioner—was purposefully restrictive.

## Commissioner’s Procedural Power: Demand for Payment, Ten‑Day Rule, Collection of Wages and Penalty

The court mapped the Commissioner’s procedure as follows:

1. **Demand for Payment.** The Commissioner issues a demand on behalf of an employee for unpaid wages or commissions. The demand is the statutory trigger for the potential penalty.
2. **Ten‑Day Window.** If the employer fails to pay within ten days of the demand, the Commissioner can collect the wages owed, plus a penalty.
3. **Collection and Distribution.** Any amount collected by the Commissioner is to be paid directly to the employee. The statutory language makes no provision for the employee to recover a penalty through a civil action separate from the Commissioner’s enforcement.

Cohen’s allegation that the statute authorizes an employee to sue for average‑daily‑wage penalties violates this chain. The MPWA never furnishes the employee with an independent or parallel avenue to invoke the penalty provision; instead, all enforcement is funneled through the Commissioner. The court therefore found that Cohen’s claim was not supported by the statutory text.

## Legislative Context: Statutes §181.13(a) and §181.14 – Authority for Employees to Recover Penalties in Other Contexts

To illustrate the Legislature’s capacity to grant employees penalty rights, the court cited §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14. These provisions create a scenario where a **dismissed** or **resigned** employee, upon paying a demand, may recover unpaid wages or commissions *and* “may charge and collect a penalty.” The custody of the penalty‑enabling language in these statutes demonstrates that the Legislature could, had it wished, have provided an employee‑initiated penalty scheme under the MPWA. However, because the MPWA’s wording is different—specifically attributing penalty authority to the Commissioner—it reveals that the Legislature purposely limited penalty authority to the Commissioner under the MPWA. Thus, the employee-initiated approach is not implied by the MPWA.

## Courthypical Cases: Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., Hull v. ConvergeOne

The court drew from three decisions that ground its reasoning:

1. **Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.** (plain‑language rule) – affirmation that statutory clarity should be respected.
2. **Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch.** – acknowledgement that the statute’s design is conclusive and that the Commissioner holds exclusive penalty authority.
3. **Hull v. ConvergeOne** – confirms that the Commissioner’s collection of penalties is intended to be passed to the employee; the court emphasized that the employer cannot retain the penalty, and thus the employer’s liability is limited to the wages earned, not a penalty.

These cases confirm the structural pattern of the MPWA: employer liability is met through wage payment (and possibly liquidated damages); civil penalties are a discretionary power of the Commissioner, not an employee claim.

## Court’s Conclusion: Penalties Unavailable to Cohen; District Court Error Not Found

The court concluded that Cohen could not seek average‑daily‑wage penalties because the MPWA expressly provides that the Commissioner, not the employee, is empowered to levy such penalties. Cohen’s claim relied on a statutory reading that the MPWA authorizes employee‑initiated penalties, which the court found was unsupported by the statutory language, the plain‑language rule, and the broader legislative framework. Therefore, the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio on the MPWA penalty claim was correct: Cohen could not, by law, sue for those penalties.

The court affirmed the district court’s ruling on the MPWA claim while vacating only the portion of the judgment that addressed Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim, which the court later found potentially moot. This partial affirmation shows that the court’s reasoning was tightly linked to the statutory requirements of § 181.101 and § 181.171.

## Implications for Employee‑Initiated Penalty Claims

Employees seeking MPWA penalties must rely on the Commissioner’s enforcement mechanism. An employee cannot file a direct civil action for penalties under § 181.101; instead, the proper procedure is to invoke the statutory demand on behalf of the employee, allowing the Commissioner to act. The court’s decision underscores that the MPWA is a *statistical* penalty scheme, not an *employee*‑directed remedy. Future litigation will likely involve challenges to the Commissioner’s authority or to the application of the ten‑day rule, rather than to the existence of employee‑initiated penalties.
