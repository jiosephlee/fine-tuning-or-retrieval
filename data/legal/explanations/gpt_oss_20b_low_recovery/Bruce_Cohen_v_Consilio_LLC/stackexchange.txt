Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What is the legal standard for a court to retain jurisdiction over a claim for statutory penalties when the alleged wrongful conduct may have ceased?
Question:
In the opinion, the court vacated summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim, saying it may be moot if Consilio’s overtime policy was no longer in place. How does the court determine whether a civil penalty claim is still actionable when the defendant has allegedly stopped the conduct that violated the statute? What authorities guide the decision to retain or dismiss jurisdiction in such cases?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court’s decision hinges on the *standing‑to‑enjoy the remedy* test and on the *moot‑ness* doctrine applied to civil‑penalty claims.  In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* the appellate court held that the district court could not dismiss Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim simply because Consilio had begun to pay the usual wages and liquidated damages; it could only do so once it was certain that the conduct that gave rise to the penalty was truly ended.

---

### 1.  Standing to seek a civil penalty

Under *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.* (528 U.S. 167, 186 at 186‑87), a plaintiff must show that he or she will actually benefit from the court’s imposition of a civil penalty.  The remedy must be *available to the plaintiff* and *offer a personal interest* that ties the plaintiff to the *continuing* illegal conduct.  The Supreme Court explained that the “requisite personal interest … must continue throughout its existence.”  (See *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F. 4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting *Arizonans for Office of Energy*).)

In *Cohen*, the Montana legislature made it clear that penalties would be paid to the Commissioner, not to the employee (most commentary in the opinion).  Because the plaintiff’s financial interest in the penalty is only contingent on the continued existence of the wrongful conduct, the court must ask whether that conduct is still present.

---

### 2.  Moot‑ness analysis

The Supreme Court in *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.* (455 U.S. 283, 289) made it clear that a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does **not** automatically strip a court of authority to decide “the legality of the practice.”  However, if a defendant has *absolutely ceased* the conduct and it is “clearly unlikely to recur,” the claim may be moot.  The court’s *Friends of the Earth* pronouncement, when read with *Mesquite*, gave the following framework:

1. **Continued conduct** – the mere fact that a defendant has stopped the conduct does not itself wipe the claim.  
2. **Likelihood of recurrence** – the claim is moot only if the conduct has ceased **and** it is “absolutely clear” that it will not resume.  
3. **Interest in the remedy** – the plaintiff must still have a personal stake in insisting on the penalty.

The *Cohen* opinion applied this test.  It noted that the parties had agreed that Cohen “has been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages,” but the *continuing* issue was whether Consilio’s “overtime policy” was still in force.  If the policy were still operative, the potential for future violations—and therefore the plaintiff’s interest in preventing future violations—remained.  (See *Cohen* at 192‑93.)  If, however, Consilio had eliminated the policy and had no realistic prospect of reinstating it, the claim would be moot.  The court emphasized that it had no jurisdiction to decide a claim that could be rendered moot by a factual change before the merits were addressed.

---

### 3.  How the court decided

Because the facts were unclear about the *current status* of the overtime policy, the appellate court could not, on summary judgment, conclude that Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim was moot.  The court therefore vacated the summary judgment entry on that claim and remanded, directing the district court to first determine whether the policy is still in force and whether the conduct that gives rise to the penalty can recur.  Only after that factual inquiry could the court decide whether it still had jurisdiction to hear the claim.

---

### Summary

- **Standing:** A plaintiff must show a *personal* interest in the civil penalty, which is tied to *continuing* violations. (Friends of the Earth; Sisney.)  
- **Mootness:** A claim is moot only if the wrongful conduct has ceased *and* it can be shown that it is unlikely to recur. (Mesquite; Friends of the Earth).  
- **Application in *Cohen***: Because the policy’s status was disputed, the court held that it could not lawfully dismiss the penalty claim on summary judgment.  It remanded to decide whether the conduct that underlies the penalties is truly absent before it could determine mootness.

These authorities—*Friends of the Earth*, *Mesquite*, *Sisney*, and the Fifth‑P. Civ. R. 1 (for the *temporary* nature of the rule in *Friends*)—concur to preserve or dismiss jurisdiction over statutory‑penalty claims when alleged wrongful conduct has ended.

### Does a Minnesota employee have standing to recover civil penalties under the MPWA when the penalties must be paid to the Commissioner, not the employee?
Question:
The court concluded that only the Commissioner can enforce the daily‑average‑wage penalty under § 181.101(a) of the MPWA, and the employee cannot collect the penalty. How does this affect the employee’s standing? Is the employee allowed to sue for those penalties at all, or is standing limited to the wage and liquidated‑damage components? What precedent supports limiting an employee’s claim to the wage portion only?
Answer:
**Answer**

No. Because § 181.101(a) gives the Commissioner, not the employee, the **right to recover daily‑average‑wage penalties**, a Minnesota employee cannot sue to recover those penalties. The only claims an employee may prosecute against an employer under the MPWA are for unpaid wages (and, where the statute allows, for liquidated damages).  

**Why the court said so**

1. **Statutory text.**  
   § 181.101(a) states that a *payment demand* must be *served by the Commissioner* and that *“if payment of wages is not made within ten days, the Commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned … and a penalty.”*  The statute goes on to say that *“any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.”*  Thus the language expressly limits the power to impose a penalty to the Commissioner.  (See *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015).)

2. **No statutory authority for employees to seek penalties.**  
   The MPWA’s injury‑relief clause (§ 181.171) states that an employee may bring an action *“for redress”* and that, where the employer is found liable, the employer is “liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”  But because § 181.101(a) wires the penalty‑imposing power exclusively to the Commissioner, the *aggrieved party* that the statute refers to is the Commissioner.  

3. **The court applied the same reasoning.**  
   In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the court declared that “only the Commissioner may seek daily average‑wage penalties… and any money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.”  The court affirmed the district court’s summary judgment on the MPWA claim, holding that an employee cannot recover penalties under § 181.101(a).  The court did not address the MFLSA or MWTA penalties because they fall under different statutes.

**Effect on standing**

*Standing* is a question of *who may sue for a particular remedy*.  The court’s holding is precisely that an employee may not sue for the MPWA’s daily‑average‑wage penalty: the statutory right to recover such a penalty is vested in the Commissioner, not in the employee.  Therefore the employee lacks standing to bring an action for those penalties.  The employee can, however, bring an action for unpaid wages and for liquidated damages, which the MPWA expressly protects the employee against.

**Supporting precedent**

- *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) – the court upheld the text that only the Commissioner may impose the penalty.  
- *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.*, 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) – the district court reiterated that the Commissioner alone may enforce the penalty.  
- *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) – the court noted that the legislature “knows how” to give an employee the right to recover penalties, but it must do so by means of language that expressly does that. The MPWA does not, so the employee cannot sue.

**Bottom line**

An employee’s *standing* to recover the MPWA’s daily‑average‑wage penalty is nil. The employee may sue for unpaid wages and liquidated damages, but not for the statutory penalty, which must be sought by the Commissioner under § 181.101(a).

### How should a court interpret the phrase “provide notice” in the MWTA when an employee claims the employer failed to notify of an overtime policy change?
Question:
The opinion dismissed Cohen’s MWTA claim, citing the absence of statutory authority for civil penalties under that statute. What is the statutory meaning of “provide notice” under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4)? Can a failure to notify an employee of a policy change be actionable, and if so, is it actionable through penalties or merely a wage‑payment claim?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the court affirmed the dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA claim in part because the Minnesota Wage‑Theft Act (MWTA) contains no statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties. The key statutory language that governs notice requirements is found in Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f)(d)(4). That provision requires an employer to:

> “**provide notice** of any change in the employer’s overtime pay policy to all employees who are covered by the act”…

The language is ordinary, unambiguous, and has no secondary interpretation. The statute is a *duty*—only a *failure* to satisfy that duty creates a tort action that is “a wage‑payment claim” rather than a claim for penalties.

### 1.  Statutory meaning of “provide notice”

* Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f)(d)(4) is a plain duty.  The plain meaning of *provide* is to “give,” “afford,” or “affair” something to the intended recipient.  In the context of a notification requirement, *provide notice* means that the employer **must deliver a written communication** (or other reasonable method of conveyance) that informs the employee of the policy change before it takes effect.  The *court* in its opinion said that there is “no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.”  § 181.032(f)(d)(4) does not create a penalty schedule or a duty to pay a fine; it merely sets out a *notice* requirement that, if breached, gives rise to a wage‑payment claim.

### 2.  Whether a failure to notify is actionable

Yes.  § 181.032(f)(d)(4) makes failure to provide notice a *tort* under the MWTA.  The statutory scheme is:

| Element | Source | Effect |
|---------|--------|--------|
| **Notice** | § 181.032(f)(d)(4) | Employer must inform employees of any change to overtime policy. |
| **Failure** | Lack of the above action | Violates the MWTA, creating a tort and a right to recover. |
| **Remedy** | § 181.032(a)(1) | The employee may recover “the wages … that were not paid because of the employer’s wrongful conduct.”  No civil‑penalty language is inserted in the act.

Thus a failure to inform an employee of a policy change is actionable, but the remedy is limited to **wage payment (and, where the MWTA expressly authorizes it, liquidated damages or statutory penalties that are separately provided for).**  The MWTA itself contains no clause that permits the recovery of civil penalties, and the court explicitly states that the statute “provides no statutory penalties.”

### 3.  Penalties vs. wage‑payment claim

* Under the MWTA* – no penalties.

* Under the MPWA* – civil penalties are available **but only if the Commissioner first “serves a demand for payment”** (see § 181.101(a) and the court’s discussion).  If the employee does not go through the Commissioner's demand process, the employee cannot recover penalties under the MPWA.

* Under the MFLSA* – statutory penalties can be recovered **only** when the employer has violated a wage‑payment requirement that the MFLSA explicitly allows for penalties.  The court noted that Cohen’s MFLSA claim potentially could be moot or lack standing for penalties, but the discussion was not resolved.  In no case does the MFLSA create a penalty for a failure to provide notice; it only provides penalties for wage‑payment violations.

**Bottom line for Cohen**

1. The failure to provide notice of the August 2019 overtime policy change *is* a wage‑payment claim under the MWTA.  
2. Because the MWTA does not authorize civil penalties, Cohen cannot recover penalties under that statute.  
3. Penalties could only be pursued under the MPWA **if** Cohen first had the Commissioner's demand served and the Commissioner found a violation, or under the MFLSA **if** a separate wage‑payment violation was proven.

The court’s holding that there is no statutory authority for civil penalties in the MWTA directly supports this conclusion.  The action remains a wage‑payment claim, and any resolution will involve the payment of wages that were unlawfully withheld, not a penalty fee.

### What is the proper procedure for a party that wants to seek injunctive relief but has not yet filed the appropriate amended complaint?
Question:
Cohen attempted to obtain an injunction in his summary‑judgment brief after the trial court had already dismissed the claim. The court said the issue was not properly before it. Under what circumstances can a judge consider a new claim for injunctive relief that was not raised in the original complaint or in filing a proper amended complaint?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the appellate court made it clear that a court will not entertain a new request for injunctive relief that was not raised in the original complaint or in a properly filed amended complaint. The opinion says that “new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs” (see *InfoDeli, LLC*, 136 F.3rd at 801 n.6) and cites Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), which requires that a party must file an amended pleading to state a new claim in order to bring it to the court’s attention.

Because Cohen sought an injunction in his summary‑judgment brief after the district court had already dismissed his MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims, the court found that “the issue is not properly before us” (see *Bruce Cohen*, § II D). The court emphasized that the district court’s denial of injunctive relief was “understandable” because Cohen had “withdrawn the 2019 policy” and had not included the injunctive claim in any pleading that the court could consider. As a result, the appellate court treated the injunction as a new claim that was never timely raised, and therefore the court could not consider it.

**Bottom line for litigants**

* If you want injunctive relief, you must state and plead the claim in your original complaint or, should the court dismiss or otherwise not consider it, file an amended complaint that includes the injunctive claim.  
* You cannot bring an injunction in a motion for summary judgment, a brief for a later dispositive motion, or any other procedural filing that is not an amended pleading.  
* Failure to do so will leave the court with no jurisdiction over the new claim, as the court will view it as “not properly before it,” exactly as the appellate court did in *Cohen*.  

Thus, the proper procedure is to amend the complaint to include the injunctive relief you seek—before the court can consider that claim.

### Is a statutory penalty reversible or subject to summary judgment if the employee has already been paid the wages and liquidated damages they are owed?
Question:
The court affirmed that the employee was paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, yet it vacated the summary judgment on the penalty claim. If the employee has already been compensated, can the court still allow a penalty claim to proceed? How does the distinction between wage‑payment relief and penalty relief affect the viability of the claim?
Answer:
**Answer**

Yes.  A statutory penalty claim is not automatically moot just because the employee has been paid the overtime wages and liquidated damages that the statute requires.  The Court explained that the two strands of relief—wage‑payment relief and penalty relief—circumvent each other in the way the statutory scheme is written and in how courts evaluate standing and mootness.

---

### 1.  What the opinion says about the two kinds of relief

* **Wage‑payment relief (MFLSA claim).**  
  § 181.171 of the MPWA and the MFLSA provide that if an employer notices that it has violated wage‑payment provisions, the employee is entitled to “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.”  The remedy is plainly the unpaid wages and the statutory liquidated damages.  Because the wage‑payment component has already been satisfied, the Court was free to grant summary judgment on that facet as it “did not err.”  (See *I(A),* paragraphs II‑IV.)

* **Penalty relief.**  
  The same statutes also reserve civil penalties for the employee *only if* the employer continues to violate the law.  The Court held that the penalty relief is distinct because it is “payable to the Commissioner, not to the employee,” so the employee’s *standing* to recover those penalties rests on an ongoing injury: the *presumed* existence of a continuing unlawful practice.  (See *I(B).*)

---

### 2.  Standing and mootness for penalty claims

* **Standing.**  The Court repeated the rule from *Friends of the Earth* that a plaintiff must show a *current* personal interest in the relief sought.  Because a penalty is dictated by the *continuing* violation, the Court said a plaintiff must still demonstrate that the employer has *not yet cured* the practice that made the penalty available.  (See *I(B) (2).*)

* **Mootness.**  A penalty claim may become moot “if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.”  The Court conducted a *mootness* analysis before it could resolve the claim.  That is why it vacated the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim and remanded for the district court to decide whether the employer’s overtime policy still existed.  (See *I(B) (3).*)  

  Because Cohen and Consilio had not yet agreed as to whether the policy was still in effect, the Court could not conclude that the penalty claim was moot.  That left the *potential* for the claim to survive even though the wage‑payment portion had been satisfied.

---

### 3.  Bottom line for the question

The Court therefore distinguished between the **wage‑payment part** (which can be satisfied and resolved by payment) and the **penalty part** (which requires an ongoing wrongful conduct to sustain standing).  Even though Cohen had received a lump‑sum payment covering overtime and liquidated damages, the penalty claim still might have survived because:

1. The employer may still be operating the illegal overtime policy (if it remains in effect).
2. The statutory penalty proceeds to the Commissioner, so the employee’s right to recover it is tied to the *continuing* practice, not merely to unpaid wages.
3. The Court carefully ruled that the issue of mootness must be decided first; it could not resolve the penalty claim without knowing whether the wrongful conduct persisted.

Thus, a statutory penalty claim is **reversible** or **subject to summary judgment** only if the Court determines that the underlying wrongful practice has ceased and that the plaintiff no longer has an ongoing stake in the relief.  Until that truth is established, the penalty claim may proceed even though wages and liquidated damages have already been paid.

### How does the court apply the plain‑meaning, unambiguous rule to determine whether the MPWA allows employees to recover penalties?
Question:
The opinion relied on the *Allan v. R.D. Offutt* principle that when a statute’s language is plain, it is presumed to reflect legislative intent. The court examined § 181.101(a) and concluded that only the Commissioner can charge penalties. What specific language in § 181.101 led the court to that conclusion, and could the employee have argued that the statute’s language was ambiguous or that the Legislature intended to give employees the right to seek penalties?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court turned to the plain‑text of § 181.101(a) to determine whether an employee like Cohen could recover the “average daily wage penalties” that the MPWA offers. The decisive language is the single sentence that says  

> “**If payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned … and a penalty**” (Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a)).  

Because the statute names *the commissioner* as the only party permitted to “charge and collect … a penalty,” the Court reasons that the legislature expressly limited the power to impose penalties to the commissioner. The surrounding provisions – that the commissioner must first “serve a demand for payment of wages on behalf of an employee” and that any money collected “must be paid to the employee concerned” – reinforce the idea that penalties are a public‑law instrument, not an employee‑driven remedy. The Court relies on the *Allan v. R.D. Offutt* standard that “when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (Allan, 869 N.W.2d at 33).

### Could Cohen have argued that the statute is ambiguous?

Yes, but such an argument would run aground on two fronts:

1. **Literal Language** – The statute is unmistakable. It says *“the commissioner”* and uses *“may charge and collect”*, which is a discretionary power granted to a specific public officer. Courts give that wording a very narrow reading.  
2. **No Counter‑Evidence of Legislative Intent** – The appellate panel cites *Lagasse v. Horton* and other cases to show that the legislature “knew how to empower an aggrieved employee with a statutory right to obtain penalties” if it wished to do so. The fact that the statute does not give employees that right is itself evidence of legislative choice, not ambiguity.

An argument that the statute is ambiguous would therefore have to hinge on an interpretation that the words “commissioner” are meant as a procedural placeholder rather than an identity. That view lacks authority in the case law cited, and the Court explicitly rejects it by stating that only the commissioner may “seek daily average wage penalties” under § 181.101.

### Could Cohen have alleged a legislative intent to allow employee penalties?

Cohen would have had to point to a statute or a legislative history that explicitly authorizes employees to pursue penalties. The Court, however, expressly notes that the Legislature “knew how to empower an aggrieved employee with a statutory right to obtain penalties” (see Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a), § 181.14). Because that power is absent in the MPWA, the Court sees no basis for inferring legislative intent in favour of employees. An inference of intent would only arise if the statute itself contained language that could be read two ways and the Court could not determine a clear meaning. Here the meaning is clear: the commissioner alone may administer penalties.

### Bottom line

The Court concluded that § 181.101(a)’s ordinary meaning – *“the commissioner may charge and collect … a penalty”* – is unambiguous and deliberately limits the authority to the commissioner. An employee may sue for wages, but cannot sue for the statutory penalties themselves. The legislative‑intent argument would have to overcome the plain‑text rule, which the Court found unnecessary.

### Why did the court hold that the parties’ stipulation that Cohen had been paid did not preclude a penalty claim under the MFLSA?
Question:
Even after a stipulation that Cohen was paid all wages and liquidated damages, the court left open the possibility of a penalty claim. Under what reasoning can a penalty claim survive a stipulation that all compensatory damages have been paid? Does the MFLSA penal clause operate independently of the compensation clause?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court allowed a penalty claim to survive because the MFLSA’s statutory penalty is *not* a form of compensatory damages that can be satisfied by a payment to the employee.  The MFLSA expressly separates “overtime wages and liquidated damages” from the “statutory penalty” that is payable to the Commissioner, and the statute does not say that the payment of wages and liquidated damages extinguishes the right to the penalty.  

In **Cohen v. Consilio** the parties stipulated that Cohen had been “paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and that he had also been “paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled” under § 177.27 of the MFLSA.  The court recognized that the parties agreed that the § 177.27 penalty would be **paid to the Commissioner, not to the employee**.  Because a statutory penalty is a *separate* remedy, its payment does not satisfy the employee’s claim for it; it merely satisfies the potential payment that would be made to the state if the court found a violation.

The court’s reasoning is articulated in paragraph B of the opinion.  It notes that a plaintiff “must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought” (Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., 581 U.S. 433) and that a statutory penalty is an *independent claim* that can be pursued even after a payment of wages and liquidated damages has been made.  The court therefore held that Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim was not moot and could still be adjudicated, provided that Consilio’s overtime policy was still in effect or could reasonably be expected to recur.

The MFLSA penal clause operates independently of the compensation clause for two reasons:

1. **Statutory Text.**  § 177.27 specifically provides that “an employee may recover… liquidated damages, **and** an additional amount equal to twice the unpaid overtime wages as a *statutory penalty*.”  The penalty is a *separate statutory remedy*, not a component of liquidated damages.

2. **Remedy Allocation.**  The opinion explains that “any statutory penalties imposed—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen.”  Thus, payment of the liquidated damages does not satisfy the statutory penalty claim; the penalty remains an outstanding claim until the court actually awards it or it is paid to the Commissioner.

Because the MFLSA penalty is a distinct remedy, a stipulation that Cohen had been paid wages and liquidated damages does not preclude a penalty claim.  The court therefore vacated the district court’s judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remanded, allowing the penalty issue to be addressed separately from the payment of wages and liquidated damages.  This reflects a standard approach that a statutory penalty remains recoverable as long as the underlying conduct could still occur (or is likely to recur) and the plaintiff retains a continuing interest.
