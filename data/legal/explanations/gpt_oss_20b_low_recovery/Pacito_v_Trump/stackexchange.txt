Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Why did the Ninth Circuit vacate the district court’s stay of the July 18 administrative stay?
Question:
In the orders leading up to the current stay, the district court issued an administrative stay of its own July 14 enforcement order. The Ninth Circuit later vacated that administrative stay. As a preliminarily confused reader, I don’t understand why a federal appellate court would reverse a lower court’s attempt to temporarily stop its own order. What legal principles governed that decision?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit vacated the district court’s *administrative stay* because the appellate court determined that the stay was *unnecessary and improper* under the *stay‑standard* that governs federal court stays. The court’s reasoning follows the four‑factor test set forth in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), and was applied specifically to the circumstances surrounding the district court’s July 14 enforcement order.

---

### 1. The *stay* standard

A federal court may issue a stay pending appeal if the applicant

1. is likely to succeed on the merits;  
2. will be irreparably injured if the stay is not granted;  
3. the stay will not substantially injure the other parties; and  
4. the public interest weighs in favor of a stay.  

In *Pacito v. Trump* the Ninth Circuit restates that “the first two factors—showing success on the merits and irreparable injury—are the most critical.”

---

### 2. Why the appellate court reversed the stay

| Factor | District‑court argument | Ninth Circuit’s finding |
|--------|------------------------|-------------------------|
| 1. Likelihood of success | Plaintiffs claimed the executive order violated the Refugee Act and the APA. | The court held “the government is **likely** to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ APA challenges” (see “For reasons to be explained…the government is **not** likely to prevail…under the APA”).  The case therefore did not satisfy the *likelihood‑to‑succeed* threshold. |
| 2. Irreparable injury | Plaintiffs feared loss of refugee admission and funding for resettlement services. | The court determined that, because the government *must* provide services that the plaintiffs were not entitled to under the executive order, the plaintiffs could not claim irreparable injury that outweighed the government’s statutory obligations. |
| 3. Harm to other parties | The government argued that the stay would hinder its ability to furnish required resettlement services. | The court held that “the stay **would substantially injure** the government,” which had a statutory duty to provide those services. This weighed heavily against the stay. |
| 4. Public interest | Plaintiffs offered national‑security justifications for the government’s pause. | The court found that the *public interest* favored the government’s duty to provide refugee services, especially given the indictment in the APA portion. |

Because factors 1 and 2 failed and factor 3 overwhelmingly prevailed, the Ninth Circuit concluded that a stay was not warranted.

---

### 3. Administrative stay vs. judicial stay

A *district‑court administrative stay* is an order that the district court itself applies to its own preliminary injunction (in this case, the July 14 enforcement order). It is not a stay “coming from the appellate court” in the traditional sense. When the Ninth Circuit “vacated our administrative stay issued July 18,” it was effectively saying:

> *The appellate court does **not** endorse or uphold the district court’s decision to pause enforcement of its own injunction; therefore, the district court’s enforcement order remains in force.*

Ordinarily, a stay may always be rescinded by the court that issued it. The appellate court’s reversal is simply a formal notice that the district court’s administrative stay is **no longer valid** and that the district court’s enforcement order stands.

---

### 4. Practical effect

The Ninth Circuit’s order *does not* suspend the original injunction from February 28 or March 24. Instead, it:

* **Removes** the July 14 administrative stay that the district court had applied to its own enforcement order.  
* **Leaves** the district court’s February and March orders intact (subject to the limitations noted in the Ninth Circuit’s order).  
* **Preserves** the government’s duty to “reinstate cooperative agreements” for reception and placement services (see the direct mandate in the Ninth Circuit’s order that “the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements”).

---

### Bottom line

*The Ninth Circuit vacated the district court’s administrative stay because, applying the *Nken* four‑factor test, the appellate court found the plaintiffs were not likely to succeed on the merits and that the stay would materially injure the government’s statutory duty to provide refugee services. The panel therefore restored the district court’s enforcement order and removed the stay that had been imposed on that order.*

### How does 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) interact with the Refugee Act of 1980 in regulating refugee admissions?
Question:
The Opinion repeatedly cites § 1182(f)’s requirement that the President find the entry of refugees ‘detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ At the same time, plaintiffs argue that the Refugee Act of 1980 obliges the federal government to accept refugees. Am I correct in thinking that these statutes are in tension, and if so, which statute prevails when the President suspends refugee admissions?
Answer:
**Answer**

### 1. 1182(f) gives the President the *binding authority* to suspend refugee admissions

“Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, he may by proclamation … suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens ….” (8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)).  The Ninth Circuit has repeatedly affirmed that this language is “clear” and that the President’s finding is the *sole* trigger for the suspension.  In Pacito v. Trump, the court noted that “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the executive order does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority” and that “the President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.”  Therefore, once the President declares the admission of refugees to be detrimental, the prohibition on entry automatically takes effect, regardless of other statutory preferences.  The Supreme Court’s decision in *Trump v. Hawaii* (585 U.S. 667) further bolstered deference to the President’s determinations in this context, describing the authority under § 1182(f) as “v[esting] [him] with ample power … to impose entry restrictions.”

### 2. The Refugee Act of 1980 does not *override* the President’s suspension

The Refugee Act (codified in 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101‑… and in the Immigration and Nationality Act) establishes a framework for the ongoing *treatment* of refugees once they are admitted, but it does not compel the federal government to **admit** refugees at all times or *prevent* a presidential suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP).  The Act’s purpose is “to provide for the continued protection of refugees … and to prescribe the procedures for their admission and resettlement” (public law 96‑212).  *Pacito* makes it clear that the Refugee Act is “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons” but that it does not give the Congress or the President a different standard for the *initial* admission decision.  In other words, the Act’s “treatment” provisions (e.g., reception, placement, and support services) are subordinate to the President’s § 1182(f) determination.

### 3. When the President suspends refugee admissions, § 1182(f) prevails

Because § 1182(f) expressly authorizes the President to suspend the *entry* of “any class of aliens,” the President’s proclamation is the controlling authority.  The Refugee Act does not contain any provision that negates the suspension or requires the President to resume admissions.  The Ninth Circuit therefore held that the President “may ... suspend the entry … for such period as he shall deem necessary.”  The only statutory obligation that remains is the duty to provide the *reception and placement* services required by § 1522 to refugees already admitted or conditionally approved before the suspension.  As noted in the opinion, “the government must provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, and the court directed the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements to meet those obligations.

### 4. Bottom line

- **Suspension of admissions:** 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) takes precedence; the President’s determination is legally binding and takes effect immediately.  
- **Ongoing services for already‑admitted refugees:** The Refugee Act’s reception and placement requirements (8 U.S.C. § 1522) remain in force, but they apply only to those refugees who are already in the United States or who had been conditionally approved before the suspension.  
- **No conflict in the statutes:** The Refugee Act does not impose an overarching duty to admit refugees; its purpose is to regulate how refugees are treated once admitted.  Consequently, a presidential suspension under § 1182(f) is perfectly lawful and does not violate the Refugee Act.

In sum, the Ninth Circuit’s opinion confirms that the President’s § 1182(f) suspension of refugee admissions is constitutionally and legally valid, and that the Refugee Act’s obligations concern only post‑admission services, not the initial admission decision.

### What standard of review applies to the President’s suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program under § 1182(f)?
Question:
The court notes that the President’s authority “exudes deference” and that it can impose entry restrictions “in addition to those elsewhere enumerated.” Yet it also references Trump v. Hawaii’s strict scrutiny dialog. Should the court apply strict scrutiny, rational basis, or some other balancing test to the executive order’s validity under a post‑Hawaii analysis?
Answer:
**Standard of Review for the President’s § 1182(f) Suspension of USRAP**

The Ninth Circuit in *Pacito v. Trump* made it clear that a review of a President’s action under § 1182(f) is not a “strict‑scrutiny” inquiry, nor is it a bare “rational‑basis” analysis.  Instead, the court applies a **deference‑based standard that is aligned with the deference that the Supreme Court has given the executive in immigration matters**.

---

### 1.  Deference to the Executive

> “The authority under which the President issued Executive Order No. 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f),  ‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]” (Court, Order of March 25, 2025, at 2, quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*).  

The court explicitly cites *Trump v. Hawaii* to support the notion that § 1182(f) “exudes deference.”  The *Hawaii* decision—and the Ninth Circuit’s own language—make clear that the purpose of § 1182(f) is to **give the President broad, plenary discretion** to suspend the entry of any class of aliens when the President “finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.”  The Court therefore **recognizes a statutory framework that is designed to grant the President the widest possible swing**.

---

### 2.  The Standard is One of “Deference‑Based” Review

The court’s analysis of whether the executive order should be stayed is framed entirely in terms of procedural stay factors (Nken *et al.*, 556 U.S. 418):

1. Likely success on the merits  
2. Irreparable injury to the applicant  
3. Potential injury to other parties  
4. Public interest

In assessing the first two factors it states:

> “The government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions … we cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.’” (Court, Order of March 25, 2025, at 2‑4)

By saying it cannot “engage in a searching inquiry” it signals that the court is **not conducting a constitutional scrutiny test (strict or otherwise), nor is it performing a bright‑line rational‑basis review**.  The court instead places the President’s findings squarely within the realm of the **“broad discretion”** that Congress granted the executive in § 1182(f).

---

### 3.  Post‑*Hawaii* Analysis

*Hawaii* settled that **the Supreme Court would not apply strict scrutiny to an executive order that suspends the admission of a class of aliens** because such orders are governed by the political question doctrine and by the statutory deference it grants the President.  The Ninth Circuit in *Pacito* simply reaffirms that deference applies under § 1182(f) — it does **not** resurrect strict scrutiny or any other balancing test.

If a case were to challenge an executive order that *used* an immigration provision (like § 1182(f)) but also raised a **constitutional claim** (e.g., a claim that the order violates the Equal Protection Clause), then the Court would apply *Hawaii*’s strict‑scrutiny framework.  In *Pacito*, however, plaintiffs’ arguments are contained entirely within statutory and administrative‑law claims (the Refugee Act and the APA).  Therefore the **appropriate standard is deference‑based, not strict scrutiny**.

---

### 4.  Bottom Line

- **Standard of review:** **Deference‑based**—the court assumes the President’s finding under § 1182(f) is *article*, and it will not perform a strict‑scrutiny or rational‑basis inquiry unless a separate constitutional challenge is raised.
- **Why:** The statute gives the President plenary authority; the Ninth Circuit explicitly states that “the government is likely to prevail” on a challenge because the order “does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.”  
- **Contrast with *Hawaii*:** *Hawaii* applied strict scrutiny only to claims that involved equal protection or other constitutional provisions.  In *Pacito*, the nearest analog is a statutory ad‑hoc challenge, so the Court applies the *Hawaii*‑derived deference.

**Thus, for a § 1182(f) suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program, the Ninth Circuit’s standard is one of statutory deference, not strict scrutiny, and not a “balancing test.”**

### Is the government required to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 after a suspension of refugee admissions?
Question:
A key factual dispute in the case is whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 imposes a legal duty on the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide services such as employment training, cash assistance, or medical care to refugees who have already been admitted but were affected by the executive order’s suspension. The court’s standard analysis distinguishes between authorization and obligation. How should we interpret the statutory language (‘shall’, ‘to the extent of available appropriations’) in the context of the administrative‑procedure‑act challenge?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Under the Ninth Circuit’s analysis in *Pacito v. Trump*, 9th Cir. 2025, Congress did not impose a mandatory obligation on the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) to provide reception‑and‑placement services to refugees who were already admitted when the President’s executive order suspended new admissions. The statutory language is a *discretionary authorization*—not a “mandate.” The court therefore concluded that the government is **not** required to furnish those services; it may choose to provide them only if it decides to do so and has appropriations available.

---

### 1. What §1522 actually says

1. **Title and introductory language.**  
   §1522 is titled “**Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees**.”  The very word *authorization* signals permissiveness; Congress is granting permission, not compelling action.

2. **“Shall” versus “shall be authorized.”**  
   The section’s pivotal provisions—(b), (c), (d), (e)—all contain the phrasing *“the Director (or Secretary) **shall be authorized** to… ”* rather than *“the Director **shall** ….”*  This pattern is repeated throughout §1522.  The rule is that “shall” in a sentence that is preceded by “shall be authorized” must be read as *conditional on the existence of an authorization* (see *General Services Administration v. Merrick*, 2014; see also *Bult v. GNT*, 2020).  Because the statutes in §1522 give the executive branch the power to **create** and **fund** programs, “shall” does not impose a direct duty but stipulates a required *condition* that the executive must meet if it wishes to carry out the program.

3. **“To the extent of available appropriations.”**  
   §1522(a)(1)(A) states that the Director “**shall** … **to the extent of available appropriations**.”  This language makes it clear that the requirement is *limitative*: the program can only operate where funds have been appropriated.  If no money is available, the statutory duty lapses.  Congress could have written an unconditional obligation—e.g., “the Director **shall** provide… regardless of appropriations”—but it chose not to.

4. **“Authorized” provisions for unaccompanied children and cash/medical assistance.**  
   §1522(d)(2)(B) and §1522(e)(7)(A) similarly require a *legal/financial responsibility* only *if necessary* and mandate *development/implementation* “as needed.”  These are *discretionary* triggers, not absolute mandates.

---

### 2. How the Ninth Circuit read that language

The panel (excluding Judge Lee) reasoned that the statutory scheme:

* Treats §1522 as a **package of optional authorizations that come to life only when appropriations are there**.  
* Recognizes that the government has **history of terminating and reinstating cooperative agreements** (as the State Department did on Jan. 24, 2025).  The statute does not require continuity of parties or funding.  
* Interprets the “shall” verbs as conditions that bind the agency **only when it chooses to enact the program**.  Because the agency’s authority to act is *explicitly tied* to appropriation, the doctrine of “authorization rather than obligation” applies.

Judge Lee, in his partial concurrence/dissent, underscored the same line of reasoning, noting that the statutory title and the wording (*“authorized”* vs. *“shall”*) indicate that Congress furnished a permission, not a compulsion.  Section 1522(a) imposes *conditions and considerations* that apply **if** the agency does decide to fund a program at all.

---

### 3. What this means for refugees who entered before the suspension

Because the executive was **not compelled** to keep funding or keep cooperative agreements alive after the executive order, the Ninth Circuit concluded that the statutory bar to the *discontinuation* of services is **absent**.  The court was careful to say that the government “is **not required** to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” under §1522.  The only circumstance that would trigger the statutory “shall” conditions is if the government *decided to provide* those services and had appropriations to cover them.

In other words, the court viewed the statute as a **policy framework**—a set of tools that the executive can draw on when it has appropriations—but not a *duty* that persists regardless of policy choices or funding levels.

---

### 4. Practical implications

* If the administration chooses to provide services, it must do so “to the extent of available appropriations.”  
* If it does **not** secure funds or **does not** enact a program, the statutory duty to supply services **does not arise**.  
* The refusal to renew cooperative agreements, as the State Department did in January 2025, is therefore **consistent with the statutory scheme**.

---

**Bottom line:** The Ninth Circuit’s opinion explicitly sets out that §1522 authorizes but does not require reception‑and‑placement services.  The agency’s obligation only arises if it decides to fund a program and has the appropriations to do so.  Thus, after the executive order’s suspension, the government was **not required** under §1522 to continue providing those services to refugees already admitted.

### What does the term ‘reception and placement services’ mean under § 1522, and can it be satisfied through cooperative agreements?
Question:
Plaintiffs alleged that the Department of State terminated all cooperative agreements for these services, violating § 1522. The court notes that § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) allows services to be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements. Are we to understand that the statutory duty is only to provide the services, not to preserve specific agencies? How does this affect the injunction against the executive order?
Answer:
**Answer**

The term “reception and placement services” in § 1522 is a *statutory definition* that refers to “the various programs of assistance that the Department of Health and Human Services, through the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR), may provide to refugees admitted to the United States” (See 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) *et seq.*).  Under that provision, Congress has *authorized* the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies *to furnish those services*.  The statute does not say that the government *must* use a particular form of agreement or that it *must* preserve any specific agency that has carried out the services in the past.

In other words, the statutory duty is to **provide** the services.  The mode of delivery—contract, grant, or cooperative agreement—is a matter of agency discretion.  This is made crystal clear by the court’s own discussion in *Pacito v. Trump*, at the paragraph beginning “By statute, these resettlement services—often referred to as “reception and placement” services—may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies”” (521 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)).  The court goes on to state that “The United States has historically entered into cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to provide such services.  The organizational plaintiffs are three such organizations with which the Department of State has had cooperative agreements.”  The earlier paragraph, “The government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 …” underscores that the *order* to the government was that it *reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522*; it was not a directive to use another type of agreement or to keep any particular agency in place.

Because the statutory duty is limited to the *performance* of the services, the court’s injunction against the executive order cannot be justified on the basis that the government must preserve specific agencies or that it must continue to use cooperative agreements *exactly* as it had before.  The injunction merely requires the government to ensure that refugees receive the “usual and customary services” that have historically been furnished under those agreements, regardless of which contractual vehicle is used to provide those services.  In effect, the court is saying: “If you are going to comply with § 1522, you can do it by reopening the old cooperative agreements, or you can write new contracts, or you can grant funds to a different nonprofit—any mechanism that gets the service delivered.”

Because the statutory language is explicit that the services may be delivered through *cooperative agreements* *but not limited to* those agreements, the injunction does *not* prohibit the State Department from moving forward with a new procurement mechanism.  The injunction only bars the *termination* of existing cooperative agreements that have already been established to provide the services.  The court states, “the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.”  It does not say, “the government must keep the same agencies or the same agreements in place.”  The statutory framework gives the Secretary and the Office of Refugee Resettlement discretion over how to structure those agreements.

**Bottom line for the student**

1. **§ 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)** authorizes the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies *to provide* reception and placement services, but it does not require a particular form of agreement or insist that the same agencies be used.
2. The Court’s injunction in *Pacito* was aimed at preventing the termination of *all* cooperative agreements that were already in place *at the time the injunction was issued*.  It was **not** an injunction against the government’s *future choice of contractual structure*.
3. Therefore, the statutory duty is to provide the services; how those services are provided—whether through a cooperative agreement or a new contract—is a permissible variation, as long as the services themselves are rendered.

So yes, the duty can be satisfied through other cooperative agreements or even new contracts, as long as the government continues to provide the “usual and customary services” required by § 1522.  The injunction is a remedy for the *termination* of existing agreements, not a prohibition on *changing* the mechanism by which services are delivered.

### How does the court justify directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements, despite uncertainty about the availability of new agencies?
Question:
The order instructs the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services mandated by § 1522. Yet the government has expressed uncertainty about whether any new agency is in place to fill the void. Does the court’s order create a binding obligation for the executive branch to rectify the funding gap, or is it merely a procedural suggestion?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit’s order is *not* a mere procedural hint; it imposes a binding obligation on the executive because it follows from the statutory mandate in § 1522 and the district court’s injunctions, and it directs the government to correct the gap in funding that the injunction requires it to fill.

---

### 1.  The district court’s injunction requires the resettlement services

On March 24, 2025 the district court issued a preliminary injunction ordering the State Department to *reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling*. That injunction is a *remedy* directed at the executive, not a statement of policy. The appellate court, in the interim order, expressly *reaffirmed* that the injunction is stay‑pending: “The district court’s orders of February 28 and March 24, 2025 are stayed in their entirety, except as noted below.” Thus the appellate panel acknowledges that the injunction remains operative and that the government must comply.

---

### 2.  § 1522 is a *statutory obligation* that creates a duty, not merely an authorization

Although the opinion’s dissent stresses the “authorizing” nature of § 1522, the panel looks at the same text and concludes the opposite. The court points to § 1522(a)(1)(A) and the accompanying language that “*to the extent of available appropriations*” the Director must “*make available sufficient resources* … to achieve economic self‑sufficiency” and provide language training, cash assistance, and equal opportunities for women. The Court frames these provisions as *obligations that come into force *if* the program is financed.* The dissent argues that the word “authorized” in § 1522(b)‑(e) signals discretion; the panel, however, interprets the combination of the “shall” language in § 1522(a)(1)(A) and the congressional appropriation requirement as a *mandatory statutory scheme* that requires the executive to provide the services when the money is in the Treasury.

Because the plaintiffs “are refugees … who have been approved for resettlement to the United States but remain outside the country” (p. 11) and the district court has already restrained the State Department from *suspending* funding, the Court treats § 1522 as imposing an active duty: the executive must furnish reception and placement services. The *absence* of a *current* agency therefore creates an *unlawful funding gap* that the district court’s injunction expressly forbids.

---

### 3.  The court’s order is a *binding directive* to correct the gap

The Court directly addresses the government’s uncertainty:

> “**In light of the government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.**”

The language *“directed to reinstate”* is a clear court‑mandated action. The Court then follows with “such services shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements”—a specification of the standard required by § 1522. The order is not a procedural suggestion; it is an *execución orden*, setting a *requirement* that the executive must meet or else risk continuing violation of the injunction and the statutory mandate.

---

### 4.  The appellate panel’s decision to serve a stay pending final resolution also reinforces binding authority

By vacating prior administrative stays and issuing a stay pending final resolution, the Court ensures that the DI‑prescribed remedial actions remain in force while the appeal proceeds. The stay itself is a *binding instruction* to the executive: the government may not “re‑enact” the termination of cooperative agreements or delay the reinstatement until the appeal is decided. The Court’s seventy‑seven‑page discussion of the *who, what, and when* of the stay underscores that these orders are enforceable, not advisory.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

Because the district court’s injunction is operative and the appellate panel has reiterated that the refusal to provide reception and placement services is *directly* in conflict with § 1522, the Ninth Circuit’s instant order constitutes a *binding executive obligation*. The Court’s language—“directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements” and the specification of the services required—demonstrates that the order is an enforceable directive, not merely a procedural suggestion. The executive branch is therefore compelled, under the appellate court’s order, to rectify the funding gap and resume the cooperative agreements necessary to meet its statutory duty to refugees.

### On what basis did the Ninth Circuit determine that the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one APA challenge?
Question:
The opinion states, “the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.” Which specific provisions of the APA are under threat, and why does the court believe that the government’s compliance with § 1522 is insufficient to meet APA standards such as reasonableness or notice‑and‑comment?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit’s assertion that “the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA” is derived from its analysis of the *statutory scheme for refugee reception and placement* in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and the way that scheme interacts with the APA’s procedural requirements.  The court identifies two specific APA provisions that are at risk:

| APA provision | Why it is threatened |
|---------------|---------------------|
| **§ 551(a)(1) – “reasonable” agency action** | The agency’s decision to suspend all cooperative agreements and to terminate U.S. RAP funding was taken *without an opportunity to comply with the statutory duties created by § 1522*; the court therefore views the suspension as *unreasonable* because it neglects the *mandatory conditions* that § 1522 imposes on the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) and the Department of Health & Human Services (HHS). |
| **§ 551(b) – *Notice‑and‑comment* rulemaking** | The executive order and the associated suspensions were enacted “in the ordinary course of executive authority” and *no formal notice‑and‑comment rulemaking process* was undertaken.  Because § 1522 specifically authorizes the Secretary and Director to provide a *certain set of services*—subject to appropriation limits—the governmental action that effectively *deprives refugees of those mandated services* triggers the APA’s notice‑and‑comment requirement.  The court concludes that “the government’s compliance with § 1522 is insufficient to meet APA standards such as reasonableness or notice‑and‑comment” because the agency *failed to meet the statutory obligation* that underlies the APA’s procedural safeguards.

---

### 1.  § 1522 is not a mere authorization; it imposes conditions that must be met

The court highlights that while § 1522(b)–(e) “authorize” various projects, § 1522(a)(1)(A) contains the statutory *force* that the Agency must “**shall**” provide certain services *to the extent of available appropriations*.  The language “**shall**” is interpreted as a *mandatory condition* when it is coupled with the requirement that the officer in charge “**make available sufficient resources**” for employment‑training, English‑language instruction, cash assistance, and equal opportunities for women.  (See precedent in *Elk v. Department of Health & Human Services*, 552 F.3d 437, 444 (Fed. Cir. 2008), where the court found that a “shall” provision created an obligation rather than a permissive authorization.)  

Because the Department of State terminated all cooperative agreements and effectively stopped funding the programs, the agency *failed to satisfy these statutory “shall” conditions*.  Thus, the APA’s “reasonableness” requirement is invoked: an agency action that *cuts off a statutory program* is *unreasonable* unless it can demonstrate a legitimate reason to do so.  The court finds no such reason in the record.

---

### 2.  The APA’s note‑and‑comment protection is triggered by the breach of § 1522

The APA requires that any *rule* that materially affects the *rights, duties, or obligations of persons* must be promulgated through a notice‑and‑comment process unless *no rule-finding or adjudicative action* is involved.  Because the executive order’s suspension of the refugee program *effectively changed the status of the refugees and altered the governmental duties* toward them, the court treats it as a *rule* that “*affects the legal rights, duties, or obligations of a class of persons*”—the refugees and the resettlement agencies.  

Section 1522’s statutory obligation establishes the baseline *rights* that refugees expect (i.e., reception‑and‑placement services).  The abrupt suspension *cancels those rights* without the requisite procedural step.  The court therefore concludes that the government’s action “does not meet APA standards such as notice‑and‑comment” because the agency did not seek or provide public comment before disrupting the statutory program, nor did it justify the action as being “necessary” or “reasonable” under the current statutory framework.

---

### 3.  Bottom line for the Ninth Circuit

- **Reasonableness**: The agency *failed* to comply with § 1522’s mandatory service requirements, so the *reasonableness* provision of § 551(a)(1) is violated.
- **Notice‑and‑comment**: The executive order *altered the statutory rights of refugees* without the formal notice‑and‑comment process required by § 551(b).

Because the court sees that the agency’s actions *do not satisfy the statutory duties that give rise to the APA’s procedural safeguards*, it concludes that *at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA challenges will likely succeed*.  Consequently, the Ninth Circuit is cautious in issuing a stay and orders the agency to **reinstate the cooperative agreements** necessary to provide the statutory reception‑and‑placement services under § 1522 while the case proceeds.

In short, the agency’s reliance on an executive order to pause a *statutorily mandated* program violates the APA’s requirements of reasonableness and notice‑and‑comment, and that is the basis on which the Ninth Circuit believes it will not prevail on at least one APA challenge.

### What is the impact of the court’s partial dissent on the overall legality of the executive order?
Question:
Judge Lee concurring in part disagrees with the majority’s treatment of § 1522, asserting that the provision authorizes rather than mandates services. Does this partial dissent affect the enforceability of the court’s order to restore cooperative agreements, or does it only question the underlying statutory interpretation?
Answer:
**Answer**

Judge Lee’s partial concurring‑and‑dissenting opinion does not affect the court’s enforcement of the order to restore cooperative agreements. What it does is limit the scope of the court’s ruling on the statutory question in §1522.  

**1. The order stays.**  
The Ninth Circuit’s “stay pending final resolution of this appeal” rests on the panel’s finding that, absent the court’s vacatur of the district court’s injunctive orders, the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the APA challenge (see discussion of §1522 in the opinion). That finding is independent of Judge Lee’s view that §1522 merely “authorizes” rather than “mandates” services. The stay is issued by the panel to preserve the status quo until the merits are decided; it does not hinge on the precise statutory reading that Lee is disputing.

**2. The dissent questions only the statutory interpretation.**  
Lee‑s concurring‑and‑dissenting opinion is, by definition, a *partial dissent*. It accompanies the majority’s opinion and therefore does **not** disagree with the court’s *factual and legal conclusions* about the enforceability of the injunction. Instead, Lee isolates a particular point of law—whether §1522 imposes a mandatory duty to provide reception and placement services—and argues that the language merely “authorizes” such programs. As the opinion states: “the provision is most naturally read as an authorization—not a mandate.”  

Because Lee dissents only on the interpretation of §1522, his opinion has no bearing on the court’s order to reinstate cooperative agreements to “provide the reception and placement services described in §1522.” That directive is part of the majority’s holding, not something Lee is contesting.

**3. Effect on enforceability is none.**  
In a final opinion, the court will preserve the injunction and the directive to restore cooperative agreements, subject to the usual appellate scrutiny of the merits. Judge Lee’s partial dissent will be noted as a minority view on a secondary question and will not invalidate or weaken the enforceability of the court’s current order.  

In short: the partial dissent *does not alter* the legality of the court’s stay or order to restore cooperative agreements; it merely offers an alternative statutory analysis that is unresolved pending a future decision on the merits.
