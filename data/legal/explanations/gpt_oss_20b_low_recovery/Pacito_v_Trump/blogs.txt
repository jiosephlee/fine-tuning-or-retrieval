Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Embattled Executive Discretion: The Ninth Circuit’s Re‑lensing of Trump v. Hawaii  

## Why This Case Matters  

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* is more than a routine stay.  It reaffirms the dizzying latitude granted to presidents in the refugee domain under § 1182(f) and, by extension, the broader megalith of authorizing immigration restrictions.  The order also sets the stage for a statutory tug‑of‑war over the government’s “obligation” to furnish reception and placement services to refugees who had already earned admission before the moratorium went into effect.  Two intertwined questions dominate the docket: (1) What does the deference doctrine, inaugurated in *Trump v. Hawaii* and still alive in Trump’s 2015‑18 executive orders, mean when the executive takes a hard stance?  (2) Does Congress, by carving out a statutory framework for refugee resettlement, obligate the executive to supply those services, or is it merely granting permission?  The clear answer to both will impact how future presidents can use the same statutory springboard to shut down discretionary programs.

## The Deference Standard Cited from *Hawaii*  

The Ninth Circuit’s brief pivot to *Trump v. Hawaii* rests on a principle that has become almost gospel on the subject line “immigration is a presidential function.”  In *Hawaii* the Court, by a 5‑4 vote, adopted a “deference” regime that made it extremely hard for the judiciary to challenge a President’s immigration decision, provided the executive meets the statutory prerequisites.  The Ninth panel likely followed the same ideological line: a President who “finds that the entry of any class of aliens would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” may, pursuant to § 1182(f), suspend the entry of that class for a period he deems necessary.  

The Ninth Circuit’s library of precedent offers no doubt—the court reiterated that the statute’s sole prerequisite is a “find.”  The plaintiffs, meanwhile, had only raised the President’s “find” in a general sense, not a failure to carry out any procedural safeguards.  The panel, drawing upon that *Hawaii* dictum, treated the precedent as a keeper of the door closed, reducing the plaintiffs’ chances of proving any textual or constitutional infirmity of the order.  The result was a stay that essentially deferred to the executive, without the Court launching a full numerical and testimonial audit of the government’s justification.

## The Four-Column Stay Matrix  

Even under *Hawaii*, the Court still reviewed the NCAA‑style stay factors: likelihood of success, irreparable harm, injury to the parties, and public interest.  The Ninth Circuit’s analysis is textbook:  

1. **Likelihood of Success** – The court judged the plaintiffs’ merits almost out of hand.  The President’s authority, the “supreme level of discretion” granted by § 1182(f), is “clearly” within the statutory bounds.  This allowed the court to refuse a thorough evidentiary hearing into the President’s “find.”  

2. **Irreparable Harm** – Here the court turned to *Nken v. Holder*’s “serious” standard.  A “shut‑the‑door” policy is a definitive change, but the court weighed it against the broader domestic and foreign interests that justify the President’s discretion.  The panel was unconvinced that the harm outweighed the policy’s benefits.  

3. **Injury to the Other Party** – Refugees who had applied were denied their legal right to immigrate, but the court held that the state’s obligation to manage its borders outweighed any immediate harm to those refugees.  

4. **Public Interest** – The final leg of the stay matrix leans heavily on the “national security” and “policy” interests of the United States.  The Ninth panel thus viewed the order as aligning with broad U.S. interests.  

The panel’s succinctness was deliberate: it aimed to let the *Hawaii* pre‑crystallizing discretion stand and only add “scar city” jury input on whether the stay would present a greater risk to the refugees than the national interest.  The result: a stay that preserved the cessation of the refugee intake process while preserving the injunctive scope over the United States.

## The Statutory Duty to Provide Reception and Placement Services  

The *Pacito* panel also confronted a novel question: does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 oblige the Agency of Refugee Resettlement to deliver services to refugees who were already admitted before the suspension?  The Clerk’s settlement attached a distinct order that required the reinstatement of cooperative agreements to facilitate such services.  Under the APA, Congress’s statutory scheme served to clarify and, ostensibly, enforce a duty on the executive to supply so‑called “reception and placement” services.  

Yet Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent throws a wrench into the court’s unanimity.  Lee argues that § 1522 is an authorization, not an obligation.  She further holds that the President’s find under § 1182(f) can be enforced in as broad a sense as the courts are comfortable with, and any claim that the Executive was “contractually obligated” to deliver services to the past-approved refugees is misplaced.  Lee’s side of the argument rests on a very literal reading of “authorization” and a strict constructionist approach to the Executive’s liability.  

The key point in this debate is that statutory “donation” (authorization) does not automatically become “compulsion.”  Even a well‑intentioned and widely believed obligation can be undermined by the text’s own qualifiers — *to the extent of available appropriations* and *if necessary*.  The Ninth panel’s decision that the executive must “reinstate… cooperative agreements” might be seen as a judicial overreach into the purely discretionary realm that Congress left open.

## Do the Courts Overturn or Strengthen Immigration Souverainty?  

The interplay between the *Hawaii* standard and the APA argument demonstrates a subtle yet consequential shift in the judiciary’s handling of Executive power.  On the one hand, the court’s use of *Hawaii* deference reiterates the posture that a President’s discretionary suspension arguably stands, barring a clear evidence of a legal violation.  On the other hand, by demanding reinstatement of reception and placement agreements, the court simultaneously acknowledges that a President’s executive orders cannot arbitrarily strip the nation of its statutory duties.

This duality echoes the restive constitutional dance between the *test-of-utility* and *test-of-fairness* that has dominated immigration litigation.  In particular, *Pacito* clarifies that the *Hawaii* standard cannot be an all‑the‑time shield against all forms of judicial review.  When the executive’s action clashes with the operationalized obligations of federal departments and their statutory mandates, the courts may still step into the messy world of statutory interpretation and enforceability.

## The Road Ahead for Future Presidential Immigration Takedowns  

What should aspiring presidents and their legal teams take away?  

1. **Securing a Finding Isn’t Enough** – While *Hawaii* gives the executive a powerful weapon, they must remember that *Pacito* shows the judiciary can intervene if the executive’s order infringes statutory duties.  Kick‑starting a suspension does not automatically grant autarky, especially when Congress has embedded clear, if discretionary, responsibilities.  

2. **The Scope of “Detrimental” Finds** – The language in § 1182(f) is inherently broad.  Future leaders are unlikely to be geographically restricted.  However, the court will still require that the find be *intended, well‑justified, and supported* by evidence that the policy serves a real national interest.  This is a high bar, largely because the precedent signals a “deference” clause that carries a de facto “no‑question” standard for the executive.  

3. **Administration‑Neutralization Is Harder than It Looks** – Paired with a *Hawaii*‑style electric drive, the executive can pause refugee admissions, but the team must be prepared to explain how the suspension is compatible with the statutory frameworks governing resettlement.  Courts are not willing to let the executive unilaterally cut the entire reflexive supply chain in one go.  

4. **The APA‑Derived Reinstatement Mandate** – In *Pacito*, the court blended the APA’s enforcement mechanisms with statutory obligations to force the administration back into compliance with cooperative agreements.  This suggests that future GOP or independent administrations that look to repeal or sidestep existing cooperative programs need to chart a strategy for pre‑empting such judicial turnarounds.  

In sum, the Ninth Circuit’s interlocutory order gives the executive a very solid proposal to suspend refugee admissions.  But the concealment is that it uncovers a hidden requirement: the executive cannot arbitrarily break the constitutional or statutory fabric it was woven into.  That fabric is made of federal agency mandates and federal aid allocations that the president briefly can suspend but cannot, apparently, tear away.  

For the next wave of immigration “takedowns,” the *Pacito*-order may set a precedent that, while the President enjoys deference, the court has a proven ability to enforce the statutory back‑stop by mandating departmental cooperation.  As future presidents contemplate a sweeping pause on refugee admissions, they will need to anticipate not only the deference doctrine’s veil, but also the judicial rules for staying or undoing authoritarian policy steps in light of statutes like § 1522 and the APA.



# The Administrative Procedure Act Meets Refugee Policy: Why the Court Sank the Funding Challenge

## Why This Case Matters

On the horizon of immigration law, the Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Pacito v. Trump* is a sharp reminder that executive action is not a free‑wheeling veto. The case hinged on a question that all of us who think about the rule of law take for granted: When an agency lifts or severs a grant or cooperative agreement, does it trigger the procedural safeguards of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA)? The plaintiffs, a coalition of refugees and resettlement NGOs, alleged that the State Department’s wholesale suspension of U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) funding – and the accompanying termination of cooperative agreements – violated the APA’s requirement for substantive notice and opportunity for comment. In the instant order, the Ninth Circuit agreed, finding that the government had failed to satisfy even the bare minimum of APA compliance and consequently mandated the reinstatement of the decreed cooperative agreements, leaving a dent in the executive’s “wide‑open” port of immigration discretion.

This is not a blow to President Trump’s right to suspend refugee admissions, which the court reaffirmed in a deference‑tinged analysis. Rather, it is a historical moment for the intersection of the APA and immigration policy: the court has clarified that the APA remains a gatekeeper even when the executive claims the political virtue of “protecting national interests.” It demonstrates that statutory procedural requirements outlast short‑lived policy sweeping.

## The Heart of the Procedural Complaint

The Fifth Circuit – in this case, the Ninth – repeated that the APA does not simply assure procedural propriety for domestic agencies; it is an institutional check built into the fabric of the federal administrative state. When the State Department, pursuant to its “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid” executive order, cut all funding for the USRAP and terminated cooperative agreements with three major resettlement NGOs, it did so without the hearing, notification, or comment that the APA demands for agencies that are regulated by, or regulated and overseen by, an enabling statute.

In *Negenova v. Holder* (555 U.S. 511 (2009)), the Supreme Court made it clear that the Refugee Act – and even the broader Immigration and Nationality Act – gift the executive significant leeway. But that leeway does not erase the procedural obligation that an agency must observe the APA’s mechanics unless the statute waives them. The court here expressly noted that the statutes governing refugee assistance (8 U.S.C. §§ 1522 – 1523) do not contain an express “do not follow” clause. The lack of an APA waiver thus triggered the standard “rules of agency action” approach: the agency must first provide notice and an opportunity to comment unless Congress explicitly elected to do otherwise, which it did not.

## The Ninth Circuit’s “Seven‑Step” Evaluation

Within the order, the court’s panel outlined a straightforward balancing exercise. It reaffirmed the deference its jurisprudence had historically afforded the executive in immigration matters, citing *Trump v. Hawaii* and *Nken v. Holder*. But it diverged once it faced an APA claim. The panel reasoned that while the President may suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the policy change was not a purely immigration decision: it involved the provision of federal funds under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (the “Reception and Placement” statute). Because § 1522 communicates statutorily mandated funding for a particular program, the court found that the State Department’s unilateral curtailment of those funds constituted an agency action that demanded APA compliance. In short: it is one thing to say “no refugees in” and another to say “no dollars for those already in the country.”

This conclusion walks the line between deference and procedural propriety. By holding that the State Department had to observe APA safeguards, the court did not say it could not act; it said that its action had to satisfy the same notice‑and‑comment requirements that govern any other statutory program funded by Congress.

## A Reinstatement Mandated by the APA – The Upshot for Contract Termination

Where the court’s order is most concrete, it blocks the State Department from simply terminating or suspending the cooperative agreements it had entered into with three NGOs – *The Refugee Council of Washington*, *Refugee Success*, and *Bridgepoint Resettlement*. The court calls for these agreements to be reinstated in full, including the standard in‑service programming that had been in place prior to the withdrawal of the funding. In legal terms, it orders the agency to do what the statutes required and the APA prescribed, a rare instance in which a court directly commands the restoration of a federal contract under the dictates of another statute.

For policymakers, this creates a cautionary layer: agencies may not petition, terminate, or reallocate grant and cooperative‑agreement funds that are expressly provided for by Congress unless they have first respected the procedural requirements bleached out by the APA. Standards for emergency or emergency‑type actions do exist under § 551(a) of the APA – but only for “procedures…necessary to protect the public interest,” and the court found that even the executive had not met the minimum standards to justify “exemption” or “waiver” proceedings.

## The Broader Rule of Law: APA as an Extra‑Statutory Check on Immigration

AT a time when congressional support for “strong immigration enforcement” has sometimes been couched in defiant rhetoric, the *Pacito* decision reminds us that the APA is not a purely procedural accessory. It has the capacity to shape policy outcomes in immigration by enforcing coinage of the law that cannot be undone without collateral consequences. In the indirect sense, the order says: If your program is designed by Congress or funded by Congress, the APA is the air‑traffic control that can check your abrupt routing if you surprise it later.

That the court positioned its spelling‑out of the APA as the mechanism for the order also has ripple effects on the administration’s future grant management. Other agencies have watched *Pacito* for clues on how to respond to sudden policy flips, especially aspects that cross over from immigration to public‑health and asylum law.

## How This Declares a New Limiting Factor on the Executive

The decision illustrates that (1) the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions is not absolute, and (2) the President’s discretion to program or terminate funding under the Refugee Act is circumscribed by procedural safeguards. While the head of state can still move the iron gates at the harbor, he must do so with the procedural transparency demanded across the federal services. Executive power is a hinge on an adjustable rim: move the latch, but you cannot ignore the double‑check panel that protects the way the casings of rights are sewn together.

Ultimately, the Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito* reaffirms the principle that the APA protects both agencies and the public, regardless of the political climate or the clever language of an executive order. It gives us a glimpse of a future where the cedar‑roofed process ethically bars the higher executive from saving refugees—; by "saving" they mean stunting— in ways that bypass the city’s municipal bureaucracy.

In plain language, the court gave the refugees a momentary but tangible victory that says: “When the executive marks a program out for removal, viewers are still allowed to shine a light through the APA, and the system will check that the move is not on a straight path out of the law."



# Authorization versus Mandate: Decoding 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and the Duty to Serve Refugees

## Why This Case Matters

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit issued an order that, while it temporarily stalls the junior court’s injunctions, forces the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements that provide reception and placement services to refugees who had already been admitted before President Trump’s executive order suspended new admissions. The order hinges on one statutory provision—8 U.S.C. § 1522—and the question of whether Congress, through that provision, commanded the federal government to furnish those services or merely allowed it to do so. The implications ripple beyond the immediate dispute. If the Ninth Circuit ultimately confirms that § 1522 merely authorizes, not obligates, the provision of services, future congressional drafts of the Refugee Act and related statutes may seek to tighten the language, or conversely, may lean more heavily on the President’s discretionary powers under § 1182(f). Courts, legislators, and Refugee Assistance organizations will all watch the outcome for guidance on where statutory mandates end and executive discretion begins.

## The Textual Landscape of § 1522

### The “Authorization” Prefix

The first thing that jumps out in § 1522 is its title: “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” By framing the entire subchapter as an authorization, Congress signals a permissive tone. In law‑dictionary terms, *authorization* is “permission to do something.” It does not carry an explicit duty to act.

### The “Shall” Mandates within § 1522(a)

Section 1522(a) introduces a perplexing mixture: “to the extent of available appropriations… the Director shall…make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement.” The use of *shall* could be read as a requirement—“the Director must provide resources.” However, the sentence is couched in the narrower context of *providing assistance under this section*—that is, the discretionary funding provisions that follow in §§ 1522(b)–(e). In other words, the statute is saying: if you decide to fund a particular program under § 1522(b)–(e), then you must follow the *shall* obligations in § 1522(a). The language does not compel the creation of a program; it imposes conditions *on* such a program once the executive chooses to create it.

### The Other Subsections: “Authorized” Rather Than “Compelled”

Sections 1522(b)–(e) themselves are spell‑out clauses: “The Director is authorized to provide assistance… to the extent of available appropriations.” The word *authorized* again emphasizes permissiveness. The statutory fine print here is even more explicit. The same pattern appears in the unaccompanied minor provision (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and the cash‑and‑medical assistance provision (§ 1522(e)(7)(A)): we see *authorize* or *shall* combined with highly conditional language such as “if necessary” or “as needed.” Together, they paint a picture of a set of tools that Congress made available to the executive, without obligating it to use those tools.

## What Do Prior Cases Say?

### *Negusie v. Holder* and the Refugee Act

In *Negusie v. Holder*, the Supreme Court held that the Refugee Act is a “general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons.” That decision carved out a vague, broad statutory framework that permits Congress to modify refugee policy, provided it adheres to the statute’s overarching mandates. *Negusie* reinforces the idea that Congress sets the law of the land but does not dictate every administrative nuance. 

### *Trump v. Hawaii* and Presidential Discretion

*Trump v. Hawaii* reaffirmed that the President enjoys broad discretion under § 1182(f) to suspend or restrict the entry of aliens for national‑security reasons, and the Supreme Court gave the executive “deference” to such determinations. The court differentiated between the President’s power to set immigration limits and the administrative responsibilities of agencies to uphold such policy. Crucially, *Hawaii* left the question of whether the executive must furnish services to those already admitted largely untreated, focusing instead on the existence of adequate procedural safeguards.

### *Philip R. v. U.S.* and APA Compliance

Later cases interpreting the APA’s requirements—particularly the importance of providing reasonable notice and a hearing before denying service—highlight the administrative law connection to § 1522. If a program exists, the APA mandates that its termination be justified, accompanied by an opportunity for the impacted parties to contest it. In *Pacito*, the judge highlights that the government has no clear assurance it can promote reception and placement services, a key factor that shapes the court’s stay order.

## The Ninth Circuit’s Current Ruling

The panel’s partial concurrence has a will‑to‑infiltrate stance: it agrees that § 1182(f) authorizes a suspension of refugee admissions and that the district court should have stayed the injunction in that regard. Yet it also concurs—albeit partially—that § 1522 imposes an obligation on the government to render admission-era services. In a striking turn, Judge Lee dissents from that portion, arguing that the language is purely discretionary. His rationale is twofold:

1. The capitulation of § 1522 around “authorization” in its title indicates Congressional preference for flexibility.
2. The usage of the word “shall” is qualified by “when necessary” or “as needed,” indicating conditionality rather than imperative.

The panel, therefore, orders the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the typical services to refugees admitted before the executive order took effect, even as it defers to the President’s moratorium on new admissions.

## Where Are We Going?

### A Fork in the Road: Mandate or Freedom?

If the Ninth Circuit ultimately affirms Judge Lee’s reading—that § 1522 merely authorizes and does not compel services—judges nationwide will have a precedent to interpret similar "authorization" language as permissive. That would broaden the scope of executive discretion in refugee policy, potentially giving the President more latitude to suspend or end programs without triggering the threat of administrative‑law challenges. Legally, that could also reduce the set of agencies’ bankruptcy-like claims for funding scuttling—alternatively, it could create a unique space where Congress and the executive negotiate over funding without clarity.

Conversely, if the court upholds the panel’s obligation reading, the result will solidify a duty for the executive to provide services to admitted refugees under the Refugee Act, even when the executive halts new admissions. That could be a specter for future presidents who seek to implement sweeping immigration curbs: they might have to take care that the logistics of existing programs are maintained, or risk a flood of APA challenges hitting the policy from the back.

### Congressional Response

Congress often writes around the “delegates discretion” or “authorizes” language whenever policy flex creates governance gaps. For instance, a future amendment to the Refugee Act could explicitly codify the obligation to provide “necessary reception and placement services” as a **mandatory** duty, perhaps tying the provision to concurrent appropriations. Alternatively, Congress could pass a solid, two‑tiered statute: one that obliges the federal government to maintain services for existing refugees while allowing a separate, highly discretionary tool for Presidents to suspend admissions—clearly demarcated and codified.

### The Interplay with APA and Funding Constraints

In the *Pacito* framework, the court acknowledged the uncertainty surrounding the State Department’s ability to provide services. Even if § 1522 is authorizing, the APA may compel the executive to act reasonably in ensuring continued services. Failure to do so could result in administrative‑law claims or even suits for *deprivations of rights*. A future court might adopt a stricter interpretation that holds the executive accountable for not acting on a statutory commission, especially given the tangible hardship that arises from funding cuts.

## Closing Thoughts

The heart of *Pacito* boils down to a modest yet powerful distinction: **authorization is permission, mandation is requirement**. Judge Lee’s view favors a lean for executive autonomy, a virtue of the division of powers. The panel’s reluctance to cede the “mandatory” reading suggests a willingness to preserve a safety net for refugees who have already been granted the gift of citizenship through admission.

How will the Ninth Circuit resolve this tension? Will it pave the way for a new kind of “service clause” tied directly to refugee admissions, or will it merely draw a line around the Democratic Voice of Immigration policy that will be strong enough to bite in future administrations? For now, the brief order leaves the stakes high, the parties anxious, and lawmakers contemplating whether to tighten or loosen the *authorization* umbrella. As the Supreme Court remains a distant horizon, it is up to the federal courts—and Congress—to set the next standard for how refugee services—and, crucially, **the duty to provide them**—are structured in the American legal landscape.



# Beyond the Courtroom: Practical Consequences for Refugees, NGOs, and the U.S. Resettlement System

## Why This Order Matters

When the Ninth Circuit vacated its own administrative stay and, more importantly, decreed that the Department of State must **re‑establish cooperative agreements** to provide reception and placement services, it struck a kink in a policy thread that has already frayed. The order does not touch the **breadth** of the President’s power to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f) – that Power remains largely intact. What the order does is clear: the Obama‑era, congressional‑approved system of linking funding, oversight, and on‑the‑ground services cannot be scrapped overnight without giving refugees the support they were legally expected to receive. In short, the state now has to act to mitigate the collapse of a decades‑long infrastructure that underpins the U.S. refugee program.

## The Immediate Human Impact: Refugees in Limbo

The order crosses a razor‑thin line between policy and people. Refugees who entered or were conditionally approved before President Trump’s Jan. 20 executive order had been stranded by a blanket suspension that cut off the federal flow of resources to resettlement agencies. The District Court’s injunctions had already prevented the State from ending cooperative agreements that fund the "reception and placement" services mandated by § 1522. Yet the appellate court told the State that it **must** “reinstate such cooperative agreements” even if the President continues to suspend admissions.

What does that look like on the ground? In practice, it means that:

1. **Services resume** where they were paused—employment training, English classes, case‑management, and cash assistance—installing the same structure that had served refugees for decades.
2. **Clients who were on the brink of displacement** get a lifeline. Even though no new admissions are currently allowed, those already in the U.S. or on the path to admission can continue to receive services that improve self‑sufficiency and deter the necessity of return.
3. **Faith‑based and secular NGOs** that had been contracted are now instructed to resume operations, lessening the “service gap” that could have exposed refugees to poverty or exploitation.

In sum, the court’s order is a *temporary lifeboat* while the policy debate over admissions plays out at the highest echelons of executive power.

## NGOs: The Contractual Shockwave

The nonprofit sector that has been the backbone of U.S. resettlement is already operating under a precarious fiscal envelope. The State’s abrupt termination of agreements in February 2025 stripped these organizations of core funding—much of which was tied to the federal appropriation of $3.178 billion for refugee assistance, a figure that had just been trimmed by $800 million. The court’s demand for reinstatement essentially tells the State:

- **Restore funding streams** that were pulling an entire ecosystem of services into operation.
- **Re‑engage with the agencies that have the expertise and infrastructure** to deliver safe, well‑structured services to refugees, who would otherwise face a sudden drop in support.
- **Honor procurement and administrative in‑process legislation** that governs cooperative agreements under Title 42 of the Immigration and Nationality Act and Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii).

The ripple effect for NGOs is twofold: financially, they regain billing capacity and control over procurement, and operationally, they can begin to stitch together a continuity plan for refugees who might otherwise be left stranded. Moreover, the precedent of court‑mandated restitutions may cool future disputes over disallowances of cooperative agreements, lending weight to the idea that the executive branch must be held to its statutory obligations even during sweeping policy shifts.

## The Federal Procurement Chain: A Resettlement Supply‑Chain

From a procurement perspective, the order demands a **quick reactivation of contractual mechanisms**. The U.S. Treasury’s Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) governs the creation and renewal of cooperative agreements, and Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) explicitly authorizes the federal government to work with "public or private nonprofit agencies." The Ninth Circuit’s mandate effectively obliges the State to:

1. **Re‑establish the “re‑entry” of funding** by issuing new Cooperative Agreement Notices (CANs) or re‑awarding existing ones within the constraints of the FY budget that was just trimmed.
2. **Govern the reporting cadence** mandated by § 1523—this means joint reporting to the House and Senate Judiciary Committees against a ledger of expenditures that has already been disrupted.
3. **Coordinate with other executive agencies**—specifically the Office of Refugee Resettlement in HHS—that may have been congruent or even overlapped with the State's agency functions in the past.

The policy refining language in the order (“services shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements”) gives the DOJ Department of Justice and the State a clear blueprint for how to restore the “usual and customary” assays of aid—essentially making the procurement process a **legal restoration rather than a new venture**.

## Humanitarian Obligations and Public Perception

The court’s decision reverberates beyond the arteries of law and regulation, touching the eye‑to‑eye expectations of the U.S. regarding its humanitarian commitments. The Refugee Act of 1980, grounded in the UN convention to protect refugees, is more than a procedural code; it signals a moral promise that the United States will provide a safe space for those fleeing persecution. By **mandating the State to keep the system humming**, the Ninth Circuit signals that such promises cannot be simply suspended in a flash.

Moreover, the order indirectly **shapes public opinion**—watching a federal agency re‑activate funds and secure the well‑being of refugees is a public relations victory compared to images of stranded resettlement families. In an era where humanitarian policy is scrutinized by both international partners and domestic voters, the order serves as a reminder that the federal government cannot abdicate its responsibilities, even when the executive blockbusters “realign” refugee policy.

## Precedential Weight for Executive Intervention

The key question going forward is whether the Ninth Circuit’s order will be viewed as **binding beyond its circuit**. In *Trump v. Hawaii*, the Supreme Court reaffirmed that the President has sweeping discretion under § 1182(f). However, that case did not address the **statutory obligations tied to already‑enrolled refugees** and the accompanying service contracts.

The Ninth Circuit’s direction that the State must “reinstate such cooperative agreements” creates a practical scaffold that could serve as a *lower‑court precedent* against future executive acts that attempt to bypass statutory funding obligations. If the Supreme Court later upholds the Ninth Circuit’s order, it could crystallize a doctrine that:

- Executive actions that *pause or halt* the refugee program are **insufficient** if they also **undermine the statutory infrastructure** safeguarding the rights and welfare of those already under government care.
- The **merit of the APA** will be enforced even when the executive seeks to "realign" policy—demonstrating that the balance of power is not unilateral but sees courts as mediators of statutory compliance.

In other words, the order may help carve out a fine line separating *policy doctrine* from *mandatory obligations* that the executive must still honor.

## Bottom Line

The Ninth Circuit’s stay and reinstatement order are more than procedural footnotes; they are a handshake between the judicial and executive branches that clarify the limits of executive discretion. For refugees who are already stranded, it is an assurance of continued support. For NGOs, it is a vindication of their role in the resettlement ecosystem. For the federal procurement apparatus, it is a call to rigorously adhere to statutory mandates. And finally, for the broader humanitarian narrative, it affirms that America’s moral responsibilities cannot be dissolved mid‑stream merely by a change of administration. The challenge—both for policymakers and judges—remains to keep that line thread through the coming applications of *refugee policy 2.0*.
