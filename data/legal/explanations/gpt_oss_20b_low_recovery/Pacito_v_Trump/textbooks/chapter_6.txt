Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 6: # 6. Judge Lee’s Concurring‑in‑Part and Dissenting‑in‑Part

## Chapter Description  

In the Ninth Circuit’s stay order in *Pacito v. Trump*, Judge Lee joined the panel in part and dissented in part. His concurrence affirms the court’s deference to the President’s immigration authority, yet he diverges on whether the federal government is legally required to provide reception and placement services to refugees already in the United States. This chapter unpacks Judge Lee’s reasoning, focusing on how he interprets § 1522 of the Immigration and Nationality Act and what that means for the court’s role in enforcing statutory mandates.

---

## Judge Lee’s concurrence on executive authority to suspend admissions  

Judge Lee’s concurrence begins by agreeing that the President possesses broad discretion under § 1182(f) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). He cites *Trump v. Hawaii* to emphasize that the statutory language “exudes deference” and “vests” the President with sufficient power to suspend refugee admissions.  

In his view, the President’s determination that “entry into the United States of refugees under the U.S.R.A.P. would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” satisfies the statutory requirement that “the President find…that the entry…would be detrimental.” Lee observes that the executive order’s suspension of refugee admissions is therefore “clear, and does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.” Consequently, he agrees with the majority that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the challenge to Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions.  

Because of this deference, Lee endorses the court’s decision to stay the district court’s injunctions regarding the suspension of refugee entry. In this portion of the opinion, he sees no reason to sustain an injunction that would hinder the President’s policy decision.

---

## Judge Lee’s dissent regarding the mandatory nature of § 1522 service obligations  

While agreeing with the majority on the suspension issue, Judge Lee diverges concerning the statutory duty to provide services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority argues that § 1522 imposes “mandatory” requirements for reception and placement services, therefore warranting an injunction to compel the State Department to reinstate funding. 

Lee contests that interpretation. He conducts a textual analysis of § 1522, concluding that the statutory language is “largely discretionary” and that it “authorizes” rather than “mandates” the programs. He repeatedly draws attention to the section’s title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the semantic distinction between “authorization” and “mandate.”  

He bases his dissent on the argument that Congress, by titling the provision and using the word “authorize,” has merely granted permission to the executive for these programs, not imposed a binding obligation. Therefore, a court lacks authority to compel the government to provide services that Congress has not explicitly required.

---

## Interpretation of § 1522 as an authorization rather than a requirement  

### Title and language  

Lee stresses that the heading of § 1522 is critical: it frames the section as an authorizing instrument. He points to the passage “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” as revealing the intended purpose of § 1522—to allow the executive branch to design and fund programs as it deems appropriate.  

### The use of “shall” versus “authorize”  

Even where the text contains the statutory keyword “shall,” Lee notes that the phrase is typically coupled with qualifiers that cushion the mandatory force. For example, § 1522(a)(1)(A) states: “to the extent of available appropriations.” This limiting phrase indicates that any obligation is subject to Congress’s appropriation decisions. Similarly, § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) discusses the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies—forms that inherently rely on agency discretion rather than a statutory mandate.

---

## Discussion of how statutory structure (a § b–e) signals discretionary spending  

Lee examines the internal organization of § 1522:

1. **Section (a) – Conditions and consideration.**  
   This part lists “shall” obligations—employment training, English language training, cash assistance—but it is in the context of “In providing assistance under this section [i.e., the discretionary funding provisions under § 1522(b)–(e)].” Thus, the requirements are conditional on the government choosing to spend those funds.

2. **Sections (b)–(e) – Discretionary funding provisions.**  
   These subsections grant the Director or Secretary the authority to allocate funding under various program items: employment training, cash assistance, medical assistance, and services for unaccompanied minors. The recurring phrase “the Director is authorized” underscores that these are initiatives the executive can elect to create or discontinue as it deems fit, again subject to appropriations.

Lee’s reading is that the statutory structure demonstrates a deliberate separation between conditions (the “shall” provisions) and the discretionary authority to provide fundable programs. The language thus signals to the courts that Congress intended the executive to have latitude in how it implements refugee services, rather than imposing a hard-and-fast obligation.

---

## Implications for future enforcement actions and judicial deference to agency discretion  

Judge Lee’s dissent has several broader implications for how courts approach statutory mandates that relate to agency discretion:

1. **Limits on judicial intervention.**  
   Courts should refrain from ordering the executive to perform programs that Congress has merely authorized. This protects agency autonomy and respects the separation of powers.

2. **Deference doctrine in immigration.**  
   The dissent reinforces the standard set in *Trump v. Hawaii*: that courts traditionally grant broad deference to the executive in immigration matters, except where statutory language is unambiguously mandatory.

3. **Potential future litigation.**  
   If subsequent challenges arise over the availability or scope of refugee services—particularly when there is a dispute over whether funding was actually provided—judicial relief will likely focus on ensuring that the executive has reached compliance with a statutory *obligation* rather than a *permission*. If the court determines, as Judge Lee argues, that Congress has not imposed an obligation, the government may be able to shift service provision to partners or even suspend it pending appropriations.

4. **Policy discretion versus statutory duty.**  
   The Leslie dissent underscores the practical reality that agencies must balance policy goals with legal constraints. In practice, this means that courts must carefully parse statutory language to discern whether an agency is bound to perform a specific function or whether it has latitude to decide how, or even whether, to perform that function.

---

Through this concurring‑in‑part and dissenting‑in‑part contribution, Judge Lee clarifies that while the President may lawfully suspend refugee admissions, the federal government is not strictly compelled by the INA to allocate or deliver reception and placement services to refugees who have already entered the United States. This distinction is essential for legal practitioners when assessing the scope of judicial relief available in immigration‑related cases involving statutory funding provisions.
