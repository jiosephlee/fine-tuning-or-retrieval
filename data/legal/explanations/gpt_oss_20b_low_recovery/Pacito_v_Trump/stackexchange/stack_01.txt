Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Why did the Ninth Circuit vacate the district court’s stay of the July 18 administrative stay?
Question:
In the orders leading up to the current stay, the district court issued an administrative stay of its own July 14 enforcement order. The Ninth Circuit later vacated that administrative stay. As a preliminarily confused reader, I don’t understand why a federal appellate court would reverse a lower court’s attempt to temporarily stop its own order. What legal principles governed that decision?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit vacated the district court’s *administrative stay* because the appellate court determined that the stay was *unnecessary and improper* under the *stay‑standard* that governs federal court stays. The court’s reasoning follows the four‑factor test set forth in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), and was applied specifically to the circumstances surrounding the district court’s July 14 enforcement order.

---

### 1. The *stay* standard

A federal court may issue a stay pending appeal if the applicant

1. is likely to succeed on the merits;  
2. will be irreparably injured if the stay is not granted;  
3. the stay will not substantially injure the other parties; and  
4. the public interest weighs in favor of a stay.  

In *Pacito v. Trump* the Ninth Circuit restates that “the first two factors—showing success on the merits and irreparable injury—are the most critical.”

---

### 2. Why the appellate court reversed the stay

| Factor | District‑court argument | Ninth Circuit’s finding |
|--------|------------------------|-------------------------|
| 1. Likelihood of success | Plaintiffs claimed the executive order violated the Refugee Act and the APA. | The court held “the government is **likely** to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ APA challenges” (see “For reasons to be explained…the government is **not** likely to prevail…under the APA”).  The case therefore did not satisfy the *likelihood‑to‑succeed* threshold. |
| 2. Irreparable injury | Plaintiffs feared loss of refugee admission and funding for resettlement services. | The court determined that, because the government *must* provide services that the plaintiffs were not entitled to under the executive order, the plaintiffs could not claim irreparable injury that outweighed the government’s statutory obligations. |
| 3. Harm to other parties | The government argued that the stay would hinder its ability to furnish required resettlement services. | The court held that “the stay **would substantially injure** the government,” which had a statutory duty to provide those services. This weighed heavily against the stay. |
| 4. Public interest | Plaintiffs offered national‑security justifications for the government’s pause. | The court found that the *public interest* favored the government’s duty to provide refugee services, especially given the indictment in the APA portion. |

Because factors 1 and 2 failed and factor 3 overwhelmingly prevailed, the Ninth Circuit concluded that a stay was not warranted.

---

### 3. Administrative stay vs. judicial stay

A *district‑court administrative stay* is an order that the district court itself applies to its own preliminary injunction (in this case, the July 14 enforcement order). It is not a stay “coming from the appellate court” in the traditional sense. When the Ninth Circuit “vacated our administrative stay issued July 18,” it was effectively saying:

> *The appellate court does **not** endorse or uphold the district court’s decision to pause enforcement of its own injunction; therefore, the district court’s enforcement order remains in force.*

Ordinarily, a stay may always be rescinded by the court that issued it. The appellate court’s reversal is simply a formal notice that the district court’s administrative stay is **no longer valid** and that the district court’s enforcement order stands.

---

### 4. Practical effect

The Ninth Circuit’s order *does not* suspend the original injunction from February 28 or March 24. Instead, it:

* **Removes** the July 14 administrative stay that the district court had applied to its own enforcement order.  
* **Leaves** the district court’s February and March orders intact (subject to the limitations noted in the Ninth Circuit’s order).  
* **Preserves** the government’s duty to “reinstate cooperative agreements” for reception and placement services (see the direct mandate in the Ninth Circuit’s order that “the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements”).

---

### Bottom line

*The Ninth Circuit vacated the district court’s administrative stay because, applying the *Nken* four‑factor test, the appellate court found the plaintiffs were not likely to succeed on the merits and that the stay would materially injure the government’s statutory duty to provide refugee services. The panel therefore restored the district court’s enforcement order and removed the stay that had been imposed on that order.*
