Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 1: # I. Case Background and Procedural Posture

The lawsuit in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* arises from a dispute over the cancellation of insurance policies that were financed through a third‑party lender. Understanding the parties, the structure of the financing arrangements, and the litigation history is essential before the court analyzes whether the insurer complied with Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of financed policies.

## Parties and Insurance Financing Structure

Plaintiff **Kimberly Williams** and plaintiff **Felita Wright** were insured by **GoAuto Insurance Company** on policies that were financed by **American Premium Assistance Company, LLC** (APAC). APAC provided GoAuto with the full premium payment in exchange for monthly installment payments from the insureds. In each financing agreement, APAC was granted a power of attorney expressly authorizing it to cancel the insured’s policy on behalf of GoAuto if the insured failed to make the required premium payments. Thus, APAC functioned as the intermediary, collecting payments from the insureds and exercising cancellation power with GoAuto’s consent.

The parties were not the only individuals involved. A third insured of GoAuto, not named in the case, was involved in a motor‑vehicle accident with **Nicholas Jenkins**, who was himself a former insured of GoAuto. Jenkins had also financed his policy through APAC, and the accident triggered the litigation that formed the basis of the class action.

## Accident Involving Nicholas Jenkins and Remaining Plaintiffs

On the day of the accident, Jenkins was driving the vehicle that was insured under a policy financed by APAC. The accident involved another car, and the driver of that car was also a GoAuto policy holder whose policy had been financed by APAC. The collision created claims of liability that Dir the insured parties sought to recover against GoAuto and APAC. Because the policies were financed, the accident also raised questions about the statutory procedures for cancelling the policies when payment default occurred.

## Class Action Complaint and Addition of Felita Wright

The initial lawsuit was brought as a **class action** by Kimberly Williams and Nicholas Jenkins against GoAuto and APAC in a Louisiana state court. Williams and Jenkins alleged that GoAuto had not properly cancelled the policies and had acted in bad faith, thereby breaching the contractual duty of good faith and fair dealing required under Louisiana insurance law. In a subsequent amendment to the complaint, **Felita Wright** was added as a plaintiff. Wright’s inclusion hinged on her status as another former insured whose policy had also been financed by APAC and whose policy had been cancelled under the same circumstances.

The two plaintiffs’ claims were consolidated under the class action framework, allowing them to seek damages for all insurable losses that they asserted resulted from the alleged improper cancellations.

## Removal to Federal Court and Summary‑Judgment Motions

The class action was removed from state court to the **United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana**, following the traditional removal procedure available when the amount in controversy exceeded $75,000 and the defendants were citizens of other states or the action involved federal questions. Upon removal, the parties filed **summary‑judgment motions** focused exclusively on the statutory question: Whether GoAuto’s method of canceling policies financed by APAC complied with Louisiana’s statutory requirements for cancellation of financed insurance policies.

The plaintiffs sought to prove that GoAuto’s processes failed to meet statutory procedural benchmarks—particularly, that the required notices were not properly delivered or that GoAuto had not received true “receipt” of the cancellation requests. The defendants argued that their procedures—e‑mail notifications to GoAuto’s computer system and subsequent manual stamping—were sufficient under the statute.

## District Court’s Summary‑Judgment Ruling and Dismissal of Claims

The district court, after reviewing the pleadings and the relevant Louisiana statutory language, found that **GoAuto’s cancellation procedures did comply with the statutory requirements**. Specifically, the court held that the e‑mail notifications to GoAuto’s system constituted a valid “notice of cancellation” and that the insurer’s receipt of such notice satisfied the statutory obligation to have “receipt” before cancelling the policy. Consequently, the court granted GoAuto’s motion for summary judgment, effectively dismissing all claims against GoAuto with prejudice.

With the motion for partial summary judgment denied and the dismissal of all claims made, the plaintiffs appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. The appellate court affirmed the district court’s ruling, holding that the statute had been duly observed and that the plaintiffs’ allegations did not raise a reversible error.

This procedural history frames the appellate court’s analysis of the statutory language governing cancellation and sets the stage for its detailed examination of “strict adherence” to Louisiana law. Understanding the sequence of events—from the initial class action and the financing structure to the removal and summary‑judgment ruling—provides a solid context for the doctrinal discussion that follows.
