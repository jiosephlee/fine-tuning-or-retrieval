Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does Louisiana law require a signature on the premium‐finance company’ s notice of cancellation?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto, the court held that the statute’s use of the word “certifying” does not mandate a signature. However, prior decisions like Benitez v. Elsayed mention that the notice was ‘not signed by any individual.’ How does Louisiana jurisprudence interpret the requirement for a signature (or lack thereof) under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Louisiana law does **not** require a signature on the premium‑finance company’s notice of cancellation. The statute’s use of the word *“certifying”* only requires a mailed or emailed statement that the listed facts are true; it does not mandate that the statement be signed by a named employee. The Supreme Court has not held that a signature is essential, and the Fifth Circuit in *Williams v. GoAuto* expressly rejected the argument that a signature is required.  

---

### 1. Statutory language

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) says that a premium‑finance company “may request cancellation … by sending … a copy of the notice of cancellation **together with a statement certifying**” several facts.  
No word in the statute—“certifying,” “statement,” or “notice”—mentions a signature. The statute centers on *form* (mail, private carrier, or e‑mail) and *content* (the four statements). The phrase “statements certifying” is a common legislative construction that requires the information to be set out and asserted, not necessarily signed.

### 2. Judicial precedent

* **Benitez v. Elsayed** (5th Cir. 2019) examined a notice that “did not identify any particular employee … is not signed by any individual” and found the lack of a signature *did not* invalidate the cancellation process, as long as the statutory elements were met.  
* *Louisiana* appellate and district courts have never held a signature is required. In the opinion cited, the court noted that no Louisiana appellate decision has treated a signature as mandatory: “*No Louisiana judicial opinion … has addressed the need for a signature*.”  

The Fifth Circuit in *Williams* reiterated this stance, emphasizing that a signature is not implied by the word *“certifying.”* The court declined to give the Supreme Court a certification request, saying a lack of existing precedent does not warrant a change in the statute.

### 3. Practical implications

* The notice sent electronically to the insurer, even without an employee’s printed name or signature, satisfies § 9:3550(G)(3)(a).  
* The insurer’s “receipt” of the notice—either by physical delivery or by an automated e‑mail system—triggers the cancellation pursuant to § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).  
* An employee’s later use of a signature stamp is irrelevant to the statutory “certifying” requirement.  

### 4. Bottom line for a law student

If you are drafting or reviewing a premium‑finance company’s cancellation notice, focus on ensuring:

1. The notice includes the four statutory statements.  
2. It is sent via an approved method (mail, private carrier, or e‑mail).  
3. The insurer receives it (by physical possession or electronic receipt).  

A signature is an *administrative* convenience, not a *legal* requirement. The courts, both in Louisiana and in the Fifth Circuit, have consistently treated the absence of a signature as harmless, provided all other statutory elements are present.

### What does “receipt” mean in the context of a cancellation notice sent electronically?
Question:
The court uses Black’s Law Dictionary definitions of receipt to conclude that an e‑mail to GoAuto’s computer system constitutes receipt. Has Louisiana law ever defined or clarified the term “receipt” for electronic communications in insurance cancellation contexts? Is the court’s reading consistent with earlier cases or modern statutory interpretation principles?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit affirmed that Louisiana law treats an e‑mail from an insurance‑premium finance company as a proper “receipt” of the cancellation notice required by § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).  The court’s reasoning hinges on two facts recovered from the statute and from earlier Louisiana decisions:

1. **The statute does not require a signature or any form of “handcashing” to create a receipt.**  
   The relevant subsection says that “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation … has been requested …” (see *Williams*, § II).  The statute gives no definition of “receipt” and relies on the ordinary sense of the word—“to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.) and come into possession of it.”  The Fifth Circuit cites Black’s Law Dictionary for this definition and finds no Louisiana case that narrows the meaning.

2. **Louisiana courts have never required a signed certificate to be considered a valid notice.**  
   Earlier appellate decisions—*Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.* (and others cited in the opinion)—state that cancellation may proceed “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.”  Even *Benitez v. Elsayed*, which noted the lack of a signature, did not hold that the absence of a signature invalidated the cancellation.  Thus, no precedent demands a signed certifying statement; the statutory requirement is satisfied by the notice itself, whether it contains a stamp, an electronic signature, or, as here, a letter‑head declaration.

Because the statute was enacted in a modern era “in which company computers do much that individuals once did” (§ II, *Williams*), the court treated the GoAuto web service that automatically receives and processes the e‑mail as a legitimate “receipt.”  The system’s immediate action—canceling the policy the moment it receives the e‑mail—provides the insurer with proper possession of the notice.  The later physical signature by an APAC employee on printed documents is irrelevant to the statutory test.

**Consistency with earlier cases and modern statutory interpretation.**  
The Fifth Circuit’s reading is consistent with the body of Louisiana jurisprudence on the statute, which emphasizes strict adherence to the statutory elements but stops short of adding unstatutory procedural requirements.  The court explicitly rejects the idea that a signature is needed, citing *Benitez* and other opinions that treat the “certifying” language literally and only as a statement of facts.  The decision also follows the liberal approach to obsolete technology that other Louisiana courts have taken when a statute was drafted in a pre‑computer age; the law has been applied to “private carriers” and “email” in an analogous way (cf. *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*).

**Bottom line for a student.**  
Under Louisiana law, “receipt” in the context of the statute governing cancellation of policies financed through a premium‑finance agreement simply means that the insurer has taken physical possession of the notice, whether that possession is through a traditional mail, a private carrier, or an electronic interface that logs the notice.  A signature on the notice is not a statutory requirement, and an e‑mail that is automatically received by the insurer’s computer system satisfies the “receipt” requirement.  This treatment aligns with earlier Louisiana cases and with the Fifth Circuit’s proper reading of the statute’s plain language.

### Is a shared filing cabinet sufficient for “receipt” under the cancellation statute?
Question:
The opinion notes that before 2017 the filing cabinet was owned by GoAuto Management Services, and after 2017 an APAC employee stamps the document as “received” before placing it in a shared cabinet. Do courts treat possession of a physical document in a shared space as satisfying the statute’s receipt requirement, or does actual hand‑off to a GoAuto employee need to occur?
Answer:
**Answer**

Yes—under Louisiana law a shared filing cabinet suffices for the statutory “receipt” requirement.  The statute does not mandate a hand‑off to a GoAuto employee; it only requires that the insurer *receive* notice.  The opinion above explains that *receipt* is satisfied by *taking possession, delivery, or custody* of the notice.  

1. **Statutory language.**  
   La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) states: *“Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation … has been requested … and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.”*  The word “receipt” is used generically; the statute provides no definition or extra requirement that a specific employee must physically hand the notice to a GoAuto person.

2. **Interpretive pronouncements in the opinion.**  
   The court notes that “receipt” is “*the act of receiving something, esp. by taking physical possession*,” and that “GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.”  This conclusion is drawn even though the notice is mailed or emailed to GoAuto’s computer system, and the APAC employee later stamps the printed documents.  The key moment is the arrival of the electronic notice in GoAuto’s system, which the court treats as the insurer’s receipt.

3. **Shared filing cabinet.**  
   Prior to 2017 the cabinet was owned by GoAuto Management Services, a GoAuto‑affiliated entity; after 2017 an APAC employee stamps the document as “received” and places it in a cabinet shared by GoAuto and APAC.  The court treats the act of placing the document in the shared cabinet as giving GoAuto possession and, thus, receipt.  The fact that the person who stamps the paper is an APAC employee is immaterial, because receipt is a physical possession or custody of the notice, not the identity of the person who places it.

4. **No requirement for a hand‑off.**  
   The opinion contrasts the situation with the statute’s provision that the notice is “sent … with a private carrier.”  The district court equated an APAC employee delivering the notice with a private carrier, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed that interpretation.  Because the statute's need for a private carrier is satisfied by the APAC employee’s delivery, possession in the shared cabinet satisfies the requirement for receipt.

**Bottom line for a law student:**  
Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550 and the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the insurer can cancel a financed policy as soon as it has *possession, delivery, or custody* of the notice—even if that possession occurs in a shared cabinet and the physical hand‑off is by an APAC employee.  No individual GoAuto employee’s direct hand‑off is required.

### Why did the court not certify the statutory questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court?
Question:
Williams v. GoAuto emphasizes that certification is a scarce resource and is reserved for compelling cases. The court cites Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n to justify denial. What threshold of importance or divisiveness does a Sixth Circuit case need to meet to warrant certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit declined to certify the statutory questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court because the questions were not “compelling” in the sense the court requires for certification.  The court’s analysis turns on the standard set in *Jefferson v. Lead Industry Ass’n* (106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997)), which is the controlling authority for all appellate certification in the Seventh Circuit.

### 1.  What “compelling” means under *Jefferson*

The court in *Jefferson* made clear that the Fifth Circuit reserves the ability to certify to the Louisiana Supreme Court for **rare, high‑stakes cases** that:

1. **Create or clarify** a question of Louisiana law that is not yet settled by the Louisiana Supreme Court; and  
2. **Has broad implications** for Louisiana law or the industry at large.

The court emphasized that “a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court is not, in itself, sufficient to warrant certification.” (id. at 1247).

### 2.  Why *Williams* did not meet that threshold

*Williams* contended that the court should interpret several statutory terms—“certifying,” “private carrier,” and “receipt”—in ways that would materially alter the scope of the Louisiana cancellation statute.  The Fifth Circuit found:

* **No signatory requirement**. The statute merely requires the finance company to “certify” facts; it does not mandate a signature. The court noted that the Louisiana Supreme Court has never held that a signature is required, and the statute itself gives no such requirement.  *Williams* therefore raised a question that had already been addressed, or at least was not undecided, by the Louisiana Supreme Court.

* **“Receipt” is satisfied by electronic delivery**. The court held that as soon as the insurer’s electronic system receives the notice, the insurer has “receipt” and may cancel.  The statute was drafted in the modern era and the court recognized that computer systems can serve the statutory purposes of delivery.  No larger industry‑wide impact is evident from this interpretation.

* **“Private carrier” is a routine interpretation**.  The district court had already interpreted “private carrier” in a manner consistent with existing precedent, and the Fifth Circuit acknowledged that no unusual application was made.

Because the problematic interpretations were already settled (or could be settled) by a straightforward application of the statute, the court did not find that the questions were *compelling*.  The court expressly noted: “*As a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.*” (*Williams*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 24559, at *2*).  The court therefore declined certification, affirming the district court’s ruling.

### 3.  Bottom line for a Sixth‑Circuit case

A case before the Sixth Circuit would need to pose a **truly novel constitutional, statutory, or regulatory question** that has neither been decided by the state supreme court nor can be settled by a simple statutory construction.  Moreover, the question must carry **significant policy or industry consequences** beyond the immediate parties.  Merely asking whether a term that the state legislature used has a certain meaning, without broader impact, is insufficient for certification.

---

**In short:** *Williams* declined certification because the statutory questions at issue were not compelling—there was no unresolved Louisiana Supreme Court precedent, and the answers had no wide‑ranging effect—so the Fifth Circuit applied the strict *Jefferson* standard and affirmed the district court’s judgment.

### How does the Louisiana Supreme Court’s definition of a “private carrier” influence cancellation notice requirements?
Question:
The district court identified an APAC employee as a private carrier, drawing an analogy from a Fifth Circuit case (Huntley v. Bayer). Has the Louisiana Supreme Court addressed what constitutes a private carrier in insurance‑financing contexts, and could a different interpretation materially affect whether GoAuto’s notices complied with § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(ii)?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not yet defined “private carrier” in the context of insurance‑financing notice, and the Fifth‑Circuit recognition of an APAC employee as a private carrier was a *district‑court* analogy—not a definitive holding. Whether a leeway or a stricter definition were adopted would not change GoAuto’s compliance.  Under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), receipt of the notice (by any means that places the request in GoAuto’s possession, including an electronic mail service) is sufficient; the statutory language gives no requirement that the carrier be a “private carrier” in a narrow sense, nor that the carrier be identified by name or signature. Thus, the issue of a private carrier does not materially affect whether GoAuto’s notices met the statutory notice requirements.

**Why the Supreme Court has not addressed the issue**

* The opinion cites that “the Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined ‘receipt’ as used in the statute, and the statute itself has no definition.”  It does likewise for “private carrier”: “The district court recognized an absence of Louisiana case law interpreting ‘private carrier’ and found analogous a panel of this court’s explanation of when a railroad was a private carrier.”  No Supreme‑Court decision has ever been cited that defines the term in a premium‑finance context.  Consequently, the court declined to certify the question (see the paragraph ending “That a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court … [is] not sufficient to warrant certification”).

**What the Fifth Circuit and the district court actually said**

* In *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* (5th Cir. 2011), the court described a railroad that was an intrastate carrier as a “private carrier” for purposes of the *Federal *infrastructure statutes.  The district court in *Williams* applied that reasoning analogically to an APAC employee, writing that the employee “neatly fits the definition of private carrier.”  This was *extrapolation* rather than a binding definition.

**Why a different definition would not change the outcome**

* § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) requires that the premium‑finance company “send … a copy of the notice of cancellation … together with a statement certifying …”  The statute speaks only of a **transfer** of the notice to the insurer, not of the carrier’s identity.  The Supreme Court, in *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion*, noted that strict adherence to the statutory procedures matters, but they did not interpret the carrier requirement as a qualitative standard beyond “a private carrier” that might be an employee or subcontractor.  The **sufficient standard is simply that the insurer *receive* the notice**.  An e‑mail that is “received” by GoAuto’s web service and triggers an automated cancellation meets that standard.

* The argument that a “private carrier” must be *identified* or *signed* by the carrier is unsupported by Louisiana authority.  In *Benitez v. Elsayed* the court observed that a missing signature does **not** invalidate a cancellation, provided all other statutory elements are satisfied.  The *Williams* opinion repeats this reasoning, concluding that no signature or staff name is required “when the notice certifies the relevant information.”

* Even if a Supreme Court were to adopt a stricter definition—e.g., that a “private carrier” must be a naturally occurring carrier of physical goods or expressly named—this would not affect GoAuto’s compliance.  The statute gives no such limitation, and the evidence shows that GoAuto received the notice in an electronic form that placed it in GoAuto’s possession.  The “private carrier” distinction is therefore **substantial but neutral** in regard to the statutory notice requirements.

**Bottom line for the student**

When you read § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) and the surrounding jurisprudence cited in *Williams*, you’ll see that the critical question is whether GoAuto took possession or custody of the notice.  The Supreme Court has not ruled on the precise definition of “private carrier” in this context, and the Fifth Circuit’s analogy offers no binding rule.  Consequently, the lack of a Supreme‑Court definition cannot change the finding that GoAuto’s e‑mail‑based notice and subsequent automated cancellation complied with Louisiana law.

### Does the statutory requirement for notifying all interested parties still apply when the insurer cancels electronically?
Question:
Clause (iv) of § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires copies of the notice to be sent to all persons listed in the finance agreement. The court accepts an e‑mail sent by APAC to GoAuto as satisfying this. Is an electronic notice sent to the insurer considered a valid method of notifying all interested parties, or must copies be sent directly to each interested person?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under Louisiana law, § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv) requires that a premium‑finance company “send…copies of the notice of cancellation to all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur thereunder.” The statute merely says *“by sending … copies … to all persons….”* It does not say that each copy must be sent *directly* to the individual; it simply requires that the notice be *sent* to each person.

In **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance**, the Fifth Circuit held that an e‑mail that automatically goes out from APAC’s system to GoAuto, with the e‑mail addressed to GoAuto and containing the notice and the certification statements, satisfies the statutory “by sending” requirement. The court stressed that the statute was drafted “in current times” and that a computer‑generated e‑mail is a perfectly adequate means of *sending* notice. It noted that the statute was silent on the need for a signature or the need for the insurer to receive the notice physically, and that the word *receipt* was understood in the ordinary sense. Consequently, GoAuto’s automated receipt of the e‑mail and its immediate cancellation of the policy complied with the statute.

The court did **not** interpret the requirement as necessitating a separate e‑mail or other form of communication to each interested party. Instead, the statutory wording is satisfied by a single e‑mail that contains the notice and the certification, provided that the e‑mail is delivered to a recipient that represents all the parties required by the finance agreement (e.g., the insurer’s records). The **e‑mail satisfies the statutory “sending” requirement and the “copies” requirement** because it contains the copies of the notice that APAC was required to send to all interested parties. The court further explained that “the notice of cancellation, with a statement certifying…”, sent via e‑mail, fulfills the statutory demand.

Hence, an electronic notice sent to the insurer *does* constitute a valid method of notifying all interested parties under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). There is no requirement to send separate copies directly to each person listed in the finance agreement. The key is that the insurer receives the notice (or a system that automatically processes the notice) and the notice includes or “contains” the copies that satisfy the statutory obligation. This conclusion follows directly from the Fifth Circuit’s analysis in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 78530**.

### What standard of proof is required for a summary judgment motion in statutory compliance disputes?
Question:
The district court granted GoAuto’s summary judgment motion, concluding its cancellation procedures complied with Louisiana law. What evidentiary standard must a party meet to establish compliance or non‑compliance with an insurance‑policy cancellation statute when the dispute centers on procedural details?
Answer:
**Answer**

In a *summary‑judgment* motion the moving party must show *no genuine issue of material fact* and that the facts, if taken as true, entitle it to judgment. In the Fifth Circuit, the presumption that the movant has met this burden is enhanced only when the issue concerns a state statute that has been *rigorously interpreted* by the courts. The opinion in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* makes the standard clear:

1. **A presumption of compliance**  
   Because the statute (“La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a)”) is a *pure statutory scheme* that spells out the exact procedural steps a finance company must follow, the court treated each element of the statutory checklist as a *presumed fact* once that element is alleged. The district court saw that GoAuto’s electronic notice satisfied each of the statutory items (the presence of a valid power of attorney, proof of default, a ten‑day notice, and copies to interested parties). The plaintiffs’ argument that a signature is required (or that GoAuto “never received” the notice) could have created a *material dispute* only if they had produced evidence that the required element was missing. But the court, noting the lack of Louisiana case law requiring a signature, treated the absence of one as *non‑material.*

2. **Burden on the non‑moving party**  
   The opinion says the plaintiffs had “no statutory violation” and “dismissed all claims with prejudice.” That dismissal reflects the Fifth Circuit’s view that the plaintiffs *could not show* a genuine factual dispute on any part of the statutory scheme that was essential to a judgment. In other words, the plaintiff had failed to meet the *summons‑and‑dismiss* standard: they did not produce evidence that the statutory requirements were *not* met in a way that would be dispositive.

3. **Conclusion**  
   Thus, in statutory‑compliance disputes such as the one at issue, the moving party’s standard is the ordinary *“no genuine issue of material fact”* standard, but the court gives *deference* to the *statutory language*. If the plaintiff can only allege that a step was imperfect (e.g., the notice was unsigned), that allegation must be supported by *evidence* showing the defect materially altered compliance. Lack of evidence leaves the court to find *“no genuine dispute”* and grant summary judgment.

In short, when the dispute hinges solely on whether the insurer’s cancellation procedure *strictly adheres* to a statute, the plaintiff must present factual evidence that the statutory checklist was *not* fully satisfied. Failure to provide such evidence permits the court to grant summary judgment under the ordinary standard, as the Fifth Circuit affirmed in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*【Citation】.
