Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # III. The Supreme Court Standing Framework  

This chapter explains the Supreme Court’s Article III standing analysis as applied in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*.  The court’s decision rests on the three‑part Lujan framework—injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability—and the relief‑focused relaxation that courts offer when a plaintiff alleges a procedural injury.  We walk through each element, using the opinion to illuminate how the court determined that the American First Legal Foundation (AFL) failed to satisfy even the more permissive procedural‑injury standard.

## Article III Case‑or‑Controversy Requirement  

Article III, § 2, cl. 1 limits federal courts to adjudicating “Cases” and “Controversies.”  The Supreme Court has held that this clause gives rise to the standing doctrine: a plaintiff must have a concrete, particularized injury that is actual or imminent, and a likely remedy must exist.  In *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83 (1998), the Court characterized the requirement as “a facial test for standing.”  In *America First*, the district court had not addressed standing, so the D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing.  The appellate court’s review is de novo, adopting the Supreme Court’s constitutional parameters.

## Lujan Injury, Causation, Redressability Triad  

The Supreme Court’s seminal injury test is found in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555 (1992).  The three prongs must be satisfied:

1. **Injury‑in‑fact**: The claim must be an “injury to a legally protected interest,” and the injury must be *concrete* and *particularized* (*not conjectural or hypothetical*).  *American First* rejected AFL’s assertion that a mere procedural right is sufficient: “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected… is insufficient to confer Article III standing” (*see* Summers v. Earth Island, 555 U.S. 488 (2009)).  
2. **Causation**: The injury must be caused by the defendant’s conduct, not by some intervening factor.  *American First* rejected AFL’s causal chain, noting that DOJ’s decision not to disclose records was “plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.”  The court also emphasized the speculative nature of any link between OSC’s refusal to investigate and DOJ’s future compliance.  As the Supreme Court explained in *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), speculation about the choice of independent actors breaching the chain of causation cannot satisfy the causation prong.  
3. **Redressability**: A favorable decision must be capable of “probably disentangling” the injury.  The *American First* court applied the Supreme Court’s principle that redressability is a question of whether judgment *binds* the parties, not whether it might alter the conduct of unrelated actors (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)).  AFL could not show that a court order compelling OSC to investigate would “likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury.

## Procedural Injury Relief – Relaxed Standards  

The Supreme Court has, at times, relaxed the three‑prong test when plaintiffs allege a violation of a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest.  The key case is *Citizens for the Preservation of Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau*, 115 F.4th 618 (D.C. Cir. 2024); earlier authority comes from *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education*, 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005), and *Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen*, 94 F.3d 658 (D.C. Cir. 1996).  In such claims, the Court still requires injury in fact and causation, but the *imminence* and *redressability* hurdles are easing, though not eliminated.  The court in *American First* explicitly noted that “the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.”

AFL attempted to invoke this relaxed standard twice:

1. **Against the FTC’s refusal to investigate DOJ**: AFL claimed that OSC’s failure to conduct a § 1216 investigation deprived it of the records it sought via FOIA.  The court rejected this because the injury—lack of the records—originates from DOJ, not OSC, and because the *cursive* link between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s compliance was “speculative and attenuated.”  
2. **Against OSC’s notice procedure under § 1214**: AFL argued that OSC’s failure to furnish § 1214 notice files an informational injury, which is a concrete injury in the sense used in *Miller v. Astri*, 347 U.S. 156 (1954).  The court held, however, that the § 1216 process is discretionary and that no statutory entitlement to the notices exists; thus, the injury was not “an invasion of a legally protected interest.”  

The court concluded that AFL does not meet even the more permissive procedural‑injury standard, and therefore had no standing to proceed.

## Key Precedent in Context  

The opinion repeatedly references Supreme Court decisions that frame the standing analysis:

- **Summers v. Earth Island Inst.**, 555 U.S. 488 (2009): Rejects standing based on mere procedural deprivation.  
- **Citizens for Law & Education v. Department of Education**, 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005): Articulates the “relaxed” approach for procedural‑injury claims.  
- **Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen**, 94 F.3d 658 (D.C. Cir. 1996): Further elaborates the procedural‑injury standard.  
- **Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC**, 126 F.4th 660 (D.C. Cir. 2025): Emphasizes the necessity of concrete injury and causation to satisfy standing in procedural‑injury cases.  
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023): Clarifies that redressability depends on the effect of judgment on the parties at issue, not speculative influence on unrelated actors.  

These decisions collectively inform the D.C. Circuit’s application of the Supreme Court’s framework in *American First*, underscoring that the lack of standing is the decisive reason to vacate the lower‑court judgment.  The case illustrates the precision with which Article III standing is analyzed and demonstrates how procedural claims must still satisfy the injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability prongs, albeit with some easing under the procedural‑injury doctrine.
