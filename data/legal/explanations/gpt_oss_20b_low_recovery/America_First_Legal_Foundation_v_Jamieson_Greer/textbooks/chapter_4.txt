Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 4: # IV. Analysis of AFL’s Standing Claims

The Court’s decision to vacate the district court’s judgment hinges on AFL’s failure to establish Article III standing.  Two standing theories were advanced: (1) a downstream informational injury resulting from OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ’s FOIA withholding, and (2) an injury tied to the lack of investigative notice that a §1216 investigation would normally require under §1214.  In the following analysis we dissect each theory against the triune elements of standing—injury in fact, causation, and redressability—showing why both fail.

## Theory 1: Injury from DOJ’s FOIA Refusal

### Causation Chain and Speculation

AFL proposes a causal chain that begins with OSC’s refusal to investigate under §1216, proceeds through the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), and ends with DOJ’s alleged continued withholding of the FOIA records.  The Court notes that any informational injury stems directly from DOJ’s refusal to disclose the requested documents, which is outside OSC’s control.  The link between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s withholding is therefore speculative and attenuated.

The chain would have to move in the following steps:

1. **OSC Conducts a §1216 Investigation**  
   Even if OSC were compelled to investigate, that step is discretionary under §1216(c).

2. **MSPB Decides Whether to Sanction**  
   OSC would refer its findings to the MSPB, which “may” decide to impose corrective or disciplinary action under §§1214 and 1215. This referral is an independent agency decision.

3. **DOJ Responds to MSPB Sanction**  
   DOJ would then have to produce the withheld records, presumably in response to a corrective action or disciplinary finding.

The Court emphasizes that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med. (2024)).  Consequently, AFL cannot demonstrate a direct, non‑speculative causal link between OSC’s refusal and DOJ’s action.  The speculative nature of the chain renders the injury “too attenuated” to satisfy the causation requirement.

### Redressability Issue: Effect of Judgment on Parties

Even if AFL were to satisfy injury and causation, we must ask whether a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury.  In this case, a court order compelling OSC to investigate §1216 would bind OSC itself, but would not bind either the MSPB or DOJ.  The Court reminds that “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court” (Haaland v. Brackeen (2023)).  Thus, a judicial order could compel OSC’s conduct but would make no direct compelling effect on DOJ’s decision to disclose or withhold records.  Without a reliable link between the judgment and DOJ’s compliance, AFL’s injury remains unrecoverable by a court order.

### Outcome

Because AFC’s first standing theory depends on a speculative causal chain and fails to satisfy the redressability requirement, it fails to establish Article III standing.

## Theory 2: Injury from Lack of OSC Notice

### Lacking Entitlement to OSC Notice

AFL’s second theory contends that OSC’s §1216 investigation should confer a procedural right to receive the notices required under §1214.  However, “§1216(a)(3) does not create a statutory entitlement to receive the procedural notice that a §1214 investigation would entail.”  AFL acknowledges that OSC’s decision to provide such notice is discretionary, citing Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns. (6th Cir. 2011).  Because the statutory scheme does not grant AFL an entitlement, the Court finds no concrete injury stemming from a lack of notice.

### Concrete Injury Requirement

A plaintiff who seeks information enjoys a concrete injury only where a statute explicitly entitles the plaintiff to that information.  The Court cites Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent. (2011) and FEC v. Akins (1998) to underscore that informational standing requires a specific statutory right to the information.  Without such an entitlement, AFL’s claim of “informational injury” fails.

### Discretionary Nature of §1216 Investigations

The discretionary character of §1216 investigations further undermines AFL’s contention.  §1216(c) allows OSC to investigate “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” but “a prosecutor’s decision whether to proceed with a §1214‑style investigation is discretionary.”  Because OSC may choose not to provide the procedural notice, AFL’s injury is not concrete and is unattached to a statutory duty on OSC.

### Outcome

AFL’s second standing theory breaks down because it lacks a statutory entitlement to the procedural notice it seeks and because the injury it alleges is not concrete.  The discretionary nature of OSC’s §1216 investigations does not oblige OSC to provide notice that would otherwise give AFL a “preemptive” injury.

## Conclusion

Both standing theories fail to satisfy the Article III requirements: causation is speculative or nonexistent; redressability is unattainable; and concrete injury is lacking.  Accordingly, the Court concludes that AFL has no Article III standing, vacates the district court’s judgment, and remands for dismissal on grounds of lack of subject matter jurisdiction.
