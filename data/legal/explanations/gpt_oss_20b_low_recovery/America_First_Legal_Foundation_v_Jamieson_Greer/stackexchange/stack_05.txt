Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### In what ways is a plaintiff’s request for OSC to investigate a FOIA policy equivalent to or different from a FOIA request for records?
Question:
AFL sought OSC’s investigation of DOJ’s policy that it does not search employee emails without consent. How does that differ from a typical FOIA claim for records, and why does that distinction matter for standing?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the Court focused on whether AFL’s recourse to OSC was, in substance, the same as a traditional FOIA request for records—or whether it was a distinct constitutional claim that might or might not meet the *standing* requirements that apply to FOIA petitions.

---

### 1. What the Court says about the *type* of request

* **FOIA record request**  
  – A plaintiff writes a FOIA request and the agency archives and produces specific records.  
  – If the agency refuses, the plaintiff can sue for the records and for a court order that the agency must comply.  

* **Request for OSC investigation**  
  – AFL asked OSC to exercise its enforcement powers under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which “shall … conduct an investigation” of alleged arbitrary or capricious withholding of information “prohibited under section 552” (FOIA).  
  – OSC’s mandate is discretionary; it “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215” if it finds a violation.  
  – Unlike a FOIA petition, the request is not for *the records themselves* but for OSC’s *procedural* action—namely an investigation that might trigger corrective or disciplinary proceedings.

In other words, a FOIA request is an *information‑seeking* petition that, if denied, gives a plaintiff a concrete right to *obtain records*. A request to OSC is a *procedural‑rights* claim that seeks *enforcement of the agency’s own statutory obligations* to investigate and correct wrongful withholdings. OSC’s investigation, if it occurs, may result in a different set of remedial actions (e.g., disciplinary action against an employee) rather than a direct delivery of the documents plaintiff wants.

---

### 2. How that difference matters for standing

The Court explained that *standing* is the constitutional prerequisite for a plaintiff to litigate any claim, including a FOIA injury. The key elements are:

1. **Injury in fact** – Something concrete and particularized.  
2. **Causation** – A clear causal link between the defendant’s conduct and the injury.  
3. **Redressability** – A favorable court decision must be likely to remedy the injury.

#### a. Injury from the *absence* of an OSC investigation

AFL tried to argue that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused a “concrete, informational injury” because it prevented AFL from receiving the requested records. The Court found that this theory fails on three fronts:

* **Causation** – The link between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s failure to produce records is *speculative*. OSC’s investigation would have to trigger a *series* of independent steps (OSC’s findings → MSPB disciplinary action → DOJ’s compliance) that the Court could not rely on.  
* **Redressability** – Even if OSC had investigated, its findings would not *necessarily* force DOJ to produce the records; the Court said that “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court.”  
* **Concrete injury** – The injury was not the *fact* that OSC didn’t investigate, but the *failure of DOJ to disclose records*, which is a separate actor.

Because the alleged injury is *vested in the agency’s refusal to comply with FOIA*, not in OSC’s procedural right, AFL could not establish standing.

#### b. Injury from the *failure* to receive OSC‑generated notice

AFL’s second attempt was to claim an informational injury from not being provided the notices and information OSC is required to give under § 1214 when investigating a prohibited personnel practice. The Court rejected this claim because:

* § 1216 does **not** entitle a plaintiff to such notices; OSC’s undertaking of a § 1216 investigation is *discretionary*, not a statutory obligation to share findings.  
* Without an entitlement to receive OSC’s investigation report, AFL’s supposed “informational injury” collapses.  
* The Court noted that even if OSC was compelled to investigate, it would still be at its discretion whether to furnish AFL with any “notice and information” under § 1214.

Thus, whether AFL sought an OSC investigation was the *wrong type of claim* to support a redressable injury, and the Court found AFL violated *standing*.

---

### 3. Bottom line

* A FOIA request *directly* seeks the records.  
* A request to OSC seeks *enforcement* of the agency’s own FOIA‑related duties and is a *procedural* claim.

Because the *law* that governs an OSC investigation is discretionary and the *injury* that can flow from it is not a direct, concrete, and redressable loss, AFL was lacking Article III standing. The Court therefore remanded, directing dismissal for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

For a law student working on a FOIA‑related case, the takeaway is: **When you want to compel an agency to act, you must frame the claim around the agency’s statutory duty to produce records, not around a third‑party enforcement body’s discretion to investigate.** Otherwise you run the risk of *standing* defeat, just as AFL did.
