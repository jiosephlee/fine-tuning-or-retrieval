Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why is the causal chain from OSC’s inaction to DOJ’s FOIA compliance considered too speculative?
Question:
The opinion outlines a multi‑legislative causal chain involving OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and DOJ. The court calls it speculative. Could someone explain why the court doesn’t allow speculation about the actions of independent agencies when determining standing?
Answer:
**Short answer** –  
The court refuses to accept the causal chain because it is “speculative” in the sense that it relies on a chain of “if‑then” events that would involve the independent choices of three separate agencies (OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and the Department of Justice (DoJ)).  Under Article III standing, a plaintiff must show that, *in the fact‑guy sense*, a favorable judgment is *likely* to bring about the injury alleged. A chain that can only be completed by guessing how each agency will act in the future does not meet that requirement.  

---

## 1. The standard for standing

The court reminds us of **Lujan v. Defensores de la Vida**, 504 U.S. 555 (1992) – a plaintiff has standing only if it can show:

1. A concrete injury;
2. The injury is “caused by the defendant;” and
3. The injury is “redressable” by the court.

When the injury is the loss of *information*, the courts hold that the plaintiff must show that the injury is *concrete* and *particularized* (see **Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.**, 659 F.3d 13).  The courts also emphasize that **causation** cannot rest on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (**FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.**, 602 U.S. 367).  The burden for a procedural‑rights case is “relaxed” (Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152), but it still requires a *plausible* causal connection between the alleged violation and the injury.  

---

## 2. What AFL alleges

AFL’s first theory is that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ under § 1216 caused AFL’s FOIA injury:

1. **OSC** should investigate DOJ for a possible arbitrary/​capricious withholding under § 1216(a)(3).  
2. If OSC investigates, it may **pursue corrective action** under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215.  
3. The **MSPB** could sanction DOJ personnel for the alleged non‑disclosure.  
4. Consequently, **DOJ** would be compelled to produce the records that AFL had asked for.  

The second theory is that the *information* that would flow from an OSC investigation (under § 1214 notice requirements) is itself an injury.  

---

## 3. Why the chain is deemed speculative

| Step in the chain | What the court says | Why it’s speculative |
|-------------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **OSC decides whether to investigate** | OSC has “discretion” (see § 1216(c) and there is no *mandatory* obligation). | The decision is within OSC’s hands, but AFL has no way to prove that OSC *will* act in a specific way. |
| **OSC pursues § 1214 or § 1215** | OSC may institute corrective action (or discipline). | Even if OSC does investigate, nothing in the statute requires it to go beyond an investigation; the decision to *pursue* corrective action is discretionary. |
| **MSPB sanctions DOJ employees** | MSPB can sanction if it finds wrongdoing (1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A)). | MSPB is an independent agency.  AFL has no evidence that, *given* OSC’s investigation, MSPB would in fact sanction the DOJ personnel. |
| **DOJ produces records** | Production would result from MSPB sanctions or from a separate FOIA compliance decision. | The chain relies on a *hypothetical* decision by MSPB and a *hypothetical* reaction by DOJ, neither of which is linked to the court’s judgment. |

The court points out that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (**FDA v. All. For Hippocratic Med.**, 602 U.S. 367).  In particular, § 1216(c) does not require OSC to “*determine whether disciplinary action is warranted*”—it only says OSC “*may investigate and seek corrective action… in the same way*.”  The court has ruled that OSC’s discretionary choice to investigate does **not** create the kind of *immediate* factual connection needed for Article III standing.  

The same logic applies to the second theory. Even if OSC *did* conduct an investigation, it is *not* required to furnish OSC‑style notice or information under § 1214.  The court therefore concludes that “AFL … has not shown that a favorable judgment here would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury.  

---

## 4. Bottom line

The causal chain that AFL proposes is **too attenuated** and **too speculative** because:

1. It depends on chained discretionary decisions by independent agencies that are not *directly* caused by the court’s ruling.  
2. The chain requires several “if‑then” steps that the plaintiff can’t plausibly prove.  
3. The injury (loss of information) is *not* a concrete, particularized injury that can be reliably redressed by a judicial order aimed at OSC.  

Under the standing doctrine, speculation about how an independent agency will act is insufficient.  The court therefore denies AFL standing and remands for dismissal.
