Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What does it mean for an OSC investigation under §1216 to be discretionary versus mandatory?
Question:
The opinion says OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214 notice requirements is discretionary in a §1216 investigation. Could someone elaborate on what is mandatory here and what OSC can decide, and how that affects a plaintiff’s claim?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court’s discussion makes it clear that a §1216 investigation is not *automatically* bound to follow the procedural steps that a §1214 investigation must.  

| Issue | What §1216 **requires** | What §1216 **permits** | What the court says |
|-------|------------------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| **Initiation** | OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of an allegation of arbitrary or capricious withholding of information. | None. The statute only says OSC *may* investigate. | “OSC’s decision… to investigate … is discretionary.” |
| **Notice & Information Requirements** | §1214(a)(1)(B)–(D) govern the notice of complaint, the investigative report, and the opportunity to respond. | §1216(c) says OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under §1214… in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” | “ … OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214 notice requirements is discretionary in a §1216 investigation.” |
| **When a §1214 procedure is *not* required** | When the alleged misconduct is a “FOIA withholding” that falls under §1216, OSC can choose *not* to provide the notice or other procedural safeguards that a §1214 complaint would normally get. | If OSC opts to conduct a §1214–style investigation, it must provide those notices; if it does not choose that model, it may skip them. | "... OSC’s decision not to investigate was based on a legal error … but the court declined to order OSC to investigate any FOIA policy because, in its view, §1216(c) committed that decision to OSC’s discretion." |

### What is *mandatory*?

* **The act of investigating** – OSC is required to investigate any allegation that a record was withheld under §552(a)(4)(F)(i), provided the three predicates (court order, attorney‑fees award, and question of arbitrariness/capriciousness) are satisfied.  
* **The remedial options** – Once an investigation is underway, OSC may bring a corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) under §1214 or a disciplinary action under §1215, *if it deems that a personnel practice is involved*. But the statute does not compel OSC to do so.

### What is *discretionary*?

* **Whether to treat the FOIA‑allegation as a “prohibited personnel practice.”**  
  * If OSC decides *yes*, it is bound by the procedural safeguards of §1214 (notice, opportunity to respond, investigative report, etc.).  
  * If OSC decides *no*, it may conduct a simpler fact‑finding inquiry without those notice requirements.  
* **Whether to actually file a §1214 or §1215 proceeding.**  
  * The statute gives OSC the *option* to do so; it is not compelled to move forward with a corrective action or disciplinary action simply because an investigation was opened.  
* **Whether to provide the plaintiff with any of the §1214‑style information** that would normally be furnished to the complainant in a personnel‑practice investigation.  
  * The court notes that “OSC need not provide §1214 notice requirements in a §1216 investigation,” and that a plaintiff who relies on receiving such notice has no entitlement to it.

### Practical effect on a plaintiff

Because the procedural safeguards are discretionary, a plaintiff who sued under §1216 cannot rely on an obligation that OSC must furnish notice or an investigative report. The only substantive right a plaintiff has in a §1216 investigation is the *potential* remedial action OSC may decide to pursue (e.g., a §1214 corrective action or a §1215 disciplinary measure). If OSC chooses not to treat the allegation as a personnel‑practice matter, the plaintiff has no statutory avenue to compel OSC to disclose the investigated facts or to require OSC to follow §1214 procedures.

In the case at hand, the court found that AFL’s standing claims rested on an *informational injury* that the plaintiff alleged the OSC *must* remedy by providing §1214‑style notices. Because that obligation is discretionary, AFL could not show a concrete injury tied to a statutory entitlement, and the court dismissed AFL’s standing. The underlying take‑away is that §1216 investigations are **discretionary** with respect to the procedural steps of §1214, and plaintiffs cannot invoke those steps as a contractual or statutory right by merely requesting a §1216 enforcement action.
