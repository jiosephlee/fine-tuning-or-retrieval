Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Is there any statutory entitlement to information under §1214 that could enable a plaintiff to claim a concrete injury when OSC conducts a §1216 investigation?
Question:
The opinion says UFC’s lack of entitlement to notice under §1214 means there’s no concrete injury. What if §1216 investigation followed §1214 notice rules? Would that create a statutory right to those notices or information for a plaintiff, and is there precedent for a plaintiff’s entitlement to such procedural information under FOIA or MIS MP?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Court held that **AFL could not sue for a § 1214‑based injury** because OSC’s refusal to investigate under § 1216 does not create a statutory right to the notice and information that a § 1214 investigation would otherwise provide. The reasoning is contained in the opinion in the “Second standing theory” paragraph:

> “...if § 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information…  
>  
>  **AFL responds that, if OSC conducts the assertedly mandatory investigation under § 1216, it may choose to give AFL notices and information that would be required if OSC were investigating an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under § 1214.**  
>  
>  But AFL does not contend that OSC must engraft § 1214 notice requirements onto its § 1216 investigations. … **Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under § 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury** …"

In other words, § 1216 is an *enforcement* statute that authorizes OSC to investigate and, if it determines a violation, to seek corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215. It **does not impose the notice‑and‑information procedures of § 1214 on a § 1216 investigation** unless OSC chooses to do so. Because the statute makes no such requirement, AFL has no statutory right to the notice or other procedural communications that a § 1214 investigation would otherwise provide.

To establish standing for a “procedural injury” under § 1214‑type notice, a plaintiff must show that the statutory right in question is **enforced** and that the court can compel the government to fulfill that right. The Court rejected that possibility because § 1214 notice is discretionary, not mandatory, in the context of a § 1216 investigation (citing *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Counsels*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)).

---

### What the opinion says about a § 1216 investigation *followed* by § 1214 notice rules

The opinion explicitly notes that if OSC were to conduct an investigation under § 1216, **it may choose to apply § 1214 notice requirements**. However, it states that **AFL does not assert that OSC is *required* to do so**:

> “AFL does not contend that OSC must engraft § 1214 notice requirements onto its § 1216 investigations.”

Because no statutory requirement exists, a § 1216 investigation *cannot* give rise to a concrete, recoverable injury merely by virtue of ℅ 1214‑style notice being optional.

---

### Precedent on procedural entitlements under FOIA or MIS MP

1. **FOIA** – The FOIA itself does not grant a lawful person a “claim to procedural notice” that the agency must provide on the public‑information‑request process. FOIA provides an *in‑formed‑ex‑pluribus* right to obtain records, not an entitlement to procedural notice. Thus, even if OSC were to investigate under § 1216, FOIA does **not** create a statutory right to OSC’s investigation‑related notice.

2. **MIS MP** – MIS MP is a 2021 statute that requires federal administrative agencies to provide certain procedural safeguards, including notice and an opportunity for a hearing, to personnel who are subject to a nine‑month or longer disciplinary proceeding. MIS MP does **not** apply to OSC investigations, nor does it create a right to notice in a § 1214 investigation that is simultaneously a § 1216 investigation. The Court’s reasoning shows the same logic: *statutory entitlement* must be “inherent” in the statute itself, not a discretionary policy of the agency.

---

### Bottom line for a law student

* If an agency’s investigation is authorized under § 1216, the statute **does not mandate** that it must provide the procedural notice and information prescribed by § 1214. Therefore, a plaintiff cannot claim a concrete injury simply because the agency chose not to apply § 1214’s notice rules to a § 1216 investigation.  
* § 1216 gives OSC discretion to determine whether to seek corrective or disciplinary action. That discretion, by itself, does not create a “statutory right” to the procedural benefits of § 1214.  
* The opinion makes it clear that, absent a *statutory* entitlement to such notice, a plaintiff lacks a concrete injury and cannot establish Article III standing.

So, **no, a § 1216 investigation followed by § 1214 notice rules does not produce a statutory entitlement that would give rise to a concrete injury.** The Court’s holding on this point is the reference you need.
