Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does the Supreme Court’s treatment of ‘redressability’ in the context of discretionary enforcement tools impact standing analysis?
Question:
The opinion references decisions that redressability focuses on the judgment’s effect on the parties, not on speculative future actions by other agencies. Could someone explain how that principle shapes the court’s conclusion that AFL cannot be redressed by a ruling to force OSC to investigate?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Supreme Court’s treatment of *redressability* in cases involving discretionary enforcement tools—the principle that a judgment must be capable of affecting the parties themselves, not merely persuading unrelated agencies—underlies the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that AFL could not claim that a court order would compel OSC to investigate.

---

### 1.  Redressability & discretionary enforcement

Redressability is the “something that the plaintiff stands to gain” that a favorable judgment would produce.  In *Lujan*, the Court emphasized that a judgment must **bind the parties** to be redressable; it may not merely influence the conduct of other actors.  This standard follows from the observation that **the judiciary has no authority to compel the exercise of discretionary powers** that belong to other governmental agencies (see e.g. *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821, 831‑32).

In *Am. First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the Court applied this principle to a FOIA‑related case in which OSC’s investigative authority was at issue.  OSC’s power to investigate under § 1216(a)(3) is expressly **discretionary**.  The Court therefore evaluated whether a court order could directly alter OSC’s discretionary decision‑making.

---

### 2.  The speculative causal chain

AFL claimed that, had OSC carried out the § 1216 investigation, it would have been compelled to pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215, that the MSPB would then sanction the DOJ employee, and that DOJ would finally produce the records.  The Court rejected this chain because it hinges on *two* independent, non‑party agencies (MSPB and DOJ) making discretionary choices that the court has no power to dictate.

The Court stated:

> “The alleged causal chain is… **speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit**… and **speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion**…” (para. 1‑2, § II).  

Because a judgment cannot compel OSC, or even *incentivize* it to act differently, the Court concluded that a favorable decision “would very likely** spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s injury is lacking.

This mirrors the Court’s earlier reasoning in *Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC* (126 F.4th 660, 667) and *Haaland v. Brackeen* (599 U.S. 255, 293‑94): redressability turns on the judgment’s effect on the litigants, not on whether it might persuade unrelated actors.  The Court expressly noted this principle in plain language:

> “Redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court.” (para. 1‑3, § II).

---

### 3.  Practical effect on standing

Because **AFL’s injury is informational—premised on DOJ’s refusal to produce records—and the only possible redress is an **execution** of DOJ’s FOIA duties (which rests with DOJ, not with OSC)**, a court’s order to compel OSC does not remedy the injury.  The Court concluded that the procedural right alleged by AFL is a “right in vacuo”; without a concrete interest that is actually affected, it fails the *injury* prong (see *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496).  The redressability analysis reinforces this: a judicial mandate to OSC would not alter DOJ’s conduct and thus would not redress AFL’s informational injury.

Similarly, under the second theory—AFL’s interest in the *fruits* of an OSC investigation—the Court reiterated that § 1216 investigation does not create an entitlement to information, and that any notice or information OSC might provide is at its discretion.  Because the order would not bind OSC, it cannot redress that injury either.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

The Court’s treatment of *redressability*—requiring that a judgment directly affect the parties and not merely sway unrelated agencies—shows that a directive to OSC to investigate a FOIA policy would not remedy AFL’s injury.  Consequently, AFL lacks Article III standing.  The opinion is a clear illustration that *redressability* is not a technical “judge‑vs‑agency” hurdle; it is the substantive standard that ensures the judiciary does not overstep its mandate by indirectly controlling the discretionary powers of other branches.
