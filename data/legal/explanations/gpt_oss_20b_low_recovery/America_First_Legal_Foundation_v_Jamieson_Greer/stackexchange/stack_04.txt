Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does the court determine that an informational injury from a FOIA request is not concrete or particularized?
Question:
AFL argues that not receiving the documents is an injury. The court rejects it, citing that an informational injury is not concrete. What factors make an informational interest concrete or not under Article III standing?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The court finds that *informational interests are concrete only when the claimant has a statutory entitlement to the information—or a concrete, particularized interest that the law protects.  In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court rejects AFL’s claim that “not receiving records” is a concrete injury because AFL has no statutory right to the records and its alleged procedural right to an OSC investigation does not create an enforceable entitlement to any information.  Consequently, AFL’s alleged informational injury fails the concreteness requirement of Article III standing.**

---

### 1.  The Article III standing framework

* Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife required an injury that is ***concrete and particularized*** and ***actual or imminent***.  
* In cases based on a *procedural* right, the Supreme Court has relaxed, but not eliminated, the injury requirement (e.g., *Citizen’s for Law & Education v. Dept. of Education*, 396 F.3d 1152).  
* Even under the relaxed standard, the claimant must show that the alleged denial of a *statutory procedural right* has caused injury to a *concrete interest* protected by law.

The district court applied this framework and found that AFL’s two attempted theories failed to meet the concreteness requirement.

---

### 2.  AFL’s first claiming injury: “not receiving the records”

1. **No statutory entitlement.**  
   *FOIA* gives a requester an entitlement only *to the records* themselves, not *to the investigative or procedural actions* that might lead to disclosure.  
   (See *Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23-24.)

2. **Informational injury is not concrete unless the law enshrines an interest.**  
   The court cites *Am. Soc’y v. Feld* and *Pub. Citizen v. DOJ* to emphasize that a concrete injury arises only when the claimant owes a protected claim to the information.  
   In this case, AFL *does not* have such a claim; it only *requested* the records.  

3. **Speculative causal chain.**  
   The court lays out the chain: OSC’d investigate → MSPB sanctions → DOJ provides records.  
   Causation collapses because it relies on speculative choices by agencies *not before the court* (*FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367).  
   Thus, even if DOJ had *not* complied, a favorable ruling for AFL would *not* guarantee that OSC would investigate or that DOJ would change its conduct.  
   The injury is therefore *not available for redress* in a meaningful way, a key component of concreteness.

---

### 3.  AFL’s second claim: “not receiving OSC‑investigation notices”

1. **Section 1216 does *not* provide a statutory entitlement to information.**  
   The court remarks that § 1216 “creates *no* entitlement to receive information” (citing *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487).  
   AFL acknowledges that OSC’s observation of a § 1214 process is *discretionary*.

2. **No concrete interest protected by law.**  
   Even if the OSC were compelled to conduct a § 1216 investigation, the court notes that AFL doesn't claim any statutory right to the *results* of that investigation.  
   § 1216 can compel *enforcement* only if OSC’s discretion is triggered by a “court order,” but that does not translate into *informational entitlement*.

3. **Redressability is unattainable.**  
   The court stresses that a judgment ordering OSC to investigate would not *bind* OSC to provide AFL with any notices or findings—OSC retains discretion.  
   Without a binding order, AFL’s informational injury cannot be remedied.

---

### 4.  Bottom line: what makes an informational injury concrete

* **Statutory right to the information.**  
  The claimant must be *entitled* to the records (or to the remedies that produce the records).  
  *FOIA* gives an entitlement only to the documents themselves; it does not constitute an interest in *procedural actions*.

* **Concrete, particularized interest that the law protects.**  
  The injury must affect an *interest* that the law recognizes as protected (e.g., privacy, freedom of information).  
  The court concludes AFL lacks such an interest here.

* **Causation and redressability that can be reasonably expected** from a court order.  
  The chain of events cannot be speculative; the court expects the judge’s ruling to directly compel the defendant to remedy the injury.

* **A non-pecuniary “information” interest is only concrete when it is *enshrined* in statute or constitutional right** (see *Am. Soc’y v. Feld*, *Pub. Citizen v. DOJ*).

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, AFL failed to satisfy any of these elements, so the court held that its so‑called “informational injury” was not concrete or particularized, and thereby denied Article III standing.
