Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why does section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) not limit OSC’s authority under section 1216(a)(3) for FOIA investigations?
Question:
The opinion says that the district court found that “s 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under section 1216.” I thought the three predicates in 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were prerequisites for any OSC enforcement action. How can OSC still investigate under 1216 if those predicates are missing?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court’s ruling hinges on the plain wording of § 1216(a)(3) and the statutory architecture that places that provision *outside* the six predicates required by § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).  In § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is told “*if* a court orders production of the records, *awards* attorneys’ fees, *and* *specifically questions* whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously…” – *then* OSC must promptly investigate.  These three triggers are a *condition* for an OSC proceeding *in the FOIA context*; they do **not** create a blanket limitation on OSC’s authority.  

§ 1216(a)(3), by contrast, is an “additional investigatory authority” that is *independent* of the FOIA‐related predicates.  It reads:  

> “OSC **shall** conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under § 552—i.e., withholdings prohibited by FOIA.”

Because the section explicitly uses the word “shall” and does not reference the “three predicates” of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), the statute is not conditional on those predicates.  The OSC’s discretion to investigate alleged FOIA wrongs is delegated by this provision itself.  The court emphasized this interpretation in its summary:  

> “The district court concluded … **section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under § 1216.**”

Thus, when OSC declined to investigate the DOJ allegation (because the three predicates were not met), the court found that OSC had *legal error* because § 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate any allegation of arbitrary or capricious withholding, irrespective of whether the FOIA‑specific conditions of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are satisfied.  The statutory line‑of‑authority is separate, and the predicates in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) do not create a prerequisite that must be met before § 1216(a)(3) can be invoked.
