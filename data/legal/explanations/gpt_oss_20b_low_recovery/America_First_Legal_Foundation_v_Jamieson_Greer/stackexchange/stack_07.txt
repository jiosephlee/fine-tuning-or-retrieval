Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why does the court hold that redressability is satisfied only if a judgment binds the parties directly, not if it merely persuades third parties?
Question:
The opinion cites Haaland v. Brackeen and FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., arguing that redressability must bind the parties, not just influence independent actors. Is there a clear doctrine or case law that explains why a court cannot order an agency to perform discretionary enforcement to prevent damage to a plaintiff?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Court’s conclusion that “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court” is the direct result of the standing doctrine as applied in **Haaland v. Brackeen** and reiterated in **FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.** In those cases the Supreme Court made clear that for a statutory injury to be remedied by a judicial decision, the decision must compel *the parties to the litigation* to change their behavior. A decree that merely “influences” a third‑party agent or a separate agency’s conduct does not satisfy the redressability element of standing.

---

### 1. Proper injury and the “discretionary” nature of OSC investigations  

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the district court held that AFL’s claim that OSC failed to investigate a FOIA abuse was a **procedural injury**. But even the relaxed standing standard for procedural violations requires that the injury be *real, concrete, and particularized* and that a favorable judgment *likely* redresses the injury. The Court found the causal chain too speculative: OSC’s decision to investigate (or not) is discretionary, and the downstream actions of the Merit Systems Protection Board and the DOJ are decisions made by agencies *outside* the lawsuit. A judgment against OSC would *not* bind the DOJ to produce the records AFL wants; it would only bind OSC to pursue an investigation, if at all. Hence the Court rejected the argument that a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s informational injury.

---

### 2. Haaland v. Brackeen – redressability requires a judgment that binds the parties  

In *Haaland*, the Court stressed that standing requires that the *judgment itself* has the power to correct the wrong. “A plaintiff will be deprived of standing only if the party who owes the injury cannot be compelled by the judgment.” The Court explained that a decree that merely *persuades* a third party—such as the DOJ to de‑classify documents—does not bind that party and therefore cannot satisfy redressability. The same rationale applies to OSC’s discretionary enforcement: a court order that simply “influences” OSC’s conduct is not enough. The judgment must compel OSC to *investigate* and, if warranted, to *escalate* the matter to the MSPB; otherwise it does not bind OSC sufficiently to remedy the claimed injury.

---

### 3. FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med. – “speculative chain is insufficient”  

*FDA v. All.* reiterated that the causal chain must be *direct and foreseeable*. When the Court examined AFL’s proposed chain—OSC → MSPB → DOJ → record production—it found the link too attenuated and based on speculation about the policies and choices of entities not before the court. The Court concluded that “redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties and not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court.” The judgment cannot compel the DOJ to act because it does not bind the DOJ; it only binds OSC to the degree that OSC may or may not act on its own discretion.

---

### 4. Bottom line for the student  

- **Redressability** is satisfied **only when a judgment compels the defendant’s own conduct**. A declaration that “may persuade” a third party does not satisfy the standing requirement.  
- The Court’s opinion explicitly applies *Haaland* and *FDA v. All.* to show that an order that merely nudges OSC’s discretion is too indirect. A judicial order would need to bind OSC to **perform a specific discretionary action** (e.g., initiate a section 1216 investigation) for a plaintiff’s standing to be established.  
- Because OSC’s enforcement discretion is not answerable to the court, the Court rightly finds that AFL’s asserted informational injury cannot be redressed by a judgment that does not compel OSC’s action.  

So, the doctrine is simple: *standing requires a judgment that binds the parties to the litigation, not a judgment that only indirectly influences unrelated agency conduct.* This is exactly what *Haaland* and *FDA v. All.* teach and what the Court applies in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*.
