Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does OSC’s refusal to investigate create an entitlement to receive notice or information under §1214?
Question:
AFL contends that if OSC were required to investigate, it would have to give it the same notices that would come in a §1214 investigation. Is there any legal basis that an investigation under §1216 creates a right to those §1214 notices, or is it purely discretionary?
Answer:
**Answer**

No.  The § 1216 authority does not create an entitlement to the § 1214 notice and information procedures.  “§ 1216 investigations are discretionary” and, when conducted, are not required to follow § 1214’s procedural rules unless the OSC itself chooses to do so.

### How the opinion reaches that conclusion

1. **Section 1216’s scope**  
   The opinion explains that § 1216 allows OSC to investigate “any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information… prohibited under section 552” and that “OSC may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” (Id. § 1216(c)).  The language still leaves the decision to use § 1214’s procedures to OSC’s discretion.

2. **Acknowledgement of discretion**  
   When the court discusses AFL’s second standing theory, it cites *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011), a case that held OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary.  The court notes that AFL “acknowledges cases holding that OSC’s decision … is discretionary.”  Because AFL does not argue that OSC *must* apply § 1214’s notice requirements, the court treats any such requirement as discretionary.

3. **No entitlement to § 1214 notices**  
   The opinion states:  
   > “Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory.”  (para. C).  Therefore, even if OSC conducts a § 1216 investigation, AFL does not have a statutory right to the “notice and information” that § 1214 would require.

4. **Result**  
   The court concludes that a § 1216 investigation “creates no entitlement to receive information” and that an OSC order to investigate would not be “reasonably likely to redress AFL’s second alleged injury.”  Hence, the § 1216 authority is purely discretionary and does not grant § 1214‑style notice to a plaintiff.

### Bottom line

A § 1216 investigation is a discretionary tool that OSC may use, but it does not carry with it the procedural mandatory notice and information regime of § 1214.  A plaintiff cannot rely on § 1216 to compel OSC to provide the same notices that a § 1214 investigation would require.
