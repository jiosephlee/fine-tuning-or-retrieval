Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Standing in the Shadows: Why a FLORA‑Like Case Fails When the Injury Is Information

## Why This Matters

The *America First Legal Foundation* v. *Jamieson Greer* decision is a textbook illustration of how courts are pushing back on “information‑based” standing. The case is not about policy or vague grievances; it asks whether a nonprofit can sue a federal agency for refusing to loosen its grip on employee email records when the agency’s own investigation apparatus refuses to bite. The Fourth Circuit’s refusal to grant standing forces litigants to examine the very anatomy of injury: it must be concrete, particularized, and traceable to the defendant – not just to a downstream bureaucratic chain. In an era where courts routinely dismiss “glimmer‑stage” claims over access to micro‑data, the ruling underscores a tightening of the “information” bar that impedes watchdogs, citizen journalists, and niche advocacy groups alike.

## The Case in Brief

AFL requested that the Department of Justice (DOJ) provide emails that might reveal pattern of judicial recusals. DOJ denied the FOIA request on the basis that it would not search employee e‑mail without consent. AFL sued DOJ and later petitioned the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate DOJ’s FOIA policy. OSC declined, citing the lack of three FOIA predicates that trigger a §552(a)(4)(F)(i) investigation. The district court found described issues “not in the lane” and remanded, but eventually denied AFL’s claims outright. AFL appealed, and the D.C. Circuit vacated the judgment for lack of Article III standing.

## The Court’s Standing Analysis

### The Injury Must Be Concrete

AFL’s narrative hinges on the idea that OSC’s refusal to conduct a “mandatory” §1216 investigation injured it in two ways: first, by denying the records it sought; second, by failing to provide the §1214 notice it expected. The court rigorously rejects both. When an agency’s failure to disclose information is the primary injury, the link is to the agency that withheld records, not to a third‑party investigator.

This follows the line laid down in *American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals* v. Feld Entertainment and *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity* v. Census Bureau: information‑based standing requires that the plaintiff be entitled to the information that the defendant has in its possession. Without that entitlement, the plaintiff cannot claim an injury that is anything more than a mere “wishful desire.” AFL’s claim that §1216 and §1214 notice requirements create such an entitlement is “neutral” – the statutes give OSC discretion, not obligation, and deserve no read‑down as a substantive right.

### Causation and Redressability Are Too Speculative

In *Summers* v. Earth Island, the Court made clear that a speculative chain across agencies can’t satisfy causation. AFL attempted to tie an OSC inaction to DOJ’s eventual compliance—an imputable chain of agency decisions intellectualised in a three‑step spiral: OSC, Milwaukee, and DOJ. The Fourth Circuit dismissed this speculative route, citing *Hecate Energy* and *FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.* Each link in the chain was an independent agency‑level decision, and the court was unwilling to let a plaintiff rely on or compel a web of federal actor steps that were not before it. The redressability requirement is a practical one: a judgment must “bind” the parties. AFL could not demonstrate that a favorable ruling would compel OSC to investigate nor that OSC’s investigation would force DOJ to comply.

The court’s strict eye echoes *Haaland v. Brackeen* and *Weitzman v. Ross*, in which redressability was gauged not on the notion of a “likely” effect but on concrete binding power over the defendants’ conduct. The fact that the OSC declined to investigate does not give the court leverage and vice versa.

### The “Procedural Right in Vacuo” Rule

AFL's second argument was that the procedural right to an OSC investigation, if lost, constituted an injury. The court again ribbed this as “procedural right in vacuo.” In *Summers* and *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity*, the courts have twice enunciated that a procedural right must affect an otherwise concrete interest. A desire for “more investigative transparency” does not fit that mold. There’s no protected legal interest that has been displaced – only an expressive frustration with bureaucratic practice.

## Broader Doctrinal Implications

### The “Concrete Injury” Threshold Is No Longer a Fiddle

The Fourth Circuit’s decision echoes a wider trend: as the *United States v. Nguyen* case in 2023, *Freedom of Information Act* proponents have found their claims routinely dismissed. The standard that the injury must “concretely” harm the plaintiff’s rights in a legal intersect has become an armor against “data‑dump” lawsuits. When the plaintiff's interest is purely informational, courts ask: does the plaintiff have a statutory entitlement to that data? If not, the claim collapses. This incremental tightening is likely to constrain plaintiffs in future FOIA and privacy suits, especially where the federal agency—rather than a non‑federal actor—holds the data.

### Use of OSC and MSPB as “Technological Linking Arms”

AFL tried to turn OSC into a “conduit” to force DOJ to comply. The Fourth Circuit treats the OSC as supreme until it chooses to intervene. There is no bar to an OSC investigation, but there is no obligation. This places heavy responsibility on plaintiffs to ensure they are suing the right defendant. Prosecution fatigue in the federal sphere means plaintiffs are more likely to hit Clause 3 of the principles of judicial economy, and without an “express rule” for investigative judicial back‑fire, informational standing can continue to be an elusive frontier.

### Digital Age and the Narrowing Definition of “Information”

With data increasingly curated by third parties, courts have begun to balk at “information” requests where no legal entitlement exists. Information is no longer an abstract commodity; it’s a protected interest that must be attached to a statute or contractual trinity. The *AFL* decision underscores this shift: information alone, devoid of statutory demand, is insufficient. This trend will threaten Florence‑type “documents” as litigation vehicles for “whistleblower” thought.  

## Takeaway for Litigants and Agencies

1. **Identify a Statutory Right** – If you need to get data, make sure the statute unequivocally grants you that right to the data.  
2. **Avoid Bypass via “Procedural” Rights** – A procedural right that fails to affect an existing legal interest is not an injury.  
3. **Tight Causal Chains** – Do not rely on speculation about agency decisions outside the court.  
4. **Know the Path for OSC** – OSC is a discretionary tool, not an attorney‑general subpoena.  
5. **Prepare for Redressability** – Your order must bind the defendant; the court will not order an agency to act unless such power is within the court’s jurisdiction.

### Final Note

*America First Legal Foundation* is a cautionary tale. It shows that the “information” bar has hardened into a Standstone for a reason: to shield the integrity of the judicial architecture from plaintiffs who wield data as a fire‑arm. The Fourth Circuit confirmed that concrete, particularized injury – anchored by a statutory right and a causal link to the defendant’s conduct – remains the bedrock of Article III standing. As the digital era continues to expand the reach of data, courts will likely keep tightening the knife on information‑based claims that fail to embody these doctrinal pillars.



# OSC’s Enforcement Limits: FOIA versus Personnel‑Practice Investigations  

## Why This Case Matters  

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* is a succinct reminder that the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) power to investigate alleged Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations is not a blanket mandate. The court held that OSC’s discretion in section 1216(c) – the remedial provision that lets OSC investigate "arbitrary or capricious withholding" of records – makes the foundation’s claim of procedural injury untenable. In a climate where agencies routinely deny FOIA requests on narrow privacy grounds or in the name of internal policy, the decision sharpens the lines between FOIA enforcement and the traditional civil‑service “prohibited personnel practice” track that OSC normally follows. The implications ripple for civil‑service employees, agency lawyers, and litigants who seek to hold agencies accountable when FOIA and personnel grievances overlap.

## The Legal Framework: FOIA, §1216, and the Civil‑Service Toolbox  

FOIA (5 U.S.C. §552) obliges federal agencies to disclose records “unless” one of its nine exclusions applies. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) adds a distinct procedural safeguard: when a court orders a record’s production, awards attorneys’ fees, and “specifically questions” whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to withholding, OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted. The statutory language, worked out by Congress, is therefore dual‑purpose: it tailors the OSC’s fire‑power to FOIA misconduct while tethering that power to particular court facts.

At the same time, 5 U.S.C. §§1215–1216 govern OSC’s broader civil‑service mandate. Section 1214 authorizes OSC to conduct investigations into "prohibited personnel practices" (like discrimination or retaliation) and to seek corrective or disciplinary action against offending employees. Section 1216’s sub‑paragraph (c) indicates that, just as for personnel practices, OSC may seek corrective action *in the same way* if a "prohibited personnel practice" is involved. The text does not, however, eliminate OSC’s discretionary handling of the procedural elements of a §1216 investigation. The D.C. Circuit’s brief opinion rests, essentially, on the idea that the statutory provision is silent on whether OSC must follow the stricter procedural checklist of §1214 when it is investigating a FOIA withholding under §1216.

## The Court’s Analysis of “Discretion” under §1216(c)

The foundation’s central argument was – roughly – that OSC has a “procedural right” to investigate whenever a FOIA withholding might be arbitrary or capricious. The court rejected that theory by stressing that a procedural right “in vacuum” does not confer Article III standing. The dish‑on-the‑plate reasoning unfolds in three steps:

1. **Procedural rights must protect a concrete, particularized interest**. The Supreme Court has repeatedly hammered out that an interest in not having to wait for a redaction or a denial is a *becoming* right, not a *standing* right.

2. **OSC’s §1216(c) grants, not imposes, procedural discretion**. When the court, in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, noted that OSC “decides whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in a §1216 investigation, it was underscoring that the section does not compel OSC to treat FOIA investigations as if they were personnel investigations. The law is ambiguous, and the court chose to read it conservatively: OSC’s role is to investigate, not to shepherd the complainant through the procedural baggage that shipped with §1214.

3. **Proving injury requires more than a factual tie**. Even if OSC had “undertaken” an investigation, the foundation would have to show that any resulting notices or disciplinary orders were *tied* causally to its own FOIA request and that an adverse resolution of its FOIA claim actually hinged on OSC’s investigative outcome. The court found that chain too speculative, citing *FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.* and *Hecate Energy* to remind us that a plaintiff’s injury must be “proximate” to the court’s decision. OSC operates among a tri‑agency ecosystem (DOJ, MSPB, FOIA relevant agencies) whose internal motions are out of the litigation’s view.

This doctrine is not a new contribution. The jury has heard similar arguments in *Food & Water Watch v. Vilsack* and *Citizen’s for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau*. Those decisions underscored that one must link the court’s judgment to a specific, enforceable right—otherwise the claim is boilerplate. *AFL v. Jamieson Greer* merely applies that well‑trod principle to a very specific statutory nexus.

## Mobility Between FOIA and Personnel Investigations

Because OSC’s §1216 investigations do not automatically carry the procedural machinery of §1214, the decision clarifies a technical but consequential feature of civil‑service enforcement: a FOIA‑oriented investigation is fundamentally a *civil‑service* investigation in that it seeks punitive or corrective action against personnel, but it does not oblige OSC to adopt §1214’s notice and evidentiary standards. The court did not question whether OSC can, in principle, impose disciplinary measures against a DOJ employee based on a FOIA violation. It simply said that the *procedural path* to that sanctions remains discretionary. In practice, that means agencies cannot rely on a procedural right to compel OSC to tell them precisely how to conduct their FOIA requests or how to draft their internal traffic‑control policies.

The decision also signals that “procedural injury” under FOIA does not automatically extend to a more expansive “information injury.” An agency’s failure to provide records is arguably an injury in its own right; *American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.* confirms that informational FOIA claims can satisfy the “concrete injury” requirement. However, the foundation’s claim hinged on OSC’s *failure* to tell them what it would do, not on the failure of DOJ to produce the records. That’s a different kind of injury, one that the court deemed too indirect to trigger standing.

## Practical Consequences for Agencies and Whistleblowers

For agencies, the ruling reaffirms that OSC’s “enforcement” power is bounded. A DOJ employee who improperly withholds records may still face disciplinary sanctions under §1215 or corrective actions under §1214, but only if OSC exercises its discretionary investigative authority. Agencies cannot compel OSC to adopt a strict procedural template, and conversely, employees seeking to hold agencies accountable for alleged FOIA violations must keep in mind that OSC’s response is not compulsory.

For whistleblowers and those dependent on FOIA transparency, the implications are more subtle. The decision does not preclude FOIA litigation when the underlying bar is a purely privacy‑or consent‑issue; it merely clarifies that OSC may not be an infallible bridge to compel agency disclosure. Whistleblowers who rely on OSC’s investigative engine must anticipate that it might well choose not to probe an agency’s policy unless it meets the narrow criteria under §552(a)(4)(F)(i). Therefore, FOIA plaintiffs may still need to mount a direct administrative appeal or a federal court case that pursues more direct statutory remedies (e.g., alternative remedy statutes, executive privilege claims, or, in exceptional cases, a §1981 discrimination claim).

One advantage—in a less than obvious way—is that the decision clarifies that the “information injury” route is less likely to be litigated on the basis of OSC’s discretionary powers. In other words, a party can plead to the statutory FOIA remedies without peppering the complaint with hypothetical speculation about whether OSC will execute a note‑less investigation of the agency’s hold policy. The process becomes cleaner: if the agency refuses to comply, the plaintiff can simply seek a court‑ordered disclosure, rather than waiting on OSC’s internal discretion.

## Codifying the Ambiguity (What Might Come Next)

The *AFL* decision sharpens the interpretation of §6216(c) but does not fully eliminate uncertainty. Future cases could flesh out whether OSC could be compelled to use §1214’s procedural safeguards when it chooses *not* to initiate a corrective‑action lawsuit under §1215. Alternatively, Congress could respond by tightening the statutory language: for instance, explicitly requiring OSC to adopt §1214’s notification regime in all §1216 investigations, or empowering OSC to issue notice *interim* to complainants who file a FOIA assertion of unlawful withholding. That legislative path would remove the “discretion” question altogether.

Another line of development concerns the *“arbitrary or capricious”* trigger. The *AFL* decision hinged on the fact that the court had not set that term in motion. If a FOIA claim is brought that *does* specifically demand an investigation into an agency’s withholding policy as “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC might be in a tighter spot. It remains to be seen how much discretion the agency reviewer will be forced to exercise. The “phrase” of the FOIA statute might be expanded or clarified in future court decisions or in legislative package amendments to the FOIA Act, perhaps under the FTC’s scheme for privacy‑focused “Arbitrary or Capricious” provisions within the federal privacy framework.

## Bottom Line

The *AFL v. Jamieson Greer* ruling is not a sweeping denial of OSC’s enforcement powers. It is a caution that OSC’s enforcement authority under FOIA is *discretionary*—you cannot rely on OSC to impose the same procedural certainty that applies to personnel‑practice investigations. That subtle but real limitation reminds agencies that they are ultimately responsible for procedural compliance. It also reminds litigants that the path to remedial action will be determined by OSC’s discretionary assessment of the FOIA allegations rather than by a statutory “must‑investigate” engine. 

In the broader constitutional landscape, the decision reinforces the wall of procedural posture: a league of “procedural rights” is insufficient to pierce an agency’s discretion or to carve a new analytic category of standing. For any FOIA litigant or civil‑service worker, the message is clear: the legal system will not automatically turn on OSC’s discretion; rather, it will respect statutory ambiguity and require concrete, plausible injury that can be directly remedied by the court’s order.



# Relaxed Procedural Injury Standards in the D.C. Circuit: A Double‑Edged Sword

## Why the *AFL* Decision Matters

In **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer**, the D.C. Circuit reaffirmed its long‑standing posture: when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a statutory procedural right, the court “relaxes” the classic Article III standing prongs of *Lujan* and *Food & Water Watch*, but it does **not** eliminate the injury and causation elements. The bench’s decision that *AFL* could not satisfy even the relaxed threshold simply underscores the fine line between a legitimate procedural injury and an “in‑flight” claim that never leaves the abstract. The implications are wide‑ranging for litigants who use the standing doctrine as a liberal remedy‑pursuit tool, especially in the D.C. Circuit’s quasi‑administrative environment.

## Re‑examining the Relaxed Standard

The D.C. Circuit’s relaxed standard, crystallized in **Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education** (D.C. Cir. 2005) and echoed in **Fla. Audubon Society v. Bentsen** (Fed. Cir. 1996), goes something like this: if a plaintiff asserts that a statutory procedure designed to protect a concrete interest was violated, the court should allow that interest to "stand in for" the traditional *invasion of a legally protected interest* requirement. In practice, this means that an injury that might otherwise be “in the realm of the abstract” can survive if the procedural violation is found to touch a “concrete, particularized interest” that the plaintiff is meant to protect.

However, the *AFL* opinion made it clear that the Court will still demand a tangible injury. *AFL*’s failure to fire the D.C. Circuit’s “armed” exception stemmed from a lack of specific information that the plaintiffs were entitled to receive. The Procedural Injury doctrine cannot become a “give‑away” to those who simply want any bits of agency correspondence.

## The Two Prongs Faced by *AFL*

### 1. Injured by Non‑Compliance with FOIA

*AFL* contended that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ under § 1216 represented a breach of a procedural right that ought to have unlocked a cascade of corrective actions, ultimately bringing affected records to the public. The court found this chain of causation too speculative. The injury was **attributable to DOJ, not OSC**. A plaintiff’s injury awards don’t cascade through a maze of agency interactions. The Court rejected the argument that a favorable judgment on behalf of *AFL* would, by virtue of “nudging” OSC or DOJ, compel paraded actions that weren't part of the litigation. As the Court noted, “casing *AFL*’s standing cases on *FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.* — a case that penalized speculation over independent actors — the *AFL* claim failed on the supremac*.” Even if OSC had been required to investigate a FOIA violation, *AFL* would have still had a hard time proving the concrete injury that invoked *Lujan*’s *invasion* requirement.

### 2. Injured by OSC’s Failure to Provide Notice

The second theory, that *AFL* was harmed by OSC’s neglect to furnish the standard 1214 notice (which would normally be triggered by \*AFL’s claim that OSC was probing a prohibited personnel practice), just as cleanly behaved under these rules. *AFL* accepted the discretion OSC has over whether it interprets § 1216 investigations as requiring § 1214 notice, but they could not marshal an explicit “entitlement” to receive that notice. Without that concrete interest, the Court is consistent with **Amberman v. Hagan** where just a procedural "right to notice" lacking an entitlement does not constitute an injury. The upshot: *AFL* was outside the range of an action the court can redress, because the “injury” was merely the *absence* of a procedural step, not a direct deprivation.

## Why the Relaxed Standard Was Not Applied

In effect, *AFL*’s two alleged injuries do not reach the threshold of *relaxed* procedural injury, because they lack an *invasion* of a protected interest. The court distributingly granted the *relaxed* approach to procedural injuries in other cases, but those are often tied to **a statutory guarantee of the injured party’s interest** (e.g., a right to participation, a right to a decision-making process, a right to a written decision). Here, no such guarantee existed; *AFL* was simply a third‑party *interested* litigant.

The Court’s analysis reminds us that the duty to consider a “procedural injury” can be understood as “a prompt – but incomplete – shift in scrutiny for claims that fall into the conceptual sphere of *process*." But if that process fails to touch a *particularized interest*, the full weight of Article III standing remains on the plaintiff.

## What This Means for Future Litigants

### 1. A Call for Concrete Stakes

The *AFL* decision reiterates the necessity for litigants to identify a concrete, particularized, and legally protected interest that would be harmed by a procedural violation. For example, a nonprofit establishing a fiduciary relationship *after* the machine permission was deemed a statutory right under a specific investment law has more standing than one simply seeking to see internal communications. In short: *Sit within the statute’s textual reach.*

### 2. The Role of “Successive Causation” is Uncertain

When plaintiffs rely on a strategy that an agency will, as a consequence of a procedural violation, trigger a secondary enforcement action, courts will treat such reasoning as a *chain of causation* that needs to be rooted in fact. Anything “speculative” or contingent on *other* agency actions is swatted. The same discretion turned for a District Court process in the *FDA* case now becomes an obstacle for *AFL*, narrowing the breadth of process‑based remedy claims.

### 3. The Reluctance of the D.C. Circuit to “Weaken” Standing

Even though *Ctr. for Law & Educ.* (2005) and *Fla. Audubon* (1996) induced a more lenient “procedural” boxes, the *AFL* case shows a clearer mastery that this leniency does not unravel the basic need for injury and causation. The Court had a keen sense for the balance between “prudential” and “executive” concerns, a balance that keeps the judiciary from stepping into the administration of policy and therefore stands on higher evidence standards.

### 4. Potential Post‑2024 Re‑examination

At this juncture, the D.C. Circuit might still revisit the doctrine if the types of cases or the nature of statutory procedural rights evolve—particularly in the data‑privacy realm or more intricate administrative data access. Because the *AFL* case hinged heavily on the statutory scaffolding of the Office of Special Counsel’s enforcement discretionary powers, some litigants may still find a legal niche: if a statute *explicitly* mandates an agency’s investigative obligation where the plaintiff is a direct stakeholder, a more unfettered procedural injury story might be constructed.

## Bottom Line

The *America First Legal Foundation* decision should be read as a cautionary tale: the “relaxed” procedural‑injury standard no longer serves as a universal safety net. Litigants must still substantively align their injury with a *legally protected interest* that the statutory procedure was designed to safeguard. And the courts, especially the D.C. Circuit, will remain vigilant that the “injury” is not simply the absence of a process. Any deviation from that core structure leads to dismissal on standing grounds.

The Court’s flirtation with procedural leniency remains an interesting footnote in the broader circuit jurisprudence on standing, particularly when it comes to administrative investigations. But in practice, *AFL* shows that a procedural argument in isolation—lacking an injury linked to a specific statutory entitlement—will not survive the second‑level scrutiny required to lift the *Article III* hurdle.



# FOIA Policy, Email Searches, and the Edge of Accountability  

## Why this case matters

The *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* ruling may at first read like a routine standing dismissal, but it turns out to be a sharper verdict on what it means for a federal agency to refuse to search employee e‑mail when seeking responsive FOIA records.  In a climate of increasing demand for open government, the D.C. Circuit reminds us that statutory “policy” claims—such as an agency’s internal rule about whether it can comb through employee mail—cannot be consulted by courts unless a concrete injury is shown.  Moreover, it clarifies the limits of the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) investigatory discretion under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3).  

At the heart of the dispute was DOJ’s procedural stance: it would not scour employee e‑mail for FOIA‑relevant content *without the employee’s written consent*.  AFL argued that this “policy” constituted an arbitrary or capricious withholding of information that SHOULD oblige OSC, the very agency charged with policing such conduct, to step in.  The court concluded that OSC did not have to investigate, and that because AFL had no standing, it could not establish that the policy violated the law.  The decision reverberates in three ways: it bears directly on FOIA’s own procedural guarantees; it shapes how OSC’s enforcement discretion is exercised; and it sets out a high bar for litigants seeking to compel a federal agency to alter its internal information‑management practices.

## The statutory backdrop

### FOIA’s own enforcement mechanism

FOIA’s “extra‑ordinary” remedy for unlawful withholding of records is contained in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).  A court that *orders* the production of withheld records, *awards* attorney’s fees, and *questions* whether the agency acted *arbitrarily or capriciously* must trigger OSC to investigate and possibly discipline the offending employee.  OSC, once it acts, is allowed to seek corrective or disciplinary action through the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) under §§ 1214 and 1215.

But this statutory trigger is precisely what AFC argued was missing: their FOIA case with DOJ had not satisfied all three predicates (court order, fees, and a specific challenge to arbitrariness).  For that reason, OSC was not compelled by § 552 to investigate.  What follows is the key question: does § 1216(a)(3) say otherwise?

### § 1216(a)(3) – “Optional” investigative power

§ 1216(a)(3) does require OSC to “conduct an investigation…of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”  Its language is unambiguous: if someone alleges that an agency withheld information arbitrarily or capriciously, OSC must investigate.  The statute stops short of giving OSC a *mandatory* investigative obligation when the case does not fall under § 552.  As the court noted, even if § 1216 were binding, § 1216(c) expressly grants OSC the discretion to decide whether to pursue an investigation akin to those under § 1214.

Thus the legal conflict resolved by the D.C. Circuit was purposeful: the court had to decide whether OSC could be compelled to investigate a policy—i.e., a procedural rule—even when its three triggers were diluted or absent.  The answer: no.  OSC can only step in when the statutory conditions—court order, fees, *and* an explicit allegation of arbitrariness—are satisfied.

## OSC’s discretionary playing field

The court elaborated that § 1216 allows OSC to act if it thinks the alleged policy is “arbitrary or capricious.”  In practice that means OSC must make a substantive determination that the policy – not just any internal rule – is unlawful.  The policy‑examination process is therefore both a *policy* interpretation and a *consequence* analysis of agency discretion.

What this piece of law means for colleagues in the FOIA courtroom is that statutes solicit, but do not mandate, investigations into internal policies unless the owner agency or the courts impose a trigger.  OSC functions as a *filter*, not as an automatic watchdog.  This outcome arguably preserves agency autonomy while still providing a means for the statute to be enforced when warranted.

## The standing quandary

Statutory standing is the real fulcrum of the case.  AFL’s approach was to show a procedural injury prima facie: OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived them of investigative notice and representation under § 1214.  The D.C. Circuit applied *Smith v. Bell*‑style procedural standing relaxation, which requires a concrete, particularized injury that results from a violation of a statutory right.  But the court held that the mere denial of OSC’s investigation does not produce a *direct* injury to AFL.  The reasoning hinged on three points:

1. **Causation is speculative.**  Even if OSC in some way could compel DOJ to amend its search policy, AFL could not reliably say that the court’s ruling would set in motion the series of steps required—MSPB sanctions, DOJ policy revision, FOIA compliance—that would remedy their FOIA denial.

2. **Redressability is limited.**  AFL’s potential benefit would be that a higher‑end tool would apply to DOJ’s policy, not that the court itself forced a change in DOJ’s e‑mail search practice.

3. **Lack of an entitlement to information.**  § 1216 does not entitle AFL to be handed the search results or any notice, it merely proscribes the policy.

From a broader standpoint, the decision underscores the typical “injury” hurdle: litigants must tie their statutory rights to *actual* harms.  A procedural “right” alone is insufficient.  That is a familiar theme that cascades through *United States v. Wanzl* and *Feltermann v. United States*, where plaintiffs failed to show a concrete injury despite a perfectly justified contention that the government violated a specific preference or accommodation under the law.

## Do we see a public policy consequence?

### On agency transparency practices

One of the intangible outputs of the ruling is an impetus for agencies to re-examine their internal policies in the face of FOIA claims, even in the absence of direct OSC involvement.  DOJ, for example, may re‑evaluate whether it should make its e‑mail–search policy explicit and open to public scrutiny.  The court’s observation that the policy can cause a line of “intangible harm” to proponents of FOIA might compel lawmakers to tailor a more explicit statutory requirement—a potential legislative response to a procedural loophole.

### On OSC’s investigatory discretion

The case clarifies that OSC is not a reactive “investigator” that can be called upon to audit any agency procedure.  In the absence of the statutory “trigger” outlined in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), OSC can only act on its own initiative.  That's a double‑edged sword: OSC retains the discretion to pursue actions in clear cases of wrongdoing, but the absence of litigation will continue to leave many procedural practices unchallenged.

Furthermore, the decision signals that OSC’s deference to agency policy must balance internal management and the FOIA mandate.  This may carry down the chain of command: agencies must acknowledge that a policy—even a seemingly innocuous one building on privacy—can be deemed arbitrarily or capriciously obstructive and open to OSC scrutiny if the relevant FOIA predicates are triggered.

### On the FOIA community

For FOIA advocates, the ruling is a reminder that claims focusing on internal procedures need to be tied explicitly to FOIA’s remedial mechanisms.  It's not enough to argue that a “policy” is unlawful; plaintiffs must show the policy *directly* caused a FOIA withholding, that the withheld records were actionable under § 552, and an OSC investigation was triggered.  The *inhabited* standard of “actual or imminent injury” forces a stricter factual standard.  This may steer advocacy toward more concrete earmarked claims—such as requesting the release of policy documents themselves—or suing on the basis of *existing* withheld records.

## Bottom line

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* confirms a at‑issue tension between administrative policy presumption and statutory enforcement.  By reaffirming that OSC’s investigatory duty under § 1216(a)(3) is discretionary absent the three statutory triggers, the court preserved both the executive branch’s autonomy and the enforcement purpose of FOIA.  The ruling also circumscribed the reach of Article III standing to “real” injuries, ensuring that only clearly affected parties can trigger judicial review.

What emerges is a useful refinement of FOIA jurisprudence: internal agency policies, even if they block access to records, are *not* automatically actionable just because they are deemed “arbitrary” or “capricious.”  The procedural right to an investigation is only a stepping stone – not the end point – to the remedy of information disclosure.  For FOIA scholars, practitioners, and policy watchers, this case serves as both a cautionary tale and a useful boundary marker.  It invites us to ask: are there other statutes or schemes that require the same balancing act?  How will OSC exercise its discretion going forward?  And can legislators craft updates that give FOIA litigants a clearer path to injunctions when agency procedures curb the public’s access rights?  Only time—and an equally sophisticated court—will tell.
