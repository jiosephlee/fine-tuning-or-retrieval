Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# I. Case Background and Procedural History

## Chapter Description  

This chapter offers a concise narrative of the facts, the parties, and the procedural journey that led the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit to consider the case. It explains how America First Legal Foundation (AFL) framed its FOIA claim, the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) response, and the district court’s rulings before the appellate panel applied a de novo review.

## Parties & Their Roles: AFL, OSC, DOJ  

America First Legal Foundation is a conservative advocacy organization that frequently files FOIA suits against federal agencies. The foundation’s surge of interest in the case centered on a particular FOIA request for a series of emails exchanged between an outside attorney and the Department of Justice (DOJ) Civil Division.  

The Office of Special Counsel is an independent investigative arm that protects federal civil service employees from prohibited personnel practices and, per § 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act, may investigate alleged arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under FOIA.  

The Department of Justice, as the responding agency, denied AFL’s FOIA request on the ground that it does not have a policy of searching employees’ email accounts without consent, a policy that AFL argued was arbitrary and capricious.

## FOIA Request for DOJ Emails & DOJ’s Denial  

In late 2024, AFL filed a FOIA request under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a) for all email communications involving an outside attorney within the DOJ Civil Division. The DOJ declined to provide the records, citing an internal policy that barred employees from searching email accounts without the employee’s consent. AFL moved to compel compliance, asserting that the DOJ’s policy violated the FOIA’s statutory mandate that agencies shall not arbitrarily or capriciously withhold records.

## OSC’s Refusal to Investigate under § 1216(a)(3)  

AFL simultaneously filed a complaint seeking OSC’s intervention under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to investigate any “alleged arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” AFL claimed that OSC’s omission to investigate was an infringement of its procedural rights and, indirectly, a denial of FOIA.

OSC responded that the statutory trigger for an investigation under § 1216(a)(3) also requires that a court have already awarded attorneys’ fees, ordered production and specifically questioned the agency’s alleged arbitrary or capricious withholding—conditions that had not yet been met. OSC thus dismissed the request for an investigation. AFL argued that § 1216(a)(3) alone mandated an investigation, regardless of the presence of a court order.

## District Court Rulings: Partial Victory, Remand, Final Dismissal  

The district court granted AFL’s motion to dismiss some claims but held that OSC’s refusal to investigate was a legal error because, per the court’s reading of the statute, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not limit OSC’s investigatory authority. The court remanded the case to OSC to reconsider its decision, “under the legal error” found.

After OSC again declined to investigate, AFL filed a motion for final judgment. The district court dismissed the remaining claims for failure to state a claim.

## Appellate Review under De novo Standard  

AFL appealed, seeking de novo review of its standing and the district court’s relief order. The appellate panel, applying the standard that appellate review of a district court’s decision on the merits is "de novo" (i.e., a fresh consideration of the facts and law), set the stage for the discussion on whether AFL possessed Article III standing to litigate the matter at all. The appellate court’s holding vacated the district court’s dismissal and remanded with instructions to dismiss the case for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction, emphasizing that AFL did not demonstrate the required “injury-in-fact” element of Article III standing.

# II. Governing Law: OSC’s Authority and FOIA Enforcement

## Civil Service Reform Act §§ 1214–1215 & 1216

Section 1214 of the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) governs the investigation of prohibited personnel practices and the corrective actions that may result. It sets out the procedural steps the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) must follow, including the obligation to notify the complaining party of investigative progress (§ 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D)). Section 1215 authorizes OSC to pursue disciplinary action against individual employees who on a “prohibited personnel practice” are found liable. Together, these provisions constitute OSC’s core investigative and enforcement toolkit with respect to personnel conduct within the federal service.

Section 1216 expands OSC’s jurisdiction beyond traditional personnel matters. Subsection (a)(3) specifically authorizes OSC to investigate “any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, FOIA‑related withholdings. Paragraph (c) of that subsection clarifies that OSC may pursue the same corrective actions outlined in § 1214 or the same disciplinary measures in § 1215 in the event of a FOIA‑related finding. Thus, § 1216(a)(3) gives OSC an investigative mandate that parallels its personnel enforcement powers but is tailored to FOIA violations.

## OSC’s Discretionary vs. Mandatory Investigative Duties

The statutory text distinguishes between duties that are obligatory and those that are encompassed in OSC’s discretion. When OSC is compelled under § 1216(a)(3) to investigate a claimed FOIA withholding, the statute does not itself prescribe how the investigation must be conducted beyond the general statutory framework. OSC retains discretion under § 1216(c) to determine whether to initiate the investigation at all, and if it does, whether to proceed under the procedural regimes of § 1214 or to rely on its own “internal procedures.” The district court, in *America First Legal v. Kerner*, held that OSC’s failure to investigate was a legal error because OSC had failed to satisfy the statutory triggers for a § 1216 investigation, yet it still left the ultimate decision to investigate to OSC’s discretion. That decision was later reiterated in the present case when OSC declined to investigate, leading the plaintiffs to argue that OSC had a statutory duty.

## FOIA §552(a)(4)(F)(i) and its Triggers

Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) of FOIA explicitly creates an enforcement mechanism that applies only after a court has already taken action. If a court orders the production of records that were improperly withheld, awards attorneys’ fees, and queries whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously, then OSC must “promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the individual chiefly responsible. This clause establishes a post‑judgment triggering mechanism that is strictly procedural within the context of FOIA enforcement. It does not, by itself, confer OSC with a pre‑judgment obligation to investigate every alleged FOIA violation.

## Contrast between §1216(a)(3) and §552(a)(4)(F)(i)

While both provisions address FOIA violations, they differ in scope and trigger. § 1216(a)(3) is a proactive investigative authority granted to OSC before any court involvement; it allows OSC to investigate arbitrary or capricious withholdings detected by the agency itself or by external parties (e.g., the plaintiffs). In contrast, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is reactive, activating only after a court has already adjudicated a FOIA dispute and found that a record was withheld arbitrarily or capriciously. The statutory architecture thus intends (1) for OSC to conduct its own investigations under § 1216, and (2) for OSC to follow up on court judgments under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

In *America First Legal v. Kerner*, the court concluded that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s pre‑judgment investigative power under § 1216. Consequently, OSC’s decision not to investigate was deemed a legal error, but the court ultimately rejected an order compelling OSC because § 1216(c) retains the discretion to conduct or forego investigation. The present case affirms that distinction while emphasizing that the statutory trigger of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is not required for OSC to act under § 1216.

## Prior Leading Decisions

- **Frazier v. MSPB (1982)**: Established that the OSC is an investigative and prosecutorial agency dedicated to maintaining a fair, efficient, and lawfully conducted civil service. It reinforced the statutory framework of § 1214 and § 1215 in protecting personnel rights.

- **America First Legal v. Kerner (2023)**: Clarified that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cap or exclude OSC’s investigatory authority under § 1216(a)(3). The district court found OSC’s refusal to investigate a procedural error to be a legal misstep, yet recognized that OSC’s discretion under § 1216(c) determines whether an investigation commences. This case sets the factual context for the current appeal, where AFL sought OSC’s investigation of DOJ’s FOIA policy.

These precedents, together with the statutory provisions, frame the legal landscape that the appellate court must navigate when assessing whether OSC's discretionary decision to forego investigation aligns with the enforceable obligations imposed by the CSRA and FOIA.

# III. The Supreme Court Standing Framework  

This chapter explains the Supreme Court’s Article III standing analysis as applied in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*.  The court’s decision rests on the three‑part Lujan framework—injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability—and the relief‑focused relaxation that courts offer when a plaintiff alleges a procedural injury.  We walk through each element, using the opinion to illuminate how the court determined that the American First Legal Foundation (AFL) failed to satisfy even the more permissive procedural‑injury standard.

## Article III Case‑or‑Controversy Requirement  

Article III, § 2, cl. 1 limits federal courts to adjudicating “Cases” and “Controversies.”  The Supreme Court has held that this clause gives rise to the standing doctrine: a plaintiff must have a concrete, particularized injury that is actual or imminent, and a likely remedy must exist.  In *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83 (1998), the Court characterized the requirement as “a facial test for standing.”  In *America First*, the district court had not addressed standing, so the D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing.  The appellate court’s review is de novo, adopting the Supreme Court’s constitutional parameters.

## Lujan Injury, Causation, Redressability Triad  

The Supreme Court’s seminal injury test is found in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555 (1992).  The three prongs must be satisfied:

1. **Injury‑in‑fact**: The claim must be an “injury to a legally protected interest,” and the injury must be *concrete* and *particularized* (*not conjectural or hypothetical*).  *American First* rejected AFL’s assertion that a mere procedural right is sufficient: “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected… is insufficient to confer Article III standing” (*see* Summers v. Earth Island, 555 U.S. 488 (2009)).  
2. **Causation**: The injury must be caused by the defendant’s conduct, not by some intervening factor.  *American First* rejected AFL’s causal chain, noting that DOJ’s decision not to disclose records was “plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.”  The court also emphasized the speculative nature of any link between OSC’s refusal to investigate and DOJ’s future compliance.  As the Supreme Court explained in *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), speculation about the choice of independent actors breaching the chain of causation cannot satisfy the causation prong.  
3. **Redressability**: A favorable decision must be capable of “probably disentangling” the injury.  The *American First* court applied the Supreme Court’s principle that redressability is a question of whether judgment *binds* the parties, not whether it might alter the conduct of unrelated actors (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)).  AFL could not show that a court order compelling OSC to investigate would “likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury.

## Procedural Injury Relief – Relaxed Standards  

The Supreme Court has, at times, relaxed the three‑prong test when plaintiffs allege a violation of a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest.  The key case is *Citizens for the Preservation of Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau*, 115 F.4th 618 (D.C. Cir. 2024); earlier authority comes from *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education*, 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005), and *Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen*, 94 F.3d 658 (D.C. Cir. 1996).  In such claims, the Court still requires injury in fact and causation, but the *imminence* and *redressability* hurdles are easing, though not eliminated.  The court in *American First* explicitly noted that “the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.”

AFL attempted to invoke this relaxed standard twice:

1. **Against the FTC’s refusal to investigate DOJ**: AFL claimed that OSC’s failure to conduct a § 1216 investigation deprived it of the records it sought via FOIA.  The court rejected this because the injury—lack of the records—originates from DOJ, not OSC, and because the *cursive* link between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s compliance was “speculative and attenuated.”  
2. **Against OSC’s notice procedure under § 1214**: AFL argued that OSC’s failure to furnish § 1214 notice files an informational injury, which is a concrete injury in the sense used in *Miller v. Astri*, 347 U.S. 156 (1954).  The court held, however, that the § 1216 process is discretionary and that no statutory entitlement to the notices exists; thus, the injury was not “an invasion of a legally protected interest.”  

The court concluded that AFL does not meet even the more permissive procedural‑injury standard, and therefore had no standing to proceed.

## Key Precedent in Context  

The opinion repeatedly references Supreme Court decisions that frame the standing analysis:

- **Summers v. Earth Island Inst.**, 555 U.S. 488 (2009): Rejects standing based on mere procedural deprivation.  
- **Citizens for Law & Education v. Department of Education**, 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005): Articulates the “relaxed” approach for procedural‑injury claims.  
- **Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen**, 94 F.3d 658 (D.C. Cir. 1996): Further elaborates the procedural‑injury standard.  
- **Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC**, 126 F.4th 660 (D.C. Cir. 2025): Emphasizes the necessity of concrete injury and causation to satisfy standing in procedural‑injury cases.  
- **Haaland v. Brackeen**, 599 U.S. 255 (2023): Clarifies that redressability depends on the effect of judgment on the parties at issue, not speculative influence on unrelated actors.  

These decisions collectively inform the D.C. Circuit’s application of the Supreme Court’s framework in *American First*, underscoring that the lack of standing is the decisive reason to vacate the lower‑court judgment.  The case illustrates the precision with which Article III standing is analyzed and demonstrates how procedural claims must still satisfy the injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability prongs, albeit with some easing under the procedural‑injury doctrine.

# IV. Analysis of AFL’s Standing Claims

The Court’s decision to vacate the district court’s judgment hinges on AFL’s failure to establish Article III standing.  Two standing theories were advanced: (1) a downstream informational injury resulting from OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ’s FOIA withholding, and (2) an injury tied to the lack of investigative notice that a §1216 investigation would normally require under §1214.  In the following analysis we dissect each theory against the triune elements of standing—injury in fact, causation, and redressability—showing why both fail.

## Theory 1: Injury from DOJ’s FOIA Refusal

### Causation Chain and Speculation

AFL proposes a causal chain that begins with OSC’s refusal to investigate under §1216, proceeds through the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), and ends with DOJ’s alleged continued withholding of the FOIA records.  The Court notes that any informational injury stems directly from DOJ’s refusal to disclose the requested documents, which is outside OSC’s control.  The link between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s withholding is therefore speculative and attenuated.

The chain would have to move in the following steps:

1. **OSC Conducts a §1216 Investigation**  
   Even if OSC were compelled to investigate, that step is discretionary under §1216(c).

2. **MSPB Decides Whether to Sanction**  
   OSC would refer its findings to the MSPB, which “may” decide to impose corrective or disciplinary action under §§1214 and 1215. This referral is an independent agency decision.

3. **DOJ Responds to MSPB Sanction**  
   DOJ would then have to produce the withheld records, presumably in response to a corrective action or disciplinary finding.

The Court emphasizes that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med. (2024)).  Consequently, AFL cannot demonstrate a direct, non‑speculative causal link between OSC’s refusal and DOJ’s action.  The speculative nature of the chain renders the injury “too attenuated” to satisfy the causation requirement.

### Redressability Issue: Effect of Judgment on Parties

Even if AFL were to satisfy injury and causation, we must ask whether a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury.  In this case, a court order compelling OSC to investigate §1216 would bind OSC itself, but would not bind either the MSPB or DOJ.  The Court reminds that “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court” (Haaland v. Brackeen (2023)).  Thus, a judicial order could compel OSC’s conduct but would make no direct compelling effect on DOJ’s decision to disclose or withhold records.  Without a reliable link between the judgment and DOJ’s compliance, AFL’s injury remains unrecoverable by a court order.

### Outcome

Because AFC’s first standing theory depends on a speculative causal chain and fails to satisfy the redressability requirement, it fails to establish Article III standing.

## Theory 2: Injury from Lack of OSC Notice

### Lacking Entitlement to OSC Notice

AFL’s second theory contends that OSC’s §1216 investigation should confer a procedural right to receive the notices required under §1214.  However, “§1216(a)(3) does not create a statutory entitlement to receive the procedural notice that a §1214 investigation would entail.”  AFL acknowledges that OSC’s decision to provide such notice is discretionary, citing Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns. (6th Cir. 2011).  Because the statutory scheme does not grant AFL an entitlement, the Court finds no concrete injury stemming from a lack of notice.

### Concrete Injury Requirement

A plaintiff who seeks information enjoys a concrete injury only where a statute explicitly entitles the plaintiff to that information.  The Court cites Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent. (2011) and FEC v. Akins (1998) to underscore that informational standing requires a specific statutory right to the information.  Without such an entitlement, AFL’s claim of “informational injury” fails.

### Discretionary Nature of §1216 Investigations

The discretionary character of §1216 investigations further undermines AFL’s contention.  §1216(c) allows OSC to investigate “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” but “a prosecutor’s decision whether to proceed with a §1214‑style investigation is discretionary.”  Because OSC may choose not to provide the procedural notice, AFL’s injury is not concrete and is unattached to a statutory duty on OSC.

### Outcome

AFL’s second standing theory breaks down because it lacks a statutory entitlement to the procedural notice it seeks and because the injury it alleges is not concrete.  The discretionary nature of OSC’s §1216 investigations does not oblige OSC to provide notice that would otherwise give AFL a “preemptive” injury.

## Conclusion

Both standing theories fail to satisfy the Article III requirements: causation is speculative or nonexistent; redressability is unattainable; and concrete injury is lacking.  Accordingly, the Court concludes that AFL has no Article III standing, vacates the district court’s judgment, and remands for dismissal on grounds of lack of subject matter jurisdiction.

# V. Court’s Holding, Procedural Order, and Significance

## Holding: AFL Lacks Article III Standing

The D.C. Circuit’s central conclusion is that the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) fails to meet the Article III standing requirements necessary to bring an individual or organization before a federal court. While the district court dismissed AFL’s FOIA claim for failure to state a claim on its merits, the appellate court set aside that judgment because, under the Constitution, a plaintiff may sue only when it has suffered an *injury in fact* that is credible and *redressable* by a court order. In evaluating AFL’s three‑part standing posture, the court found that the foundation’s asserted injuries were either speculative or unrelated to the alleged defect in OSC’s conduct. Accordingly, the Supreme Court’s traditional three‑prong test—injury in fact, causation, and redressability—cannot be satisfied. The result was a dismissal on the ground of *lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction*.

## Remand with Instruction to Dismiss for Lack of Subject‑Matter Jurisdiction

The appellate court remanded the case to the district court with a clear directive: the court must dismiss the action because it lacks jurisdiction. Jurisdiction in this context is the court’s authority to render a meaningful judgment, which is inextricably linked to standing. By ordering dismissal rather than simply granting a motion to dismiss, the court emphasises that the claim is barred before the court can even entertain the merits. This procedural outcome precludes the district court from issuing any further order on OSC’s alleged failure to investigate or on any FOIA‑theoretic violations. The remand also leaves open the possibility that, were a new claim or a new party with a different factual matrix brought before the court, jurisdiction could be preserved.

## Effect on OSC’s Discretionary Enforcement Powers

The decision marks a reaffirmation of the Office of Special Counsel’s discretion in exercising its enforcement powers under § 1216 and § 1214 of the Civil Service Reform Act. The court was careful to note that OSC is not compelled to launch an investigation unless the particular statutory triggers—most notably the predicates set out in FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—are satisfied. Even if a request for investigation is made, OSC retains discretion over the presence of a mandatory “inquiry” and the content of any resulting notifications. The holding reinforces that, absent a clear statutory requirement, OSC’s decision to decline an investigation does not open a forum for litigation in Article III courts.

The ruling also underscores the distinction between OSC’s *discretionary* enforcement authority and its *procedural obligations* under the statutory scheme. By rejecting AFL’s claim that OSC is required to provide notices under § 1214 in a § 1216 investigation, the court clarified that procedural notices attachment is contingent on a formal enforcement action, not on the mere occurrence of an alleged FOIA‑withholding. This delineation serves as a caveat to future litigants that OSC’s investigative actions—or lack thereof—do not automatically translate into civil‑stance obligations for the parties to such investigations.

## Implications for Future FOIA Challenges and Standing Doctrine

The case crystallises several doctrinal points that will shape the litigation landscape for FOIA and similar administrative‑law claims:

1. **Standing Requires a Concrete, Personal Injury**  
   AFL’s pain at not obtaining records is, under the court’s analysis, a *concrete* injury, but it is *not* injured by OSC’s inaction because the causal chain inevitably goes through the agency actually withholding the records. The decision reaffirms that a plaintiff must show that *the defendant* is the nexus of the alleged injury, not an intermediary or a potential third party. This nuance strengthens the protective filter that bars speculative FOIA claims where the true source of the injury is a separate governmental entity.

2. **Discretionary Enforcement Does Not Imply Formal Research Rights**  
   Even when a statute such as § 1216 mandates an investigation in certain circumstances, the court emphasizes that such investigations do not, in themselves, confer entitlement to the investigative findings. Plaintiffs cannot rely on an agency’s procedural oversight to claim a right to information unless the statute explicitly provides that right. This will caution future litigants from conflating procedural remedies with substantive access guarantees.

3. **Reinforcement of the *Redressability* Exception in Procedural‑Law Cases**  
   While the court acknowledged the “relaxed” standards that apply to procedural‑right claims, it also confirmed that such relaxation does not eliminate the need for a causal link and a realistic chance that a favorable judgment will remedy the injury. This signals that procedural‑law claims still sit within the narrow bounds of federal jurisdiction and that plaintiffs must still articulate a credible, non‑speculative path to relief.

4. **Diminished Prospects for “Indirect” FOIA Injuries**  
   The decision effectively precludes plaintiffs from seeking relief based on another agency’s refusal to comply with a FOIA request, unless the *direct* injurious party is named. That is, if a plaintiff wishes to challenge an agency’s withholding, the plaintiff must either show a personal injury originating from that agency, or bring the agency itself as a defendant. The claim that a third party—here, OSC—can be a proper defendant for the withholding is rejected. This narrowing will likely steer future attorneys toward more focused FOIA claims.

5. **Procedural Transmit of Discretionary Actions**  
   Finally, the holding will influence the design of FOIA litigation strategies. Since OSC’s discretion is protected, litigants might focus on triggering statutory mandates (e.g., ensuring that a FOIA case satisfies the *arbitrary or capricious* test under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)) rather than on appealing OSC’s refusal to investigate. The decision underscores the importance of building a case grounded in a specific statutory trigger rather than on a general call for investigation.

In sum, the D.C. Circuit’s reasoning not only invalidated AFL’s claim but also reaffirmed the contours of standing in administrative‑law contexts. It clarified that discretionary enforcement agencies retain the compulsion to act only under narrowly defined statutory predicates, and that plaintiffs must satisfy stringent injury and causation requirements before a court can entertain their claims. The decision will serve as a touchstone for future FOIA litigants, reinforcing the principle that a court’s jurisdiction is never beyond the lawful limits of Article III.
