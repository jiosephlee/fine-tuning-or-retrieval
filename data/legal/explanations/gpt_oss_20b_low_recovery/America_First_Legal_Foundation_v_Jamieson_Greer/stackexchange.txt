Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why does section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) not limit OSC’s authority under section 1216(a)(3) for FOIA investigations?
Question:
The opinion says that the district court found that “s 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under section 1216.” I thought the three predicates in 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were prerequisites for any OSC enforcement action. How can OSC still investigate under 1216 if those predicates are missing?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court’s ruling hinges on the plain wording of § 1216(a)(3) and the statutory architecture that places that provision *outside* the six predicates required by § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).  In § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is told “*if* a court orders production of the records, *awards* attorneys’ fees, *and* *specifically questions* whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously…” – *then* OSC must promptly investigate.  These three triggers are a *condition* for an OSC proceeding *in the FOIA context*; they do **not** create a blanket limitation on OSC’s authority.  

§ 1216(a)(3), by contrast, is an “additional investigatory authority” that is *independent* of the FOIA‐related predicates.  It reads:  

> “OSC **shall** conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under § 552—i.e., withholdings prohibited by FOIA.”

Because the section explicitly uses the word “shall” and does not reference the “three predicates” of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), the statute is not conditional on those predicates.  The OSC’s discretion to investigate alleged FOIA wrongs is delegated by this provision itself.  The court emphasized this interpretation in its summary:  

> “The district court concluded … **section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under § 1216.**”

Thus, when OSC declined to investigate the DOJ allegation (because the three predicates were not met), the court found that OSC had *legal error* because § 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate any allegation of arbitrary or capricious withholding, irrespective of whether the FOIA‑specific conditions of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are satisfied.  The statutory line‑of‑authority is separate, and the predicates in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) do not create a prerequisite that must be met before § 1216(a)(3) can be invoked.

### What is the difference between a procedural injury and a substantive injury under the relaxed standing standard?
Question:
The court talks about a relaxed standing requirement for procedural injuries but says AFL fails even that standard. I’m confused about the line between a procedural right‑in‑vacuo and a substantive injury, and why the court concluded that preventing OSC from investigating does not amount to a concrete injury.
Answer:
**Short answer**

A procedural‑in‑vacuo injury is a “lost” right to a statutory procedure that has no concrete interest attached to it.  Under the relaxed standing rule, the court still requires an injury in fact – *i.e.* the loss of a protected, concrete, particularized interest – even though it can ignore the usual red‑closure tests of imminent or redressability.  AFL’s complaint fails because the only injuries it alleges (the loss of FOIA records and the loss of OSC notice) are *not* concrete interests; they are simply the loss of information that is not itself an article‑III right.  Consequently, regardless of whether the injury is “procedural” or “substantive,” AFL cannot satisfy the injury clause of standing.

---

### 1.  What the opinion says about the “procedural‑in‑vacuo” doctrine

The court cites **Summers v. Earth Island Inst.**, *555 U.S. 488* (2009) and **Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau**, *115 F.4th 618* (2024) to point out that a *procedural right in vacuo*—a statutory procedural remedy that does not attach to any concrete interest—is **not sufficient** to confer Article III standing.  
In *AFL*, the plaintiff’s claim at issue is that OSC failed to investigate a FOIA‑violating policy “under section 1216.”  That chapter of the *Civil Service Reform Act* is a procedural statute: it allows OSC to *decide* whether or not to investigate alleged wrongdoing.  The statute itself does **not** provide AFL with an entitling right to any information or other benefit.  Hence, the mere loss of a “procedural right” (OSC’s non‑investigation) does not automatically create a tangible injury.

The court’s language – “the ‘deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” – makes it clear that the *concrete interest* test cannot be waived by invoking the relaxed standing standard.

---

### 2.  The relaxed standing standard

When a plaintiff alleges a violation of a *procedural* right that is *designed to protect* a concrete interest, the court may “relax”—but not eliminate—two elements of the ordinary standing test:  

1. **Imminence** (the injury must be “actual or imminent”), and  
2. **Redressability** (a favorable judgment *must* redress the injury).  

See **Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.**, *396 F.3d 1152* (2005).  
Nonetheless, the court still requires that:

* the plaintiff suffer an *injury in fact*, and  
* the injury be *causal* and *rival* (the injury must be caused by the defendant’s conduct).

The *AFL* opinion reiterates these remaining requirements: “plaintiffs must show that the violation of a procedural right ‘resulted in injury’ to some ‘concrete, particularized interest.’”  The court then proceeds to reject AFL on both injury‑and‑causation grounds.

---

### 3.  Why AFL’s injuries are not concrete

**First theory – loss of FOIA records**

* The only “injury” alleged is that DOJ has failed to provide the records AFL requested.  
* The court points out that this injury is *directly traceable to DOJ*, not OSC.  
* Even if OSC had investigated, the chain of causation to DOJ’s compliance is speculative: “a favorable judgment here would not very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress.”  See **Hecate Energy** v. FERC, *126 F.4th 660*.  
* Because the injury is attributable to an independent actor (DOJ), AFL cannot show “causation general” that is required for standing.  
* Thus the injury is *not* a concrete, particularized interest that the defective procedural right was meant to protect.

**Second theory – loss of OSC notice**

* AFL alleges that OSC should have provided notices and information as required under 5 U.S.C. § 1214.  
* The court notes that § 1214 is an *ionic* procedural requirement that OSC may exercise discretion over in § 1216 investigations (**Carson** v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., *633 F.3d 487*).  
* Because AFL does **not** assert an entitlement to receive such notice, there is no *protected interest* in the nonsent information.  
* Consequently, even under the relaxed standard, the injury is insufficient: a “concrete injury” requires an actual loss of a right the plaintiff is entitled to.  

---

### 4.  Substantive versus procedural injury

A *substantive injury* is the loss of a tangible right—e.g., a property right, a contractual right, or a statutory entitlement to a benefit.  A *procedural injury* under the relaxed standard is still a loss of a protected interest, but that interest is a *procedural* one (a right to a fair or timely procedure).  

In *AFL*, the injury alleged is informational, not a protected statutory entitlement.  That makes it **neither substantive nor a protected procedural right in vacuo**.  The court ultimately concludes that AFL lacks standing because it cannot satisfy “the injury in fact” requirement, whether the injury were deemed procedural or substantive.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

* A procedural‑in‑vacuo claim yields no standing.  
* The relaxed standing standard lets the court ignore imminence and redressability, but **the injury‑in‑fact and causation prongs still apply**.  
* AFL’s injuries are informational in nature and lack an underlying entitling right, so they do **not** meet the injury requirement.  
* Therefore, the court correctly held that AFL lacks Article III standing, whether its alleged injury is viewed as procedural or substantive.

### Why is the causal chain from OSC’s inaction to DOJ’s FOIA compliance considered too speculative?
Question:
The opinion outlines a multi‑legislative causal chain involving OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and DOJ. The court calls it speculative. Could someone explain why the court doesn’t allow speculation about the actions of independent agencies when determining standing?
Answer:
**Short answer** –  
The court refuses to accept the causal chain because it is “speculative” in the sense that it relies on a chain of “if‑then” events that would involve the independent choices of three separate agencies (OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and the Department of Justice (DoJ)).  Under Article III standing, a plaintiff must show that, *in the fact‑guy sense*, a favorable judgment is *likely* to bring about the injury alleged. A chain that can only be completed by guessing how each agency will act in the future does not meet that requirement.  

---

## 1. The standard for standing

The court reminds us of **Lujan v. Defensores de la Vida**, 504 U.S. 555 (1992) – a plaintiff has standing only if it can show:

1. A concrete injury;
2. The injury is “caused by the defendant;” and
3. The injury is “redressable” by the court.

When the injury is the loss of *information*, the courts hold that the plaintiff must show that the injury is *concrete* and *particularized* (see **Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.**, 659 F.3d 13).  The courts also emphasize that **causation** cannot rest on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (**FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.**, 602 U.S. 367).  The burden for a procedural‑rights case is “relaxed” (Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152), but it still requires a *plausible* causal connection between the alleged violation and the injury.  

---

## 2. What AFL alleges

AFL’s first theory is that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ under § 1216 caused AFL’s FOIA injury:

1. **OSC** should investigate DOJ for a possible arbitrary/​capricious withholding under § 1216(a)(3).  
2. If OSC investigates, it may **pursue corrective action** under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215.  
3. The **MSPB** could sanction DOJ personnel for the alleged non‑disclosure.  
4. Consequently, **DOJ** would be compelled to produce the records that AFL had asked for.  

The second theory is that the *information* that would flow from an OSC investigation (under § 1214 notice requirements) is itself an injury.  

---

## 3. Why the chain is deemed speculative

| Step in the chain | What the court says | Why it’s speculative |
|-------------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **OSC decides whether to investigate** | OSC has “discretion” (see § 1216(c) and there is no *mandatory* obligation). | The decision is within OSC’s hands, but AFL has no way to prove that OSC *will* act in a specific way. |
| **OSC pursues § 1214 or § 1215** | OSC may institute corrective action (or discipline). | Even if OSC does investigate, nothing in the statute requires it to go beyond an investigation; the decision to *pursue* corrective action is discretionary. |
| **MSPB sanctions DOJ employees** | MSPB can sanction if it finds wrongdoing (1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A)). | MSPB is an independent agency.  AFL has no evidence that, *given* OSC’s investigation, MSPB would in fact sanction the DOJ personnel. |
| **DOJ produces records** | Production would result from MSPB sanctions or from a separate FOIA compliance decision. | The chain relies on a *hypothetical* decision by MSPB and a *hypothetical* reaction by DOJ, neither of which is linked to the court’s judgment. |

The court points out that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (**FDA v. All. For Hippocratic Med.**, 602 U.S. 367).  In particular, § 1216(c) does not require OSC to “*determine whether disciplinary action is warranted*”—it only says OSC “*may investigate and seek corrective action… in the same way*.”  The court has ruled that OSC’s discretionary choice to investigate does **not** create the kind of *immediate* factual connection needed for Article III standing.  

The same logic applies to the second theory. Even if OSC *did* conduct an investigation, it is *not* required to furnish OSC‑style notice or information under § 1214.  The court therefore concludes that “AFL … has not shown that a favorable judgment here would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury.  

---

## 4. Bottom line

The causal chain that AFL proposes is **too attenuated** and **too speculative** because:

1. It depends on chained discretionary decisions by independent agencies that are not *directly* caused by the court’s ruling.  
2. The chain requires several “if‑then” steps that the plaintiff can’t plausibly prove.  
3. The injury (loss of information) is *not* a concrete, particularized injury that can be reliably redressed by a judicial order aimed at OSC.  

Under the standing doctrine, speculation about how an independent agency will act is insufficient.  The court therefore denies AFL standing and remands for dismissal.

### How does the court determine that an informational injury from a FOIA request is not concrete or particularized?
Question:
AFL argues that not receiving the documents is an injury. The court rejects it, citing that an informational injury is not concrete. What factors make an informational interest concrete or not under Article III standing?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The court finds that *informational interests are concrete only when the claimant has a statutory entitlement to the information—or a concrete, particularized interest that the law protects.  In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court rejects AFL’s claim that “not receiving records” is a concrete injury because AFL has no statutory right to the records and its alleged procedural right to an OSC investigation does not create an enforceable entitlement to any information.  Consequently, AFL’s alleged informational injury fails the concreteness requirement of Article III standing.**

---

### 1.  The Article III standing framework

* Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife required an injury that is ***concrete and particularized*** and ***actual or imminent***.  
* In cases based on a *procedural* right, the Supreme Court has relaxed, but not eliminated, the injury requirement (e.g., *Citizen’s for Law & Education v. Dept. of Education*, 396 F.3d 1152).  
* Even under the relaxed standard, the claimant must show that the alleged denial of a *statutory procedural right* has caused injury to a *concrete interest* protected by law.

The district court applied this framework and found that AFL’s two attempted theories failed to meet the concreteness requirement.

---

### 2.  AFL’s first claiming injury: “not receiving the records”

1. **No statutory entitlement.**  
   *FOIA* gives a requester an entitlement only *to the records* themselves, not *to the investigative or procedural actions* that might lead to disclosure.  
   (See *Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23-24.)

2. **Informational injury is not concrete unless the law enshrines an interest.**  
   The court cites *Am. Soc’y v. Feld* and *Pub. Citizen v. DOJ* to emphasize that a concrete injury arises only when the claimant owes a protected claim to the information.  
   In this case, AFL *does not* have such a claim; it only *requested* the records.  

3. **Speculative causal chain.**  
   The court lays out the chain: OSC’d investigate → MSPB sanctions → DOJ provides records.  
   Causation collapses because it relies on speculative choices by agencies *not before the court* (*FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367).  
   Thus, even if DOJ had *not* complied, a favorable ruling for AFL would *not* guarantee that OSC would investigate or that DOJ would change its conduct.  
   The injury is therefore *not available for redress* in a meaningful way, a key component of concreteness.

---

### 3.  AFL’s second claim: “not receiving OSC‑investigation notices”

1. **Section 1216 does *not* provide a statutory entitlement to information.**  
   The court remarks that § 1216 “creates *no* entitlement to receive information” (citing *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487).  
   AFL acknowledges that OSC’s observation of a § 1214 process is *discretionary*.

2. **No concrete interest protected by law.**  
   Even if the OSC were compelled to conduct a § 1216 investigation, the court notes that AFL doesn't claim any statutory right to the *results* of that investigation.  
   § 1216 can compel *enforcement* only if OSC’s discretion is triggered by a “court order,” but that does not translate into *informational entitlement*.

3. **Redressability is unattainable.**  
   The court stresses that a judgment ordering OSC to investigate would not *bind* OSC to provide AFL with any notices or findings—OSC retains discretion.  
   Without a binding order, AFL’s informational injury cannot be remedied.

---

### 4.  Bottom line: what makes an informational injury concrete

* **Statutory right to the information.**  
  The claimant must be *entitled* to the records (or to the remedies that produce the records).  
  *FOIA* gives an entitlement only to the documents themselves; it does not constitute an interest in *procedural actions*.

* **Concrete, particularized interest that the law protects.**  
  The injury must affect an *interest* that the law recognizes as protected (e.g., privacy, freedom of information).  
  The court concludes AFL lacks such an interest here.

* **Causation and redressability that can be reasonably expected** from a court order.  
  The chain of events cannot be speculative; the court expects the judge’s ruling to directly compel the defendant to remedy the injury.

* **A non-pecuniary “information” interest is only concrete when it is *enshrined* in statute or constitutional right** (see *Am. Soc’y v. Feld*, *Pub. Citizen v. DOJ*).

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, AFL failed to satisfy any of these elements, so the court held that its so‑called “informational injury” was not concrete or particularized, and thereby denied Article III standing.

### In what ways is a plaintiff’s request for OSC to investigate a FOIA policy equivalent to or different from a FOIA request for records?
Question:
AFL sought OSC’s investigation of DOJ’s policy that it does not search employee emails without consent. How does that differ from a typical FOIA claim for records, and why does that distinction matter for standing?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the Court focused on whether AFL’s recourse to OSC was, in substance, the same as a traditional FOIA request for records—or whether it was a distinct constitutional claim that might or might not meet the *standing* requirements that apply to FOIA petitions.

---

### 1. What the Court says about the *type* of request

* **FOIA record request**  
  – A plaintiff writes a FOIA request and the agency archives and produces specific records.  
  – If the agency refuses, the plaintiff can sue for the records and for a court order that the agency must comply.  

* **Request for OSC investigation**  
  – AFL asked OSC to exercise its enforcement powers under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which “shall … conduct an investigation” of alleged arbitrary or capricious withholding of information “prohibited under section 552” (FOIA).  
  – OSC’s mandate is discretionary; it “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215” if it finds a violation.  
  – Unlike a FOIA petition, the request is not for *the records themselves* but for OSC’s *procedural* action—namely an investigation that might trigger corrective or disciplinary proceedings.

In other words, a FOIA request is an *information‑seeking* petition that, if denied, gives a plaintiff a concrete right to *obtain records*. A request to OSC is a *procedural‑rights* claim that seeks *enforcement of the agency’s own statutory obligations* to investigate and correct wrongful withholdings. OSC’s investigation, if it occurs, may result in a different set of remedial actions (e.g., disciplinary action against an employee) rather than a direct delivery of the documents plaintiff wants.

---

### 2. How that difference matters for standing

The Court explained that *standing* is the constitutional prerequisite for a plaintiff to litigate any claim, including a FOIA injury. The key elements are:

1. **Injury in fact** – Something concrete and particularized.  
2. **Causation** – A clear causal link between the defendant’s conduct and the injury.  
3. **Redressability** – A favorable court decision must be likely to remedy the injury.

#### a. Injury from the *absence* of an OSC investigation

AFL tried to argue that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused a “concrete, informational injury” because it prevented AFL from receiving the requested records. The Court found that this theory fails on three fronts:

* **Causation** – The link between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s failure to produce records is *speculative*. OSC’s investigation would have to trigger a *series* of independent steps (OSC’s findings → MSPB disciplinary action → DOJ’s compliance) that the Court could not rely on.  
* **Redressability** – Even if OSC had investigated, its findings would not *necessarily* force DOJ to produce the records; the Court said that “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court.”  
* **Concrete injury** – The injury was not the *fact* that OSC didn’t investigate, but the *failure of DOJ to disclose records*, which is a separate actor.

Because the alleged injury is *vested in the agency’s refusal to comply with FOIA*, not in OSC’s procedural right, AFL could not establish standing.

#### b. Injury from the *failure* to receive OSC‑generated notice

AFL’s second attempt was to claim an informational injury from not being provided the notices and information OSC is required to give under § 1214 when investigating a prohibited personnel practice. The Court rejected this claim because:

* § 1216 does **not** entitle a plaintiff to such notices; OSC’s undertaking of a § 1216 investigation is *discretionary*, not a statutory obligation to share findings.  
* Without an entitlement to receive OSC’s investigation report, AFL’s supposed “informational injury” collapses.  
* The Court noted that even if OSC was compelled to investigate, it would still be at its discretion whether to furnish AFL with any “notice and information” under § 1214.

Thus, whether AFL sought an OSC investigation was the *wrong type of claim* to support a redressable injury, and the Court found AFL violated *standing*.

---

### 3. Bottom line

* A FOIA request *directly* seeks the records.  
* A request to OSC seeks *enforcement* of the agency’s own FOIA‑related duties and is a *procedural* claim.

Because the *law* that governs an OSC investigation is discretionary and the *injury* that can flow from it is not a direct, concrete, and redressable loss, AFL was lacking Article III standing. The Court therefore remanded, directing dismissal for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

For a law student working on a FOIA‑related case, the takeaway is: **When you want to compel an agency to act, you must frame the claim around the agency’s statutory duty to produce records, not around a third‑party enforcement body’s discretion to investigate.** Otherwise you run the risk of *standing* defeat, just as AFL did.

### What does it mean for an OSC investigation under §1216 to be discretionary versus mandatory?
Question:
The opinion says OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214 notice requirements is discretionary in a §1216 investigation. Could someone elaborate on what is mandatory here and what OSC can decide, and how that affects a plaintiff’s claim?
Answer:
**Answer**

The court’s discussion makes it clear that a §1216 investigation is not *automatically* bound to follow the procedural steps that a §1214 investigation must.  

| Issue | What §1216 **requires** | What §1216 **permits** | What the court says |
|-------|------------------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| **Initiation** | OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of an allegation of arbitrary or capricious withholding of information. | None. The statute only says OSC *may* investigate. | “OSC’s decision… to investigate … is discretionary.” |
| **Notice & Information Requirements** | §1214(a)(1)(B)–(D) govern the notice of complaint, the investigative report, and the opportunity to respond. | §1216(c) says OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under §1214… in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” | “ … OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214 notice requirements is discretionary in a §1216 investigation.” |
| **When a §1214 procedure is *not* required** | When the alleged misconduct is a “FOIA withholding” that falls under §1216, OSC can choose *not* to provide the notice or other procedural safeguards that a §1214 complaint would normally get. | If OSC opts to conduct a §1214–style investigation, it must provide those notices; if it does not choose that model, it may skip them. | "... OSC’s decision not to investigate was based on a legal error … but the court declined to order OSC to investigate any FOIA policy because, in its view, §1216(c) committed that decision to OSC’s discretion." |

### What is *mandatory*?

* **The act of investigating** – OSC is required to investigate any allegation that a record was withheld under §552(a)(4)(F)(i), provided the three predicates (court order, attorney‑fees award, and question of arbitrariness/capriciousness) are satisfied.  
* **The remedial options** – Once an investigation is underway, OSC may bring a corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) under §1214 or a disciplinary action under §1215, *if it deems that a personnel practice is involved*. But the statute does not compel OSC to do so.

### What is *discretionary*?

* **Whether to treat the FOIA‑allegation as a “prohibited personnel practice.”**  
  * If OSC decides *yes*, it is bound by the procedural safeguards of §1214 (notice, opportunity to respond, investigative report, etc.).  
  * If OSC decides *no*, it may conduct a simpler fact‑finding inquiry without those notice requirements.  
* **Whether to actually file a §1214 or §1215 proceeding.**  
  * The statute gives OSC the *option* to do so; it is not compelled to move forward with a corrective action or disciplinary action simply because an investigation was opened.  
* **Whether to provide the plaintiff with any of the §1214‑style information** that would normally be furnished to the complainant in a personnel‑practice investigation.  
  * The court notes that “OSC need not provide §1214 notice requirements in a §1216 investigation,” and that a plaintiff who relies on receiving such notice has no entitlement to it.

### Practical effect on a plaintiff

Because the procedural safeguards are discretionary, a plaintiff who sued under §1216 cannot rely on an obligation that OSC must furnish notice or an investigative report. The only substantive right a plaintiff has in a §1216 investigation is the *potential* remedial action OSC may decide to pursue (e.g., a §1214 corrective action or a §1215 disciplinary measure). If OSC chooses not to treat the allegation as a personnel‑practice matter, the plaintiff has no statutory avenue to compel OSC to disclose the investigated facts or to require OSC to follow §1214 procedures.

In the case at hand, the court found that AFL’s standing claims rested on an *informational injury* that the plaintiff alleged the OSC *must* remedy by providing §1214‑style notices. Because that obligation is discretionary, AFL could not show a concrete injury tied to a statutory entitlement, and the court dismissed AFL’s standing. The underlying take‑away is that §1216 investigations are **discretionary** with respect to the procedural steps of §1214, and plaintiffs cannot invoke those steps as a contractual or statutory right by merely requesting a §1216 enforcement action.

### Does OSC’s refusal to investigate create an entitlement to receive notice or information under §1214?
Question:
AFL contends that if OSC were required to investigate, it would have to give it the same notices that would come in a §1214 investigation. Is there any legal basis that an investigation under §1216 creates a right to those §1214 notices, or is it purely discretionary?
Answer:
**Answer**

No.  The § 1216 authority does not create an entitlement to the § 1214 notice and information procedures.  “§ 1216 investigations are discretionary” and, when conducted, are not required to follow § 1214’s procedural rules unless the OSC itself chooses to do so.

### How the opinion reaches that conclusion

1. **Section 1216’s scope**  
   The opinion explains that § 1216 allows OSC to investigate “any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information… prohibited under section 552” and that “OSC may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” (Id. § 1216(c)).  The language still leaves the decision to use § 1214’s procedures to OSC’s discretion.

2. **Acknowledgement of discretion**  
   When the court discusses AFL’s second standing theory, it cites *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011), a case that held OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary.  The court notes that AFL “acknowledges cases holding that OSC’s decision … is discretionary.”  Because AFL does not argue that OSC *must* apply § 1214’s notice requirements, the court treats any such requirement as discretionary.

3. **No entitlement to § 1214 notices**  
   The opinion states:  
   > “Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory.”  (para. C).  Therefore, even if OSC conducts a § 1216 investigation, AFL does not have a statutory right to the “notice and information” that § 1214 would require.

4. **Result**  
   The court concludes that a § 1216 investigation “creates no entitlement to receive information” and that an OSC order to investigate would not be “reasonably likely to redress AFL’s second alleged injury.”  Hence, the § 1216 authority is purely discretionary and does not grant § 1214‑style notice to a plaintiff.

### Bottom line

A § 1216 investigation is a discretionary tool that OSC may use, but it does not carry with it the procedural mandatory notice and information regime of § 1214.  A plaintiff cannot rely on § 1216 to compel OSC to provide the same notices that a § 1214 investigation would require.

### How does the Supreme Court’s treatment of ‘redressability’ in the context of discretionary enforcement tools impact standing analysis?
Question:
The opinion references decisions that redressability focuses on the judgment’s effect on the parties, not on speculative future actions by other agencies. Could someone explain how that principle shapes the court’s conclusion that AFL cannot be redressed by a ruling to force OSC to investigate?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Supreme Court’s treatment of *redressability* in cases involving discretionary enforcement tools—the principle that a judgment must be capable of affecting the parties themselves, not merely persuading unrelated agencies—underlies the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that AFL could not claim that a court order would compel OSC to investigate.

---

### 1.  Redressability & discretionary enforcement

Redressability is the “something that the plaintiff stands to gain” that a favorable judgment would produce.  In *Lujan*, the Court emphasized that a judgment must **bind the parties** to be redressable; it may not merely influence the conduct of other actors.  This standard follows from the observation that **the judiciary has no authority to compel the exercise of discretionary powers** that belong to other governmental agencies (see e.g. *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821, 831‑32).

In *Am. First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the Court applied this principle to a FOIA‑related case in which OSC’s investigative authority was at issue.  OSC’s power to investigate under § 1216(a)(3) is expressly **discretionary**.  The Court therefore evaluated whether a court order could directly alter OSC’s discretionary decision‑making.

---

### 2.  The speculative causal chain

AFL claimed that, had OSC carried out the § 1216 investigation, it would have been compelled to pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215, that the MSPB would then sanction the DOJ employee, and that DOJ would finally produce the records.  The Court rejected this chain because it hinges on *two* independent, non‑party agencies (MSPB and DOJ) making discretionary choices that the court has no power to dictate.

The Court stated:

> “The alleged causal chain is… **speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit**… and **speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion**…” (para. 1‑2, § II).  

Because a judgment cannot compel OSC, or even *incentivize* it to act differently, the Court concluded that a favorable decision “would very likely** spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s injury is lacking.

This mirrors the Court’s earlier reasoning in *Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC* (126 F.4th 660, 667) and *Haaland v. Brackeen* (599 U.S. 255, 293‑94): redressability turns on the judgment’s effect on the litigants, not on whether it might persuade unrelated actors.  The Court expressly noted this principle in plain language:

> “Redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court.” (para. 1‑3, § II).

---

### 3.  Practical effect on standing

Because **AFL’s injury is informational—premised on DOJ’s refusal to produce records—and the only possible redress is an **execution** of DOJ’s FOIA duties (which rests with DOJ, not with OSC)**, a court’s order to compel OSC does not remedy the injury.  The Court concluded that the procedural right alleged by AFL is a “right in vacuo”; without a concrete interest that is actually affected, it fails the *injury* prong (see *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496).  The redressability analysis reinforces this: a judicial mandate to OSC would not alter DOJ’s conduct and thus would not redress AFL’s informational injury.

Similarly, under the second theory—AFL’s interest in the *fruits* of an OSC investigation—the Court reiterated that § 1216 investigation does not create an entitlement to information, and that any notice or information OSC might provide is at its discretion.  Because the order would not bind OSC, it cannot redress that injury either.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

The Court’s treatment of *redressability*—requiring that a judgment directly affect the parties and not merely sway unrelated agencies—shows that a directive to OSC to investigate a FOIA policy would not remedy AFL’s injury.  Consequently, AFL lacks Article III standing.  The opinion is a clear illustration that *redressability* is not a technical “judge‑vs‑agency” hurdle; it is the substantive standard that ensures the judiciary does not overstep its mandate by indirectly controlling the discretionary powers of other branches.
