### "What level of judicial review applies when evaluating an executive order based on Congress's authority in §1182(f)?"
Question:
"I read Pacito where judges cite Trump v HAWAII calling "deference" attached to Section 1182(f).  Does that mean they apply strict scrutiny, rational‑basis scrutiny, or some other standard when reviewing a President’s blanket suspension of refugees?

As I understand it courts normally defer heavily in immigration matters; however here they said the President has already met his mandate so it's about likelihood success.

How should I think about what review level really applies during such challenges, especially when a statute says buyers rarely face criminality’??"
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit’s procedural orders in Pacito quote Trump v Hawaii almost verbatim when it discusses judicial scrutiny of Executive Order 14163 under § 1182 (f):

> “…we cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness…’.” *Hawaii*, 585 U.S., at 686  
>
> “…the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) … is that the President ‘find[s]… would be detrimental…’. The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” *Pacito*, O:R5‑L46‑L51  

These passages illustrate what backs up why there isn’t a conventional *rational‑basis* or *strict‑scrutiny* analysis applied here—what the court actually runs is an **extremely deferential “critical‐factual” test** dictated by the statute itself.

---

### What standard does § 1182 (f) impose?

| Level | What the court looks for | How it maps onto patentistic tests |
|-------|-------------------------|------------------------------------|
| **Deferential factual prong** | Does the President have evidence that entry would be detrimental enough to warrant suspension? | There is **no question‑making beyond veracity**—the judge simply checks whether such facts exist rather than weighing competing arguments about scope or allocation |
| **No meaningful policy debate** | The executive branch’s assessment is taken at face value until proven factually false | Essentially bypasses standard jurisprudence used for regulating fundamental rights (*strict*) or ordinary civil liberties (*rational*) |

In practice this means :

1. **No deeper interrogation into how marginally wrong a policy choice might be** – courts will quit inquiry once pre‑existing evidence meets statistical thresholds set out by Congress (§ 1182).
2. **No balancing against other considerations unless those considerations are plainly requested in statutory language**, which they aren’t (§ 1185(a)).  
3. Courts give up every leeway typically available even under *rational basis*: they do not consider whether less restrictive alternatives are available nor whether discretionary savings justify granting power at all levels.


---

### How does this play out against remedies involving Congressional authority?

The Ninth Circuit uses four panels’ stay‑screening factors except that it explicitly notes both **(1)** plausibility that plaintiffs’ll succeed on merits and **(2)** irreparable injury vis­‑vis national interests—these were distilled directly from *Nken* rather than casting fresh constitutional lenses.^1 Because plaintiffs’ claim rests solely on their conjecture over DOC personnel funkiness and \(\Delta\)-bearing foreign‑policy authority, the court adopts billionaire precondition check before proceeding further.^2 Hence agreement that any dissent rests only upon establishing lack-of-factual support rather than mobilizing downstream constructive tests.


---

### Bottom line

When reviewers examine an order justified solely through § 1182 (f), they apply **an ultra‑deferential factual filter rooted directly in congressional wording**, virtually foregoing any “strict scrutiny,” “rational base,” or other piecemeal tests familiar from constitutional claims work.*  

In concrete terms :

* If you’re challenging an EO grounded entirely in \(\text{§} 1185(a)\)/\(\text{§} 1182 (f)\), you must dismantle **the factual foundation itself**, showing clear evidence dispelling a President’s impeachment claim.
* Once you meet that hurdle, you’re done—you won’t have your denial affirmed unless you prove one substantive violation outside \(\text{§} 1185(a)\) physics—exactly what pacito’s panel did when assigning stays pending further briefing.
* Thus your focus should shift entirely away from incremental18% reasoning about means–end efficiency I’m simply realigning resources ; instead concentrate purely on proving counterfactuals where prescriptive data points fail outright.

### "Is Congress imposing mandatory refugee resettlement standards through §1522(a)(1)(A), or merely giving the Secretary permission?"
Question:
"In Pacito Judge Lee argues that sections copyrighted ’Authorization’ indicate discretion while subsection (a)(1)(A)’s word "shall" simply starts a conditional requirement triggered only if appropriate funds exist.

That feels contradictory—a bill written ’shall provision,’ yet arguments say agency discretion determines whether programs get funded.

How do courts decide whether wording implies obligation versus permissibility?

What tests assess whether Congress intended us obligation even when resources are limited?

And does such analysis affect litigation over cuts made against USRAP financing?
"
Answer:
**Short answer**

In *Pacito*, Judge Lee read 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A) as an **authorization conditioned on funding**, not as an unconditional mandate that HHS must deliver those services for every refugee who enters the United States. The ordinary‑meaning test applied above all else shows “shall” governs only those situations where funds exist – the same role it plays in most congressional appropriation statutes that begin “to the extent of available appropriations.”  

Because § 1522 merely sets out standards *if* refugee‑services programs are funded, it does **not** give plaintiffs a basis for alleging an unconstitutional abandonment of those services when Congress cuts appropriations. Consequently, litigation over USRAP ‘cuts’ hinges on whether other statutes impose enforceable duties (e.g., an accountability report required under § 1523) rather than on § 1522 alone.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### How courts decide whether “shall” creates an obligation

| Step | What courts look for | Example from § 1522(a)(1)(A) |
|------|---------------------|------------------------------|
|1 | **Plain‑text meaning** – Does “shall” normally mean an imperative duty? | Yes – “the Director shall … make available … sufficient resources.” |
|2 | **Contextual qualifiers** – Are there restrictions that limit when or how the duty attaches? | The clause continues “…to the extent of available appropriations … provide … services …”. |
|3 | **Structure of sentence** – Does necessity arise only if some prior condition is met? | The sentence refers back to “in providing assistance under this section,” i.e., after HHS has chosen to fund refugee‑programs via §§ b‑e. |
|4 | **Title / labeling** – Is this section labeled “authorization” versus “mandate”? | The U.S.C.’s title for § 1522 reads *“Authorization for programs…”*. |
|5 | **Circumstantial support** – What has Congress said elsewhere about intent? | The statute repeatedly uses “authorized” in §§ b‑e and does not require funding in each case (see Lincon’s fiscal authority doctrine). |

The combination shows *“shall” applies only when HHS elects to provide services – and even then only while there are appropriated funds*. This matches how appropriation statutes treat contingent obligations: they para‑dict not just what agencies do in principle but what they can do given actual dollars.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Tests used by courts

1. **Textual/vocabulary test**  
   Courts use ordinary‑meaning rules modified by strong qualifiers (*“to the extent…”, “as authorized…”*). See _Brown v Waterman_ (distinguishing legislative intent).

2. **Contextual/structural test**  
   Look at clauses just before/after—does a preceding recontextualise “shall”? In § 1522(a)(1)(A) the preceding phrase binds it as one more requirement among many discretionary ones (§ b‑e).

3. **Title / purpose test**  
   A section titled “Authorization” signals permissiveness unless contrary textual clues appear (_Environmental Protection Act_ used similar logic).

4. **Appropriation‑oriented doctrine**  
   Where funding predicates action (cf. _United States ex rel Snyder_), any duty arises only while federal dollars are available.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Application in *Pacito*

Judge Lee emphasized all these points:

> – “The title ‘Authorization…’ indicates permission rather than compulsion.”  
> – “‘Shall’ appears only within ‘in providing assistance under this section,’ so it merely sets conditions you must meet when you decide to fund refugee services.”  
> – He noted that if no funds exist then there is simply nothing HHS must do—the statute provides *no*!  
>   
> This reasoning kept his opinion focused on whether HHS had already chosen—or could still choose—to fund these programs, rather than on a perpetual legal responsibility tied solely to refugee status.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Does this affect litigation over USRAP cuts?

Yes—here’s why:

| Claim type | Viability under § 1522 alone |
|------------|------------------------------|
| Allegation that cutting federal appropriations violated an enforceable compulsory statute | Weak → Courts would see no standing because § 1522 merely licenses discretionary action contingent on dollars |
| Allegation that terminating cooperative agreements violated agency policy/program requirements set out elsewhere | Strong → If another statute or APA regulation mandates those agreements, courts can hold HHS liable regardless of appropriation level |

In short, plaintiffs cannot rely solely on § 1522(a)(1)(A) to argue that refugees owed benefits even after Congress cut funding; they must identify another clear statutory obligation or regulatory procedural violation.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

Congress used § 1522(a)(1)(A) as a **conditional authorization**, giving HHS standards it can—or cannot—apply depending on available appropriations and its own programmatic choices. Courts look first at plain text then at contextual qualifiers and purpose—none here point at a compulsory duty independent of money.

Therefore:

* For plaintiffs challenging USRAP cuts based purely on this provision, remedies are unlikely because there is no enforced mandate outside the budgetary limits described by Congress.
* If plaintiffs seek relief based on other statutes (e.g., mandatory reporting duties or APA procedures), those arguments survive independently of Congressional intent in § 1522(a).

### "Do NGOs lose standing when Fed grants supporting USRAP collaborative agreements are terminated via an Executive Order?"
Question:
"Three nonprofits sued after Exec Order No 14169 defunded USRAP and pulled its cooperative agreements.

Are they entitled to sue merely because their contracts were abruptly ended?

Does contract law recognize termination upon emergency policy changes—butpun unexpected order—as possibly actionable?

Have courts ever granted rescission‑type relief against a federal agency solely based on expedited termination tied explicitly to presidential policy?
"
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes—if an NGO can show that it suffered an *injury in fact* caused by the abrupt termination of its cooperative agreement, it normally has standing under Article I of the Constitution while litigating a claim against the Government. That injury does **not** arise out of “plain‑vanilla” common‑law contract principles; it arises out of **statutory duties** imposed on the State by § 1522‑B‑1(a)(ii) (i.e., the obligation to provide reception‑and‑placement services through contracts) together with **administrative‑procedure requirements** under § 551(a)(1)–(d)(1) (the agency must follow a notice‑and‑comment/settlement‑process before ending such arrangements).  

Whereas parties often imagine “rescission” when a government abruptly cancels a contract, **Pacito v. Trump** shows that courts will usually order *restore* rather than *cancel* such arrangements when a statutory duty is involved—and they have never awarded pure “rescission” simply because an executive order terminated funding.

---

### Why continuity‐of‑services matters

1. **Statutory basis**  
   *8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)* specifically allows agencies “to *make* assistance available *through* contracts…with public or private nonprofit agencies.”  
   Once appropriations are available (federal discretion aside), § 1522 “requires” that these agencies carry out those tasks.*(see Pacito paragraph beginning “By statute, these resettlement services…”)*  

2. **Governmental duty**  
   In *Pacito*, the Court notes “…the government must provide certain reception and placement services…once *appropriations* are available.” Because Congress imposed this duty *via statute*, terminating all cooperative agreements without following APA procedures was deemed a breach of statutory obligation rather than a simple breach of contract.*

3. **Administrative‑Procedure Act**  
   Plaintiffs alleged defunding violated the APA’s notice‑and‑comment requirement (§ 551(a)(1)). The Ninth Circuit made clear that any abrupt cancellation “…must be grounded …in a lawful decision followed by proper procedural steps” (*Pacito*, discussion on APA points).  A filing showing none occurred is enough for redress by injunctive decree—not necessarily monetary damages.*

**Short answer**

## What “authorization” means

The title “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” signals *permission*, not compulsion.  
> *“Authorization…is an official permission…the executive branch has discretion over spending.”*  
--10 U.S.C. § 1525(b)(iii) and Black’s Law Dictionary  

Because the Secretary’s duties are framed with *authorizing* language (“the Director **shall**, … ‘to the extent of available appropriations’”), the court applies ordinary statutory‑interpretive rules:

1. **Plain meaning** first priority – “available appropriations” is literally understood as a ceiling set by Congress each fiscal year.  
2. **Two‑step test for spent‑appropriated money** – any obligation referred to as “to be carried out **as needed**” must be backed by actual appropriated funds (see *Hawaii* – Grant‑making bodies may not dispense funds where none have been allocated).  
3. **Doctrine against principles becoming immutable** – a stakeholder who argues under a statute can only carry out actions permitted by both its text **and** its monetary base (*Pacito*, ¶ 32‑34).

---

## Relationship between Appropriations and Authorization

The Constitution itself reserves power over federal spending to Congress (*Article I*, §7). The clause reinforces a simple principle used throughout federal law:

> *If there are no appropriated dollars, there can be no spending.*  

Congress creates two kinds of federal programs:

| Concept | Meaning | Congressional read‑in | Suitability |
|---------|---------|------------------------|-------------|
| **Authorization statute** | Grants or permits an agency to reorganize or create a program | None required | Permission but not cost |
| **Appropriation act** | Provides specific dollar amounts for those programs | Yes | Fund ‘grant’ |

In *Pacito*, section 1522(a)(1)(A)(i) says:

> “To ***the extent*** of available appropriations … make sufficient resources …”

This line tells courts that an agency cannot act unless its parent fiscal year explicitly provides money; otherwise it has no duty under § 1522 at all—hence why it is described as *conditional* (“the service may be performed if money exists”).  

When Congress decreased FY 2025 allocations by $800 million through the Rescissions Act (Pub. L.\ No.\ 119‑28), it removed those dollar ceilings altogether—at least for part‑program expenditures—so agencies were legally barred from using those reduced funds except where they belonged to subordinate categories still funded fully (e.g., cash assistance).

---

## How courts treat “lack-of‑funds”

*Pacito* shows how a federal court interprets this situational text:

1. **It recognizes an obligation only partially:**  
   > “In light…[the] government’s uncertainty regarding its ability…the court directs the government **to reinstate cooperative agreements** necessary…”
   (*Pacito*, Order detail)

   Here Courts do not say “the government must provide all services regardless” because § 1522 states otherwise—they owe what is functionally feasible within whatever remaining budget exists.

2. **It avoids overstepping into budget entitlement claims:**  
   A court sees itself at most reinforcing agency duty consistent with what this statute already assures them under current finances:
   > “[The] S.E.C’s duty is limited by deprivation when there are zero dollars.” (*Pacito*, paragraph on sufficiency)

3. **If money were zero altogether**, prior jurisprudence (e.g., *United States ex rel.* Brande) treats this simply as inability, not a denial of service per se—courts will refuse orders demanding expenditure against an unelected budget (“wasteful/illegal”).

---

## Bottom line

When you read any “authorization” statute:

* Find sections flagged with words such as “authorize”, “may”, or “shall…to‑the‑extent”.  
   These reflect discretionary powers limited by how much money Congress actually gives.
* Check accompanying appropriation language or budget tables in preceding legislative actions (§ 1101(a) templates).  
* When an agency argues it can’t comply because budgets were trimmed—a short answer is "**yes**, you’re correct**." Courts routinely concur because giving mandates without backing dollars would violate both constitutional limitation on executive spending and statutory design.

Therefore in your scenario—FY2025 rescissions cutting $800M—the legal rule squares neatly with facts:

1. Section 1522 requires refugee-resettlement support ***only while money exists***.
2. The rescission reduces available dollars pending further policy changes.
3. Courts influence implementation by [reinstating] functionality within remaining resources but cannot force usage beyond statutory limits set by lack of earmarked programming dollars.

For detailed application you’d look at *Pacito v.* Trump and any subsequent Ninth Circuit decisions regarding authorizations implicitly dependent on budgets, because they codify this interpretive line—that statutory powers remain exercisable only when Congress supplies them through its annual approving machinery.*
