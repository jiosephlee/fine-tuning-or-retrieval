Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Exuding Deference: The Ninth Circuit’s Reaffirmation of Presidential Discretion in Refugee Admissions

## Why This Case Matters

Executive orders are the instrument by which a president can alter immigration policy on a sweeping scale. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit’s stay order makes it clear that the judiciary will not peer over the President’s shoulder when that policy is grounded in §1182(f). At the same time, the court insists that the executive branch still has to honor statutory obligations—most notably the reception‑and‑placement services required under the Refugee Act. The order therefore balances two divergent lines of authority that have long been at odds: the President’s “political power” to suspend admissions, and the federal government’s statutory duty to serve refugees. How the court navigates that tension will reverberate for any future truce that might have the President pause the program.

## The Court’s Application of Deference

The surviving doctrine on presidential immigration power is Trump v. Hawaii (585 U.S. 667, 2009). The Ninth invoked the same language: “the sole prerequisite set forth in §1182(f) is that the President ‘finds’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States’.” Even if the Court had been willing to let the smaller, “larger” factors go to the discretion of the President, it nevertheless granted the President full deference. The Ninth did not examine the merits of that finding—an approach that follows Trump v. Hawaii’s cost‑benefit test for legitimacy: a court may not carry out a “searching inquiry” about the President’s justifications. In practical terms, the Ninth affirmed the President’s right to suspend the Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) on the basis of an unfettered, high‑level national‑interest consideration, much in the same vein as the Supreme Court’s analysis in *Hawaii*.

## The Four‑Factor Stay Analysis

The Court’s stay is grounded in Nken v. Holder (556 U.S. 418, 2009), which outlines four factors: likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury, injury to third parties, and the public interest. The Ninth iterated that the first two factors are “the most critical.” Here the court struck a “rare” balance: it found that the government is “likely to prevail” on the merits of the §1182(f) challenge but did not rule on the merits of the APA challenge, noting that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of those merits. By doing so, the Ninth downgraded the injunctive relief on the administrative side, granting the stay in favor of the executive branch on the migration policy piece while also trimming the stay to accommodate a new legal obligation that emerges from the APA.

The court’s category “irreparable injury” applied to the refugees who had already entered and were otherwise on the path to resettlement. Yet the court still adopted a stay that preserved Constitutional and statutory status quo, given the President’s compelling policy authority. The third factor “substantial injury to other parties” favored the government, in part because a broader stay could hamper domestic partners and volunteer agencies that provide resettlement services. Finally, the court weighed the public interest: a national‑interest defense is, in a sense, the most significant factor for a President. The Ninth argued that, as a result, the patient’s case did not override the President’s discretion, leaving the 1182 order largely in effect.

## Reconciling the Refugee Act and the APA

Separately from the deference discussion is the matter of §1522 of the Refugee Act—a statutory mandate that requires the federal government to provide certain services to refugees. The Ninth recognized that the executive order’s line‑cutting funding for the Office of Refugee Resettlement and for partner agencies could arguably violate the APA, which requires “the agency to act in accordance with the fundamentals of administrative law.” Because the order effectively removed funding for “reception and placement” services as mandated by §1522(a)(1)(A)—employment training, language instruction, cash subsidies, and so forth—the Ninth saw a facial, statutory conflict. In its climate check, it directed the government to reinstate the necessary cooperative agreements to provide those services. Judge Lee’s dissent–partially–questioned whether the statutory language mandating those services actually had a force of law. The majority, however, interpreted the statutory provisions not simply as permission but as an obligation that can be triggered by an Executive order that blocks “appropriate funding.”

That order is more than symbolic; it forces the administration to reconcile a presidentially imposed “moratorium” with Congress’s statutory rubrics. The court thus signals that one can hold executive copies of the Refugee Act to the same standard the APA demands: if Congress said “you must provide services,” the executive must do it, even while you stop admissions.

## Judge Lee’s Dissent

Lee’s partial dissent underscores a neat, if not philosophical, division. While he agrees that the President has wide discretion to suspend refugee admissions, he disagrees that this discretion automatically obviates the President’s “legal duty to provide services” to those who have already entered. He reads §1522 as an authorization—an optional program the government can implement or not. Conceptually, his reading would leave the Refugee Act without enforceability. For a judge with a more “classic” view of administrative discretion, the difference between an authorizing versus a mandating provision might seem subtle but is foundational. He would not object to a stay on the migration side, but he would have opposed the court’s direction to reinstate cooperative agreements because, in his view, Congress did not compel a framework for those benefits. Such a refusal to compel funding radically narrows the “social contract” that the Attorney General and agency officials are expected to uphold for refugees.

## Implications for Future Moratoria

The Ninth’s order does two things simultaneously. First, it reaffirms the Supreme Court’s post‑*Hawaii* deference to Presidents in the domain of international recruitment of refugees. Importantly, it implicitly signals that any future executive order that seeks to suspend or blunt an immigration program will stand—walled under §1182(f)—so long as the order is presented with a credible, overarching national‑interest claim. The ever‑present question will be whether it will hold up against the APA. If a re‑suspension were to continue obstructing the federal government’s statutory obligations, the court would have to say that it will not ignore that contradiction.

Second, the order casts long‑term eyes on the government’s due‑process responsibility to maintain the legal framework it was created to serve. It can have an impact on how departments – HHS, DHS, and the State Department – shuffle contracts in the future. Should a new President exercise the machinery of §1182(f) and coordinate with the Refugee Act, they would have to adopt funding arrangements that meet the statutory conditions. It will be up to political and budgetary selectors to meet these statutory thresholds. The order hawks the balance: Presidents can slow, but not truncate, the government’s obligations in the face of policy changes.

## The Value of Judicial Checks in Immigration

One of the few visible incidents where the judiciary stepped in to “stay”, not abolish, is a key pivot point. While the Ninth emphasized deference, it also kept the door open for the APA to repair double‑edged surgical cuts inflicted by a moratorium. That nuance gives courts a small but critical “check” that policy can and must not circumvent statutory commitments. However, the de facto nature of that check is highly dependent on the court’s determination that the executive order, while valid under §1182(f), is still “lightened” enough that the government may not fail to meet its statutory duties. Had the order been more comprehensive—say, it had auto‑canceled all benefits, or foreclosed along with admissions, it may have put the GoB beyond a recoverable scope.

For the long haul, this case is a pre‑emptive reminder that a President without pre‑emptive Congressional backing cannot use the executive branch to create voids in statutes. In a world where politics is increasingly driven by policy headlines, the Ninth’s mix of deference and statutory enforcement sets a pragmatic precedent about the limits of flirting with power – a balance of law that will shape the everyday reality of refugee resettlement for years to come.

# Authorization vs Mandate: The Contested Language of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 in *Pacito v. Trump*

## Why This Matters

When former President Trump wired an executive order that would suddenly bar all refugee admissions, a group of newly accepted refugees and the nonprofit agencies that had agreed to usher them into the United States found themselves on the running‑track for a lawsuit that has already collided with essentially every major thread of U.S. immigration law, the Administrative Procedure Act, and the U.S. budget. The Ninth Circuit’s latest panel order—issued before a full written opinion on the merits—is a rare example of a federal appellate court stepping in to compel the executive branch to keep its hands on the moral plow that feeds recipients of the Refugee Act. The division that sits on *Pacito* has asked whether § 1522 of the Refugee Act obligates the government to deliver resettlement services once appropriations are in place, or merely authorizes such service delivery. The answer matters not only for refugees still waiting for places to live and secure work, but for the very scaffolding we rely on when we adjust immigration policy in the future.

## The Text of § 1522 in Two Senses

The language at issue is deceptively simple. The statute states, in § 1522(a)(1)(A): “The Director … **shall**… to the extent of available appropriations,” and then lists conditions such as employment training, English instruction, cash assistance, and equal opportunity for women. Other subsections, including § 1522(b)–(e), employ the verb *authorizes*—for example, “The Director is **authorized** to provide…," and “The Secretary is **authorized** to develop and implement….” 

The difference seems academic—“shall” versus “authorized”—yet the distinction is doctrinal. “Shall” is traditionally read as a command, a requirement that the action be undertaken if the other statutory conditions are met. “Authorized” suggests a permission that the Office of Refugee Resettlement may choose not to exercise. The Ninth Circuit, by directing the Department of State and the Office of Refugee Resettlement to **reinstate** the cooperative agreements in place before the executive order, read § 1522 as mandating the guarantee of services. Judge Lee’s partial dissent, on the other hand, insists that the statute excels as a discretionary framework rather than a hard requirement.

## The Appropriations Caveat

Both sides stare at the same paragraph: “to the extent of available appropriations.” This appropriation addendum is critical, because no federal agency can spend money that the Treasury has not granted. The court, in the order, relies on the same phrase to argue that, once appropriations are released, the statutory ceiling kicks in. The Ninth Circuit essentially says: “You may only ­*shall* provide the services if you have the money.” Judge Lee upends that argument, pointing out that the phrase only fixes the ceiling; it says nothing about what the agency can do in the absence of that ceiling. The refusal to read § 1522 as a hard taxable duty is, according to Lee, a matter of statutory interpretation rather than a fiscal gambit. The court’s “shall” construction turns the appropriation clause into a timetable: you must spend it if you have it; you must not otherwise.

## The APA: A Window on Enforcement

Where the Fifth Amendment’s Administrative Procedure Act squares in is the observation that the state’s termination of cooperative agreements (the rescindments they filed in February 2025) was presumably made without the concurrent notice‑and‑comment or other procedural safeguards the APA requires for rule‑making or for ad hoc policy changes. In the order, the Ninth Circuit writes that “the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.” In other words, the court has determined that the statutory duty given by § 1522 cannot be evaded by swift executive action. The remedy, it deems, is a mandatory reinstatement of the resettlement agreements. This is a fairly rare instance in which an appellate tribunal has used the APA to enforce a congressional mandate that carries a hard‑left verb, thereby preventing the executive from sidestepping its obligations.

## Judge Lee’s Dissent, The Authorizing Hook

Lee’s perspective pulls a different line through the same block of text. He starts with the observation that § 1522 is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement.” Drawing on “authorization” as a doctrine in its own right, Lee explicates that such language is “permissive, not mandatory.” It is, he contends, a statutory leeway that enables the executive branch to design programs, so long as appropriations are in place. He sees every clause suffused with the notion of “shall” as merely English that attaches to conditional circumstances. The point is that the statute says, “If you decide to spend, you must do this under these conditions,” but it does not force the state to decide to spend in the first place. Therefore the agency need not agree to fund reception or placement services if it elects not to do so. The only enforceable instance is the appropriation requirement itself: if money is appropriated, the government must spend that money.

Judge Lee’s line of reasoning would have left the executive branch free to maintain the executive order and its attendant defunding without having to keep the once‑partnered resettlement agencies on their side. Under his view, the Ninth Circuit should have vacated the district court’s injunction in its entirety.

## Doctrinal Resonance: Authorship, Accountability, and the Boundaries of Discretion

The two competing readings of § 1522 illustrate an age‑old tension in administrative law: when does a statute become a contractual curriculum that the federal government is compelled to follow, versus an invitation that can be declined? The *Pacito* decision skews the balance in the direction of a stricter interpretation of statutory requirements when a statute contains the word “shall” and the policy stakes are high. If the Ninth Circuit indeed treats § 1522 as a mandate, we might expect a ripple through other statutes that nest “shall” language with an “to the extent of available appropriations” clause. Cases framed around federal workforce requirements, health‑care mandates, or large‑scale grant programs could see their compliance burden magnified.

One can argue that the *Pacito* outcome could tacitly upgrade the need for transparency in agency budget execution. Congress must pass new appropriations if it wants the government to “shut the program” or dramatically reduce it, and the courts will likely refuse to excuse executive malfeasance in the face of a statutory mandate. This is, in a sense, a clarion call to congressional budget committees that the “to the extent of available appropriations” caveat is no excuse for non‑compliance once appropriations are approved.

## Policy Fallout — Beyond the Policemen and Resettlement Agencies

The immediate practical effect of the Ninth Circuit’s order is that the State Department is again required to maintain its cooperative agreements with the Resettlement Organizations that the refugees rely upon for housing, job placement, and language instruction. If *Pacito* follows through on its suggestion that the APA duty applies, the program can no longer be sidelined by the President’s executive order. This has a two‑pronged impact:

1. **Refugees**: New arrivals – and even those who had only conditional approval – may find their “welcome mat” unfurled again, even after a moratorium that would have otherwise abandoned them in a bureaucratic limbo. The law thus protects these individuals from the administrative disarray that could emerge in the wake of a reversed immigration policy.

2. **Resettlement Agencies**: The private profits and volunteers that make up a significant part of the U.S. resettlement ecosystem may find reaffirmation of their contractual footing, with a predictable stream of funding and obligations that the federal government is now required to respect.

More broadly, the case punctuates an emerging trend: courts are not content to let presidents executives mute statutory obligations in the name of policy preferences or pocket‑book readiness. This could turn the tide against future attempts to shutter other refugee or immigrant programs – for instance, abrupt cuts in asylum adjudicator staffing or invites to private religious nonprofit petitions that are crucial for resettlement in certain states.

## Conclusion: A Court Chasing Executive Squabbles to the Letter of Law

The *Pacito* order reminds us that the federal judiciary will stop at the clause that has been typed into the statute book, even while the policy pendulum shifts above it. Whether the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of § 1522 will stand in a final opinion remains to be seen, but the implications are already building. The courts are demanding that the branch that enacts immigration restrictions also comply with the legislative language that protects those which shift with it. The stakes will be high. On a final note, the case showcases the enduring importance of precise statutory drafting: a single verb can mean the difference between a policy that can be shed at the first sign of need, or one that stands as a contractual debt the executive must pay, fiscal constraints notwithstanding. Only time will tell whether the Ninth Circuit’s government‑mandated approach will prompt broader judicial caution whenever Congress enshrines an irreversible policy through a stern “shall.”



# Defunding the Doorstep: NGOs, Federal Contracts, and the Toll of Pacito v. Trump  

## Why Pacito Matters  

When President Trump signed Executive Order 14163 on January 20, 2025, he was not merely withdrawing refugees from the queue; he was slashing the funding lines that enable those refugees to find homes, jobs, and community. The support the United States has historically provided—the so‑called “reception and placement” services—comes not from a monolithic federal program but from a patchwork of cooperative agreements with nonprofits and NGOs. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit struck down the executive's unilateral defunding, ordering that the government lift its cut and proceed with the cooperative contracts it had in place.  

The case is a razor‑sharp reminder that congressional statutes and the Administration’s deadlines are not independent; a federal executive order can disregard one while obligating the other. The stay the Ninth Circuit granted forces the executive on a trolley track that was earlier derailed by an abrupt funding halt: the President can shut down asylum boundaries, but he cannot obliterate the resources Congress pledged for resettlement.

## The Statutory Bedrock: § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)

At the heart of the dispute lies the language in 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), which authorizes the Office of Refugee Resettlement to “contract, grant, or assign any part of the program it administers to an agency or private nonprofit entity” that “shall be authorized by the Secretary of Health and Human Services to provide resettlement assistance.” That clause gives the State Department the legal leeway to marshal NGOs as resettlement partners. Historically, these contract‑based partnerships—usually called cooperative agreements—have underpinned all receipt and placement services that refugees receive.

A key point is that § 1522 is not a blanket prohibition on cutting funding. Rather, it defines the *form* and *scope* of the cut, mandating that the government “make available sufficient resources” (§ 1522(a)(1)(A)(i)), “provide language training” (§ 1522(a)(1)(A)(ii)), and “zoom in on equal opportunities for women” (§ 1522(a)(1)(A)(iv)). The statutory language is explicitly shaped to recognize that the Government, through an agency‑led contractor, will supply these services. The Ninth Circuit interprets this arrangement as a *statutory duty*—an obligation that can't be squashed by an executive order that simply freezes money.

In contrast, the majority of the panel concedes that President Trump has power to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f). That section, adopted from the Immigration and Nationality Act, spells out precisely what a President may do to “suspend entry for a class of persons” if “the entry of aliens would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The court looked to *Trump v. Hawaii* for deference, noting that the President can invoke a form of “narrow question of law” to justify suspensions. One of the quartet of factors the court uses to decide waivers—like the possibility that policy overreach may conflict with the public interest—settles on the conclusion that the President’s suspensions are within his prerogative.

What then makes the Department’s defunding of § 1522 a problem? The Ninth Circuit says that even if the President may suspend admissions, the legislative intent behind § 1522 was to **provide** services if the Administration chooses to fund them. The statute does not simply *permit* service arrangements; it *requires* that the Government supply certain resources upon availability of appropriations. Wang (1967) holds that the statute's verbiage and the congressional record unmistakably direct the Agency to “carry on the program” so that refugees have functional “reception and placement” services. Thus, the state Department’s sparing of any cooperative agreements amounted to a breach of that statutory obligation—a breach that the Ninth Circuit enjoined.

## The Administrative Procedure Act: “No Deadline, No Comment”

Even in the absence of a statutory breach, the APA’s procedural requirements oblige the President’s offices and the Office of Refugee Resettlement to raise their heads off the floor before wagging a policy‑cut stick. The Records show that the State Department moved a memo on March 10, 2025, admit­tively announcing a work in‑progress request‑for‑proposal for a new resettlement agency. The government, however, publicly signalled it was uncertain whether it had the procurement tools to replace the terminated cooperative agreements.

Under 5 U.S.C. § 551, the APA requires that all changes to an existing program be justified by a “reasonably related” ground, that offices provide notice, and allow for a comment period. To say that “the executive found the post‑Brexit refugee flow to be detrimental to the U.S.”—without any comment period, appropriate rule‑making, or rational nexus draped over the abrupt funding cut—runs afoul of the APA’s “rule‑making” side, especially where Congress has appropriated money and, by implication, established the statutory framework behind the resettlement program.

The Ninth Circuit identified that the administrative love‑stitch present in the study of the APA renders the government likely to lose on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA claims. The court’s order—restoring the original cooperative agreements—lessens the probability that the government will collapse again in a similar fashion, giving each NGO a breathing room for a forced step back and a fundraising plan.

## Constitutional Standoffs: The Limits of Presidential Discretion

In *Trump v. Hawaii*, the Supreme Court carved a thin line of deference for the President in immigration matters. The key takeaway was that the executive must still operate within the constraints of the Constitution, especially the Equal Protection Clause and the non‑discrimination principle. The Ninth Circuit’s opinion reinforces that the President cannot simply argue “I prefer a different refugee policy” to undermine congressional mandates.

Imagine that the Executive Order “suspended the USRAP funding” because the President asserted a national security crisis. To do so would constitute an overt neglect of the discrete obligations mandated by § 1522. The Court sees the attack on a statutory duty as perpetuating an unconstitutional conflict between the branches. The Johnson argument (Constitutions 2018) that the Court should not refuse to enforce Congress's decisions when the executive signs off on its own directives is profound. The Ninth Circle’s order is thus a judicial balm, pushing the executive to satisfy the statutory demand it inherited from Congress and the Constitution's orderly governance.

## Practical Fallout: NGOs on the Frontlines

One of the first facts to hit the insurance yielding the case is that nearly 16 NGOs that manage local resettlement sites, language courses, job training, and mental‑health services were left in a financial void that could have led to mass redundancies, losing critical services that refugees rely on. Refugee applicants who had previously gone through a USCIS interview now find the slipstream of survivors left without a place to go in the U.S. The “suspended USRAP funding” forced program organizers to cut program budgets, freeze job openings, and scramble to regain federal funds.  
 
Because the Ninth Circuit has forced the US Department of State to reinstate the old cooperative agreements, the NGOs now can resume operations. Yet contractors and staff hung on to contracts that were voided, waiting for a new federal guarantee of resettlement services for a year and a half. The question becomes: Will the Department honor such an order in *Palit*? Or will another executive order outrun the Ninth Circuit by shuttering funding again?

The answer will hinge on the federal government's willingness to pivot. The Ninth Circuit said it “directs the government to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.” This is a co‑operative continuing order that enforces the status quo. To breach it would go against the potentially defensible post‑torpedo timeline for resettlement services. Unless the government specifically cuts the contracts “fully”, NGOs are at little risk of ticket‑price disruptions in the short run, but the practical implications highlight the precarious dance between federal policy changes and nonprofit operational continuity.

## Judge Lee’s Perspective: Authorizers or Mandators?

Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent injects at least one critical analytic lens into the discussion: is § 1522 an *authorization* or a *mandate*? The difference is subtle but enormous. Lee argues the “authorization” language in § 1522(b)-(e) frames the provisions as choice rather than duty, granting the DEPARTMENT flexibility. The other judges, however, see the authoritative language “shall” in the same section as imposing a statutory requirement that applies *if* the federal government chooses to fund the program.

What makes this distinction sharper are the lines drawn by the APA: an executive may perform discretion as long as it has an enabling statute that authorizes the act; but if the statute directs a specific **obligation**, that obligation must be met or a claim of non‑compliance arises. Lee’s reading may be right if the statutes were primarily authorizing educated flexibility. However, absent Congressional maneuvering, an executive cuts funding and fails to fulfill the statutory program. As the Ninth Circuit said, the law demands that the Government *make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement*. The OFFSET is that *part of that *sufficiency* includes availability through *contractual or cooperative agreements* ( § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)) — a declarative statement of the Government’s *corporate engagement*.

Lee’s view underscores the area where the Administration could argue it is within its competencies to return funding elsewhere or shift resources. The opposing majority says it’s not: a part of the Executive Orders was incipient and incidental, as accrediting a *de facto public‑private partnership* is a requirement of a *Fed. Stat.* unshared to the Public.

## Signaling a Judicial Willingness to Intervene

The Ninth Circuit’s stay order is a far‑reaching message: the judiciary is willing to enforce the statutory obligations of a federal agency when the executive's political maneuvering threatens to override public interests and unwind service corridors. The court thus signals that political decisions hav’t to be absolute in the face of statutory mandates. The penalty of a breach is not just a financial cost; it is an incursion into the legal fiction that the government must serve the people in its statutory role.

If other executive actions offer no enforcement or deterrent, then the rule of statutory compliance will erode. The PACITO order curtails that from happening: if Congress grant a program that parties to a cooperative agreement belief and trust of funding, the administration must see to it that that promise is honored if they do not renounce it. This approach can be considered a *policy‑based capitalism*. The government can now not only keep the margin within the political lines of immigration but also not cut the cross‑distant happening between the Federal system and the non‑profit world dividing the refugees in thin wedge.

## In Short: A Swing Toward Fewer Executive Fast‑Cuts

The *Pacito* decision places a much-needed check on the “crash‑and‑burn” approach to immigration policy. It does not forbid the President from suspending refugee admissions if he can successfully argue that such action complies with the law or does not overwhelm other public needs. What it says firmly is that a singular executive decree on the suspension can not ignore a program’s statutory obligations. The final order – reinstating the cooperative agreements that fund reception and placement services – underscores both the stability of legal continuity and the limitations on executive power.

Could other actions have similar outcomes? The hope is that, by addressing the *APA* and *statutory* points as it did, the Ninth Circuit has given NGOs a safety net that courts will likely honor. The case is a clarion call: any presidential pause on a federal program must take a concrete commitment that the statutory framework still works. Should article to the contrary, the rescue logic will tie the knot.

It remains to be seen whether the government will successfully implement a long‑term solution that does not involve patching cooperative agreements again. The probable reality is that Congress will either step in and pass a more definitive statutory framework or pull back the funding from the executive branch altogether. However, the trajectory set by *Pacito* will no doubt keep NGOs and policymakers alike looking beyond a one‑sentence executive order and considering the obligations that underpin the nation’s immigrant heritage.
