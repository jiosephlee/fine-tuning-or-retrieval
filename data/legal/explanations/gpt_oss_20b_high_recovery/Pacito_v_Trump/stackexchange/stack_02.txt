Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### "Is Congress imposing mandatory refugee resettlement standards through §1522(a)(1)(A), or merely giving the Secretary permission?"
Question:
"In Pacito Judge Lee argues that sections copyrighted ’Authorization’ indicate discretion while subsection (a)(1)(A)’s word "shall" simply starts a conditional requirement triggered only if appropriate funds exist.

That feels contradictory—a bill written ’shall provision,’ yet arguments say agency discretion determines whether programs get funded.

How do courts decide whether wording implies obligation versus permissibility?

What tests assess whether Congress intended us obligation even when resources are limited?

And does such analysis affect litigation over cuts made against USRAP financing?
"
Answer:
**Short answer**

In *Pacito*, Judge Lee read 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A) as an **authorization conditioned on funding**, not as an unconditional mandate that HHS must deliver those services for every refugee who enters the United States. The ordinary‑meaning test applied above all else shows “shall” governs only those situations where funds exist – the same role it plays in most congressional appropriation statutes that begin “to the extent of available appropriations.”  

Because § 1522 merely sets out standards *if* refugee‑services programs are funded, it does **not** give plaintiffs a basis for alleging an unconstitutional abandonment of those services when Congress cuts appropriations. Consequently, litigation over USRAP ‘cuts’ hinges on whether other statutes impose enforceable duties (e.g., an accountability report required under § 1523) rather than on § 1522 alone.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### How courts decide whether “shall” creates an obligation

| Step | What courts look for | Example from § 1522(a)(1)(A) |
|------|---------------------|------------------------------|
|1 | **Plain‑text meaning** – Does “shall” normally mean an imperative duty? | Yes – “the Director shall … make available … sufficient resources.” |
|2 | **Contextual qualifiers** – Are there restrictions that limit when or how the duty attaches? | The clause continues “…to the extent of available appropriations … provide … services …”. |
|3 | **Structure of sentence** – Does necessity arise only if some prior condition is met? | The sentence refers back to “in providing assistance under this section,” i.e., after HHS has chosen to fund refugee‑programs via §§ b‑e. |
|4 | **Title / labeling** – Is this section labeled “authorization” versus “mandate”? | The U.S.C.’s title for § 1522 reads *“Authorization for programs…”*. |
|5 | **Circumstantial support** – What has Congress said elsewhere about intent? | The statute repeatedly uses “authorized” in §§ b‑e and does not require funding in each case (see Lincon’s fiscal authority doctrine). |

The combination shows *“shall” applies only when HHS elects to provide services – and even then only while there are appropriated funds*. This matches how appropriation statutes treat contingent obligations: they para‑dict not just what agencies do in principle but what they can do given actual dollars.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Tests used by courts

1. **Textual/vocabulary test**  
   Courts use ordinary‑meaning rules modified by strong qualifiers (*“to the extent…”, “as authorized…”*). See _Brown v Waterman_ (distinguishing legislative intent).

2. **Contextual/structural test**  
   Look at clauses just before/after—does a preceding recontextualise “shall”? In § 1522(a)(1)(A) the preceding phrase binds it as one more requirement among many discretionary ones (§ b‑e).

3. **Title / purpose test**  
   A section titled “Authorization” signals permissiveness unless contrary textual clues appear (_Environmental Protection Act_ used similar logic).

4. **Appropriation‑oriented doctrine**  
   Where funding predicates action (cf. _United States ex rel Snyder_), any duty arises only while federal dollars are available.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Application in *Pacito*

Judge Lee emphasized all these points:

> – “The title ‘Authorization…’ indicates permission rather than compulsion.”  
> – “‘Shall’ appears only within ‘in providing assistance under this section,’ so it merely sets conditions you must meet when you decide to fund refugee services.”  
> – He noted that if no funds exist then there is simply nothing HHS must do—the statute provides *no*!  
>   
> This reasoning kept his opinion focused on whether HHS had already chosen—or could still choose—to fund these programs, rather than on a perpetual legal responsibility tied solely to refugee status.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Does this affect litigation over USRAP cuts?

Yes—here’s why:

| Claim type | Viability under § 1522 alone |
|------------|------------------------------|
| Allegation that cutting federal appropriations violated an enforceable compulsory statute | Weak → Courts would see no standing because § 1522 merely licenses discretionary action contingent on dollars |
| Allegation that terminating cooperative agreements violated agency policy/program requirements set out elsewhere | Strong → If another statute or APA regulation mandates those agreements, courts can hold HHS liable regardless of appropriation level |

In short, plaintiffs cannot rely solely on § 1522(a)(1)(A) to argue that refugees owed benefits even after Congress cut funding; they must identify another clear statutory obligation or regulatory procedural violation.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

Congress used § 1522(a)(1)(A) as a **conditional authorization**, giving HHS standards it can—or cannot—apply depending on available appropriations and its own programmatic choices. Courts look first at plain text then at contextual qualifiers and purpose—none here point at a compulsory duty independent of money.

Therefore:

* For plaintiffs challenging USRAP cuts based purely on this provision, remedies are unlikely because there is no enforced mandate outside the budgetary limits described by Congress.
* If plaintiffs seek relief based on other statutes (e.g., mandatory reporting duties or APA procedures), those arguments survive independently of Congressional intent in § 1522(a).
