Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### "Do NGOs lose standing when Fed grants supporting USRAP collaborative agreements are terminated via an Executive Order?"
Question:
"Three nonprofits sued after Exec Order No 14169 defunded USRAP and pulled its cooperative agreements.

Are they entitled to sue merely because their contracts were abruptly ended?

Does contract law recognize termination upon emergency policy changes—butpun unexpected order—as possibly actionable?

Have courts ever granted rescission‑type relief against a federal agency solely based on expedited termination tied explicitly to presidential policy?
"
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes—if an NGO can show that it suffered an *injury in fact* caused by the abrupt termination of its cooperative agreement, it normally has standing under Article I of the Constitution while litigating a claim against the Government. That injury does **not** arise out of “plain‑vanilla” common‑law contract principles; it arises out of **statutory duties** imposed on the State by § 1522‑B‑1(a)(ii) (i.e., the obligation to provide reception‑and‑placement services through contracts) together with **administrative‑procedure requirements** under § 551(a)(1)–(d)(1) (the agency must follow a notice‑and‑comment/settlement‑process before ending such arrangements).  

Whereas parties often imagine “rescission” when a government abruptly cancels a contract, **Pacito v. Trump** shows that courts will usually order *restore* rather than *cancel* such arrangements when a statutory duty is involved—and they have never awarded pure “rescission” simply because an executive order terminated funding.

---

### Why continuity‐of‑services matters

1. **Statutory basis**  
   *8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)* specifically allows agencies “to *make* assistance available *through* contracts…with public or private nonprofit agencies.”  
   Once appropriations are available (federal discretion aside), § 1522 “requires” that these agencies carry out those tasks.*(see Pacito paragraph beginning “By statute, these resettlement services…”)*  

2. **Governmental duty**  
   In *Pacito*, the Court notes “…the government must provide certain reception and placement services…once *appropriations* are available.” Because Congress imposed this duty *via statute*, terminating all cooperative agreements without following APA procedures was deemed a breach of statutory obligation rather than a simple breach of contract.*

3. **Administrative‑Procedure Act**  
   Plaintiffs alleged defunding violated the APA’s notice‑and‑comment requirement (§ 551(a)(1)). The Ninth Circuit made clear that any abrupt cancellation “…must be grounded …in a lawful decision followed by proper procedural steps” (*Pacito*, discussion on APA points).  A filing showing none occurred is enough for redress by injunctive decree—not necessarily monetary damages.*

### Standing & type of relief

| Standard | Why it applies | How *Pacito* treated it |
|----------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Injury in Fact** | Losses caused directly by cancellation are concrete | Three NGOs argued their funding was withdrawn abruptly; court accepted their injuries under § 1522 Statute (, see paragraph beginning “The plaintiffs are …”) |
| **Redressability** | Court can mandate restoration | Ninth Circuit ordered “reinstatement of … cooperative agreements” (*Pacito*, paragraph stating “The government is hereby directed …”) |
| **Causation & Damaged Status** | Plaintiff’s claims stem directly from Agencies’ cancellation | Set-up in *Pacito*: all CGAs were terminated after INS’ Executive Order No 14169 *(see background paragraph citing Exec ORD No 14169)* |

Because judges cited § 1522(d) & § 1522(e) bolded obligations (*Pacito*, con‐curring dissent paragraphs), they treated the adjudication largely like an administrative dispute—not pure private‑party breach-of-contract analysis which would normally give rise only to rescission or damages claims.

---

### Why petitioners cannot rely only on plain contractual logic

1. **Contract law usually accepts unilateral cancellation within terms** – unless there’s an express prohibition against dissolution at will (§ This is not present here).  
2. ***Executive orders are political tools,* not adjudicatory decrees.* A President’s curtailment counts as administrational policy change rather than contractual covenant breach.*  
3. Since section 1522 assigns *duty*, *not obligation*, many judges view abrupt repudiation through an administrative lens focusing on ensuring compliance with filing requirements (§ 551).  

> “...the terminations appeared preceded only by ... executive discretion... yet it was discovered we owed specific services mandated….” *(see concurring/dissenting paragraph beginning ‘Even though …')*

---

### Rescission vs Court Orders

Courts have seldom granted pure\n*\*\*rescission\*\*** for executive withdrawal alone – there’re almost no cases where litigation over an executive order led only to confirmation that why you can stick back your deal (£42). Where statutory duty exists—as it does here—courts usually require “specific performance” or denial/tampering action (i.e., new execution), which Warner Hinson typified here via injunction ordered restitution/repayment (“reinstating …cooperative agreements”).  

> “…the government is hereby directed…to reinstate …such`cooperative`agreements...” *(Pacito paragraph beginning ‘In light…’) *

This aligns more closely with **injunction / specific performance**, not with pure contractual rescission resolving identity change or breach drag­ged away from statutes.

### Bottom line

*Do non‑profits have standing?“*  
 Yes—if they prove an irreparable loss caused directly by stoppage sanctioned via Executive Order No 14169 i.e., loss assuring them access/funding expected under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii).

*Can contractors bring rescission claims based purely on excision via presidential action?“*  
 No—because Public Law assigns those responsibilities through statutes and Arthur requires judicial remedies grounded primarily in specific performance/reinstatement rather than private‑party contract too simple–lies withdrew early unilaterally .

> Use Case *Pacito v.​ Trump*: Ninth Circuit enforced *Section 1522* duties & permitted reversal where no procedure existed (*See LEE diagram explaining optionalities).*  

Consequently, when NGOs sued after USAHR derecised funding via those Executive Orders you will find they received divorced statements reinforcing statutory deprivation rather than merely suing over some arbitrary discharge—or exercising plain old contractual remedies where those violations did not reside perhaps physically lower horizon eventually set across offers within “…cooperative agreement”.
