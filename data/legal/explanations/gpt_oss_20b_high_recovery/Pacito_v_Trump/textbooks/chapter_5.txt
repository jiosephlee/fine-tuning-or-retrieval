Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 5: # Disposition, Holding, and Policy Implications

## Summary of the stay order and the vacated administrative stay of July 18  
In its expedited stay order, the Ninth Circuit vacated the administrative stay the court had entered on July 18, 2025, as well as its earlier stay of March 25, 2025 and the amendments made on April 21 and May 9. The panel then stayed in its entirety the district court’s preliminary injunctions issued February 28 and March 24, 2025, preserving the prohibitions on enforcement of the executive order’s sections and on the suspension of U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) funding. The stay thereby maintains the status quo imposed by the district court while allowing the case to proceed pending a final ruling on the merits.

## Legal determination that the government is likely to succeed on the merits of the Executive Order’s suspension of refugee admissions  
The court applied the four‑factor stay analysis from *Nken v. Holder*, placing the most weight on the likelihood of success on the merits and the irreparable injury to the government. It concluded that, under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the President’s finding that “entry into the United States of refugees…would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” meets the statutory requirement for suspension. The court emphasized that the Supreme Court has repeatedly held this requirement to be the sole test of a valid suspension and that the President’s discretion in this context is broad and well respected. Accordingly, the Ninth Circuit found that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of its challenge to the executive order that suspends refugee admissions.

## Unresolved outcome of the APA challenge concerning §1522 obligations  
While the court affirmed the government’s probable success on the suspension claim, it did not resolve the long‑standing question of whether § 1522 imposes a mandatory duty for the State Department (and related agencies) to provide reception and placement services to refugees. The order reflects the divide among the panel: one opinion insists that § 1522 is an authorization, not a mandate, whereas the majority interprets it as a statutory obligation tied to appropriations. The Court’s decision left the APA issue unresolved, noting that a final determination would require further analysis of § 1522’s statutory language and the government’s compliance with appropriations requirements.

## Court’s instruction to the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements for reception and placement services  
In the absence of a clear statutory requirement, the court directed the State Department to restore the cooperative agreements that had been terminated in February 2024. The directive mandated that the State Department bring these agreements back into force to provide the “usual and customary” reception and placement services that refugees had been receiving under the prior arrangements. By doing so, the court sought to ensure that any suspension of refugee admissions would not leave America in violation of statutory funding and service obligations set forth in § 1522.

## Implications for the Refugee Admissions Program, future executive orders, and the judiciary’s role in reviewing executive fiscal actions  
The Ninth Circuit’s order signals that presidential discretion over immigration policy will continue to enjoy strong deference, allowing the executive to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f) so long as it can justify the restriction as detrimental to the United States. At the same time, the court’s insistence that the State Department immediately reinstate cooperative agreements underscores that fiscal appropriations tied to immigration programs cannot be unilaterally withdrawn without judicial intervention. For future executive orders affecting the Refugee Admissions Program, administrations will likely need to provide clear evidence that they can meet statutory funding and service obligations or face court‑issued stays or directives. The order also reaffirms the judiciary’s willingness to supervise and enforce executive compliance with congressional spending mandates, especially in areas where statutory duties intersect with executive policy initiatives.
