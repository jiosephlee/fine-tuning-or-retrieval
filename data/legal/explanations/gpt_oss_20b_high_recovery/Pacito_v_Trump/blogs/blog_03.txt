Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Defunding the Doorstep: NGOs, Federal Contracts, and the Toll of Pacito v. Trump  

## Why Pacito Matters  

When President Trump signed Executive Order 14163 on January 20, 2025, he was not merely withdrawing refugees from the queue; he was slashing the funding lines that enable those refugees to find homes, jobs, and community. The support the United States has historically provided—the so‑called “reception and placement” services—comes not from a monolithic federal program but from a patchwork of cooperative agreements with nonprofits and NGOs. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit struck down the executive's unilateral defunding, ordering that the government lift its cut and proceed with the cooperative contracts it had in place.  

The case is a razor‑sharp reminder that congressional statutes and the Administration’s deadlines are not independent; a federal executive order can disregard one while obligating the other. The stay the Ninth Circuit granted forces the executive on a trolley track that was earlier derailed by an abrupt funding halt: the President can shut down asylum boundaries, but he cannot obliterate the resources Congress pledged for resettlement.

## The Statutory Bedrock: § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)

At the heart of the dispute lies the language in 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), which authorizes the Office of Refugee Resettlement to “contract, grant, or assign any part of the program it administers to an agency or private nonprofit entity” that “shall be authorized by the Secretary of Health and Human Services to provide resettlement assistance.” That clause gives the State Department the legal leeway to marshal NGOs as resettlement partners. Historically, these contract‑based partnerships—usually called cooperative agreements—have underpinned all receipt and placement services that refugees receive.

A key point is that § 1522 is not a blanket prohibition on cutting funding. Rather, it defines the *form* and *scope* of the cut, mandating that the government “make available sufficient resources” (§ 1522(a)(1)(A)(i)), “provide language training” (§ 1522(a)(1)(A)(ii)), and “zoom in on equal opportunities for women” (§ 1522(a)(1)(A)(iv)). The statutory language is explicitly shaped to recognize that the Government, through an agency‑led contractor, will supply these services. The Ninth Circuit interprets this arrangement as a *statutory duty*—an obligation that can't be squashed by an executive order that simply freezes money.

In contrast, the majority of the panel concedes that President Trump has power to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f). That section, adopted from the Immigration and Nationality Act, spells out precisely what a President may do to “suspend entry for a class of persons” if “the entry of aliens would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The court looked to *Trump v. Hawaii* for deference, noting that the President can invoke a form of “narrow question of law” to justify suspensions. One of the quartet of factors the court uses to decide waivers—like the possibility that policy overreach may conflict with the public interest—settles on the conclusion that the President’s suspensions are within his prerogative.

What then makes the Department’s defunding of § 1522 a problem? The Ninth Circuit says that even if the President may suspend admissions, the legislative intent behind § 1522 was to **provide** services if the Administration chooses to fund them. The statute does not simply *permit* service arrangements; it *requires* that the Government supply certain resources upon availability of appropriations. Wang (1967) holds that the statute's verbiage and the congressional record unmistakably direct the Agency to “carry on the program” so that refugees have functional “reception and placement” services. Thus, the state Department’s sparing of any cooperative agreements amounted to a breach of that statutory obligation—a breach that the Ninth Circuit enjoined.

## The Administrative Procedure Act: “No Deadline, No Comment”

Even in the absence of a statutory breach, the APA’s procedural requirements oblige the President’s offices and the Office of Refugee Resettlement to raise their heads off the floor before wagging a policy‑cut stick. The Records show that the State Department moved a memo on March 10, 2025, admit­tively announcing a work in‑progress request‑for‑proposal for a new resettlement agency. The government, however, publicly signalled it was uncertain whether it had the procurement tools to replace the terminated cooperative agreements.

Under 5 U.S.C. § 551, the APA requires that all changes to an existing program be justified by a “reasonably related” ground, that offices provide notice, and allow for a comment period. To say that “the executive found the post‑Brexit refugee flow to be detrimental to the U.S.”—without any comment period, appropriate rule‑making, or rational nexus draped over the abrupt funding cut—runs afoul of the APA’s “rule‑making” side, especially where Congress has appropriated money and, by implication, established the statutory framework behind the resettlement program.

The Ninth Circuit identified that the administrative love‑stitch present in the study of the APA renders the government likely to lose on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA claims. The court’s order—restoring the original cooperative agreements—lessens the probability that the government will collapse again in a similar fashion, giving each NGO a breathing room for a forced step back and a fundraising plan.

## Constitutional Standoffs: The Limits of Presidential Discretion

In *Trump v. Hawaii*, the Supreme Court carved a thin line of deference for the President in immigration matters. The key takeaway was that the executive must still operate within the constraints of the Constitution, especially the Equal Protection Clause and the non‑discrimination principle. The Ninth Circuit’s opinion reinforces that the President cannot simply argue “I prefer a different refugee policy” to undermine congressional mandates.

Imagine that the Executive Order “suspended the USRAP funding” because the President asserted a national security crisis. To do so would constitute an overt neglect of the discrete obligations mandated by § 1522. The Court sees the attack on a statutory duty as perpetuating an unconstitutional conflict between the branches. The Johnson argument (Constitutions 2018) that the Court should not refuse to enforce Congress's decisions when the executive signs off on its own directives is profound. The Ninth Circle’s order is thus a judicial balm, pushing the executive to satisfy the statutory demand it inherited from Congress and the Constitution's orderly governance.

## Practical Fallout: NGOs on the Frontlines

One of the first facts to hit the insurance yielding the case is that nearly 16 NGOs that manage local resettlement sites, language courses, job training, and mental‑health services were left in a financial void that could have led to mass redundancies, losing critical services that refugees rely on. Refugee applicants who had previously gone through a USCIS interview now find the slipstream of survivors left without a place to go in the U.S. The “suspended USRAP funding” forced program organizers to cut program budgets, freeze job openings, and scramble to regain federal funds.  
 
Because the Ninth Circuit has forced the US Department of State to reinstate the old cooperative agreements, the NGOs now can resume operations. Yet contractors and staff hung on to contracts that were voided, waiting for a new federal guarantee of resettlement services for a year and a half. The question becomes: Will the Department honor such an order in *Palit*? Or will another executive order outrun the Ninth Circuit by shuttering funding again?

The answer will hinge on the federal government's willingness to pivot. The Ninth Circuit said it “directs the government to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.” This is a co‑operative continuing order that enforces the status quo. To breach it would go against the potentially defensible post‑torpedo timeline for resettlement services. Unless the government specifically cuts the contracts “fully”, NGOs are at little risk of ticket‑price disruptions in the short run, but the practical implications highlight the precarious dance between federal policy changes and nonprofit operational continuity.

## Judge Lee’s Perspective: Authorizers or Mandators?

Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent injects at least one critical analytic lens into the discussion: is § 1522 an *authorization* or a *mandate*? The difference is subtle but enormous. Lee argues the “authorization” language in § 1522(b)-(e) frames the provisions as choice rather than duty, granting the DEPARTMENT flexibility. The other judges, however, see the authoritative language “shall” in the same section as imposing a statutory requirement that applies *if* the federal government chooses to fund the program.

What makes this distinction sharper are the lines drawn by the APA: an executive may perform discretion as long as it has an enabling statute that authorizes the act; but if the statute directs a specific **obligation**, that obligation must be met or a claim of non‑compliance arises. Lee’s reading may be right if the statutes were primarily authorizing educated flexibility. However, absent Congressional maneuvering, an executive cuts funding and fails to fulfill the statutory program. As the Ninth Circuit said, the law demands that the Government *make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement*. The OFFSET is that *part of that *sufficiency* includes availability through *contractual or cooperative agreements* ( § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)) — a declarative statement of the Government’s *corporate engagement*.

Lee’s view underscores the area where the Administration could argue it is within its competencies to return funding elsewhere or shift resources. The opposing majority says it’s not: a part of the Executive Orders was incipient and incidental, as accrediting a *de facto public‑private partnership* is a requirement of a *Fed. Stat.* unshared to the Public.

## Signaling a Judicial Willingness to Intervene

The Ninth Circuit’s stay order is a far‑reaching message: the judiciary is willing to enforce the statutory obligations of a federal agency when the executive's political maneuvering threatens to override public interests and unwind service corridors. The court thus signals that political decisions hav’t to be absolute in the face of statutory mandates. The penalty of a breach is not just a financial cost; it is an incursion into the legal fiction that the government must serve the people in its statutory role.

If other executive actions offer no enforcement or deterrent, then the rule of statutory compliance will erode. The PACITO order curtails that from happening: if Congress grant a program that parties to a cooperative agreement belief and trust of funding, the administration must see to it that that promise is honored if they do not renounce it. This approach can be considered a *policy‑based capitalism*. The government can now not only keep the margin within the political lines of immigration but also not cut the cross‑distant happening between the Federal system and the non‑profit world dividing the refugees in thin wedge.

## In Short: A Swing Toward Fewer Executive Fast‑Cuts

The *Pacito* decision places a much-needed check on the “crash‑and‑burn” approach to immigration policy. It does not forbid the President from suspending refugee admissions if he can successfully argue that such action complies with the law or does not overwhelm other public needs. What it says firmly is that a singular executive decree on the suspension can not ignore a program’s statutory obligations. The final order – reinstating the cooperative agreements that fund reception and placement services – underscores both the stability of legal continuity and the limitations on executive power.

Could other actions have similar outcomes? The hope is that, by addressing the *APA* and *statutory* points as it did, the Ninth Circuit has given NGOs a safety net that courts will likely honor. The case is a clarion call: any presidential pause on a federal program must take a concrete commitment that the statutory framework still works. Should article to the contrary, the rescue logic will tie the knot.

It remains to be seen whether the government will successfully implement a long‑term solution that does not involve patching cooperative agreements again. The probable reality is that Congress will either step in and pass a more definitive statutory framework or pull back the funding from the executive branch altogether. However, the trajectory set by *Pacito* will no doubt keep NGOs and policymakers alike looking beyond a one‑sentence executive order and considering the obligations that underpin the nation’s immigrant heritage.
