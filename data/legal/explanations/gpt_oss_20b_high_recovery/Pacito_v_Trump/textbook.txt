Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Procedural History and Context

The case now before the Ninth Circuit concerns President Trump’s suspension of the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) and the consequent defunding of domestic resettlement services. The procedural chain unfolded over a few months in early 2025, moving from executive orders through district‑court injunctions to the appellate court’s interim stay. Understanding this sequence clarifies how the court’s current order fits into the broader litigation.

## Executive Order 14163 (Realigning USRAP) and Order 14169 (Realigning Foreign Aid)

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” The order invoked 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and § 1185(a) to suspend USRAP on the grounds that admitting refugees would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Three days later, on January 24, 2025, the State Department issued Executive Order No. 14169, “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Under this new order the department suspended all USRAP funding, including payments to agencies that provide domestic reception and placement services for refugees. These executive actions set the factual backdrop for the civil action filed by refugees and resettlement‑agency plaintiffs.

## Plaintiffs: refugees and three resettlement organizations, and their claims under the Refugee Act and the APA

The suit was filed in the Western District of Washington in February 2025 by:
* Refugee plaintiffs who were either already admitted to the United States or had pending approvals for resettlement; and
* Three nonprofit resettlement organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to process refugee applications abroad and provide domestic services upon admission.

The plaintiffs alleged that Order 14163 violated the Refugee Act of 1980 (codified in § 1101 et seq. of the Immigration and Nationality Act). They also claimed that the successive defunding of USRAP services violated the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), specifically § 551, which requires fair and reasoned agency action. The Joint claim rests on the argument that the President’s suspension and the State Department’s funding cuts created an unconstitutional or procedurally deficient pause of the refugee program.

## District court preliminary injunctions (Feb. 25, 28; Mar. 24) and the administrative stay (July 18)

The district court heard the case expeditiously. On February 25, 2025, it entered a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement of the portions of Order 14163 that suspend USRAP and its related funding. Two days later, on February 26, the government terminated all cooperative agreements providing reception and placement services, and on February 28 the court clarified the scope of the injunction – specifically extending the prohibition to the suspension of USRAP funding itself.

A second preliminary injunction was issued on March 24, ordering the State Department to reinstate all terminated cooperative agreements except for one that remained in place. The government filed notices of appeal with the Ninth Circuit on February 28 and March 25. The issues raised in the appeal focused on the breadth of the injunction and the statutory authority of the executive orders.

On July 14, the district court issued an order creating a framework to enforce the limited carveout created by the Ninth Circuit’s March 25 stay, delegating oversight to a magistrate judge. The government’s emergency motion on July 17 sought clarification or amendment to that framework. In response, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay on July 18, suspending the district court’s enforcement order pending further judicial review.

## Notice of appeal, motions panel orders, and vacated stays with limited carveouts

The Ninth Circuit’s motions panel granted the plaintiff a partial stay on March 25, citing *Trump v. Hawaii* to justify deference to the President’s immigration authority. The panel confined the stay to individuals who were not conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20. This narrow scope was contested, so the panel issued supplemental orders on April 21 and May 9 to clarify the carveout and to adjust the earlier stay in light of new arguments presented during briefing.

When the panel’s original stay was vacated (the duo of stays from March, April, and May) the Ninth Circuit affirmed that the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions would remain in force, but the court’s own stay order (the one issued June  or July?) was simply terminated. This left the district court’s orders pending, subject to the appellate court’s usual interim stay procedures.

## Scope of the Ninth Circuit stay: vacated administrative stay, continuation of the district court orders, and the directive to reinstate cooperative agreements

In its current order, the Ninth Circuit vacated its own administrative stay issued July 18 and the preceding stays from March, April, and May. The court held a new stay pending appeal of the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions, but with a specific carveout: “Such services shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.” The court thereby required the State Department to restore the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services mandated by § 1522 of the Immigration and Nationality Act. All other provisos in the district court’s injunctions remain in effect until the Ninth Circuit issues a final decision on the merits.

In sum, the Ninth Circuit has held that the district court’s injunctions remain operative, but the executive branch must immediately reinstate critical cooperative agreements. This interim stay preserves the underlying injunction while allowing the disputed federal agencies to continue providing refugee services that Congress, through § 1522, has expressly authorized. The court’s order therefore stays—vacates the previous administrative stay and partially reverses earlier stays—while directing remedial action that aligns with statutory requirements and the appellate court’s deference to executive immigration authority. The merits of the case will be addressed in a forthcoming opinion.

# Statutory Framework: Presidential Discretion and the Refugee Act

## 8 U.S.C. § 1182 (f) and the President’s power to suspend refugee entry

Section 1182 (f) of the Immigration and Nationality Act is the statutory foundation for any presidential order suspending entry of a class of aliens. It reads: *“Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may, by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or non‑immigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate.”* The statute thus grants the President a unilateral, constitutionally sanctioned authority to suspend
entry when he believes national interest is threatened. The only requirement is that the President *find* the threat; no weight or standard for that finding is imposed by the statute.  

Section 1185(a) further authorizes the President, pursuant to the same “detrimental” finding, to execute the Refugee Program on a temporary basis. The statutory text communicates political judgment: the President volunteers to act when national security, foreign relations, or public welfare are at risk, and the law gives him the decisive tool—suspension of entry and restriction of refugee admissions.  

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit relied precisely on this language to assess the validity of Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program. The court found that the President’s commitment to “determine whether entry would be detrimental” was fulfilled, rendering the executive order within the bounds of § 1182 (f). The statute therefore underpins any argument that the suspension was a permissible exercise of executive power.  

## Deference doctrine as articulated in Trump v. Hawaii and its relevance to §1182 (f)

The Supreme Court’s ruling in *Trump v. Hawaii* crystallized the deference standard that applies to presidential actions in the domain of immigration. The Court held that the President’s findings under § 1182 (f) receive “wide deference” because they are grounded in broad policy judgments about national security, insurance, and foreign affairs. The Court further noted that the deference is not absolute; it applies as long as the President’s findings are *grounded in evidence*, *provide a rational policy rationale*, and are *not arbitrary or capricious*.  

In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit invoked *Hawaii* to reinforce that the court cannot question the President’s “find” of detriment. The court emphasized that the “sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182 (f) is that the President find[…] that the entry of the covered aliens would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Accordingly, the court concluded that it “cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” This deference framework is critical to understanding why the court found the administrative stay of the suspension vacatable and why it granted limited relief to refugees who were conditionally approved prior to the order.  

## Congressional intent behind the Refugee Act and its overarching commitment to refugees

The Refugee Act of 1980, codified in 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq., was enacted to harmonize the United States’ refugee policy with international humanitarian norms and to give the Administration a clear framework for resettlement. Congress’s intent was to establish a “general rule for the ongoing treatment of refugees and displaced persons,” making resettlement a regulatory and humanitarian priority. The Act conditions the preservation of the Refugee Admissions Program on the continued delivery of assistance services, the timely adjudication of claims, and cooperation with international agencies. 

Section 1101 authorizes the Secretary of State to convene a Refugee Coordination Council and provides for the formation of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR), a subagency of the Department of Health and Human Services. These statutory entities are tasked with the economic integration and welfare of refugees. The Refugee Act also creates substantive procedural standards—processing times, eligibility rules, and reception and placement requirements—underscoring that the government cannot simply halt the refugee program without confronting the statutory obligations that accompany it. The court in *Pacito* interpreted the refugee law as binding, not merely advisory, thereby forming the basis for the plaintiffs’ claim that the executive order violated the Act.  

## 8 U.S.C. § 1522: statutory duties for employment, training, cash aid, and responsibility for unaccompanied minors

Section 1522, the “Authorization for Programs for Domestic Resettlement of and Assistance to Refugees,” spells out the executive branch’s responsibilities once a refugee is admitted. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the ORR “to the extent of available appropriations” to make available resources for employment training, placement, and supportive services, to provide English‑language training, to ensure that cash assistance does not discourage self‑sufficiency, and to guarantee equal opportunities for women. Each mandate is framed in terms of funding availability; Congress expressly conditions the mandate on appropriations, reflecting the discretionary nature of the financial allocation.  

Other subsections target particular groups. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility—and to make necessary decisions—for unaccompanied minor refugees “when necessary.” Likewise, § 1522(e)(7)(A) compels the Secretary of Health and Human Services to design and implement interim projects offering medical, cash, and support services for refugees who have resettled for less than thirty‑six months. The statutory language uses *shall* in a manner that denotes required action *when the obligation is invoked*; it does not compel the creation of a program absent funding or a clear need.  

The interplay of these statutes creates a two‑tiered duty: Congress provides the framework and, by appropriating funds, motivates the executive branch to deliver specific services. However, the statutory architecture allows the executive branch discretion over how, when, and where services are provided. The *Pacito* court, for example, inferred that the refusal to fund cooperative agreements undermined these statutory duties and was thus subject to scrutiny under the Administrative Procedure Act.  

## Funding mechanisms through cooperative agreements and the role of the State Department

Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) authorizes the Secretary to contract, grant, or enter cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies for the delivery of reception and placement services. This statutory language has long provided a blueprint for the federal government’s partnership with civil‑society organizations to manage resettlement logistics, housing, language training, and employment. The State Department, through its Office of Refugee Resettlement, typically drafts such agreements; the governing agency must ensure compliance with federal statutes and with Congressional appropriations.  

The Executive Order in *Pacito* terminated all cooperative agreements provisionally, citing an administrative review; this action removed the statutory mechanisms for delivering the services mandated by § 1522. The court’s decision to reinstate the agreements, directed in Part (4) of the order, reflects the statutory requirement that the government, “to the extent of available appropriations,” provide these services. In the absence of the agreements, the defendants were effectively denied the statutory benefits. The court’s order therefore exemplifies how statutory frameworks predict remedial actions when governmental actions contravene statutory mandates.

Understanding these statutes is essential for reading *Pacito v. Trump*, because the court’s decision hinges on the balance between executive discretion and congressional mandates, the deference accorded to the President, and the statutory obligations attached to the Refugee Act and § 1522.

# The Standard for a Stay: Nken, APA, and Judicial Balancing  

In Pacito v. Trump, the Ninth Circuit, on an expedited agenda, exercised the curative function of a stay to preserve the status quo pending a full merits decision.  The court’s written order analyzes the stay by applying the four‑factor framework set out in *Nken v. Holder*, evaluates the President’s statutory authority under § 1182(f), and considers whether the plaintiffs’ arguments under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) against the refugee‑admissions shutdown and its funding cuts survive judicial scrutiny.  This chapter walks through those analytical pillars so that students can see how a federal appellate court balances the constitutional and statutory claims that arise when an executive order—and its collateral regulatory actions—impinge on a protected class.  

## The Nken v. Holder Four‑Factor Test for Stays  
* *Nken* established the four factual categories that a court must weigh:

1. **Likelihood of success on the merits.**  
   The applicant must convince the court that it will probably prevail when the case is finally decided.  The court must have “a strong showing” that the claims or defenses at issue survive the merits analysis.  ( *Nken*, 556 U.S. at 418–19).  

2. **Irreparable injury if the stay is denied.**  
   Even if the merits claim may eventually be decided in the applicant’s favor, the refusal to grant a stay must not inflict harm that cannot be remedied later.  The injury must be real, not speculative.  ( *Nken*, 556 U.S. at 432).  

3. **Detrimental impact on third parties.**  
   The decision must take into account the potential injury to parties without standing who would be hurt by the stay.  This balances the public‑interest calculus against the injunction’s effect on non‑claimants.  ( *Nken*, 556 U.S. at 434).  

4. **Public interest.**  
   Finally, the court must weigh the public interest in the modality of the litigants’ rights against the public interest in the status quo.  This factor is inherently balancing and can tilt the decision one way or the other.  ( *Nken*, 556 U.S. at 434).

The Ninth Circuit reiterates that “the first two factors are the most critical.”  ( *Nken*, 556 U.S. at 434).  The court applies these factors to the stay of a district‑court injunction that barred the enforcement of the President’s suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) and the funding cuts that accompanied it.  

## Evaluation of the Executive Order’s Validity under § 1182(f) and Likelihood of Government Success  
Under § 1182(f), the President may, by proclamation, “suspend the entry of any class of aliens into the United States, as the President finds detrimental to the interests of the United States” and “impose restrictions he may deem appropriate.”  The court points to the Supreme Court’s deference to the President in *Trump v. Hawaii* and its characterization of 1182(f) as an authority that “exudes deference … and vests the President with ample power to impose entry restrictions.”  ( *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. at 708).  

In the Pacito order, the Ninth Circuit notes that the President has “undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here” by issuing Executive Order No. 14163, which suspends all refugee admissions pending further review.  The court therefore concludes that the Executive Order is likely valid, so the plaintiff’s challenge on its face is unlikely to succeed.  Consequently, the first factor tilts strongly toward maintaining the preliminary injunction: the government is probably going to win should the case reach a merits determination.  

## Analysis of Irreparable Injury to Refugees and to the Receiving Agencies  
*Irreparable injury* is a classic concern for asylum and immigration plaintiffs, who often face an urgent need for protection that time‑based remedies cannot satisfy.  In the immediate context, the defendants—the refugees and the three cooperating resettlement agencies—suffered the following:

* **Refugees.** The preliminary injunction, carried forward by the district court, barred the enforcement of the Executive Order’s suspension of USRAP, which, if reinstated, would mean the refugees could no longer be admitted.  The court acknowledged an “irreparable hurt” to those who were “conditionally approved for refugee status before the suspension.”  The injunction’s limited carve‑out meant that some refugees would still be denied, catalyzing the “irreparable injury” analysis.  

* **Cooperative agencies.** The Department of State had terminated all agreements to provide reception and placement services. Those agreements had funded the logistical and support services a refugee needs to settle in the United States.  The agencies claim a loss of function and potential impairment of their liaison roles with federal authorities.  The court says the agencies “suffer injury … if services are withheld” but balances that against the government’s budgetary constraints.  

While the court emphasizes the “irreparable injury” factor as a positive for the plaintiffs, it also notes that the government’s statutory duties under § 1522—a matter the APA is inspecting—are uncertain. Thus, the injury factor does not decisively outweigh the likelihood of success.  

## Balancing Interests of the Government’s Fiscal Discretion and the Public Interest in Refugee Resettlement  
Public‑interest jurisprudence requires a pragmatic look at how a stay will affect non‑claimants and the country’s broader interests.  The Ninth Circuit weighs several elements:

1. **Fiscal discretion.** The court notes that the federal government has a broad discretion to structure refugee‑admissions budgets.  Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance yet also rescinded $800 million under the Rescissions Act of 2025.  Absent clear statutory or constitutional demand that those resources be dedicated to resettlement, the government may claim the purse‑strings logic that support the “injury to third parties” factor.  

2. **Public interest.** Refugee resettlement is a traditional foreign‑policy instrument and element of the U.S. moral obligation.  The court acknowledges that a moratorium on admissions “can substantially and objectively injure the interests of the United States” in the foreign‑policy arena.  Accordingly, the public‑interest case leans toward permitting the suspension’s enforcement, especially given the President’s vetting rationale under § 1182(f).  

3. **Practical impact on agencies.** The resettlement agencies are non‑governmental entities that rely on federal funds for services. The court recognizes their injury but also contemplates the potential knock‑on effect on the public if resources are reallocated to urgent humanitarian needs elsewhere.  

Overall, the balancing tips toward the government’s fiscal discretion yet does not outweigh the weight of the first two Nken factors, leading the court to stay the injunction.  

## The APA’s Role in Evaluating the Government’s Statutory Obligations under § 1522  
The plaintiffs brought an APA claim that the Executive Order’s funding cuts violated § 1522, the statutory framework for domestic resettlement activities. The court’s observation is that, “For reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow, however, the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.”  

The court does not elaborate the exact reasoning but outlines the relevant statutory apparatus for the analysis:

* **§ 1522(a)–(e).** These sections, codified under the Refugee Act, authorise “reception and placement services” but do not impose a mandatory obligation.  The statutory language is descriptive of “conditions and considerations” that the Director may implement if the program is funded.  
* **Statutory language regarding funding.** The statutory authority is exercised “to the extent of available appropriations.” The court sees that the funding for resettlement services is discretionary, hinging on appropriations that Congress annually determines.  
* **APA procedural requirements.** The government must act with reasonableness and transparency. The plaintiffs argued that the unilateral termination of agreements and the revocation of funding had been performed “without particular notice” or a hearing, violating APA procedural safeguards.  

The Ninth Circuit therefore owes a full opinion in which it will judiciously assess whether the government’s termination of cooperative agreements and cuts to funding contravene § 1522 and the APA.  Until that opinion arrives, the court issues the present stay, suspending only the preliminary injunctions that created the conflict and continuing to enforce the existing district‑court orders.  

In sum, Pacito teaches how a court reconciles the President’s broad statutory authority under § 1182(f) with the remedies available to claimants under the APA, all through the lens of the *Nken* four‑factor framework.  The balancing of government discretion, irreparable injury, and public interest often requires a nuanced, step‑by‑step analysis—an analytical scaffold that law students should master when examining future stay orders.

# Does §1522 Require Mandatory Services? Mandatory or Authorization?

The Ninth Circuit’s stay order in *Pacito v. Trump* gives the court an opportunity to clarify how 8 U.S.C. § 1522 should be understood. The panel—through its ruling that the district court was unjustly preventing the executive branch from implementing the President’s refugee‑policy—treats § 1522 as imposing a substantive duty on the government to furnish certain reception and placement services. Judge Lee, in his partial concurrence/partial dissent, argues the opposite: that § 1522 merely authorizes a program, does not enforce a mandatory obligation, and therefore the court cannot require the government to bind itself to provide those services. The chapter dissects the textual and conceptual differences that underlie these contrasting views, then considers the practical consequences for the court’s directive to reinstate cooperative agreements and for subsequent litigation involving federal duties toward refugees.

## Textual analysis of §1522(a)(1)(A) “shall” language versus “authorized” language in §1522(b)–(e)

Section 1522(a)(1)(A) is the most frequently cited provision when the court frames § 1522 as a statutory mandate. The language reads, in its entirety, that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement “shall” and “to the extent of available appropriations” make sufficient resources available for employment training, language training, cash assistance, and gender‑equal participation. Because the verb *shall* is present, the majority panel reads it as an unambiguous requirement. The accompanying qualifier—*to the extent of available appropriations*—does not negate the mandatory nature of the *shall*; rather, it simply limits the scope of the obligation to whatever resources are appropriated. The court therefore interprets § 1522(a)(1)(A) as a binding duty: whenever a refugee has been admitted into the United States and the government has the authority, through appropriation, to spend money, it must do so to provide the stated services.

In contrast, §§ 1522(b)–(e) are framed with the verb *authorize*. The statutes read, for example, that the Director is *authorized* to “provide assistance” in the form of employment training (b), to “provide cash assistance” (c), and to “provide medical services” (e). Notably, the word *shall* is absent from these subsections. The use of *authorize* signals that Congress merely permits the Department to act, but does not compel it. Judge Lee notes that the statutory title, “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees,” reinforces this interpretation: authorization confers permission, not obligation. Thus, the plurality of the panel accepts the *shall* in (a)(1)(A) as a mandate, while Judge Lee treats the *authorize* in (b)–(e) as granting discretionary power.

The court’s analysis hinges on the relative weight of the two verb choices. In the majority view, the presence of the *shall* verb in (a)(1)(A) creates an enforceable statutory obligation. The detractors—particularly Judge Lee—argue that the statutory scheme is balanced: the *shall* is coupled with the *to the extent of available appropriations* limitation, and that overall, the statute is an authorization. The technical distinction between *mandates* and *authorizations* thus becomes the fulcrum on which the court's next steps pivot.

## The distinction between mandatory funding and discretionary financing under the statute

The debate over *mandatory* versus *discretionary* financing under § 1522 draws attention to the interplay between appropriations law and statutory duty. Under § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director is required to “make available sufficient resources … to the extent of available appropriations.” The majority panel reads this as a binding requirement: if the appropriations exist, the government must provide those resources. The law treats the *shall* clause as a condition; the *to the extent of available appropriations* clause modifies, but does not eliminate, the obligation. Accordingly, the court holds that the government must act when it has appropriated authority.

Judge Lee counters that the same clause undercuts a strict regulatory demand. By framing the obligation in terms of *available appropriations*, the statute effectively ties the government’s duty to the political process of appropriations. Because Congress controls funding allocations and can withhold appropriations entirely, the statutory framework preserves a degree of discretion: if Congress did not appropriate funds for refugee services, the Director cannot be compelled to provide them. In light of this, Lee argues that the text does not force the executive branch to allocate resources beyond what authorities have accepted. Thus, the statutory text allows the executive to decide whether to use available funds for reception and placement, thereby making the program *discretionary* rather than mandatory.

The divergence over fund‑level discretion also informs the court’s assessment of the APA claim. The panel concluded that the executive had no legal duty to provide the services and is therefore unlikely to succeed on the plaintiffs’ APA challenge. Lee, however, maintains that the absence of an obligation in § 1522 suggests there is nothing for the court to enforce at all; there is no statutory duty unmet that could give rise to an APA claim.

## Judge Lee’s argument that §1522 is an authorization, not a mandate, based on the section’s title and language

Judge Lee’s concurring‑in‑part and dissenting‑in‑part opinion supplies a concise doctrinal framework. He opens by noting that the very title of § 1522—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—signals that Congress intended to *permit* certain activities, not *prescribe* them. The language of any legal title is not arbitrary; it serves as a primer for textual interpretation. Lee emphasizes that authorization, a term whose definition appears in Black’s Law Dictionary as a “permission,” signals a permissive direction rather than a compulsory directive.

Lee then systematically reviews the statutory language. He points out that the majority of the subsections in § 1522(b)–(e) use *authorize*, not *shall*. Here, “authorize” is a deferential signifier: it tells the executive that it can act within bounds but does not oblige action. The *shall* usage in § 1522(a)(1)(A) is confined to the fact that if the government has appropriations, it shall operate certain programs. Lee calls this a *statutory condition*, not a statutory mandate. He notes that the preceding clause—“In providing assistance under this section”—functions as a preamble that channels the *shall* to situations where the government elects to promulgate the service. In other words, the *shall* becomes operative only if the executive chooses to make funding available under the statute’s umbrella.

Furthermore, Judge Lee dissects the statutory provisions that empower the Director to make “necessary decisions” relative to unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)). He observes that while the section calls for the Director to “make necessary decisions,” it simultaneously introduces a qualification that such decisions are only required if “necessary.” The quotation he highlights demonstrates that the executive is being granted discretion rather than being bound by a duty.

In his conclusion, Judge Lee articulates that § 1522 is “an authorization” that does not compel the government to provide any specific services. Because the statute does not require the executive to provide services out of the available appropriations, the court cannot impose that obligation. Lee therefore dissents from the majority in part, notably on the enforcement of the order requiring the government to reinstate cooperative agreements.

## Implications of the statutory analysis for the court’s directive to reinstate cooperative agreements

The court’s directive that the government “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services” rests directly on the majority's reading of § 1522 as a mandatory duty. By interpreting the statutory *shall* language as a binding obligation, the panel finds that the executive branch is legally required to fulfill the programmatic commitments embodied in those cooperative agreements. The court therefore instructs that, in the absence of appropriate restoration, the government is violating the APA. This approach accords the provisional relief nature of a stay and directs the executive to comply with the statutory requirements.

Judge Lee’s alternative reading, however, would remove the foundation for such a directive. Since a pure authorization does not create a compensatory or punitive standard for the District Court to enforce, a stay to compel the administrative agency to enter into or restore cooperative agreements is unwarranted. If the court adhered to Lee’s reasoning, the order would collapse: the government would no longer be legally compelled to create or sustain the receiving agencies’ contracts. Consequently, the court’s instruction would be deemed mistaken.

For both parties, the distinction is material. The plaintiffs’ group of refugees and resettlement organizations had been deprived of statutory support. If the court’s reading that § 1522 mandates federal service provision is accepted, the plaintiffs’ relief spreads beyond mere injury to the executive branch; it obliges the government to meet an explicit statutory standard and therefore validates the plaintiffs’ claim under the APA. If, on the other hand, the court treats § 1522 as an authorization, the plaintiffs would lose that claim as a matter of law, and federal court would be unable to compel the executive to provide any specific services.

## Potential impact on future litigation over statutory duties of the government to refugees

Whether § 1522 imposes a statutory duty will reverberate far beyond the present case. A majority decision that treats § 1522 as a mandatory obligation strengthens the precedent that immigration statutes with *shall* language impose enforceable duties. Future litigants seeking to compel the federal government to fund or provide services under similar statutes might lean on *Pacito* as a reference point. Courts would, in such cases, be more inclined to analyze only the statutory text and the presence of *shall* clauses when assessing the enforceability of APA claims.

Conversely, should a court adopt Judge Lee’s framing—seeing § 1522 as an authorization—this would leave the door open to a more permissive view of immigration statutes. Plaintiffs would need to prove that the executive branch’s failure to act constitutes inequitable or unlawful conduct that falls under the APA’s construction, enforcement, or review guarantees; they could not rely on a statutory mandate per se. It would also potentially raise the bar for obtaining injunctive relief, thereby limiting the court’s ability to order the federal government to finance refugee resettlement programs. In sum, *Pacito* serves as a barometer: the court’s chosen interpretation will shape how future litigants argue whether the federal government can be compelled to fulfill its designated responsibilities toward refugees.

# Disposition, Holding, and Policy Implications

## Summary of the stay order and the vacated administrative stay of July 18  
In its expedited stay order, the Ninth Circuit vacated the administrative stay the court had entered on July 18, 2025, as well as its earlier stay of March 25, 2025 and the amendments made on April 21 and May 9. The panel then stayed in its entirety the district court’s preliminary injunctions issued February 28 and March 24, 2025, preserving the prohibitions on enforcement of the executive order’s sections and on the suspension of U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) funding. The stay thereby maintains the status quo imposed by the district court while allowing the case to proceed pending a final ruling on the merits.

## Legal determination that the government is likely to succeed on the merits of the Executive Order’s suspension of refugee admissions  
The court applied the four‑factor stay analysis from *Nken v. Holder*, placing the most weight on the likelihood of success on the merits and the irreparable injury to the government. It concluded that, under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the President’s finding that “entry into the United States of refugees…would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” meets the statutory requirement for suspension. The court emphasized that the Supreme Court has repeatedly held this requirement to be the sole test of a valid suspension and that the President’s discretion in this context is broad and well respected. Accordingly, the Ninth Circuit found that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of its challenge to the executive order that suspends refugee admissions.

## Unresolved outcome of the APA challenge concerning §1522 obligations  
While the court affirmed the government’s probable success on the suspension claim, it did not resolve the long‑standing question of whether § 1522 imposes a mandatory duty for the State Department (and related agencies) to provide reception and placement services to refugees. The order reflects the divide among the panel: one opinion insists that § 1522 is an authorization, not a mandate, whereas the majority interprets it as a statutory obligation tied to appropriations. The Court’s decision left the APA issue unresolved, noting that a final determination would require further analysis of § 1522’s statutory language and the government’s compliance with appropriations requirements.

## Court’s instruction to the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements for reception and placement services  
In the absence of a clear statutory requirement, the court directed the State Department to restore the cooperative agreements that had been terminated in February 2024. The directive mandated that the State Department bring these agreements back into force to provide the “usual and customary” reception and placement services that refugees had been receiving under the prior arrangements. By doing so, the court sought to ensure that any suspension of refugee admissions would not leave America in violation of statutory funding and service obligations set forth in § 1522.

## Implications for the Refugee Admissions Program, future executive orders, and the judiciary’s role in reviewing executive fiscal actions  
The Ninth Circuit’s order signals that presidential discretion over immigration policy will continue to enjoy strong deference, allowing the executive to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f) so long as it can justify the restriction as detrimental to the United States. At the same time, the court’s insistence that the State Department immediately reinstate cooperative agreements underscores that fiscal appropriations tied to immigration programs cannot be unilaterally withdrawn without judicial intervention. For future executive orders affecting the Refugee Admissions Program, administrations will likely need to provide clear evidence that they can meet statutory funding and service obligations or face court‑issued stays or directives. The order also reaffirms the judiciary’s willingness to supervise and enforce executive compliance with congressional spending mandates, especially in areas where statutory duties intersect with executive policy initiatives.
