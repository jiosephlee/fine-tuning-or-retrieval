Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How did the district court’s summary judgment on statutory compliance override the plaintiffs’ claims of breach of good faith and fair dealing?
Question:
The plaintiffs filed a class action alleging GoAuto did not act in good faith and respected the statutory cancellation procedures. The district court granted summary judgment on those statutory claims and dismissed all claims with prejudice. The appellate court affirmed, stating the district court did not err. How does the court justify that the lack of violation of the statutory procedure bars the plaintiffs’ broader claims of breach of contract or bad faith? Does the statute preclude claims of misrepresentation or unfair dealing if the statutory procedures were technically met?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The court held that the statutory notice requirement is a *definitive, no‑fault* standard governing cancellation.  
When the insurer meets every element of that notice requirement—as the district court and the appellate court found—there is no violation of the contract or of the statutory duty.  Because a breach of contract or a good‑faith claim must be founded on a statutory or contractual violation, the plaintiffs’ claims cannot survive.  The statute does not expressly forbid a misrepresentation or unfair‑dealing claim, but the opinion shows that once the statutory notice is satisfied the insurer’s conduct is deemed lawful and “good‑faith” under Louisiana law.  

---

### 1.  The statutory procedure is the governing test

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)‑(iv) sets out the *exact* facts that a finance company must certify on its notice of cancellation.  
In *Williams* the court explained that this notice “serves two functions”: (1) it fixes the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it guarantees a minimum cure period for the default.  Because the statute is clear that *strict adherence* is required, any alleged deviation can be found a contractual breach.  The court cited *Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993), and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990) in affirming that a failure to follow the statutory notice makes the cancellation “ineffective.”

The plaintiffs argued that no signature appeared on the notice and that GoAuto had not “received” the request before canceling.  The court treated those arguments separately:

* **No signature required.**  The text of § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) uses the word *certifying* but does not say “signed.”  The court contrasted earlier opinions (*Benitez v. Elsayed*, 285 So. 3d 572, 579) that noted the absence of a signature but declined to hold it fatal.  When the court said “The statute says *the insurance premium finance company* [s]er... by *certifying* certain information,” it was interpreting *certifying* as a statement, not a signed signature.  Therefore, the notice was compliant.

* **Receipt of the notice.**  The statute says “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation … the insurer shall consider that cancellation has been requested.”  The court applied the ordinary legal meaning of *receipt* (ownership or possession) and held that the computer‑generated “e‑mail” was an adequate receipt.  (See *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*, 546 So. 2d 898, 901‑02; *Britten v. Reavis*, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154.)  The court therefore concluded that GoAuto’s automatic, computer‑triggered cancellation was perfectly lawful.

Because the *statutory* standard was met, the court found no violation of the contract’s term allowing cancellation.

---

### 2.  A breach‑of‑contract or bad‑faith claim relies on a statutory violation

The plaintiffs’ claims of “breach of good faith and fair dealing” were apparently *predicated on* the alleged improper cancellation.  In Louisiana, a breach‑of‑contract claim (and an accompanying claim of bad faith) requires that the defendant’s conduct *contravene* a contractual obligation or a statutory duty.  

The appellate court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment on that basis alone:

> “Because GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute, the district court did not err in denying their motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.”  

By showing that *the statutory procedure was followed* the court effectively discharged the affirmative defense that the insurer had acted in breach of the policy.  The problem is not whether the insurer’s conduct was “nice” or “fair,” but whether it *violated the law that governs cancellation.*

---

### 3.  The statute does not preclude misrepresentation or unfair‑dealing claims, but it makes them impossible here

The opinion never expressly says the statute *precludes* a claim of misrepresentation or unfair dealing.  However, it implicitly confirms that once the statutory notice is satisfied, the insurer’s cancellation is *lawful and good‑faith* under Louisiana law.  Because those claims were contingent on a *statutory* deficiency, their basis evaporated when the court ruled the procedural certificate was valid.

In short, the plaintiffs could bring a misrepresentation claim only if they could show that GoAuto consciously misrepresented the cancellation process.  The court’s analysis of the notice abstractly ignored any such wrong doing; it ruled that the “certifying” requirement was fulfilled, even without a signature, and that the “receipt” requirement was satisfied by the e‑mail.  Therefore the alleged “misrepresentation” was not a *legal* misrepresentation at all—it was an alleged *misinterpretation* of the statutory language.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for a law student

* *Good‑faith and fair‑dealing* claims can be barred if the defendant properly follows the statutory scheme for cancellation.  
* The court’s reasoning rests on the plain language of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)‑(iv) and the fact that the insurer’s notice met those elements.  
* Because the statutory procedure was satisfied, the plaintiffs had no factual basis to allege breach of contract or bad faith—an essential element of those claims.  
* A separate, independent claim of misrepresentation would require evidence that the insurer *intentionally* misled the insured; the court’s opinion shows no such evidence, and it was unnecessary given the compliance with the statutory notice.  

Thus, the district court’s summary judgment was appropriate, and the appellate court affirmed it because the statutory compliance rendered the plaintiffs’ broader claims untenable.
