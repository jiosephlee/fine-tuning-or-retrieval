Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Is an e‑mail considered a "private carrier" under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) and the Fifth Circuit’s precedent?
Question:
In the opinion, the district court sidestepped the absence of case law on the concept of a "private carrier" by analogizing to a railroad carrier. My confusion lies in whether an email sent from APAC’s system to GoAuto’s web service can be seen as a private carriage of a legal notice. How do the Fifth Circuit and Louisiana courts define "private carrier" in this context, and what distinguishes an electronic delivery from a traditional paper carrier?
Answer:
**Short answer: No – the Fourth‑Circuit and Louisiana courts have never defined an email as a “private carrier” in the statutory sense.  What matters is that the insurer receives the notice, and the commerce‑age language of § 9:3550(G)(3) recognises electronic delivery as a form of “private carriage.”  The Fifth Circuit’s only precedent on the term comes from *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* (5th Cir. 2011) where a railroad was called a private carrier.  The district court in *Williams* analogized a computer‑based email to that same railroad carrier, and the Fifth Circuit did not challenge that analog.  With no binding higher‑court definition, the email satisfies the statute under the ordinary reading that “receipt” can be effected by any means that delivers the notice to the insurer—physical or digital.**

---

## 1.  The statutory question

*La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)* requires a premium‑finance company to
“send … a copy of the notice of cancellation … with a private carrier,
or via electronic mail …” and to “certify” certain facts.  
The statute itself imposes no further definition of “private carrier.”  
Louisiana appellate courts that have considered the provision have treated
the term only as a way of requiring that the insurer actually receive the
notice, not as a classification of the delivery vehicle.

### 1.1  Louisiana appellate usage

*Delatte v. Lemotte* (1993) and *Eaglin v. Champion* (1990) stress that any
defect in the “process” contemplated by § 9:3550(G)(3) invalidates a
cancellation.  Neither case, however, narrows the meaning of “private
carrier.”  The opinion cites these cases only to underline the need for
strict adherence to the statutory procedure, not to define the term.

### 1.2  Fifth‑Circuit precedent

The only Fifth‑Circuit case that discusses the word “private carrier” is
*Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* (2011).  In *Huntley* the court
said that a railroad is a “private carrier” “when it transports goods for
payment” and that the term is not a special category but a descriptor of a
transportation service.  The *Huntley* decision has never addressed
electronic delivery; it simply explained the railroad analogy.

---

## 2.  How the district court treated the email

In *Williams* the district court said:

> “The APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly
>  fits the definition of private carrier.”

(See *Williams*, 5th Cir. 2025 [district‑court reasoning]).

The Oklahoma court’s handling was “analogized” to *Huntley*’s railroad
example.  It imagined that the APAC employee, who prints the notice and
hands it to GoAuto, was a private carrier.  The court did **not** say the
email itself was a private carrier; it viewed the email as the
*medium* through which that “private carrier” conveyed the notice.

---

## 3.  What the Fifth Circuit decided in *Williams*

The Fifth Court held that APAC’s electronic delivery “satisfied the
statute’s *receipt* requirement” because the notice arrived in GoAuto’s
computer system before the policy was cancelled.  The court said:

> “The district court’s definition of ‘private carrier’ was both imaginative
>  and unconventional … Because we find the e‑mail satisfies the statute,
>  we will not consider the validity of the definition.”

(5th Cir. 2025, “I. Strict Adherence to the Statute” paragraph 2).  
The appellate court thus decided *not* to examine the validity of the
district court’s analogy; it accepted the notice as having been
“received.”  The court never parsed the difference between a paper
carrier and a computer‑based carrier.

---

## 4.  Distinguishing electronic from paper delivery

Louisiana case law and the Fifth‑Circuit opinion treat the two modes
treatably the same under the statute:

| Feature | Paper carrier | Email / computer service |
|---------|---------------|--------------------------|
| **Transmission mode** | Physical, hand‑to‑hand | Digital, network‑based |
| **Receipt** | Physical possession or custody | Digital receipt in a system |
| **Statutory satisfaction** | “A copy … with a private carrier” | “Via electronic mail” and “receipt” |
| **Legal effect** | Same – the insurer must have *received* the notice | Same – the insurer must have *received* the notice |

The statute requires *receipt* of the notice; an electronic mail that is
delivered, logged, and stored in the insurer’s system satisfies that
requirement.  The Fifth Circuit’s *Williams* decision rejects any need
for a separate “private carrier” analysis beyond the statutory
language.

---

## 5.  Bottom line for a law student

1. **There is no Fifth‑Circuit authority that defines “private carrier”
   as an email.**  The only authority is *Huntley*’s railroad example,
   which deals with physical transport.
2. **The statute’s relevant element is that the insurer *receives* the
   notice.**  The method (paper, email, could be any way that results in
   “receipt”) is not litigated in the current record.
3. **The district court’s analogy to a private carrier was not struck
   down, but the appellate court found that the *receipt* requirement
   was satisfied by the electronic delivery alone.**
4. **Practically, an email from APAC to GoAuto’s web service can be
   treated as the statutory “notice of cancellation” delivered by a
   private carrier insofar as it results in receipt by the insurer.**
   The difference from a paper carrier is only the medium, not the
   legal effect.

---

**In sum, an email itself is not formally defined as a private carrier
by Louisiana or Fifth‑Circuit precedent.  What matters is that the
insurer receives the notice; the court in *Williams* regarded the
electronic delivery as adequate.  The statute and the Fifth Circuit
require no special treatment for electronic delivery beyond that
receipt, so the email satisfies the statutory requirement in the case
record.**
