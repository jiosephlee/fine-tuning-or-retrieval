Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How does Louisiana law interpret the term "receipt" of a cancellation notice when it is delivered via e‑mail?
Question:
Section (G)(3)(b)(i) of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550 says the insurer must have *receipt* of the notice: “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation…the insurer shall consider that cancellation…has been requested.” The court accepts that GoAuto’s computer system receiving the e‑mail constitutes receipt, but other cases often require physical possession or a signed acknowledgement. Given that the statute is recent and technology has advanced, how do courts define *receipt* in a modern context? Is there precedent that embraces electronic receipt, and if so, what elements make it sufficient?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Louisiana courts read *receipt* in § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) as the insurer’s having **possession of the notice**—in modern terms, a clear record that the insurer’s system has received it.  
In *Williams v. GoAuto*, the Fifth Circuit held that a notice received by the insurer’s computer system (via e‑mail and a web‑service “listener”) satisfies the statutory requirement.  No physical copy or signed acknowledgement is required, and the “private carrier” phrase is satisfied by the electronic delivery itself.  

---

### 1. Statutory mandate

> “**Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation … the insurer shall consider that cancellation…has been requested**.”  
> La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)

The statute does **not** mandate a particular form of receipt, a signature, or a physical carrier.  In *Williams*, the court explained that the legislature’s use of “receipt” is consistent with the ordinary legal definition— “the act of receiving something … by taking possession, delivery, or custody” (Black’s Law Dictionary).  The insurer is required to *have* possession of the notice, not to hold a signed paper.

### 2. How the court applied that definition

The Fifth Circuit examined the facts of the GoAuto‑APAC transaction.  
* APAC’s computer system automatically emailed GoAuto a notice that included copies of the “Ten‑Day Notice” and a certification that the statute’s prerequisites had been met.  
* GoAuto’s computer system, a “web service” that “listens” for such requests, received the e‑mail **in the early morning hours** and immediately cancelled the policy.  
* No APAC employee physically delivered or signed the notice until hours later.

The court concluded that the insurer’s **computer system “taking possession” of the e‑mail satisfied “receipt.”**  The “private carrier” requirement was met by the web service, which the court likened to a private carrier in *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* (a more recent Fifth‑Circuit analogy, its reasoning applied here by an analogue).  

> *We accept these definitions and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.*  
> Williams, 5th Cir. at § II, ¶ 19–20

### 3. What makes an electronic notice sufficient?

The opinion stresses that the insurer must simply have “receipt” of the notice; it does **not** need to prove physical delivery or a stamp of acknowledgment.  The essential elements, as illustrated by *Williams*, are:

1. **Delivery to the insurer’s system** – the e‑mail must be transmitted to an account controlled by the insurer, not merely sent into the post office.  
2. **Record of receipt** – the insurer’s system should log that the notice was received (a timestamp, network log, etc.), providing evidence the insurer “took possession.”  
3. **Statutory compliance of the notice** – the notice must contain the required certifications.  In *Williams*, APAC’s email met all certification requirements; the lack of an individual signature was irrelevant because the statute does not require one.  
4. **Immediate response** – the insurer (or its system) may proceed to cancel “upon receipt.”  The automatic cancellation performed by GoAuto’s system satisfies this trigger.

### 4. Relation to other Louisiana decisions

Other appellate opinions (Delatte, Eaglin, Benitez, etc.) have emphasized strict adherence to the statutory checklist but have **not** imposed additional formalities on *receipt*.  None of them have held that a physical, signed copy is necessary.  *Benitez v. Elsayed*, for instance, noted that the absence of a signature "does not by itself disqualify the cancellation if other elements were present."  The Fifth Circuit in *Williams* extended that reasoning to electronic notices.

---

**Bottom line for practitioners**

If you send a cancellation notice to a Louisiana insurer by e‑mail, the insurer can consider the cancellation requested *as soon as* its computer system receives the message and logs it.  The insurer has not been required to obtain a signed acknowledgement or to hand the notice to a physical employee.  The statutory “receipt” is satisfied by the insurer’s possession of a clear electronic record that the notice has reached it.
