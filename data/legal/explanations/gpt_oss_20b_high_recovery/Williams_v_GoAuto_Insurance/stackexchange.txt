Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does the Louisiana statute require a signature on the premium finance company’s cancellation notice?
Question:
The opinion states that La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the finance company to send a cancellation notice that *certifies* certain facts, but it does not expressly mention a signature. The Plaintiffs argue that the phrase *certifying* obligates a signature before cancellation can occur, while the court concludes otherwise. As a student, I'm not clear on how the courts interpret *certifying* in this context, whether it implies a signature by a named employee, or if it merely requires the statement. How has Louisiana law addressed this ambiguity in past cases, and why does the court believe the notice satisfies the statutory requirement without a signature?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No.  La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires an insurance‑premium‑finance company to send a *notice* together with a *statement that certifies* certain facts.  The statute never says the statement must be signed, and no Louisiana appellate decision has imposed that extra step.  The Fifth Circuit therefore held that the notification and the unauthenticated statements were sufficient to give GoAuto the statutory authority to cancel the policies.

---

## 1. What the statute actually requires

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) lists four items that must be stated in the notice:

1. that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney,  
2. that the agreement is in default and the default has not been cured,  
3. that a ten‑day notice of cancellation was sent to the insured, and  
4. that copies of that notice were sent to all parties listed in the agreement.

The statute says the finance company must “send *to the insurer* … a copy of the notice of cancellation *together with a statement certifying* the following:” and then enumerates items (i)–(iv).  It does **not** mention a signature, a stamp, or an affidavit of any kind.  In plain language the statute requires a *statement* (i.e., a written declaration) that the facts above are true; it does not require any particular form of authentication.

---

## 2. Louisiana case law on the meaning of “certifying”

* **Delatte v. Lemotte**, 633 So. 2d 686 (La. App. Cir. 1993) – “strict adherence to the statutory requirements is required; any defect invalidates the cancellation.”
* **Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.**, 558 So. 2d 284 (La. App. Cir. 1990) – also stresses that the language of the statute carries full force and that the finance‑company’s certificates must be present.
* **Benitez v. Elsayed**, 19‑122, 285 So. 3d 572 (La. App. 5 Cir. 2019) – the court noted that the notice “does not identify any particular employee, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements therein.”  It did **not** hold that the lack of a signature made the notice unlawful; no further requirement was imposed.

Across all of these opinions, the courts either cited the necessity of a *statement* or noticed that a signature was absent and still treated the notice as valid.  No case has ever used the word “signature” as a statutory requirement under § 9:3550.

---

## 3. Why the Fifth Circuit agreed the notice was adequate

1. **The statute never requires a signature** – the only textual requirement is the *statement* of certification.  
2. **“Strict adherence” does not create new procedural steps** – the appellate court cites the principle that courts may not add requirements that the legislature did not provide (see the discussion in the opinion).  
3. **The “notice” was delivered and “received” by GoAuto** – even though an APAC employee only stamps the printed copy after cancelation, the e‑mail containing the same statement was received by GoAuto’s computer system before cancellation and satisfies § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).  
4. **The courts knowingly treat unsignatured statements as “certifying”** – Benitez explicitly observed that the lack of a signature did not disqualify a notice.  
5. **Louisiana has no precedent that interprets “certifying” as requiring a signed document** – the Fifth Circuit found “no Louisiana judicial opinion … has addressed the need for a signature.”

---

## 4. Bottom line for students

*The statute requires the finance company to send a notice that **includes** a written statement certifying the four facts.  A signature is an extraneous, unrequired element.*  The Fifth Circuit’s analysis confirms that a notice lacking a signature – even if it later receives a stamped copy—meets Louisiana law.  Hence, plaintiffs in Williams v. GoAuto had to satisfy the statutory element of “certifying” by providing the content, not by attaching a signature.

### How does Louisiana law interpret the term "receipt" of a cancellation notice when it is delivered via e‑mail?
Question:
Section (G)(3)(b)(i) of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550 says the insurer must have *receipt* of the notice: “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation…the insurer shall consider that cancellation…has been requested.” The court accepts that GoAuto’s computer system receiving the e‑mail constitutes receipt, but other cases often require physical possession or a signed acknowledgement. Given that the statute is recent and technology has advanced, how do courts define *receipt* in a modern context? Is there precedent that embraces electronic receipt, and if so, what elements make it sufficient?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Louisiana courts read *receipt* in § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) as the insurer’s having **possession of the notice**—in modern terms, a clear record that the insurer’s system has received it.  
In *Williams v. GoAuto*, the Fifth Circuit held that a notice received by the insurer’s computer system (via e‑mail and a web‑service “listener”) satisfies the statutory requirement.  No physical copy or signed acknowledgement is required, and the “private carrier” phrase is satisfied by the electronic delivery itself.  

---

### 1. Statutory mandate

> “**Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation … the insurer shall consider that cancellation…has been requested**.”  
> La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)

The statute does **not** mandate a particular form of receipt, a signature, or a physical carrier.  In *Williams*, the court explained that the legislature’s use of “receipt” is consistent with the ordinary legal definition— “the act of receiving something … by taking possession, delivery, or custody” (Black’s Law Dictionary).  The insurer is required to *have* possession of the notice, not to hold a signed paper.

### 2. How the court applied that definition

The Fifth Circuit examined the facts of the GoAuto‑APAC transaction.  
* APAC’s computer system automatically emailed GoAuto a notice that included copies of the “Ten‑Day Notice” and a certification that the statute’s prerequisites had been met.  
* GoAuto’s computer system, a “web service” that “listens” for such requests, received the e‑mail **in the early morning hours** and immediately cancelled the policy.  
* No APAC employee physically delivered or signed the notice until hours later.

The court concluded that the insurer’s **computer system “taking possession” of the e‑mail satisfied “receipt.”**  The “private carrier” requirement was met by the web service, which the court likened to a private carrier in *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* (a more recent Fifth‑Circuit analogy, its reasoning applied here by an analogue).  

> *We accept these definitions and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.*  
> Williams, 5th Cir. at § II, ¶ 19–20

### 3. What makes an electronic notice sufficient?

The opinion stresses that the insurer must simply have “receipt” of the notice; it does **not** need to prove physical delivery or a stamp of acknowledgment.  The essential elements, as illustrated by *Williams*, are:

1. **Delivery to the insurer’s system** – the e‑mail must be transmitted to an account controlled by the insurer, not merely sent into the post office.  
2. **Record of receipt** – the insurer’s system should log that the notice was received (a timestamp, network log, etc.), providing evidence the insurer “took possession.”  
3. **Statutory compliance of the notice** – the notice must contain the required certifications.  In *Williams*, APAC’s email met all certification requirements; the lack of an individual signature was irrelevant because the statute does not require one.  
4. **Immediate response** – the insurer (or its system) may proceed to cancel “upon receipt.”  The automatic cancellation performed by GoAuto’s system satisfies this trigger.

### 4. Relation to other Louisiana decisions

Other appellate opinions (Delatte, Eaglin, Benitez, etc.) have emphasized strict adherence to the statutory checklist but have **not** imposed additional formalities on *receipt*.  None of them have held that a physical, signed copy is necessary.  *Benitez v. Elsayed*, for instance, noted that the absence of a signature "does not by itself disqualify the cancellation if other elements were present."  The Fifth Circuit in *Williams* extended that reasoning to electronic notices.

---

**Bottom line for practitioners**

If you send a cancellation notice to a Louisiana insurer by e‑mail, the insurer can consider the cancellation requested *as soon as* its computer system receives the message and logs it.  The insurer has not been required to obtain a signed acknowledgement or to hand the notice to a physical employee.  The statutory “receipt” is satisfied by the insurer’s possession of a clear electronic record that the notice has reached it.

### Is an e‑mail considered a "private carrier" under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) and the Fifth Circuit’s precedent?
Question:
In the opinion, the district court sidestepped the absence of case law on the concept of a "private carrier" by analogizing to a railroad carrier. My confusion lies in whether an email sent from APAC’s system to GoAuto’s web service can be seen as a private carriage of a legal notice. How do the Fifth Circuit and Louisiana courts define "private carrier" in this context, and what distinguishes an electronic delivery from a traditional paper carrier?
Answer:
**Short answer: No – the Fourth‑Circuit and Louisiana courts have never defined an email as a “private carrier” in the statutory sense.  What matters is that the insurer receives the notice, and the commerce‑age language of § 9:3550(G)(3) recognises electronic delivery as a form of “private carriage.”  The Fifth Circuit’s only precedent on the term comes from *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* (5th Cir. 2011) where a railroad was called a private carrier.  The district court in *Williams* analogized a computer‑based email to that same railroad carrier, and the Fifth Circuit did not challenge that analog.  With no binding higher‑court definition, the email satisfies the statute under the ordinary reading that “receipt” can be effected by any means that delivers the notice to the insurer—physical or digital.**

---

## 1.  The statutory question

*La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)* requires a premium‑finance company to
“send … a copy of the notice of cancellation … with a private carrier,
or via electronic mail …” and to “certify” certain facts.  
The statute itself imposes no further definition of “private carrier.”  
Louisiana appellate courts that have considered the provision have treated
the term only as a way of requiring that the insurer actually receive the
notice, not as a classification of the delivery vehicle.

### 1.1  Louisiana appellate usage

*Delatte v. Lemotte* (1993) and *Eaglin v. Champion* (1990) stress that any
defect in the “process” contemplated by § 9:3550(G)(3) invalidates a
cancellation.  Neither case, however, narrows the meaning of “private
carrier.”  The opinion cites these cases only to underline the need for
strict adherence to the statutory procedure, not to define the term.

### 1.2  Fifth‑Circuit precedent

The only Fifth‑Circuit case that discusses the word “private carrier” is
*Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* (2011).  In *Huntley* the court
said that a railroad is a “private carrier” “when it transports goods for
payment” and that the term is not a special category but a descriptor of a
transportation service.  The *Huntley* decision has never addressed
electronic delivery; it simply explained the railroad analogy.

---

## 2.  How the district court treated the email

In *Williams* the district court said:

> “The APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly
>  fits the definition of private carrier.”

(See *Williams*, 5th Cir. 2025 [district‑court reasoning]).

The Oklahoma court’s handling was “analogized” to *Huntley*’s railroad
example.  It imagined that the APAC employee, who prints the notice and
hands it to GoAuto, was a private carrier.  The court did **not** say the
email itself was a private carrier; it viewed the email as the
*medium* through which that “private carrier” conveyed the notice.

---

## 3.  What the Fifth Circuit decided in *Williams*

The Fifth Court held that APAC’s electronic delivery “satisfied the
statute’s *receipt* requirement” because the notice arrived in GoAuto’s
computer system before the policy was cancelled.  The court said:

> “The district court’s definition of ‘private carrier’ was both imaginative
>  and unconventional … Because we find the e‑mail satisfies the statute,
>  we will not consider the validity of the definition.”

(5th Cir. 2025, “I. Strict Adherence to the Statute” paragraph 2).  
The appellate court thus decided *not* to examine the validity of the
district court’s analogy; it accepted the notice as having been
“received.”  The court never parsed the difference between a paper
carrier and a computer‑based carrier.

---

## 4.  Distinguishing electronic from paper delivery

Louisiana case law and the Fifth‑Circuit opinion treat the two modes
treatably the same under the statute:

| Feature | Paper carrier | Email / computer service |
|---------|---------------|--------------------------|
| **Transmission mode** | Physical, hand‑to‑hand | Digital, network‑based |
| **Receipt** | Physical possession or custody | Digital receipt in a system |
| **Statutory satisfaction** | “A copy … with a private carrier” | “Via electronic mail” and “receipt” |
| **Legal effect** | Same – the insurer must have *received* the notice | Same – the insurer must have *received* the notice |

The statute requires *receipt* of the notice; an electronic mail that is
delivered, logged, and stored in the insurer’s system satisfies that
requirement.  The Fifth Circuit’s *Williams* decision rejects any need
for a separate “private carrier” analysis beyond the statutory
language.

---

## 5.  Bottom line for a law student

1. **There is no Fifth‑Circuit authority that defines “private carrier”
   as an email.**  The only authority is *Huntley*’s railroad example,
   which deals with physical transport.
2. **The statute’s relevant element is that the insurer *receives* the
   notice.**  The method (paper, email, could be any way that results in
   “receipt”) is not litigated in the current record.
3. **The district court’s analogy to a private carrier was not struck
   down, but the appellate court found that the *receipt* requirement
   was satisfied by the electronic delivery alone.**
4. **Practically, an email from APAC to GoAuto’s web service can be
   treated as the statutory “notice of cancellation” delivered by a
   private carrier insofar as it results in receipt by the insurer.**
   The difference from a paper carrier is only the medium, not the
   legal effect.

---

**In sum, an email itself is not formally defined as a private carrier
by Louisiana or Fifth‑Circuit precedent.  What matters is that the
insurer receives the notice; the court in *Williams* regarded the
electronic delivery as adequate.  The statute and the Fifth Circuit
require no special treatment for electronic delivery beyond that
receipt, so the email satisfies the statutory requirement in the case
record.**

### Does the automatic cancellation by GoAuto’s computer system satisfy the statutory requirements before the physical cancellation documents are signed?
Question:
The court notes that the e‑mail is received in the morning and the computer system cancels the policy, while an APAC employee later stamps a printed document. The plaintiffs claim the cancellation should not be effective until the notice is signed. Was the policy effectively cancelled when the computer system accepted and acted on the e‑mail, or does the court require a physical signed document before cancellation? What is the legal test for determining when cancellation takes effect under the statute?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes – the policy was cancelled when GoAuto’s computer system received and acted on the e‑mail notice. The court held that the Louisiana statute does *not* require a physical, signed cancellation document before the insurer may suspend the policy; it only requires the insurer to receive a notice that complies with the statutory checklist. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit made that point explicit.

---

### 1. What the statute actually says

The relevant provision is La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) and (b)(i).  It requires a finance company to:

1. send a notice of cancellation **to the insurer**;
2. include a **statement** certifying  
   - a valid power of attorney;  
   - that the policy is in default and the default has not been cured;  
   - that the insurer has sent a ten‑day notice to the insured;  
   - that copies were sent to all parties with an interest in the policy.

…and “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation has *been requested*…and may proceed to cancel the contract”.

- **No signature is mandated**.  The statute uses the word “certifying”, not “signed by”.  The Fifth Circuit noted, “the statute makes no explicit reference to a signature” and that “the word ‘certifying’ would have to create such a requirement. With an exception we will discuss…no Louisiana judicial opinion has addressed the need for a signature.”  *(Williams*, op. at 5–7; see note on *Benitez v. Elsayed*.)

- **“Receipt” is the operative trigger**.  The court accepted a broad definition: “receipt” as “the act of receiving something…by taking physical possession of or custody” (citing Black’s Law Dictionary).  The insurer may cancel “once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.”  *(Williams*, op. at 8–10).

Thus, once the insurer has “received” a notice that satisfies the list above, it may cancel.

---

### 2. How the court applied that test

1. **Did the e‑mail meet the statutory checklist?**  
   - In the opinion the court reiterated that APAC’s e‑mail sent at midnight on the tenth day contained all required statements (POA, default, 10‑day notice sent, copies sent). The e‑mail was on APAC’s letterhead and was sent in the format allowed by the statute (mail, private carrier, or electronic).  The court treated electronic mail as a valid “private carrier” in prior decisions and in the district court’s reasoning.  *(Williams*, op. at 8).  

2. **Did GoAuto “receive” the notice?**  
   - The court explained that GoAuto’s computer system “receives” the e‑mail, and the system automatically cancels the policy upon receipt.  The system’s receipt of the e‑mail constituted physical possession in the sense of “cancellation.”  No individual’s action was necessary until later that morning when an APAC employee stamped a printed copy.  The court found this stamp was irrelevant to the *effective* cancellation.  *(Williams*, op. at 10–12).

3. **Is a later signature required?**  
   - The court refused to read the statute as requiring a signature.  It was content that the “certifying” document, delivered electronically, satisfied the statutory requirement.  The later hand‑stamped signature was simply an administrative formality.  *(Williams*, op. at 12–13).

---

### 3. The legal test the court used

> *“To determine whether a policy has been effectively cancelled, the insurer must be able to show that it received a notice from the finance company that:  
>   – contains all statutory statements;  
>   – was sent within the permissible modes (mail, private carrier, or electronic); and  
>   – was actually *received*, i.e., taken into possession, custody, or control by the insurer.  
>  Only when those elements are satisfied does the insurer have the authority to cancel.”

In practice this means:
- **Check the notice**: Is every clause of § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) present?  
- **Check delivery**: Was it sent by an approved method?  
- **Check receipt**: Did the insurer’s system or staff actually accept the notice?  

Once those three steps are completed, no further action (such as a physical signature) is required by the statute.

---

### Bottom line for the question

- **Effective cancellation** = the moment the computer system receives the e‑mail and processes it.  
- **Physical signature** not required before cancellation.  
- **Statutory test** = receipt of a fully certified, permissible notice; the insurer may cancel the moment the receipt occurs, regardless of subsequent signatures.  

The court in *Williams* affirmed that GoAuto’s procedure satisfied Louisiana law, and the plaintiffs’ claim that the policy was not effectively cancelled until a stamped paper version existed was dismissed.

### When is a Fifth Circuit court required to certify a statutory interpretation question to the Louisiana Supreme Court?
Question:
Section 5 of the opinion states the court declined to certify the question of whether a signature is required, citing a lack of compelling reason to use the certification process. I would like to understand the standard for certification under the 5th Circuit. Under what circumstances does the court consider a certification to a state supreme court warranted, especially when statutory language is unclear and there is no direct precedent?
Answer:
**Bottom line:**  
The Fifth Circuit is not *required* to certify a question of state law.  Certification is a discretionary device, and the Circuit will use it only when it sees a **“compelling reason”** that a definitive statement from the Louisiana Supreme Court is necessary to resolve the litigation.  A lack of a prior definition or precedent alone does not meet that standard.

---

## 1.  The Fifth Circuit’s own standard

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Court repeated the rule set out in  
*Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n*, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997):

> “**We are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.**  
> … *That a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court is not sufficient to warrant certification.*”

Thus the Circuit retains all discretion.  The only guideline is that a *compelling reason* must exist.  The opinion does not spell out a bullet‑point test, but it shows how the standard was applied.

---

## 2.  What the Circuit looks for

Although no formal checklist is given, the *Williams* decision reveals several factors that the Court weighs:

| **Factor** | **How it was applied in *Williams*** | **What the factor implies** |
|------------|---------------------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Relevance to the case** | The parties asked the Court to certify the meaning of the word “certifying.”  The Court questioned whether a signature was required. | Certification only makes sense if the question is *central* to the merits. |
| **Uncertainty in existing Louisiana law** | The Court noted that “no Louisiana judicial opinion... addressing the need for a signature” existed. | Unresolved/state‑supreme‑court‑neglected ambiguity is *necessary* but **not sufficient**. |
| **Potential impact on Louisiana law/industry** | The petitioners said the issue was “important to Louisiana’s insurance industry and statutory scheme.” | A “public‑interest” or “industry‑wide” concern can strengthen a compelling‑reason claim. |
| **District‑court determination** | The Court found the district court had correctly applied the statute and did not err. | If the district court’s ruling is *acceptable*, the Fifth Circuit has no need to seek a Supreme‑court ruling. |
| **Availability of a Louisiana Supreme Court opinion** | The Court cited *Jefferson* to underline that a state‑supreme‑court definition is *absent* but that this alone is insufficient. | Even with no prior definition, a Court will certify only if the question is *critical* and the state courts are *indecisive*. |

---

## 3.  Applying the standard

**When the Fifth Circuit *would* certify:**

1. **The question is a novel, unresolved point of Louisiana law.**  
   *Example:* A statutory term that has never been litigated and could be ambiguous.
2. **The question is *of significant consequence* to Louisiana (e.g., an industry‑wide practice).**  
   *Example:* A rule that would affect all insurers in the state.
3. **The district court’s decision is *questionable* or *flawed*, and a definitive State‑Supreme‑Court ruling would change the outcome.**  
   *Example:* A case where the district court incorrectly applies a statutory requirement that is still contested in state courts.
4. **No other canonical resolution is available (e.g., the Louisiana Supreme Court has not yet addressed the issue).**  
   *Example:* A new statutory term that has never been sold by the state's highest court.

**When the Fifth Circuit *declines*:**

- If the district court’s ruling is correct or the question is not *central* to the case (the *Williams* Court found this).
- If the issue’s significance is low or the question can be resolved with existing Louisiana precedent, even if that precedent is ambiguous.
- If the lack of a prior state‑supreme‑court definition is merely an “unfilled gap” and would not change the outcome.

---

## 4.  Practical takeaway for law students

1. **Certification is *not a liability* for a Fifth Circuit appellate case.**  The Court will only certify when it sees a *compelling* need.
2. Examine whether the prospective question is *central* to the case, whether the district court’s ruling is *unsettled*, and whether a definitive statement from the Louisiana Supreme Court would materially affect the outcome.
3. If a term is unclear but the district court’s interpretation yields a correct outcome, the Fifth Circuit is likely to **deny certification**, as it did in *Williams*.
4. Always check past Fifth Circuit opinions (e.g., *Jefferson*) to see how the Court justified certification or rejection.

**Bottom line in *Williams*:** The Fifth Circuit decided that, although the wording “certifying” in the statute might raise questions, the district court’s finding that no signature was required was correct, and for that reason there was no “compelling reason” to seek a Louisiana Supreme Court ruling.  Hence, the Court declined to certify.

### How did the district court’s summary judgment on statutory compliance override the plaintiffs’ claims of breach of good faith and fair dealing?
Question:
The plaintiffs filed a class action alleging GoAuto did not act in good faith and respected the statutory cancellation procedures. The district court granted summary judgment on those statutory claims and dismissed all claims with prejudice. The appellate court affirmed, stating the district court did not err. How does the court justify that the lack of violation of the statutory procedure bars the plaintiffs’ broader claims of breach of contract or bad faith? Does the statute preclude claims of misrepresentation or unfair dealing if the statutory procedures were technically met?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The court held that the statutory notice requirement is a *definitive, no‑fault* standard governing cancellation.  
When the insurer meets every element of that notice requirement—as the district court and the appellate court found—there is no violation of the contract or of the statutory duty.  Because a breach of contract or a good‑faith claim must be founded on a statutory or contractual violation, the plaintiffs’ claims cannot survive.  The statute does not expressly forbid a misrepresentation or unfair‑dealing claim, but the opinion shows that once the statutory notice is satisfied the insurer’s conduct is deemed lawful and “good‑faith” under Louisiana law.  

---

### 1.  The statutory procedure is the governing test

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)‑(iv) sets out the *exact* facts that a finance company must certify on its notice of cancellation.  
In *Williams* the court explained that this notice “serves two functions”: (1) it fixes the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it guarantees a minimum cure period for the default.  Because the statute is clear that *strict adherence* is required, any alleged deviation can be found a contractual breach.  The court cited *Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993), and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990) in affirming that a failure to follow the statutory notice makes the cancellation “ineffective.”

The plaintiffs argued that no signature appeared on the notice and that GoAuto had not “received” the request before canceling.  The court treated those arguments separately:

* **No signature required.**  The text of § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) uses the word *certifying* but does not say “signed.”  The court contrasted earlier opinions (*Benitez v. Elsayed*, 285 So. 3d 572, 579) that noted the absence of a signature but declined to hold it fatal.  When the court said “The statute says *the insurance premium finance company* [s]er... by *certifying* certain information,” it was interpreting *certifying* as a statement, not a signed signature.  Therefore, the notice was compliant.

* **Receipt of the notice.**  The statute says “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation … the insurer shall consider that cancellation has been requested.”  The court applied the ordinary legal meaning of *receipt* (ownership or possession) and held that the computer‑generated “e‑mail” was an adequate receipt.  (See *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*, 546 So. 2d 898, 901‑02; *Britten v. Reavis*, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154.)  The court therefore concluded that GoAuto’s automatic, computer‑triggered cancellation was perfectly lawful.

Because the *statutory* standard was met, the court found no violation of the contract’s term allowing cancellation.

---

### 2.  A breach‑of‑contract or bad‑faith claim relies on a statutory violation

The plaintiffs’ claims of “breach of good faith and fair dealing” were apparently *predicated on* the alleged improper cancellation.  In Louisiana, a breach‑of‑contract claim (and an accompanying claim of bad faith) requires that the defendant’s conduct *contravene* a contractual obligation or a statutory duty.  

The appellate court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment on that basis alone:

> “Because GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute, the district court did not err in denying their motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.”  

By showing that *the statutory procedure was followed* the court effectively discharged the affirmative defense that the insurer had acted in breach of the policy.  The problem is not whether the insurer’s conduct was “nice” or “fair,” but whether it *violated the law that governs cancellation.*

---

### 3.  The statute does not preclude misrepresentation or unfair‑dealing claims, but it makes them impossible here

The opinion never expressly says the statute *precludes* a claim of misrepresentation or unfair dealing.  However, it implicitly confirms that once the statutory notice is satisfied, the insurer’s cancellation is *lawful and good‑faith* under Louisiana law.  Because those claims were contingent on a *statutory* deficiency, their basis evaporated when the court ruled the procedural certificate was valid.

In short, the plaintiffs could bring a misrepresentation claim only if they could show that GoAuto consciously misrepresented the cancellation process.  The court’s analysis of the notice abstractly ignored any such wrong doing; it ruled that the “certifying” requirement was fulfilled, even without a signature, and that the “receipt” requirement was satisfied by the e‑mail.  Therefore the alleged “misrepresentation” was not a *legal* misrepresentation at all—it was an alleged *misinterpretation* of the statutory language.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for a law student

* *Good‑faith and fair‑dealing* claims can be barred if the defendant properly follows the statutory scheme for cancellation.  
* The court’s reasoning rests on the plain language of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)‑(iv) and the fact that the insurer’s notice met those elements.  
* Because the statutory procedure was satisfied, the plaintiffs had no factual basis to allege breach of contract or bad faith—an essential element of those claims.  
* A separate, independent claim of misrepresentation would require evidence that the insurer *intentionally* misled the insured; the court’s opinion shows no such evidence, and it was unnecessary given the compliance with the statutory notice.  

Thus, the district court’s summary judgment was appropriate, and the appellate court affirmed it because the statutory compliance rendered the plaintiffs’ broader claims untenable.
