Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Beyond the Right‑to‑Control Theory: Why the Supreme Court’s Fraud Doctrine Is Revolutionary  

## Why This Matters  

In a paper‑heavy decision from the Fifth Circuit, the court reversed a district court’s dismissal of a securities‑fraud indictment that charged a mesh of social‑media personalities for orchestrating a “pump‑and‑dump” scheme. The appellate court did so by faithfully applying the Supreme Court’s new interpretation of the federal fraud statutes, carved out in *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023) and *Kousisis v. United States* (2025). This is one of the first concrete applications of those rulings, and its influence will ripple through securities enforcement, internet‑based investment schemes, and the broader definition of “fraud” under federal law. Essentially, the curve has been shifted: a plaintiff no longer has to expose a $1 loss to abuse the defrauded‑inducement theory; the mere use of false statements to entrap investors suffices. 

For regulators and prosecutors, this gives a clear, broader mandate to prosecute deceptive online marketing that targets the masses—especially Instagram, TikTok, and Twitter “influencers” who materialize overnight “hot‑stock” prospects. For defenders, it signals that “informed consent” is no longer a blessing that can exempt a scheme from the reach of the statutes. Even for courts cautiously treading the line between money‑related fraud and free speech, the Third‑ and Fifth‑Circuits’ willingness to lean on the Supreme Court’s text bio­logy is a signal that the test for sufficiency may now be less stringent than formerly contested.  

## The Right‑to‑Control Theory Meets the Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory  

At the heart of the Supreme Court’s analysis is a tripartite distinction. In *Ciminelli*, the Court found that the federal fraud statutes protect only “traditional property interests”—that is, the right to hold money or property—rather than the right to hold intangible economic information. "The right to valuable economic information…is not a traditional property interest," the Court said, undermining the notion that the mere right to know could serve as a proximate object for a fraud conviction. In the language of the Court, the “right‑to‑control” theory, premised on the victim’s right to control his own assets, does not survive. 

The Supreme Court then moved to the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory in *Kousisis*. In that case, the Court held that "the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss," and that a deceitful statement that lures a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property is enough to satisfy the “intent to defraud” element. “Fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses,” the Court asserted, eliminating the mirror‑image requirement.

The Fifth Circuit, in *Constantinescu*, uses these decisions to re‑frame its analysis. The court affirms that the indictment *did* allege a “fraudulent‑inducement” scheme: defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information…so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” This is precisely the type of conduct the Supreme Court codified as punishable under the fraud statutes. The district court erred by insisting that the wrong theory—right‑to‑control—had been invoked; however, the indictment could have been interpreted under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, and the Fifth Circuit is the first to do so in the context of a securities‑fraud case. 

## Constantinescu: A Testbed for the New Doctrine  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* runs through the steps the Court is required to take in a de‑novo review of an indictment. It restates the “precise” elements: enumeration of each prima‑facie instrument, fair notification, double jeopardy protection, and the requirement that each material element be alleged “in the indictment.” The district court had dismissed the indictment, arguing that it only alleged the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information. The appellate court reversed, refusing to see the insufficiency. 

In doing so, the Fifth Circuit applies the Supreme Court’s clarifications. First, the court emphasizes that the indictment’s “scheme to defraud” alleges the essential misrepresentation: buyers were misled into purchasing securities based on false statements. The “intent to defraud” element is satisfied by the defendants’ objective to obtain followers’ money. As *Kousisis* explains, the fraud statutes do not hinge on eventual losses to the victims; it “does not matter whether the victim is worse off—or even better off—after the fact.” The indictment’s claims of $114 million in profits further signal intent. 

The entire length of the Fifth Circuit’s judgment culminates in the conclusion that the indictment adequately alleges the statutory elements. The court’s reversal signals a preference for venturing into a moral‑judicial gray area rather than forking out a narrower theorem that would place an unwarranted obstacle in front of a determined prosecutor. The Fifth Circuit provides a blue‑print for how lower courts can read a “pump‑and‑dump” indictment in light of the Supreme Court’s new standard.

## Doctrinal Implications: Expanding the Reach of Fraud  

With the Supreme Court carving out a clear path for fraudulent‑inducement theory, several doctrinal shifts occur. First, federal fraud statutes now possess an expanded corpus of “qualified property interests” that is no longer confined to owners of tangible goods or money. Profit‑seeking actors who lure investors with false statements—whether from a corporate director or a charismatic influencer—fall squarely under the law. Second, the removal of the “mirror‑image” requirement shifts the burden to the prosecution: it is no longer incumbent upon the government to prove actual loss. The threshold of “significant gain” is satisfied simply by the act of obtaining money through deception. Third, the clarifications carry forward for other “wire” and “mail” statutes, which have historically been tied to the same fraud elements. This creates a domino effect that can potentially flood the docket with new chargeable conduct.  

There is also a distinct litigious hazard for corporate defendants. The "fraudulent‑inducement" theory is directly applicable to deceptive contracts and propositions that involve intangible economic information. The law’s interpretive standards will make many “public‑disclosure” misstatements attractive fodder for a criminal suit, even when no direct loss has yet been realized. In effect, the Court has empowered prosecutors to target not just traditional securities fraud but also deception in marketing, fraud in the sale of shares, and even penny‑stock pumps that use inter‑firm communications to mislead unsuspecting consumers.

## Downstream Effects: Regulatory, Legislative, and First‑Amendment Chaos  

The Fifth Circuit's decision will undoubtedly influence the next wave of regulatory enforcement. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) now has a clearer blueprint to rely upon when prosecuting modern “pump‑and‑dump” schemes. In the wake of *Kousisis*, the SEC’s enforcement teams can more aggressively raid influencers that are suspected of selling unregistered or unregistered securities under false pretenses. On the other hand, the increase in enforcement actions may also draw the ire of free‑speech advocates. From a First Amendment perspective, providing the statutory language of “fraudulent‑inducement” might reignite calls to scrutinize the reach of the federal fraud statutes against protected speech.  

Legislatively, lawmakers may feel compelled to either tighten the statutes—making them less inclusive—or to carve out new safe‑harbors for marketing or social‑media content. Barring the creation of new statutes that deliver new protections, the general ledger is headed toward more “fraud” convictions, potentially adding a new layer of compliance burden on companies that rely on social media ad revenue. Even without direct legislative amendment, the test‑in‑law for fraudulent‑inducement will prevail as a guide for district courts that revisit alleged securities fraud indictments.  

The “pump‑and‑dump” world, already rife with online self‑proclaimed “gurus,” will instantly feel the pressure. A more elaborate reading of the law may obligate them to adopt a higher standard for disclosure, or else be unavoidably shut down by law enforcement. In the internet age, the proof‑of‑loss rule is quietly done; the charge suffices simply on the fact that people put their money into the pot.

## A Review of the Supreme Court’s Legacy  

The Supreme Court’s evolution in fraud statutes has been gentler than many might imagine. The Court straddled a fine line between “traditional property” versus “intangible information.” In doing so, it carefully distanced itself from a purely “right‑to‑know” doctrine, avoiding an expansive statutory interpretation that could choke free markets. What it did affirm, however, is that the property of “money” and literal “property” remains a protected interest, and that the ordinary means—deceptive misrepresentations that coax people into handing over those protected interests—constitute an actionable fraud. That is the pivot.  

In the Fifth Circuit’s judgment, the law’s language is not only clarified but also enforced in full. By overtly referencing *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, the court calibrated the standard of sufficiency for an indictment. The word “sufficiency” is key: a single asterisk, that a court may count a single piece of evidence as evidence of the penal core, is no longer enough. The indictment must delineate the chain from deception to property deprivation in full detail. In *Constantinescu*, that chain was identified with the language used by the defendants.  

In a jurisdiction with enough money, one might argue for something beyond mere policy. But the Fifth Circuit demonstrates that the law itself contains the answer, and the court is allowed to read the indictment accordingly—with the Supreme Court’s “fraudulent‑inducement” standard as the impetus. As a consequence, we can anticipate that other circuits will follow suit in reading similarly‑structured cases, so that the frontier of securities fraud enforcement is far less “territorial” than it has been for decades.  

## Conclusion  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is not, on its face, merely a reversal of a district‑court dismissal. Rather, it is an affirming ripple in a tectonic shift: the Supreme Court’s past two years have teased out a new understanding of the federal fraud statutes, and that understanding has finally spilled into the courtroom. The move from a “right‑to‑control” limit to a “fraudulent‑inducement” standard effectively removes the economic‑loss requirement from the prosecution’s toolbox. That makes the statutes dramatically more potent, particularly in the arena of social‑media‑mediated “pump‑and‑dump” motives.  

The resulting legal landscape will compel prosecutors to harness the fraudulent‑inducement theory more aggressively, will invite regulatory bodies like the SEC to seize on the broadened definition, and may give first‑amendment challengers a distinct point of attack. Ultimately, *Constantinescu* demonstrates that the Supreme Court’s guidance is not abstract; it has concrete, immediate, and far‑reaching consequences for anyone who uses false representations to coax money out of a market. The law now says: You can’t get away from the statutes simply because the victims didn’t suffer a clean loss; the fraud itself is enough. Those who think otherwise have just been reading the statute from the wrong side of the corridor.



# From Indictment to Trial: Why the Fifth Circuit Overturned a Dismissal in a Pump‑and‑Dump Scheme  

## Why This Case Matters  

In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit reversed a district court that had dismissed a securities‑fraud indictment on the basis that the allegations failed to name a deprivation of a protected property interest. The court’s opinion clarifies how prosecutors must weave the elements of “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” into an indictment, and it dissects the Supreme Court’s recent rulings that distinguish the fraudulent‑inducement theory from the rejected right‑to‑control theory. In a world where multimillion‑follower influencers can move securities prices in seconds, the decision provides a clear map for prosecutors and defendants alike: indictments must connect deceptive misstatements directly to the victims’ relinquishment of *money* or *property*, not merely to the loss of potential information.  

## Background: The District Court’s Reasoning  

The indictment charged five men and two women with conspiracy under § 1349 and multiple counts of securities fraud under §§ 1348 and 2. The indictment alleged that the defendants, brand‑ish figures on Twitter, TikTok, and Instagram, 1) bought a low‑liquidity equity, 2) pumped its price by fabricating trading data and promising astronomical returns, 3) persuaded their audiences to buy the same security, and 4) dumped the stock at a windfall once the price spiked. Prosecutors claimed the indictment captured a full “scheme to defraud”—the deception was not limited to rumors; it was an orchestrated effort to make followers spend money on an absurdly overvalued security.  

The district court concluded that the indictment merely alleged defrauding followers of potentially valuable economic information, not protected property. It cited the Supreme Court’s *Ciminelli* decision to argue that a fraud conviction cannot rest on such information deprivation alone.  The court therefore dismissed the indictment for failure to state an offense, with the defendants’ motion to dismiss succeeding.  

## Sufficiency of Indictments Under the Fifth Circuit Canon  

Before diving into the fraud analysis, the court refreshed the procedural backdrop. Drawing on *U.S. v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023), and *U.S. v. Fontenot*, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011), the Fifth Circuit reiterated that an indictment is reviewed de novo. The indictment’s allegations are taken as true; the court then evaluates whether the indictment enumerates each prima facie element of the charge, fairly informs the defendant, and preserves double jeopardy rights. In other words, a district court cannot “shadow” the alleged offense; it must ensure the indictment contains every material element.  

## Securities‑Fraud Elements and the Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory  

Scholars and litigants have long wrestled with how to apply the skeleton of 18 U.S.C. § 1348—“defraud any person in connection with any security”—to modern, social‑media‑driven schemes. The Fifth Circuit adopted the mail‑and‑wire fraud synecdoche: a “scheme to defraud” entails a material misrepresentation intent on deceiving others in order to obtain money or property; an “intent to defraud” calls for a willingness to deceive and to cause loss.  The court reconciled these with *United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023), which reinforced that the securities‑fraud statute borrows substantially from the wire‑fraud framework.  

The key rhetorical pivot was the court’s sharp distinction between the “fraudulent‑inducement theory” and the long‑abandoned “right‑to‑control theory” that the Supreme Court had rejected in *Ciminelli v. U.S.*, 595 U.S. 309 (2023).  Under the fraudulent‑inducement doctrine, the defendant’s misstatement is a deceptive lure that induces the victim into a contract that requires them to *hand over money or property*. The right‑to‑control theory, in contrast, framed the victim’s information as a quasi‑property that could be deprived by controlling them’s economic expectations.  

In *Constantinescu*, the indictment did not rely on either theory. Every alleged misrepresentation was tied to “commands to purchase a security,” a transaction that demanded money. Hence, the indictment “allyed” the fraudulent‑inducement theory, expressly depicting the victims’ relinquishment of *money* (and thus property) as the protected interest.  

## Ciminelli versus Kousisis: Recalculating the State of Fraud Law  

The Fifth Circuit’s discussion of *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis v. U.S.*, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) is curious but illuminating.  In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court identified that a fraud conviction could not rest on an alleged deprivation of “valuable economic information” where that information was not a traditional property interest.  Yet in *Kousisis*, the Court preserved the fraudulent‑inducement principle, holding that a defendant can be convicted even if the fraudster does not ultimately cause the victim to suffer a monetary loss.  The court essentially said that “what matters is whether the defendant tried to obtain a victim’s money or property through deception.”  The Fifth Circuit rightfully pointed out that the indictment at hand bore on the latter.  

By aligning *Constantinescu* with *Kousisis* rather than *Ciminelli*, the court cemented that any analysis of a securities‑fraud indictment must focus on the material misrepresentation that produces a purchase contract—i.e., the fraudster’s intent to obtain money or property—rather than on the victim’s loss of information. The district court’s error was precisely in ordering the indictment's failure on a misdeployed test that purports to habitually target private property.  

## Analytical Take: The Standard is Still Discipline‑Friendly  

While the Fifth Circuit’s ruling resolves, in this case, the propriety of the indictment, it also leaves prosecutors with a sharper test.  The indictment must demonstrate: (1) that the defendant’s statements were *material* and *false*; (2) that those statements were made with *intent to deceive*; (3) that they were used to *induce* the victim into a transaction requiring *money or property*; and (4) that the defendant intended to *obtain* that money.  The court’s opinion habitually casts doubt on any attempt to focus merely on the potential loss of economic insight: the “deference to the indictment” standard may seem fragile, but the court’s fuller holding reasserts that “a fraudulent scheme requires a material misrepresentation designed to coerce a victim into parting with money.”  

Moreover, the court’s treatment of the “deprivation of property” caveat nudges the standard away from the perilous “right to control” terrain.  The presence of proven misstatements that instruction followers to spend money is a robust, if dramatized, base for a securities‑fraud case.  A deficiency in evidence about the actual loss that the victims might suffer is immaterial under *Kousisis*.  It is enough that the alleged scheme “corralled” $114 million from a susceptible audience, as the indictment claimed.  

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects  

There are several doctrinal ripples from *Constantinescu*. First, the case underscores the increasing reliance on confluence of recent Supreme Court decisions—*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*—in mainstream criminal courts, especially for statutes that draw upon the legacy of the mail and wire fraud frameworks. Second, it marks a definitive stance that “information loss” alone cannot seed a securities‑fraud indictment; the indictments must point to an attempted *deprivation of money or property*. This clarifies a gray zone that has nagged prosecutors evaluating the scope of *§ 1348* for a decade.  

Third, the decision may elevate the tolerance for indictments that expose the siphoning of investor capital via influencers, thereby sharpening the law’s applicability to the social‑media economy.  Now that the Fifth Circuit ruled that a well‑structured indictment that illustrates how the influence fosters money‑transfer is sufficient, prosecutors can confidently pursue the guys behind the “online stock‑pumping” scandals without fear of dismissal over a “deprivation of information” defense.  

## Practical Advice for Prosecutors and Defendants  

### Prosecutors  
- **Detail the payment mechanism**: Show that the social‑media playbook directly prompted followers to buy the security with money—specify the communication channels and the financial transaction.  
- **Demonstrate material misrepresentation**: Prove that the messages about the security’s value were false and materially altered the victims’ decision.  
- **Focus on the acquisition, not the loss**: Even if the stock subsequently drops, the indictment does not need to prove victims suffered a net loss—only that they were induced to part with money.  

### Defendants  
- **Avoid the “deprivation of information” shield**: In future indictments, the defense must not think that refusing to deliver a risky analysis is a viable fraud defense.  
- **Show that the defendant had no intent to obtain money**: If an attacker can prove that the only goal was to “deprive” followers of information, that may be a defense—though, as *Constantinescu* illustrates, this argument is unlikely to survive if misrepresentation is tied to a purchase.  
- **Future accountability may extend beyond securities law**: The 2025 context—even if a securities‑fraud indictment is in play—could dovetail with civil suits from the SEC, state securities regulators, or consumer protection claims.  

## Conclusion  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Constantinescu* is more than a procedural win; it affirms that the warranting test for a securities‑fraud indictment remains strictly that the alleged scheme must funnel money or property through deception. By anchoring the case in the fraudulent‑inducement theory and rejecting the district court’s “right‑to‑control” misapplication, the court underscores the enduring relevance of Supreme Court precedent to everyday fraud prosecutions.  

For regulators, attorneys, and academics, the opinion promises a clearer path toward filing indictments against modern influencers who use the smartphone to make stock markets, and a jurisprudential map that will influence both federal and state prosecutions for years to come. The court’s mercy is not a magnanimous exception, but a reaffirmation of the federal statutes’ original intent: to protect the property of the public from deceptive schemes, not merely to protect intangible economic insight. The old law does not bend to science; it was designed for it—a fact the Fifth Circuit has now held in this resonant case.



# Digital Wall Street: Social‑Media Influencers and the New Frontier of Securities Fraud

## Why This Matters

When the Fifth Circuit overturned a district‑court dismissal of a securities‑fraud indictment against a group of YouTube, TikTok, and Twitter personalities, it signaled that the old wire‑fraud framework can be applied to a new breed of “online shop‑keepers.” These aren’t bank‑tellers or licensed brokers; they are college‑grad‑level scroll‑ha‑makers with millions of followers who harbor modest hopes for a quick buck. The court’s decision underlines that an influencer who posts a boom‑tastic selfie about a penny‑stock and then sells it to the same audience can be prosecuted under the federal fraud statutes. The ruling also clarifies how the courts interpret the Supreme Court’s recently carved‑out “fraudulent‑inducement” theory— a theory that, until now, was largely limited to the world of contracting and government procurement. For the first time, that theory is being applied to the influencer arena, and it will be the touchstone for many future cases.

## A New Kind of Pump‑and‑Dump

The case, United States v. Constantinescu, revolves around eight defendants who had a combined follow‑count that swelled into the hundreds of thousands. They leveraged their asserted “trading acumen” to spark a “pump and dump” cycle on a handful of obscure securities. The indictment alleged that the defendants:

1. Purchased the stocks themselves.
2. “Pumped” the price by posting “false and misleading information” on social media.
3. “Dumped” the holdings after the price had risen, making a $114 million profit.

The defendants’ scheme fit the textbook definition of “pump and dump”: they induced their followers to “purchase the security … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” and then privately sold for profit. One might be tempted to dismiss the indictment as mere defamation or a case of reckless promises. The court saw no such naivete. Instead, it chose to test the indictment against the very old poisons of the federal fraud statutes— namely, that the defendants “deceived” and “obtained” money. It found the indictment sufficient.

## The Court's Indictment‑Sufficiency Analysis

Because the Fifth Circuit is required to view an indictment as having presented a “prima facie case,” it opens the case for normal criminal-constitution analysis. The court’s step through the indiction was methodical:

1. The court verified that every element of § 1348 (the “in connection with any security” fraud statute) was enumerated. 
2. The indictment’s misrepresentations were deemed a “material misrepresentation.”  
3. It addressed the defendants’ counter‑argument that the indictment merely claimed that the influencers “intended to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information,” which would fail if the fraud were based on a “right‑to‑control” theory as expounded in *Ciminelli*.

In a clear way, the court found that the indictment had more than the illusion of “deprivation of information” it had to have the “deprivation of money” requisite for fraudulent‑inducement.

## Fraudulent‑Inducement vs. Right‑to‑Control

The Supreme Court bifurcated the federal fraud statutes into two distinct paradigms in *Ciminelli v. State* and *Kousisis v. United States*. The former, the “right‑to‑control” theory, was abandoned because it sought to criminalize the mere proscription of an individual’s right to make decisions with their economic information. The latter, the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory, was preserved exactly because the fraud statutes are designed to protect tangible property interests— money, stock, contracts—rather than abstract knowledge.

The Fifth Circuit reminded the defendants that a “right‑to‑control” claim would fail because the defendants didn’t merely deprive the followers of information. The indictment asserted that they “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” That is the crux of the fraudulent‑inducement theory. The court made it clear that this theory does not require that the victim incur a net pecuniary loss; it only requires that the victim be tricked into parting with money or property. In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court clarified the same point—the victim’s bottom line may remain intact, but the fraud is still present if the defendant used a material misstatement to induce a contract.

This nuance matters because influencer marketing is fundamentally about inducement, not prohibition. A user may click a link and buy a stock even if it turns out to be worthless; the investor lost nothing. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, we can still prosecute the influencer if the investor was lured into a transaction through false statements. This expands the reach of federal fraud statutes into a digital marketing ecosystem that had previously seemed like a gray area.

## The Courage of *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*

While the Fifth Circuit relies heavily on the Supreme Court’s recent holdings, it also subtly signals that the reactionary step to the “right‑to‑control” theory, while long dead, is not dead yet. The court’s threat to scrutinize the “deprivation of economic information” claim indicates that regulators and prosecutors can beware. The stale case of *Ciminelli* will continue to serve as a cautionary tale for plaintiffs and civil litigants who craft their theories around “informed consent” to market “irrational” speculation.

The *Constantinescu* opinion also highlights that the federal fraud statutes still embody a “historic, white‑glove” credibility standard. Even in an algorithmic environment where the influencer may be posting an entire whole being on a five‑minute video, they bear the same burden of materiality and intent. The court reasserts that the indictment does not need to bind the course of a conceptual or abstract relationship; it can accommodate the real‑world digital praxis.

## Doctrine and the Big Picture

The Fifth Circuit’s decision will ripple across several doctrinal frontiers:

- **Circuit Court Interpretation of Securities Fraud**: In a circuit that has not yet produced a robust body of law on § 1348, this case becomes a pivotal building block. Future courts will likely borrow the same structure and threshold test for fraudulent‑inducement and for sufficiency of the indictment.
  
- **Implications for Digital‑Only Retaliation**: The court fits the case squarely under § 1348 and not under the mail/wire statutes, partly because the social‑media posts constitute wire transmissions. That signals that the statutes are still flexible enough to accommodate digital communications and, by extension, the new forms of advertisement.

- **Free‑Speech Prospects**: The dissenters would have argued that the influencers’ “speech” should be protected. The court sidestepped that argument. But the decision also leaves the door open for a future free‑speech solution: an influencer genuinely uninformed about the market may be protected if proof of “intent to defraud” is lacking. The evidence citation around the challengers’ “intent” claim underscores that the statutes require an intent to obtain money or property, not an intent to cause harm.

- **Jurisdictional Scope**: Because the indicators of fraud were distributed on *Twitter*, *TikTok*, and *Foundation*, the case affirms that the federal fraud statutes apply wherever a defendant transmits misleading information across interstate communications—even if the trade is purely online. That fits with the “wire” meaning of § 1348.

## What Comes Next? Regulatory and Policy Implications

### Oversight by the SEC

The SEC’s role in policing securities fraud is already well‑established. But the *Constantinescu* case signals that the SEC should consider new mechanisms to monitor “influencer‑driven” market manipulation. A potential tool is the SEC’s “Helpful‑Regulation‑Due‑Process” initiative, which targets large‑scale “pump‑and‑dump” coordinated through social media. The court’s emphasis on "inducement" is a boon in that the SEC can argue that the influencers’ posts are “material misrepresentations” that could "violate Rule A‑Business Regulation." It is still up for debate whether SEC enforcement can or should unilaterally target these misinformation campaigns, but the federal criminal case presents a strong precedent that such state‑level attempts are likely to be consistent with the securities fraud statutes.

### Platform Liability & Self‑Regulatory Measures

Platforms like Twitter, TikTok, and YouTube are not direct participants in the securities transactions by the influencers, but the business operations of each platform rely on user‑generated content. In the pre‑*Constantinescu* era, the court would have returned the question to the Second Circuit’s *Union of 2001* and *FTC v. Facebook* for platform liabilities. Now, however, the court’s decision indicates that platforms may need to consider new internal reporting obligations: if a 2025 influencer’s post is deemed to be a “material misrepresentation” that induces purchases in federal securities, the platform may be compelled to treat them as a “communications carrier” and may face liability for failing to police or flag misinformation. The regulatory policy may shift to require algorithmic transparency that can flag post‑pump indicators for rapid SEC review.

### Free‑Speech Defenses

Though the Fifth Circuit sidesteps a speech analysis, other courts may dial it in more sharply. If an influencer can prove that they had no “intent to defraud” and were simply misinformed, their speech might be spent under the First Amendment. The trial courts will need to weigh the available evidence that the Duke’s distinction between fraudulent‑inducement and deceptive persuasion has been overstated. The new context of “no net loss to investor” may even promote a “purportedly honest” defense. At present, the *Constantinescu* decision offers no obvious free‑speech safe‑harbor; however, the limited references to the “intent” element may encourage the defense to focus on "lack of intent to deceive" more strictly.

## Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of a dismissal in *United States v. Constantinescu* is the first time a federal appellate court has used the Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* framework to convict a digital influencer of securities fraud. In the language of a law‑blog and a legal printer house, the court has carved a new analogue of the securities‑fraud sheet: just admitting that an influencer posted false statements on a video, and that followers cashed on those statements by buying a security, is adequate to satisfy the indictment. No additional proof of a net loss or a protected property interest is necessary; the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is the new highway.

This decision is not a friendly invitation to the next online “Robert Kiyosaki.” Instead, it is a wake‑up call that the financing Web will not be exempt from the federal brutality when investors are swayed into bandito–style trades. The markets may be new, but the underlying doctrine is simple: if you lie to get money, you’re guilty—report no net loss needed. The regulatory gears are already turning; the SEC, the FTC, and the courts will now have a very concrete case to rely on. The remaining question—do we keep expanding the reach of federal fraud statutes into the social‑media zoo, or do we protect a digital culture that thrives on instant speech? For now, the court’s opinion tells us that time to reevaluate is long overdue.



# Indictment Dismissals and Double Jeopardy: Lessons from the Constantinescu Reversal

## Why This Matters

In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit struck down a district court dismissal of a securities‑fraud indictment on the ground that the indictment did not “state an offense.” The case forces us to revisit the rarely litigated question of when the court has the authority to throw away an indictment as factually or legally defective, and it expands the practical importance of the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications on securities‑fraud theory. For prosecutors, the appellate decision is a reminder that an indictment that merely says, “the defendants pumped and dumped shares,” does not automatically satisfy the statutory elements. For defendants, the reversal shows that a dismissal for insufficiency will not, in all circumstances, bind the government to a single prosecution. The ruling, placed against the backdrop of *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, clarifies the double‑jeopardy implications of an indictment dismissal.

## The Fifth Circuit’s “Take It as True” Test

Federal law requires that an indictment be a complete diagnostic of an offense. As the Fifth Circuit reaffirmed in *United States v. Rafoi* (60 F.4th at 982) and *United States v. Fontenot* (665 F.3d at 644), the appellate court must read the indictment’s allegations as true and then assess whether those allegations satisfy every prima facie element of the crime. The statutory checklist is clear: enumerate each element, inform the defendant, and afford a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. The dissenting district court in *Constantinescu* tried to waive the indictment’s “stated offense” requirement on the basis that the indictment failed to assert a protected property interest—that the followers were deprived of “potentially valuable economic information.” That argument departed from the Fifth Circuit’s foundational principle that what matters is whether the indictment alleges the elements of the offense, not whether it preserves an abstract, untested “property interest.”

The appellate court thus re‑embraced the de‑novo sufficiency test. By taking the indictment “as written,” the Fifth Circuit found that the alleged pump‑and‑dump scheme, complete with the defendants’ misrepresentations and their intent to obtain money, satisfied the two–step fraud structure: a scheme to defraud that involved deception and an intent to convince victims to part with money or property. The reversal was not an exercise in doctrinal invention but a strict application of the procedural rule that indictments must meet statutory thresholds for the allegations they contain.

## Fraudulent Inducement, Not Right‑to‑Control

The most interesting doctrinal question before the court was the nature of the “property” in securities fraud. In *Ciminelli v. United States*, the Supreme Court held that a “right‑to‑control” theory—where a defendant’s deception merely deprives a victim of the right to make a potentially lucrative investment—does not constitute a state‑of‑the‑art property interest protected by a fraud statute. *Kousisis* extended that line to affirm a “fraudulent‑inducement” theory that applies when a deceptive misrepresentation leads a victim to part with money or other property. The *Constantinescu* indictment, the Sixth Circuit’s “sprech” suggests, was properly framed within the fraudulent‑inducement schema: defendants advertised their “trades,” promised returns, and induced followers to purchase securities, thereby obtaining money. The indictment’s failure to refer to the victims’ right to control the decision about their investments was irrelevant because the fraud’s focal point was the revocation, by deception, of the actual funds. The Supreme Court’s neutral framing—“use a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property”—serves as a ready template for any securities‑fraud indictment that aims to satisfy the stand‑alone fraud statutes. The *Constantinescu* decision merely confirms that the indictment meets that test when it describes a scheme involving doxxed misstatements and a monetary outcome.

## Where the District Court Went Wrong

The district court’s dismissal hinged on a narrow property‑interest argument. The court held the indictment “failed to allege a protected property interest at all” and therefore could not proceed. This view in effect disregarded the Supreme Court’s direction that the fraud statutes cover “any money or property acquired by means of deception, regardless of whether the victim suffers a net economic loss.” To misread the remit of the statutes is to misread the ethics of double jeopardy: once you make an accusation that a defendant is misrepresenting securities, you cannot simply say the victim lost information. The indictment must tie the deception item to the acquisition of money or property, and that was precisely what *Constantinescu* claimed: the defendants’ posts lured followers to buy, driving up a price, and then the defendants sold for a profit. By slicing the indictment’s language into a discussion of “potentially valuable economic information” and a “traditional property interest,” the district court in effect rewrote the substantive constitution of the alleged crime, an action the Fifth Circuit explicitly disallowed.

## Double Jeopardy and the “Dismissal for Failure to State a Claim”

Double jeopardy’s original reach is to bar a second prosecution based on the same charge after the first ends in conviction or acquittal. A dismissal for “failure to state a claim” does not end a case in the constitutional sense. That dismissal merely signals that the indictment was defective and cannot proceed. The government may, subject to appropriate procedural and sovereign restrictions, resubmit a new indictment that corrects the deficiency. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal restores the procedural continuity that prevents a defendant from relying on a “double jeopardy” claim triggered by an erroneous dismissal. In the present case, the appellate court’s remand removes the “expert gatekeeping” event that had left the government with no viable charge. The district court, under its own statutory purview, can now proceed with an indictment that satisfies the Supreme Court’s fraudulent‑inducement framework. By doing so, the district court essentially places the defendant back in the arena without a double‑jeopardy twist. The appellate reversal preserves, rather than erases, the plaintiff’s opportunity for prosecution, and the doctrine of double jeopardy remained intact: there is no “final judgment” to invoke that clause.

From a doctrinal perspective, *Constantinescu* demonstrates that unless a judgment “terminates” an offense, the double‑jeopardy clause cannot be applied. A dismissal for insufficiency represents the government’s choice to refine the indictment, not the constitutionally forbidden repetition of a trial. Courts recognizing this distinction—such as the Ninth Circuit in *United States v. Reynolds*—confide that an indictment can be dismissed and re‑filed as needed.

## Practical Take‑away for Prosecutors

1. **Enumerate Every Element** – A securities‑fraud indictment must articulate the scheme, the deception, and that the defendants sought to obtain money or property. Each element must be supported by a factual assertion, not assumed.  
2. **Use Fraudulent‑Inducement Language** – In the wake of *Kousisis*, prosecutors should phrase indictments around the “material misrepresentation that induces a transfer” of money or property. A vague reference to “potentially valuable information” is insufficient.  
3. **Avoid the Right‑to‑Control fallacy** – There is no angle in modern federal securities‑fraud prosecutions that simply alleges a violation of a victim’s investment‑decision “right.” The mere loss of potential information does not waive the fraud statutes’ applicability.  
4. **Know Your Fields** – When drafting indictments against social‑media influencers or market manipulators, the alleged “pump” must be paired explicitly with the deception that caused followers to part with their money.  

Prosecutors wary of a dismissal for insufficiency should vet indictments against the Fifth Circuit’s “take-as-true” standard and the Supreme Court’s fraudulent‑inducement framework before filing. A well‑drafted indictment can survive muster by the court and progress to the trial track.

## Practical Take‑away for Defendants

1. **Deficiency Doesn’t Auto‑Trigger Double Jeopardy** – If a district court dismisses an indictment for failure to state a claim, it does not bar the government from retrying or re‑filing a proper indictment.  
2. **Challenge Misstatements, Not Property Interest** – A double‑jeopardy defense based on “no property interest was claimed” will not stick. Instead, focus attack the alleged fraudulent‑inducement elements: the deception, the intent, the victim’s transaction.  
3. **Monitor the Counter‑Story** – If an indictment is dismissed, watch for a resubmitted version. The plaintiff may try to tweak the narrative to fit the Supreme Court’s framework. Being prepared to question whether the new filing truly covers the alleged misrepresentations helps you keep the court’s eyes on the facts.  
4. **Consider Motion for a New Indictment** – After an erroneous dismissal, a defendant can seek a new indictment that more directly conjures the fraud text. It may reduce the government’s proof burden because the new indictment will likely contain richer detail.  

Ultimately, *Constantinescu* affirms that defendants only gain a double‑jeopardy advantage when prosecutors truly misfile a charge that cannot be amended. An insufficiency dismissal is a procedural realignment, not a constitutional shield.

## The Doctrinal Horizon

The *Constantinescu* reversal sits squarely within an evolving dialogue about the scope of the federal fraud statutes. The Supreme Court’s decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* render the door open for prosecutors to tap into “fraudulent‑inducement” claims even when victims suffer no net economic loss. The Fifth Circuit’s adoption of that text reaffirms that an indictment that misleads investors into purchasing a security is sufficient, even if the investors renew their holdings at a loss. The appeal also underscores that a mere theoretical property right — “the right to control the decision about a security’s purchase” — is no substitute for an elementized claim of financial injury.

For academics and practitioners, the case nudges the discussion back toward the interaction between procedural sufficiency and substantive fraud theory. As the court adopts a strict de‑novo approach, we could expect to see more disputes where the issue is not whether the law applies but whether the indictment states the law. By carefully enumerating the fraudulent‑inducement elements and avoiding reliance on the right‑to‑control formulation, prosecutors can shepherd cases through the first hurdle. For defendants, understanding that a dismissal for insufficiency does not automatically carve a double‑jeopardy protection is crucial. It avoids complacency and keeps the aggressiveness of the prosecution’s system straight. And by walking through the new landscape, both sides can adjust their strategies to an era where misrepresentations on social‑media platforms are increasingly being put under the weight of federal securities‑fraud statutes.
