Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Background and Procedural History

## Defendants’ identities and social‑media influence  

The case concerns a group of nine defendants: Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight.  Each defendant has cultivated large followings on multiple social‑media platforms, presenting themselves as experienced traders of stocks and other securities.  By consistently posting their trading activity and purported market analyses, they have positioned themselves as credible sources of investment advice to a broad audience of followers who are unaware of the defendants’ intent to manipulate markets.

## Description of the alleged pump‑and‑dump scheme  

The indictment alleges that the defendant group conducted a coordinated “pump‐and‐dump” scheme.  Under this scheme, the defendants would first purchase a security and then use their social‑media presence to disseminate false and misleading information about the security, thereby inflating its price.  The exaggerated, unsubstantiated claims were purported to create artificial demand, encouraging other investors—including their followers—to buy the security at the inflated price.  Once the price rose, the defendants would sell (or “dump”) the security, thereby profiting from the artificial price increase.

## Indictment contents: conspiracy and individual counts, estimated profits  

United States v. Constantinescu is filed under docket No. 24‑20143.  The indictment charges the defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1349) and, for each defendant, multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2.  The indictment alleges that the defendants collectively profited an estimated $114 million from the scheme.  Each defendant is alleged to have been an active participant in the false‑information component, thereby fulfilling the material element of the alleged fraud.

## Knight’s guilty plea and other defendants’ motion to dismiss  

Among the nine defendants, Daniel Knight entered into a guilty plea for the offenses charged against him.  Following Knight’s plea, the remaining defendants filed a motion to dismiss the indictment.  The motion argued that the indictment failed to establish a violation of the federal securities‑fraud statutes because it did not allege a deprivation of a traditional property interest.  The defendants claimed that the indictment merely alleged that they sought to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information, rather than money or other property.

## District court’s reasoning for dismissal (failure to allege deprivation of property interest)  

The United States District Court, after considering the defendants’ motion, concluded that the indictment could not survive.  The court held that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme to deprive the followers of a protected property interest.  According to the court, the alleged conduct—depriving followers of potential economic information—did not constitute a “deprivation of property” within the meaning of the federal fraud statutes.  Consequently, the district court dismissed the indictment, finding that the indictment did not state an offense.

## Timeline of proceedings from indictment to appeal  

- **Grand jury indictment**: The federal grand jury returns an indictment against the nine defendants, docketed as No. 24‑20143.  The indictment alleges conspiracy to commit securities fraud and multiple counts of securities fraud, with an estimated $114 million in profits.  
- **Knight’s plea**: Daniel Knight enters a guilty plea for the charges brought against him.  
- **Motion to dismiss**: The remaining defendants file a motion to dismiss the indictment, arguing that the indictment fails to state an offense.  
- **District court dismissal**: The district court grants the motion and dismisses the indictment, holding that the indictment does not allege a deprivation of a traditional property interest.  
- **Appeal**: The United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit receives the appeal.  On October 2, 2025, the Fifth Circuit issues its decision, reversing the district court’s dismissal and remanding the case for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.  

This procedural history sets the stage for the Fifth Circuit’s analysis, which focuses on whether the indictment adequately alleged the elements of securities fraud, particularly the existence of a scheme to defraud and the requisite intent.

# Indictment Sufficiency under the Fifth Circuit

In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit revisited the classic sufficiency analysis governing indictment pleadings. The district court had dismissed the indictment on the ground that it failed to state a federal offense, a determination the appellate court reversed because the indictment, as construed, satisfied the Fifth Circuit’s well‑to‑date requirements that an indictment allege the elements of a crime, inform the defendant, and shield him from double jeopardy. This chapter dissects the statutory and case‑law framework that informs that decision, and shows how the court applied the framework to the present scheme‑to‑defraud allegations.

## Definition of “sufficiently” alleging an offense

For a federal indictment to be “sufficiently” alleging an offense, the Fifth Circuit requires that the indictment enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense, fairly inform the defendant of the charges, and provide a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. See *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996); *United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo*, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000). “Sufficiency” does not demand that the indictment present conclusive evidence; rather, it must present a complete and credible set of allegations that, at face value, constitute a crime. That demand follows the plain statutory prohibition against pleading a false or deceptive statement without articulating the crime’s core elements. The rule that an indictment must state a crime stems from the notion that a defendant is entitled to a chance to prepare a defense based on real allegations, and that the court should not be compelled to inquire into evidentiary sufficiency beyond the indictment’s assertions. 

## De novo review standard for indictments

The Fifth Circuit reviews the sufficiency of indictments de novo; the court gives no deference to the district court’s resolution and takes the indictment’s allegations as true. See *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). In that case the court explained that “the question of sufficiency is a matter of law that must be decided independently of the merits of the evidence.” The de novo standard rests on a fundamental evidentiary principle: the indictment is the prosecutor’s “first statement of charge,” and its bearing is decided solely by the allegations, not by a presumption that the evidence supporting them exists. Consequently, the Fifth Circuit applies the three‑part test of *Gaytan*: enumeration of elements, fair informing, and double‑jeopardy protection.

## Criteria: enumeration of elements, fair informing, double‑jeopardy protection

The first criterion—that the indictment enumerates each element—requires the prosecutor to show, with specific facts, how the defendant conspired, misleadingly represented, and obtained property. The second criterion—fair informing—mandates that the indictment be sufficiently clear and non‑technical so that the defendant knows what he is being accused of. A vague or ambiguous allegation fails, because it deprives the defendant of a meaningful opportunity to prepare. The third criterion—double‑jeopardy protection—insists that the indictment avoid stipulations that, if proven, would expose the defendant to future prosecutions for the same conduct. Together, these criteria ensure that the indictment is both legally sound and substantively fair.

## Leading Fifth Circuit precedent (Rafoi, Fontenot, Crow, Gaytan, Guzman‑Ocampo)

The Fifth Circuit’s body of precedent builds a robust framework. *United States v. Rafoi*, the most recent decision, articulated the de‑novo standard and reaffirmed that the court must treat inductive allegations as fact. *United States v. Fontenot*, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011), clarified that a prosecutor need not present the evidence that ultimately proves each element; the indictment itself must, however, convey the alleged conduct. *United States v. Crow*, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999), laid early groundwork by emphasizing that an indictment must be more than a mere statement of accusation. *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996), codified the three‑criteria test that has guided the circuit ever since. Finally, *United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo*, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000), reiterated that sufficiency is a question of pleadings, not evidence.

These cases are cumulative: each one refines the same underlying principles. Together, they form the procedural backbone that courts use to evaluate whether an indictment properly states an offense. When a Fifth Circuit judge is faced with a questioned indictment, these authorities supply a roadmap: give the indictment the benefit of the doubt, excise vague charges, and make sure the required elements appear described in detail.

## The indictment as the prima facie case vs mere allegations

If an indictment satisfies the sufficiency criteria, it rises above mere allegations and constitutes a prima facie case. The prosecution may then introduce evidence to support each alleged element. *United States v. Constantinescu* embodies this principle: the indictment alleged that the defendants “purchased a security, “pumped” the price by posting false and misleading statements on social media to induce investors to buy, and then “dumped” the security for profit.” Those specific facts—together with the alleged monetary gains—positioned the indictment as a prima facie case within the Fifth Circuit’s framework. The district court’s dismissal equated the indictment with a set of unsubstantiated rumors and failed to accept that the allegations, when viewed face‑to‑face, articulate a complete scheme to commit fraud. The appellate court, applying the de‑novo review and the three criteria, concluded that the indictment went beyond mere allegations and furnished the requisite primum.

## Defendants’ argument regarding lack of material elements

The defendants contended that the indictment omitted the core material elements of securities fraud: a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” Their argument hinged on the notion that the indictment merely accused the conspirators of depriving followers of “potentially valuable economic information,” and that the fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests. The court, however, rejected those claims by reaffirming that the indictment described a fraudulent‑inducement scheme under which the defendants misrepresented securities to cause followers to part with money or property—a form that satisfies both the “scheme to defraud” and the “intent to defraud” elements. The indictment’s explicit language that the conspirators “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” satisfies the element of deception, and the identification of the $114 million profit demonstrates intent to obtain property through deceit. By encapsulating those facts, the indictment met the criteria from *Gaytan* and *Guzman‑Ocampo*, and the appellate court concluded that the defendants’ lack of evidence did not render the indictment insufficient. The court’s treatment of the indictment under the fraudulent‑inducement theory—distinct from the right‑to‑control theory addressed in *Ciminelli*—provided the legal mechanism to sustain the indictment’s allegations. 

In sum, *United States v. Constantinescu* is a textbook illustration of how the Fifth Circuit applies its sufficiency doctrine. The court reaffirmed that an indictment must precisely enumerate the crime’s elements, be clear enough to inform the accused, and protect against double jeopardy. When those requirements are met, the indictment itself becomes a prima facie case, regardless of the defendant’s later admissions or the ultimate sufficiency of evidence. The decision underscores the robust procedural safeguard that a defendant has against pleading violations of the law based on vague or insufficient allegations.

# Securities Fraud Statutes, Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory, and Supreme Court Guidance

## 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and § 1349: statutory purpose and language  
The federal securities‑fraud statutes are codified in two complementary provisions. 18 U.S.C. § 1348 prohibits the execution of “any scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.” 18 U.S.C. § 1349 permits the prosecution of conspiracy to commit the conduct set out in § 1348.  The statutory language is deliberately broad, allowing the courts to define what constitutes a “scheme” and what “deceive” means in each context.  The Court’s opinion underscores that § 1348 draws “key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” (United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6), and that the same elements—scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and material misrepresentation—apply to this statute.

## Elements of securities fraud: scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, material misrepresentation  
To satisfy § 1348, an indictment must allege: (1) a *scheme to defraud*—a plan that uses false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises; (2) an *intent to defraud*—the defendant’s purpose to deceive and cause some harm; and (3) a *material misrepresentation*—an untruthful or misleading statement intended to influence the victim’s decision.  The Court lays out the elements in the opinion’s discussion of the statute, noting that “the ‘scheme to defraud’ element requires a material misrepresentation ‘intended to deceive others … to obtain something of value, such as money.’”  The Court further clarifies that the *intent to defraud* “requires an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.”  These elements are the foundation for the remainder of the analysis.

## Traditional property‑interest requirement  
The federal fraud statutes are limited to protecting *traditional* property interests.  The Court explains that “the law protects only traditional property interests” and references that the Supreme Court has characterized the *right‑to‑control* theory as attempting to treat the mere possession of valuable economic information as property.  Since such information is not a property interest, a claim based solely on depriving someone of that information cannot satisfy the statutory requirement.  This limitation is pivotal when distinguishing the right‑to‑control theory from the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

## Right‑to‑control theory: definition and Supreme Court rejection (Ciminelli)  
The right‑to‑control theory proposes that a “victim’s property is the right to control the victim’s assets,” thereby allowing a defendant to defraud by depriving a victim of that right.  The Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli v. United States* rejected this theory, holding that “a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information,” and that such information is not a traditional property interest.  The Court emphasized that the federal fraud statutes do not extend to purely intangible rights to information.  Importally, the Court noted that the *right‑to‑control* theory has no authority to support a securities‑fraud conviction.

## Fraudulent‑inducement theory: definition and application to securities fraud  
By contrast, the fraudulent‑inducement theory posits that a defendant uses a *material misstatement* to induce a victim to *truly* part with money or property.  The Supreme Court in *Kousisis v. United States* affirmed that this theory remains viable: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.”  This theory is distinct from the right‑to‑control theory and permits the prosecution of schemes that simply rely on deceptive inducement to obtain payment, without requiring actual financial harm to the victim.  In the context of securities fraud, it allows the government to charge a defendant for pushing investors to buy securities through false statements, as the defendant’s objective is the monetized gain of the investor’s money, not merely the loss of information.

## Supreme Court cases: Ciminelli and Kousisis in context  
The *Ciminelli* decision clarified that the securities‑fraud statutes are limited to traditional property rights, effectively rejecting the right‑to‑control framework.  The *Kousisis* decision extended the series of decisions that recognize the fraudulent‑inducement framework, stating that the burden rests on the defendant to prove that “no true mistake” was made and that the defendant *obtained the victim’s money or property* through fraudulent means.  These rulings supply the Court’s guiding principle in evaluating whether an indictment alleges sufficient fraud: the defendant must be shown to have used false representations to gain money or property, regardless of the victim’s net loss.

## Alignment of the indictment’s allegations with the fraudulent‑inducement theory  
In the present case, the indictment alleged that the defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” (indictment ¶ 13).  This language satisfies the *material misrepresentation* requirement and specifies that the defendants sought the money of their followers.  The indictment further contends that the scheme “‘pump’ the price … and ‘dump’ by secretly selling the security for a profit,” demonstrating an explicit *intent to defraud* by profiting from the induced purchases.  Because the indictment identifies the *deprivation of money* rather than the mere loss of information, it aligns squarely with the fraudulent‑inducement theory.  Accordingly, the elements enumerated in § 1348 are satisfied.

In its holding, the Court reversed the district court’s dismissal, holding that the indictment sufficiently states a crime under § 1348 and § 1349 because it satisfies the elements of a scheme to defraud, introduces intent to defraud, and alleges a material misrepresentation that leads to the victim’s transfer of money or property.  The Court’s analysis shows that the indictment does not rely on the discarded *right‑to‑control* theory but rests on the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory affirmed by the Supreme Court.  The appellate court therefore remanded the case for further proceedings, consistent with its findings.

# Court’s Analysis of the Indictment’s Allegations  

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu* offers a clear blueprint for how a district court, and an appellate court reviewing that decision, must work through the statutory elements of 18 U.S.C. § 1348. In this chapter we dissect the court’s methodical examination of the indictment, drawing out the substantive standards it applies, and showing step‑by‑step how the facts on record satisfy those standards.  

---

## Assessment of the “Scheme to Defraud” Element  

The securities‑fraud statute requires that the defendant *engage in a scheme to defraud.* Under the court’s analysis, this “scheme” is a conspiratorial arrangement that uses **material misrepresentation** to deceive others and obtain money or property. In *Constantinescu* the court first restated this threshold: “[i]n securities fraud requires (1) a scheme to defraud … and (2) an intent to defraud”【III】.  

The indictment makes the consequential findings that the defendants *purchased a security, pumped its price by posting false and misleading information on social media, induced other investors to buy the security, and then dumped the shares for profit*【I】【I】. These charge‑allegations collectively portray a definite plan. The court noted that the indictment *enumerates* each element of the scheme: (1) “purchase of the security” (aggravated by Step small plan), (2) “posting of false information” (which is the material misrepresentation), (3) “induced purchase” (the point of *deception*), and (4) “dump for profit” (the resultant *obtaining* of property).  

Because the indictment reaches this conclusion, the court found that the fact that the defendants *conspired*—as the indictment lists nine defendants—was unnecessary; what mattered was the *act of misrepresentation* that *was intended to deceive.* The court emphasized that the “structural” of the fraud—what the Supreme Court calls the *fraudulent‑inducement theory*—does not rely on a “right‑to‑control” theory, but on *deceiving* and *obtaining* property. Thus the indictment’s asserted scheme satisfied § 1348’s first element.

---

## Assessment of the “Intent to Defraud” Element  

The second element involves an *intent to defraud,* which the court defined as “an intent to deceive *and* to cause some harm to result from that deceit”【III】【A】. In the analysis the court deliberately examined whether the indictment provides evidence of *intent* apart from *deceptive conduct.* The indictment read that the defendants “were intended to *profit* from this scheme”【I】【I】. In this context, the court adopted the Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* holdings, which hold that the fraud statutes do not require the defendant to *intend to leave the victim worse off.* The policy purpose is to prevent *any* fraudulent scheme from resulting in the victim being misled into parting with money or property, *regardless* of a monetized loss.  

Because the indictment ties the defendants’ *particular act of misrepresentation* to a *financial objective*—the $114 million profit—it satisfies the “intent to deceive” requirement. The fact that the defendants are “banking on the money” further underlines an *intent* that is demonstrable. The court concluded that the indictment thoroughly alleges the *intent to defraud* element and did not err in that respect.

---

## Discussion of Victim Loss Requirement and Why It Is Unnecessary  

A key question raised by the defendants was whether the indictment must allege the *victim’s loss* to meet the statutory pleading standard. The court gave particular attention to the Supreme Court’s rulings that clarified that an *evidence of loss* is not a *mirror‑image* requirement. The *Ciminelli* decision rejected the “right‑to‑control” theory, which relied on deprivation of the right to information, but that theory was not called upon in this indictment. *Kousisis* clarified that the fraudulent‑inducement theory protects money and property and dispenses with a requisite for the victim’s net loss. 

The court explained explicitly: “...the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm”【A】【B】. The defendants’ followers did, in fact, receive securities; they retained *market‑value* property. Yet that is no defense to the alleged fraud because the fraud’s *object* was the *deprivation of money or property* via deception. As the court repeated, “[a] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—unexpected loss need not be proved. This case is therefore *not* a case of “informative” fraud that simply deprived victims of information but *did* induce them to part with money in exchange for securities.

---

## Distinguishing Material Misrepresentation from Mere Misinformation  

At the heart of a securities‑fraud indictment is the distinction between *mere misinformation*—facts that are simply wrong but not materially significant—and *material misrepresentation.* The court made a point that the indictment’s description of false, misleading statements about security performance satisfies this distinction.  

The language: “*postulated false and misleading information about the security*”【I】 serves to mark statements as *material* because the statements are relevant to any acquisition decision. The court referenced the standard adopted from *Evans* (2025) that a *material misrepresentation* is “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value.” The alleged posts—done publicly on social media—had clear *commercial intent*, and the alleged investors *accepted* them as credible.  The indictment, therefore, satisfies the *materiality requirement* that the Court deems essential.  Because the factual allegations go beyond mere claims or rumors and assert a *false statement* that was *deliberately communicated* to *induce an investment*, the indictment moves safely beyond the realm of misinformation.

---

## Application of Supreme Court Reasoning to Present Facts  

The Court systematically applied the Supreme Court’s *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* doctrines:  

1. **Ciminelli** forced the federal fraud statutes to capture only a *traditional property interest.* The *Constantinescu* indictment did not rely on a right‑to‑control theory; rather it thrust the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory forward.   
2. **Kousisis** eliminated the *harm* requirement, reinforcing that *deception alone* suffices. The court found the indictment adequately documented that the defendants had “intended to profit” via misrepresentation and therefore satisfied the *intent to defraud* element.  
3. The Court also underscored that the *fraudulent‑inducement* approach does *not* depersonalize the misrepresentation; it still holds the defendant liable for *materially false statements* that *obtain* money or property in a *deceptive* manner.  

By heightening the relevance of these cases, the court reaffirmed that the indictment *did* meet all the statutory requisites, and reinforced that the *district court* erred in its dismissal.

---

## Summary of Why the Indictment Meets Sufficiency  

In closure, the court’s opinion lays out a clear framework: sufficiency demands that an indictment *enumerate each prima facie element* (mens rea, actus reus, and for securities fraud, the *scheme* and *intent*). The indictment in *Constantinescu* lists a full scheme: purchase, misrepresentation, induced purchase, dump; and an intent: profit from deception. The Supreme Court’s rulings on the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory and the *absence of a loss requirement* further shore up the indictment’s soundness.  

Thus, the court concluded that the indictment satisfies the requisite elements of § 1348, and that the dismissal by the district court was therefore an error. It reversed and remanded, allowing the case to proceed under the court’s analysis.

# Holding, Remand, and Significance

## Fifth Circuit’s holding: reversal of dismissal, remand for further proceedings  

The Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal of the indictment of the remaining seven defendants—Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, and Mitchell Hennessey—on the ground that the indictment satisfied the statutory requirements for sufficiency under 18 U.S.C. § 1348.  By incorporating the Supreme Court’s clarifications in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, the court held that the indictment properly alleged a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud: it detailed that each defendant bought a security, then used false and misleading information on social‑media platforms to induce other investors to purchase the same securities, artificially inflating the prices, and finally dumped the securities for personal profit.  The chapter’s text states that the indictment therefore enumerated the material elements of the offense—rising prices tied to the defendants’ misrepresentations, the solicitation of investment from unwary followers, and the resulting gain of $114 million.  Because the indictment meets the requisite standard, the court remanded for further proceedings in a manner consistent with this opinion.  

## Procedural implications for defendants and prosecution  

The reversal restores the indictment’s validity, meaning the defendants are once again subject to the district court’s criminal process.  They will be arraigned on the charges of conspiracy to commit securities fraud and multiple counts of securities fraud, and the district court may now consider motions for dismissal, plea negotiations, or other pre‑trial motions.  The defendants retain the right to contest the indictment’s sufficiency on subsequent procedural grounds, but the earlier dismissal can no longer be cited as antedecent to the indictment’s propriety.  For prosecutors, the ruling obligates them to comply with the standards set forth in *Rafoi*, *Fontenot*, and the circuit’s precedent for indictment drafting, while also benefiting from the Supreme Court’s clarification that deceptive inducements do not require a demonstrable economic loss to the victim.  The outcome underscores that the district court must now proceed under the premise that the indictment is lawfully actionable, encouraging the prosecution to collect and present supporting evidence of the alleged social‑media–based pump‑and‑dump scheme.  

## Broader implications for securities fraud prosecutions, including influencer cases  

This decision marks a tangible expansion of the reach of § 1348 against individuals who exploit social media influence for financial gain.  By affirming that misrepresentations posted on social‑media platforms that coax followers into buying securities constitute a “fraudulent‑inducement” scheme, the court signals that prosecutors may successfully target modern influencers who otherwise might have evaded traditional fraud statutes.  It therefore clarifies that the statutory prohibition against “spraying false or misleading information” extends to the virtual marketplaces nurtured by online content creators.  The holding also demonstrates that the right‑to‑control theory, rejected in *Ciminelli*, cannot be a substitute where the misrepresentation is aimed at inducing a purchase rather than depriving someone of a future decision‑making right.  Consequently, state and federal prosecutors will likely utilize the insights from this case to draft indictments and build cases that hinge on the sophisticated interplay between social‑media influence, deceptive statements, market manipulation, and the acquisition of money or property.  

## Clarification of fraudulent‑inducement theory licensing for future cases  

The opinion makes explicit that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is a legitimate analytic framework under the federal fraud statutes.  The court counters the defendants’ claim that the indictment fails to allege a “protected property interest” by stressing that the misrepresentations compel followers to part with money or securities.  It points out that, unlike the right‑to‑control theory, the fraudulent‑inducement theory “protects money and property” and does not require the victim to experience a net pecuniary loss.  Thus, future cases may lawfully rely on the premise that a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property” as a sufficient basis for a securities‑fraud conviction.  Prosecutors and defense counsel will therefore pay particular attention to the precise portrayal of deceit in an indictments’ factual matrix and recognize that the absence of a demonstrable loss is no barrier to securing a valid fraud charge.  

## Potential policy considerations on the victim loss requirement  

The reversal re‑enforces the Supreme Court’s stance—codified in *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*—that a victim’s economic loss is not a prerequisite for proving a fraud conviction.  This policy direction broadens the statutory enforcement envelope but also raises practical questions about the protection of protected expression.  In an age where investors watch a single tweet and decide on a trade, practitioners will need to balance the enforcement of deceitful conduct with safeguarding legitimate speech—the periphery of which might be labeled as financial advice but is lawful.  The decision prompts discussion about whether virtue‑of‑the-law or harm‑based tests appear more appropriate in guiding future prosecutions, and it may influence legislative efforts that aim to refine the interplay between fraud statutes and free‑speech concerns.  

## Key take‑aways for law students regarding indictment drafting and prosecution  

1. **State each material element** – The district court’s review of indictment sufficiency hinges on whether the indictment enumerates every element of the offense.  
2. **Use the fraudulent‑inducement theory** – This theory can cover deceptive statements that convince victims to hand over money or property, even if no net loss occurs.  
3. **Avoid reliance on “right‑to‑control”** – The Supreme Court’s rejection of that theory in *Ciminelli* means prosecutions must pivot to a scheme that directly obtains property.  
4. **Highlight the inducement to purchase** – Specifying that followers bought securities because of false claims is central to connecting the defendants to the fraud.  
5. **Clarify the defendants’ intent** – Allegations that the defendants intended to profit by tricking investors satisfy the “intent to defraud” requirement.  
6. **Include evidentiary references** – Pointing to the posts on social‑media and the resulting price inflation underpins the factual basis of the indictment.  
7. **Recognize that loss is not required** – Acknowledging that the fraud statutes do not mandate a victim’s economic loss removes a potential argument the defense might raise.  

For students learning how to read and understand appellate opinions, this case demonstrates how a court can traverse the line between statutory wording and evolving social‑media phenomena, affirm a longstanding fraud doctrine, and jointly shape future prosecutorial strategy.
