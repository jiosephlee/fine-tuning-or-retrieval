Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 3: # Securities Fraud Statutes, Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory, and Supreme Court Guidance

## 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and § 1349: statutory purpose and language  
The federal securities‑fraud statutes are codified in two complementary provisions. 18 U.S.C. § 1348 prohibits the execution of “any scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.” 18 U.S.C. § 1349 permits the prosecution of conspiracy to commit the conduct set out in § 1348.  The statutory language is deliberately broad, allowing the courts to define what constitutes a “scheme” and what “deceive” means in each context.  The Court’s opinion underscores that § 1348 draws “key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” (United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6), and that the same elements—scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and material misrepresentation—apply to this statute.

## Elements of securities fraud: scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, material misrepresentation  
To satisfy § 1348, an indictment must allege: (1) a *scheme to defraud*—a plan that uses false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises; (2) an *intent to defraud*—the defendant’s purpose to deceive and cause some harm; and (3) a *material misrepresentation*—an untruthful or misleading statement intended to influence the victim’s decision.  The Court lays out the elements in the opinion’s discussion of the statute, noting that “the ‘scheme to defraud’ element requires a material misrepresentation ‘intended to deceive others … to obtain something of value, such as money.’”  The Court further clarifies that the *intent to defraud* “requires an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.”  These elements are the foundation for the remainder of the analysis.

## Traditional property‑interest requirement  
The federal fraud statutes are limited to protecting *traditional* property interests.  The Court explains that “the law protects only traditional property interests” and references that the Supreme Court has characterized the *right‑to‑control* theory as attempting to treat the mere possession of valuable economic information as property.  Since such information is not a property interest, a claim based solely on depriving someone of that information cannot satisfy the statutory requirement.  This limitation is pivotal when distinguishing the right‑to‑control theory from the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

## Right‑to‑control theory: definition and Supreme Court rejection (Ciminelli)  
The right‑to‑control theory proposes that a “victim’s property is the right to control the victim’s assets,” thereby allowing a defendant to defraud by depriving a victim of that right.  The Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli v. United States* rejected this theory, holding that “a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information,” and that such information is not a traditional property interest.  The Court emphasized that the federal fraud statutes do not extend to purely intangible rights to information.  Importally, the Court noted that the *right‑to‑control* theory has no authority to support a securities‑fraud conviction.

## Fraudulent‑inducement theory: definition and application to securities fraud  
By contrast, the fraudulent‑inducement theory posits that a defendant uses a *material misstatement* to induce a victim to *truly* part with money or property.  The Supreme Court in *Kousisis v. United States* affirmed that this theory remains viable: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.”  This theory is distinct from the right‑to‑control theory and permits the prosecution of schemes that simply rely on deceptive inducement to obtain payment, without requiring actual financial harm to the victim.  In the context of securities fraud, it allows the government to charge a defendant for pushing investors to buy securities through false statements, as the defendant’s objective is the monetized gain of the investor’s money, not merely the loss of information.

## Supreme Court cases: Ciminelli and Kousisis in context  
The *Ciminelli* decision clarified that the securities‑fraud statutes are limited to traditional property rights, effectively rejecting the right‑to‑control framework.  The *Kousisis* decision extended the series of decisions that recognize the fraudulent‑inducement framework, stating that the burden rests on the defendant to prove that “no true mistake” was made and that the defendant *obtained the victim’s money or property* through fraudulent means.  These rulings supply the Court’s guiding principle in evaluating whether an indictment alleges sufficient fraud: the defendant must be shown to have used false representations to gain money or property, regardless of the victim’s net loss.

## Alignment of the indictment’s allegations with the fraudulent‑inducement theory  
In the present case, the indictment alleged that the defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” (indictment ¶ 13).  This language satisfies the *material misrepresentation* requirement and specifies that the defendants sought the money of their followers.  The indictment further contends that the scheme “‘pump’ the price … and ‘dump’ by secretly selling the security for a profit,” demonstrating an explicit *intent to defraud* by profiting from the induced purchases.  Because the indictment identifies the *deprivation of money* rather than the mere loss of information, it aligns squarely with the fraudulent‑inducement theory.  Accordingly, the elements enumerated in § 1348 are satisfied.

In its holding, the Court reversed the district court’s dismissal, holding that the indictment sufficiently states a crime under § 1348 and § 1349 because it satisfies the elements of a scheme to defraud, introduces intent to defraud, and alleges a material misrepresentation that leads to the victim’s transfer of money or property.  The Court’s analysis shows that the indictment does not rely on the discarded *right‑to‑control* theory but rests on the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory affirmed by the Supreme Court.  The appellate court therefore remanded the case for further proceedings, consistent with its findings.
