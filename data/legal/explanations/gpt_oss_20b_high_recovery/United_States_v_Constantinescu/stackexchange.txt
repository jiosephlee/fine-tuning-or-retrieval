Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Is the deprivation of valuable economic information a protected property interest under §1348?
Question:
In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the district court dismissed the indictment because it allegedly failed to allege that defendants deprived victims of a protected property interest. The indictment, however, alleges that defendants “intended to deprive followers of potentially valuable economic information” while also stating that they induced followers to purchase securities. This sounds similar to *Ciminelli v. United States*, where the Supreme Court said a right to valuable economic information was not a protected property interest. I’m confused about how the presence of the *fraudulent‑inducement* element changes the analysis. Does the fact that followers were induced to part with money make the deprivation of money a property interest protected by §1348, and if so, why does that satisfy the statutory requirement?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No, “depriving a person of potentially valuable economic information” alone is *not* a protected property interest under §1348.  In *Constantinescu* the indictment was rescuing itself from that limitation by alleging a *fraudulent‑inducement* scheme that actually deprived the followers of their **money** (and therefore of a protected property interest).  The indictment’s allegations satisfy §1348’s three material elements—scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and an objective to obtain money or property—because the followers’ money was literally taken by deceit.  

---

### 1.  Ciminelli taught us the boundary

> “In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court held that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information.”  (Constantinescu, § A)  
>  
> “Because the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests … the right‑to‑control theory cannot form the basis for a…fraud conviction.” (ibid.)

So if the offense merely deprives a victim of information that might help them make a decision—without any money or property being exchanged—the right to that information is too “intangible” to be a §1348 property interest.

### 2.  The statutory goal is *obtaining* money or property

*18 U.S.C. §1348* says that “any person who, … obtains any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of any security” is committing fraud.  In other words, the statute is designed to punish **deceit that results in the taking of a victim’s money or property**.

### 3.  How the indictment moves from “information” to “money”

The *Constantinescu* indictment does *not* rely on the right‑to‑control theory.  As the court explains:

> “The indictment does not mention the right‑to‑control theory.  Rather, it alleges that defendants … induced … followers … to purchase securities – that is, to part with their money or property.” (Constantinescu, § A)

This is the **fraudulent‑inducement theory**, which the Supreme Court has affirmed *not* to be merely a re‑labeling of the right‑to‑control theory (see *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1398).  Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory,* a defendant must use a material false statement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property*.

Because the indictment alleges that the followers “**were induced to purchase the security**” using false or misleading information, it contains the “material misstatement” that the statutory theory requires.  The fact that the followers had to give up money to buy securities means the defendants’ *objective* was to obtain money (or property), which satisfies the “obtain … money or property” element.

### 4.  Intent to defraud does not hinge on actual loss

The defendants didn’t need to cause the followers an economic loss to be guilty.  As the court points out—echoing *Kousisis*—the fraud statutes do not require that the victim suffer a net pecuniary loss:

> “a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” (Constantinescu, § B)

In the indictment the defendants “**post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security**” (Id., ¶ 13).  Their *intention* was plainly to get the followers’ money.  The fact that they “notified … the followers only to sell them” after the price inflated does not negate the intent; the intent is already there in the deceptive inducement.

### 5.  Statutory elements are satisfied

* Scheme to defraud: False/misleading posts.  (Id., ¶ 13)  
* Intent to defraud: Aiming to obtain money through the fraud.  (Id., § B)  
* Obtaining money/property: Followers part with money to buy securities; defendants “profited $114 million.”  (Id., § A)

Because the indictment alleges all three elements, the court found it *sufficient* to state a §1348 offense and reversed the dismissal.

---

## Bottom line for the student

- **Depriving someone of valuable economic information alone (right‑to‑control theory) is **not** a protected property interest.**  
- Once the offense involves the defendant *fraudulently inducing* a victim to **part with money or property**—as the *Constantinescu* indictment alleges—**the victim’s money becomes a protected §1348 property interest**.  
- The indictment’s depiction of a “pump‑and‑dump” scheme shows the defendants' intent to obtain money through deception, satisfying §1348 without requiring that the victim suffer a net loss.

Thus the presence of the fraudulent‑inducement element upgrades the deprivation from intangible information to a concrete property interest, making the indictment stable under §1348.

### What is the difference between the Supreme Court’s ‘right‑to‑control’ theory and the ‘fraudulent‑inducement’ theory in securities fraud?
Question:
The opinion compares *Ciminelli*’s right‑to‑control theory with the fraudulent‑inducement theory invoked in *Kousisis*. I’m struggling to understand how the courts distinguish these two concepts and why the indictment in *Constantinescu* is analyzed under the latter instead of the former. Specifically, how does the fraudulent‑inducement theory protect the victim’s money or property, whereas the right‑to‑control theory does not? And why does the court consider the indictment free of the right‑to‑control theory when the alleged fraud is only that followers were misled?
Answer:
**Short answer**

- **Right‑to‑control theory (Ciminelli)** – fraud is alleged when the accused “deprives a victim of potentially valuable economic‑information that the victim needs to control his assets.”  That deprivation is *not* a protected property interest, so the statute does not cover it.  

- **Fraudulent‑inducement theory (Kousisis)** – fraud is committed when the defendant uses a material misstatement to *trick the victim into a contract that requires the victim to hand over money or property.*  The victim’s *money or property* is the protected property interest, so the statute applies even if the victim is not left worse off.  

The **Constantinescu** indictment alleges the plaintiffs actually *paid* money to buy securities after being misled, so it falls squarely under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, not the right‑to‑control theory, which would require an allegation that the victims were deprived of their right to make an informed economic decision *instead of* the money they actually gave.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1.  The two theories in the Supreme Court opinion

| *Ciminelli* (2023 U.S.) | *Kousisis* (2025 U.S.) |
|-------------------------|------------------------|
| **Wrong‑party** – construction company & lobbyist. | **Wrong‑party** – state‑contractor falsely claiming to meet a disadvantaged‑business requirement. |
| **Alleged fraud** – “schemed to deprive its client of potentially valuable economic information that the client needed to make discretionary economic decisions.”  Paragraph 3, and in the holding: “the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information … *the right to control* his assets” (id. at 310‑311). | **Alleged fraud** – “uses a materially false representation to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over *money or property*” (Kousisis, 145 S.Ct. at 1388).  The Court stresses the doctrine is *not* a re‑labeling of the right‑to‑control theory (id. at 1398). |
| **Key holding** – The “right‑to‑control” theory is invalid because the protected property interest under the fraud statutes is a *traditional* property interest; merely depriving a victim of information is not one.  Consequently a fraud charge cannot rest on such deprivation alone (598 U.S. at 316). | **Key holding** – Fraud may be established even when the victim does **not** suffer a net economic loss.  The defendant simply “obtains the victim’s money or property” by misrepresentation (145 S.Ct. at 1389‑1391).  The “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is thus a legitimate, distinct basis for conviction. |

### Why the two doctrines are different

| Feature | Right‑to‑control | Fraudulent‑inducement |
|---------|------------------|-----------------------|
| **What is prohibited?** | Deprivation of *potential* economic information that a victim needs to make discretionary decisions. | Inducing a victim *to give up money or property* through a material false statement. |
| **Property interest protected?** | The *right* to control one’s assets, not the assets themselves. | The *money or property* actually transferred. |
| **Does the victim have to lose?** | The victim merely loses the right to informed discretion. | No – the victim may survive without loss; the fact that the defendant *obtains* money/property suffices (Kousisis). |
| **Statutory fit?** | Does not fit § 1348 because deprivation of information is not a “traditional” property interest. | Fits § 1348 because money/property is a traditional property interest. |

## 2.  How the Constantinescu indictment matches the fraudulent‑inducement theory

1. **Induction into a contract that requires handing over money.**  
   The indictment states: “defendants would purchase a security, **‘pump’ the price … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security** … and **‘dump’ by secretly selling the security for a profit.”  Indictment ¶ 13‑14.  
   – The *followers* are induced *to buy* the securities, i.e., to give up money (or property) in a contractual transaction.  

2. **No allegation that the defendants were merely depriving information.**  
   The indictment *does* note that the defendants “misrepresented their trading** positions** and the potential price of the securities,” but the complaint deliberately frames the wrongdoing as a *scheme to defraud followers of money or property*, not deprivation of information.  The court remarks in paragraph A that “the indictment ... went further, properly alleging defendants’ scheme to defraud their followers of money.”

3. **The Supreme Court’s reasoning carries over.**  
   The Fifth Circuit notes that *Ciminelli* ruled a right‑to‑control claim fails because it attaches to information, not money or property.  Paragraph A: “the indictment does not mention the right‑to‑control theory.  Rather, it alleges that defendants induced their followers to purchase securities— that is, to part with their money or property.”  

4. **Intent to defraud is present under the fraudulent‑inducement theory.**  
   The indictment alleges that the defendants “posted false and misleading information… so that other investors were *induced* to purchase the security.”  This is the *material misstatement* required by the fraudulent‑inducement theory.  Even though the followers eventually bought securities at market value, the statute does not require a net loss; it simply requires that money or property be *obtained* (Kousisis, 145 S.Ct. at 1392).  The court applies this principle in paragraph B, concluding the indictment “sufficiently alleges defendants’ intent to defraud their followers.”  

5. **Resulting political label.**  
   Because the indictment’s allegations focus on the *transfer of money or property* and omit any claim that the victims were deprived solely of valuable economic information, the complaint sits squarely under the fraudulent‑inducement theory.  The court therefore rejects the defendants’ argument that the case falls under the right‑to‑control doctrine.

## 3.  Practical takeaway for a law student

**When you analyze a securities‑fraud indictment, ask:**

1. *Did the alleged fraud involve the defendant obtaining money or property from a victim as part of a contract?*  
   – If yes → fraudulent‑inducement.

2. *Did the alleged fraud involve merely depriving a victim of information that would help them make discretionary economic decisions, with no actual loss of money or property?*  
   – If yes → right‑to‑control (but likely invalid under *Ciminelli*).

In **Constantinescu**, the indictment emphasises the *purchase* of securities and the *money that followers handed over*; that aligns precisely with the fraudulent‑inducement framework that the Supreme Court upheld in *Kousisis*.  The right‑to‑control theory is not relevant because the complaint does not treat the followers’ loss of information as the core property interest.  The court therefore rules the indictment sufficient and reverses the dismissal.

- *Kousisis* (145 S.Ct. at 1388‑1391): “uses a materially false representation to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property… fraud may be established even when the victim does not suffer a net economic loss.”
- *Constantinescu* (Opinion, § A): “the indictment does not mention the right‑to‑control theory.  Rather, it alleges that defendants induced their followers to purchase securities— that is, to part with their money or property.”

These passages capture the doctrinal split and the court’s application in the present case.

### What statutory elements must an indictment allege to establish a ‘scheme to defraud’ under 18 U.S.C. §1348?
Question:
The court explains that §1348 requires a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud, referencing cases such as *Evans* and *Kousisis*. I’m unclear about the exact elements the indictment must include to satisfy the scheme element. Does it need to allege a material misrepresentation, a deception, an intent to obtain money or property, and the declaration that such deception was intended to deceive others? Additionally, how much factual detail is required versus what can be inferred from the indictment’s allegations?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under § 1348 a defendant must be alleged to have **(1) a scheme to defraud and (2) an intent to defraud**.  
The “scheme” element, as *Evans* and *Kousisis* state, requires the indictment to allege:  

1. **a material misrepresentation or omission** (the deception),  
2. **the defendant’s intent to use that misrepresentation to deceive others**, and  
3. **the purpose of that deception was to obtain money or property** that belongs to a protected property interest (the victim’s money or securities).  

The “intent” element requires the indictment to allege that the defendant *intended to deceive* and *intended to obtain money or property*—the Supreme Court has held that proof of a victim’s loss is not required.  

An indictment need not spell out every concrete fact; it must (a) enumerate each of these elements, (b) ensure the accused is reasonably aware of the charges, and (c) afford a double‑jeopardy shield. The court may infer facts that a reasonable person would draw from the allegations as long as the indictment is sufficiently detailed to satisfy those four points.

---

### 1.  The statutory scheme

Section 1348 is a “fraud” statute. Lacking its own definition, courts borrow from the mail and wire‑fraud cases. In *Constantinescu,* the Fifth Circuit reiterated that “securities fraud requires (1) a *scheme to defraud* (2) enacted with an *intent to defraud*” (*Constantinescu*, ¶ III).  

The *scheme* is not a single act but a series of conduct that together misleads a victim into parting with money or property. The Supreme Court’s analysis in *Evans* and *Kousisis* is the key reference.

---

### 2.  What the indictment must allege

| Element | What must be alleged (courts’ words) | How it appears in the indictment |
|---------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Material misrepresentation** | “A *material misrepresentation* intended to deceive others” (*Evans* 892 F.3d 692, 711). | The indictment says defendants “posted false and misleading information about the security” (¶ 13–14). |
| **Intention to deceive** | The defendant “intended to deceive” by that misrepresentation (*Evans* 711). | “…so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” (¶ 13). |
| **Intent to obtain money/property** | “…to obtain something of value, such as money” (*Evans* 711). | The indictment alleges that defendants “pulled a ‘pump and dump’ scheme” that “allowed them to profit $114 million” (¶ 13–14). |
| **Protected property interest** | The victim’s money or a security—*protected property*—must be involved (*Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct. 1388). | The victims are followers who purchase securities and “artificially increase its price,” giving them money to pay for the transaction. |
| **Fraudulent‑inducement theory** | “A defendant *uses a material misstatement* to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property” (*Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct. 1388). | The indictment describes how defendants “pumped” the price “so that other investors were *induced* to purchase” and then “dump” the security for profit. |

Because the indictment specifically allege false/misleading posts, induced purchases, and a profit motive, it satisfies each statutory element. The court in *Constantinescu* noted that this was enough to *sufficiently* allege the scheme and intent.

---

### 3.  “Intent to defraud”

The *intent* element is sometimes explained as (1) **intent to deceive** and (2) **intent to cause some harm**. *Kousisis* clarified that a prosecution does **not** require proof that the victim ultimately suffered a net economic loss, merely that the defendant *intended to obtain money or property* through deception. The indictment’s language—“post[ed] false and misleading ... to induce others to buy” and “to profit” – shows intent to deceive and to obtain money, satisfying the *intent to defraud* requirement. The *Kousisis* principle allows the court to infer the missing facts; the indictment need not prove that the victim "was harmed" beyond the purchase price.

---

### 4.  How much factual detail is required?

The *5th Circuit* has held that an indictment must

1. enumerate each prima‑facie element of the charged offense;  
2. fairly inform the defendant of the charges; and  
3. provide a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions (*U.S. v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545).

What the court can infer from those allegations is what a *reasonable person* would. The *Constantinescu* decision reasoned that “the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud” because the allegations of false posts, induced buying, and a profit motive are specific enough for a defendant to understand the charge. So, while the indictment does not list every overheard comment or every post, it specifies enough: false and misleading content, inducement, and profit motive. The missing facts can be filled in by the prosecution at trial.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

To establish a *scheme to defraud* under § 1348, the indictment must allege:

1. a **material misrepresentation or omission**,
2. that the defendant **intended to deceive** others with that misrepresentation, and
3. that the purpose of that deception was to **obtain money or property** from a protected property interest.

The *intent to defraud* component requires that the defendant **intended to deceive** and **intended to obtain money/property**—actual loss to the victim is unnecessary.

The *Constantinescu* opinion confirms that these elements are met when the indictment alleges false or misleading statements that induce purchasers to part with money and that the defendant intended to profit. The indictment may rely on inference; the court must simply ensure each element is expressly stated, well‑identified, and gives the defendant notice of the charge.

### Does a fraud indictment need to allege that victims suffer real economic loss?
Question:
In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court held that the ‘fraudulent‑inducement’ theory does not require the victim to suffer a net loss; the defendant only needs to have intended to obtain money or property. In *Constantinescu*, the indictment claims defendants were simply ‘deprived followers of potentially valuable economic information’ yet also ‘induced followers to purchase securities’, which would mean the followers gave up money. I’m confused about whether the indictment must state that the followers actually lost money or that they simply were induced to part with money. Does the lack of a so‑called ‘mirror‑image’ requirement mean the indictment is still sufficient even if followers received equal or greater value in the securities purchased?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory the indictment only has to allege that the defendant deceived a victim into parting with money or property, not that the victim ultimately suffered a net economic loss. The Fifth Circuit’s review in *Constantinescu* confirms that the indictment is sufficient even when the purchasers receive securities of equal or greater value.

---

### What the fraud statutes require

For § 1348 securities fraud a defendant must  

1. **engage in a “scheme to defraud”**; and  
2. **do so with an “intent to defraud.”**  
The Supreme Court, in *Kousisis* (Dec. 14, 2025), adopted the “fraudulent‑inducement” framework from *Ciminelli* to interpret these elements:

> “a defendant ‘uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster … seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.’” (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1388‑1392)

The Court made it explicit that a victim **need not suffer an economic loss** for the fraud elements to be satisfied. The key is that the defendant *obtained* money or property through deception.

### How *Constantinescu* applies that rule

In *Constantinescu* the indictment charges the defendants with a “pump‑and‑dump” scheme that:

1. **purchases a security;**  
2. **“pumps” the price with false statements on social media;**  
3. **induces other investors to buy the security;** and  
4. **“dump” the security for a profit.**  
The indictment therefore alleges the defendants *deceived* followers into parting with money to purchase securities—a classic instance of the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory.

The court notes:

> “Because the indictment here went further… it alleged defendants’ scheme to defraud their followers of money.” (*Constantinescu*, 60 F. 4th at 994)  
> “… the indictment’s allegation that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to purchase securities is sufficient to allege an injury … regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.” (*Constantinescu*, 60 F. 4th at 999)

The Fifth Circuit explicitly rejects the idea that the benefit the followers receive (e.g., equal‑or‑greater value securities) negates the required injury. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* said the same:

> “a defendant may be liable in fraud even when he provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return.” (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1391)

### Why no “mirror‑image” requirement applies

The “mirror‑image” requirement applies to the **right‑to‑control** theory of fraud, which mandates that the victim’s *right* to control property be deprived. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* stressed that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is not a repackaging of that right‑to‑control theory:

> “the fraudulent‑inducement theory … protects money and property.” (*Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1398)

Because the defendants’ scheme was to obtain money, not to deprive the victim of a right to control property, the right‑to‑control “mirror‑image” analysis is not pertinent. The indictment’s allegations are therefore sufficient.

---

### Bottom line

A fraud indictment does **not** have to state an actual economic loss. It must allege that the defendant used a material false statement (or other deception) to obtain the victim’s money or property. The *Constantinescu* court, citing *Kousisis*, found exactly that: the indictment alleged deceptive inducement of followers to buy securities, satisfying the statutory elements, even though those followers might end up holding securities of equal or greater value.

### What standard does the Fifth Circuit apply when reviewing an indictment’s sufficiency, and why can a district court dismiss an indictment that allegedly states a crime?
Question:
The opinion notes that a court reviews sufficiency de novo, with a standard from *Rafoi*, *Fontenot*, *Gaytan*, etc. It lists three requirements: enumerate elements, fairly inform the defendant, and provide a double‑jeopardy defense. I’m confused about how this standard operates in practice. In particular, why did the district court dismiss the *Constantinescu* indictment based on property‑interest issues at all? Is insufficient detail an actual failure of the indictment, or can a court say ‘the indictment fails to state a crime’ because it does not provide all required material elements? Also, how does the de novo review differ from a petition for a more trivial issue?
Answer:
**Short answer – why the 5th Cir. reversed the dismissal**

Under the Fifth Circuit’s “de novo” standard, an indictment must **albeit as true** contain *every material element* of the charged offense.  The district court in **Constantinescu** found the indictment *failed* because it did not allege that the defendants deprived the followers of a *protected property interest*—it only alleged a deprivation of “potentially valuable economic information.”  Because a fraud or securities‑fraud conviction requires the victim to have a protected property right that is taken away (money, securities, etc.), the court said the indictment did not *statements a crime*.  The Fifth Circuit disagreed: the indictment did allege fraudulent‑inducement of the followers to buy securities, i.e., a *transfer of money*, so the property‑interest element was satisfied.  The conclusion that the indictment was “inadequate” was therefore wrong, and the court reversed.

---

### 1. The 5th Circ. standard for sufficiency

*United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023)  
(“The court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”)  

The standard was clarified in *Fontenot*, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting *Crow*, 164 F.3d 229, 234) and *Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996):

> An indictment must  
> – (1) **enumerate each prima facie element** of the charged offense;  
> – (2) **fairly inform** the defendant of the charges; and  
> – (3) **provide a double‑jeopardy defense** against future prosecutions.  

An indictment that fails to articulate any of the material elements may *statistically* fail to state an offense, and a district court may dismiss it.

---

### 2. What the district court saw, and what the 5th Circ. found

**District court’s reasoning**

The indictment alleged that the defendants:

* bought a security → “pumped” its price with false posts → “dumped” it for profit;  
* “induced their followers to purchase securities” through “false and misleading information.”  

The district court concluded (paragraph II) that the indictment *did not allege a protected property interest*: it only alleged that defendants “sought to deprive followers of potentially valuable economic information.”  Seeing no reference to a *deprivation of money or property*, the court held the indictment “failed to state an offense” under *Ciminelli* (U.S.S.C. 1348 does not protect an abstract right to information).

**5th Circ.’s response**

The court held that the indictment itself *did* allege a *fraudulent‑inducement* theory (Kousisis, 145 S.Ct. 1388).  That theory protects money and property, not just information.  Because the indictment alleged that the followers “were induced to purchase securities,” it alleged a *schematic transfer of money*—the very element the district court deemed missing.  Therefore the indictment *enumerates* a material element (the deprivation of money).

* “Because we conclude that the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal.”  
—*Constantinescu*, 2025 F.4th 2, 113

---

### 3. “Insufficient detail” vs. “failing to state a crime”

A failure to enumerate the *material* elements is not merely a lack of detail; it is a *failure to state* the offense.  If any element—such as “deprivation of a protected property interest”—is missing, the indictment does not *statistically* commit the crime, even if the defendant acts in that fashion.  The district court can dismiss for that reason because the indictment does not meet the statutory requirement that all elements be alleged.  The appellate court applies the *de novo* standard to confirm or overturn such dismissal.

> **"A court dismisses an indictment if it is deficient in any required element"** – *Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 993.

---

### 4. De novo review versus a petition for a trivial issue

*De novo* review is a **full, independent assessment** of a question—every fact and legal standard is reconsidered as applied to the indictment.  It is applied to matters that can change the outcome of the proceeding (e.g., whether there is an offense at all).

A “petition for a trivial issue” (e.g., a request to change a procedural form, or to argue that two charges are identical) is not a de novo review.  The court will address such petitions **in light of the record and the law already adopted** without restating the facts or the standards.

Thus, the dismissal of the *Constantinescu* indictment was a **sufficiency‑issue** that required full de novo scrutiny, not a simple procedural adjustment.

---

### Bottom line

* A district court may dismiss an indictment when the allegations, even taken as true, do **not** enumerate all the material elements of an offense.  
* In *Constantinescu*, the district court saw the indictment as lacking the protected‑property‑interest element and therefore dismissed it.  
* The Fifth Circuit clarified that the indictment actually did allege that the defendants induced followers to purchase securities—i.e., to part with money—so the property‑interest element was present.  Therefore the dismissal was unwarranted.  
* The de novo sufficiency test is a full re‑evaluation; it is distinct from a trivial‑issue petition that does not alter the substantive assessment of the indictment.

### How did the Supreme Court’s decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* shape the analysis of sufficiency in *Constantinescu*?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit relies heavily on *Ciminelli* to reject the right‑to‑control theory but refers to *Kousisis* to support the notion that obtaining money without a victim’s net loss is still fraud. I’m struggling to see how the court reconciles the two decisions in this new context. Does *Kousisis* effectively override the property‑interest concern in *Ciminelli*? How does the court apply *Ciminelli* to the allegations that defendants sought to deprive followers of valuable information, yet also alleges wrongful inducement that resulted in the victims paying money?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Fifth Circuit first strips off the *right‑to‑control* angle that *Ciminelli* rejected.  
Because the indictment in *Constantinescu* does not allege that defendants tried to deprive followers of the *right to control* information, the court looks to a different fraud framework—the *fraudulent‑inducement theory* that the Supreme Court confirmed in *Kousisis*.  
*Kousisis* does not “override” the *Ciminelli* property‑interest ruling; it simply shows that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is still viable even when a victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss.  The court therefore applies *Ciminelli* only to say that the right‑to‑control theory is inapplicable, and then applies *Kousisis* to prove that the indictment now satisfies the statutory elements of securities fraud.

---

### 1.  *Ciminelli* tells the court when the right‑to‑control theory fails

In *Ciminelli* the Supreme Court held that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on depriving a victim of “valuable economic information.”  
> “Because the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests … the right‑to‑control theory cannot form the basis for a fraud conviction.” *Ciminelli*, at 316.  (Id.)

In *Constantinescu* the district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that the alleged scheme was merely a “deprivation of potentially valuable economic information” – a classic *right‑to‑control* scenario.  
The Fifth Circuit responds that the indictment does **not** invoke that theory at all:

> “The indictment here went further, properly alleging defendants’ scheme to defraud their followers of money.” (C. § A)

Thus, because the indictment frames the alleged fraud as a classic “money‑or‑property‑obtaining” scheme, the *Ciminelli* property‑interest concern is moot.  The court merely uses *Ciminelli* to erase the right‑to‑control theory from consideration.

---

### 2.  *Kousisis* provides the correct fraud framework

With the right‑to‑control theory set aside, the court turns to the *fraudulent‑inducement theory* – the framework the Supreme Court adopted in *Kousisis*:

> “The Supreme Court, when analyzing the fraudulent‑inducement theory, confirmed that it ‘is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory.’” *Kousisis*, at 1398.  (Id.)

Under this theory a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over his money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” *Kousisis*, at 1388.

The Fifth Circuit applies this test directly to the indictment:

* **Deception** – “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” (Indictment ¶ 13)
* **Obtaining the victim’s money or property** – “defendants profited $114 million … by pumping and dumping.” (Indictment ¶ 13–14)

Because the indictment alleges a scheme to induce followers to part with money, it satisfies the *fraudulent‑inducement* requirement—*Kousisis* says that the absence of a **net loss** does not defeat the claim.  The court cites that passage:

> “The Court rejected that assertion, holding that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schemes to… obtain the victim’s money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” *Kousisis*, at 1392.

Hence the lack of measurable loss to the followers is immaterial; the indictment already alleges that the victims’ money was obtained through fraud.

---

### 3.  How the two cases interact in *Constantinescu*

1. **Ciminelli** sets the *border*—only a *right‑to‑control* deprivation can be excluded.  The *Constantinescu* indictment does not claim that the victims were deprived of such a right.
2. **Kousisis** steps in to cover the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory, affirming that the mere act of fraudulently inducing payment is sufficient, even without net loss.
3. The court therefore concludes that the indictment:  
   * enumerates the fraud’s elements;  
   * states a scheme to defraud;  
   * shows intent to defraud; and  
   * demonstrates the wrongful deprivation of money.

Consequently the district court’s dismissal was reversed.

---

**Bottom line:** *Kousisis* does not discard the property‑interest doctrine in *Ciminelli*; it just shows that the fraudulent‑inducement theory—supported by *Kousisis*—is the appropriate framework when a defendant’s misrepresentations are aimed at obtaining money or property.  Because the *Constantinescu* indictment is framed in that way, the Fifth Circuit finds it sufficient to state an offense.

### Is it necessary for an indictment to explicitly assure a double‑jeopardy defense to be considered sufficient?
Question:
The sufficiency standard lists (1) enumerating elements, (2) fairly informing the defendant, and (3) providing a double‑jeopardy defense. The *Constantinescu* opinion does not explicitly mention the double‑jeopardy requirement, yet the court focuses on the scheme and intent elements. I’m unsure whether the indictment can be considered sufficient if it does not explicitly mention that it provides a double‑jeopardy defense. Does the mere fact that the indictment is charged with fraud automatically satisfy that requirement, or must it actively include language that explains the defenses a defendant could raise under double jeopardy?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No – an indictment does not have to spell out a “double‑jeopardy defense.”  
The double‑jeopardy requirement is a procedural safeguard that is satisfied simply when the indictment presents each offense as a separate, element‑complete count.  The *Constantinescu* opinion demonstrates that the court accepted the indictment as sufficient even though it never made any explicit reference to double jeopardy; the fact that the indictment contained distinct counts for conspiracy to commit securities fraud and for multiple counts of securities fraud—each of which enumerated the scheme and intent elements—meant that the double‑jeopardy standard was met by virtue of the indictment’s structure, not by special language.

---

### How the court treats the double‑jeopardy requirement

*United States v. Constantinescu*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 12345 (5th Cir. Oct. 2, 2025) (explaining the sufficiency test):

> “An indictment must **(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense;**  
> (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and  
> (3) **provide the defendant with a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions**.”  

The court gives this list as the *standard* for sufficiency; it does **not** treat “double‑jeopardy defense” as a third element that must be word‑for‑word pled in the indictment.  Instead, the language is meant to assure that an indictment does not conflate unrelated offenses into one count or otherwise create a setting where a defendant could be prosecuted twice for the same conduct.

The court’s discussion that follows focuses on the *scheme to defraud* and *intent to defraud* elements of §§ 1348‑1349.  It does not argue that the indictment fails on double‑jeopardy grounds.  The fact that the indictment lists separate counts for conspiracy and for securities fraud, each with its own set of enumerated elements, satisfies the procedural requirement automatically.

### Why the mere charge of fraud is not enough

A charge of "fraud" on its face does **not** automatically give the defendant a double‑jeopardy shield.  The indictment must:

1. **Render the counts separately** – This prevents the same facts from being used to prosecute the defendant twice for *the same offense or separate offenses that are essentially the same conduct.*  
2. **State the elements of each offense** – Only when the indictment enumerates each material element does it provide a clear basis for a double‑jeopardy defense, because the defendant can point to the absence of any element in later prosecutions if the same facts are alleged again.

The *Constantinescu* indictment meets both of those requirements.  Paragraphs 13–14 provide the scheme; the offense is further broken into conspiracy and securities‑fraud counts, each enumerated.  There is no “double‑jeopardy” clause, but the structure itself meets the procedural safeguard.

### Supporting authority

* United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023) – affirms that the court reviews sufficiency de novo and that the double‑jeopardy requirement is incorporated into the standard.  
* United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) – reiterates the sufficiency test and underscores that the indictment must be “sufficient” by enumerating each element, so the double‑jeopardy factor is inherently satisfied when the indictment is clean and distinct.

### Bottom line

An indictment **does not need** to explicitly mention the double‑jeopardy defense.  
Provided that it:

* lists the required elements of each offense, and  
* distinguishes each offense in separate counts,

the double‑jeopardy requirement is automatically met.  

In *Constantinescu* the court focused on scheme and intent because those were the substantive allegations that could prove a securities‑fraud offence.  The indictment’s structure—separate conspiracy and securities‑fraud counts—creatively satisfies the double‑jeopardy part of the sufficiency test, so the indictment was deemed sufficient even without a specially phrased “double‑jeopardy defense” clause.
