Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What standard does the Fifth Circuit apply when reviewing an indictment’s sufficiency, and why can a district court dismiss an indictment that allegedly states a crime?
Question:
The opinion notes that a court reviews sufficiency de novo, with a standard from *Rafoi*, *Fontenot*, *Gaytan*, etc. It lists three requirements: enumerate elements, fairly inform the defendant, and provide a double‑jeopardy defense. I’m confused about how this standard operates in practice. In particular, why did the district court dismiss the *Constantinescu* indictment based on property‑interest issues at all? Is insufficient detail an actual failure of the indictment, or can a court say ‘the indictment fails to state a crime’ because it does not provide all required material elements? Also, how does the de novo review differ from a petition for a more trivial issue?
Answer:
**Short answer – why the 5th Cir. reversed the dismissal**

Under the Fifth Circuit’s “de novo” standard, an indictment must **albeit as true** contain *every material element* of the charged offense.  The district court in **Constantinescu** found the indictment *failed* because it did not allege that the defendants deprived the followers of a *protected property interest*—it only alleged a deprivation of “potentially valuable economic information.”  Because a fraud or securities‑fraud conviction requires the victim to have a protected property right that is taken away (money, securities, etc.), the court said the indictment did not *statements a crime*.  The Fifth Circuit disagreed: the indictment did allege fraudulent‑inducement of the followers to buy securities, i.e., a *transfer of money*, so the property‑interest element was satisfied.  The conclusion that the indictment was “inadequate” was therefore wrong, and the court reversed.

---

### 1. The 5th Circ. standard for sufficiency

*United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023)  
(“The court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”)  

The standard was clarified in *Fontenot*, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting *Crow*, 164 F.3d 229, 234) and *Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996):

> An indictment must  
> – (1) **enumerate each prima facie element** of the charged offense;  
> – (2) **fairly inform** the defendant of the charges; and  
> – (3) **provide a double‑jeopardy defense** against future prosecutions.  

An indictment that fails to articulate any of the material elements may *statistically* fail to state an offense, and a district court may dismiss it.

---

### 2. What the district court saw, and what the 5th Circ. found

**District court’s reasoning**

The indictment alleged that the defendants:

* bought a security → “pumped” its price with false posts → “dumped” it for profit;  
* “induced their followers to purchase securities” through “false and misleading information.”  

The district court concluded (paragraph II) that the indictment *did not allege a protected property interest*: it only alleged that defendants “sought to deprive followers of potentially valuable economic information.”  Seeing no reference to a *deprivation of money or property*, the court held the indictment “failed to state an offense” under *Ciminelli* (U.S.S.C. 1348 does not protect an abstract right to information).

**5th Circ.’s response**

The court held that the indictment itself *did* allege a *fraudulent‑inducement* theory (Kousisis, 145 S.Ct. 1388).  That theory protects money and property, not just information.  Because the indictment alleged that the followers “were induced to purchase securities,” it alleged a *schematic transfer of money*—the very element the district court deemed missing.  Therefore the indictment *enumerates* a material element (the deprivation of money).

* “Because we conclude that the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal.”  
—*Constantinescu*, 2025 F.4th 2, 113

---

### 3. “Insufficient detail” vs. “failing to state a crime”

A failure to enumerate the *material* elements is not merely a lack of detail; it is a *failure to state* the offense.  If any element—such as “deprivation of a protected property interest”—is missing, the indictment does not *statistically* commit the crime, even if the defendant acts in that fashion.  The district court can dismiss for that reason because the indictment does not meet the statutory requirement that all elements be alleged.  The appellate court applies the *de novo* standard to confirm or overturn such dismissal.

> **"A court dismisses an indictment if it is deficient in any required element"** – *Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 993.

---

### 4. De novo review versus a petition for a trivial issue

*De novo* review is a **full, independent assessment** of a question—every fact and legal standard is reconsidered as applied to the indictment.  It is applied to matters that can change the outcome of the proceeding (e.g., whether there is an offense at all).

A “petition for a trivial issue” (e.g., a request to change a procedural form, or to argue that two charges are identical) is not a de novo review.  The court will address such petitions **in light of the record and the law already adopted** without restating the facts or the standards.

Thus, the dismissal of the *Constantinescu* indictment was a **sufficiency‑issue** that required full de novo scrutiny, not a simple procedural adjustment.

---

### Bottom line

* A district court may dismiss an indictment when the allegations, even taken as true, do **not** enumerate all the material elements of an offense.  
* In *Constantinescu*, the district court saw the indictment as lacking the protected‑property‑interest element and therefore dismissed it.  
* The Fifth Circuit clarified that the indictment actually did allege that the defendants induced followers to purchase securities—i.e., to part with money—so the property‑interest element was present.  Therefore the dismissal was unwarranted.  
* The de novo sufficiency test is a full re‑evaluation; it is distinct from a trivial‑issue petition that does not alter the substantive assessment of the indictment.
