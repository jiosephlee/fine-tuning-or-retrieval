Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Is the deprivation of valuable economic information a protected property interest under §1348?
Question:
In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the district court dismissed the indictment because it allegedly failed to allege that defendants deprived victims of a protected property interest. The indictment, however, alleges that defendants “intended to deprive followers of potentially valuable economic information” while also stating that they induced followers to purchase securities. This sounds similar to *Ciminelli v. United States*, where the Supreme Court said a right to valuable economic information was not a protected property interest. I’m confused about how the presence of the *fraudulent‑inducement* element changes the analysis. Does the fact that followers were induced to part with money make the deprivation of money a property interest protected by §1348, and if so, why does that satisfy the statutory requirement?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No, “depriving a person of potentially valuable economic information” alone is *not* a protected property interest under §1348.  In *Constantinescu* the indictment was rescuing itself from that limitation by alleging a *fraudulent‑inducement* scheme that actually deprived the followers of their **money** (and therefore of a protected property interest).  The indictment’s allegations satisfy §1348’s three material elements—scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and an objective to obtain money or property—because the followers’ money was literally taken by deceit.  

---

### 1.  Ciminelli taught us the boundary

> “In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court held that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information.”  (Constantinescu, § A)  
>  
> “Because the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests … the right‑to‑control theory cannot form the basis for a…fraud conviction.” (ibid.)

So if the offense merely deprives a victim of information that might help them make a decision—without any money or property being exchanged—the right to that information is too “intangible” to be a §1348 property interest.

### 2.  The statutory goal is *obtaining* money or property

*18 U.S.C. §1348* says that “any person who, … obtains any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of any security” is committing fraud.  In other words, the statute is designed to punish **deceit that results in the taking of a victim’s money or property**.

### 3.  How the indictment moves from “information” to “money”

The *Constantinescu* indictment does *not* rely on the right‑to‑control theory.  As the court explains:

> “The indictment does not mention the right‑to‑control theory.  Rather, it alleges that defendants … induced … followers … to purchase securities – that is, to part with their money or property.” (Constantinescu, § A)

This is the **fraudulent‑inducement theory**, which the Supreme Court has affirmed *not* to be merely a re‑labeling of the right‑to‑control theory (see *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1398).  Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory,* a defendant must use a material false statement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property*.

Because the indictment alleges that the followers “**were induced to purchase the security**” using false or misleading information, it contains the “material misstatement” that the statutory theory requires.  The fact that the followers had to give up money to buy securities means the defendants’ *objective* was to obtain money (or property), which satisfies the “obtain … money or property” element.

### 4.  Intent to defraud does not hinge on actual loss

The defendants didn’t need to cause the followers an economic loss to be guilty.  As the court points out—echoing *Kousisis*—the fraud statutes do not require that the victim suffer a net pecuniary loss:

> “a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” (Constantinescu, § B)

In the indictment the defendants “**post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security**” (Id., ¶ 13).  Their *intention* was plainly to get the followers’ money.  The fact that they “notified … the followers only to sell them” after the price inflated does not negate the intent; the intent is already there in the deceptive inducement.

### 5.  Statutory elements are satisfied

* Scheme to defraud: False/misleading posts.  (Id., ¶ 13)  
* Intent to defraud: Aiming to obtain money through the fraud.  (Id., § B)  
* Obtaining money/property: Followers part with money to buy securities; defendants “profited $114 million.”  (Id., § A)

Because the indictment alleges all three elements, the court found it *sufficient* to state a §1348 offense and reversed the dismissal.

---

## Bottom line for the student

- **Depriving someone of valuable economic information alone (right‑to‑control theory) is **not** a protected property interest.**  
- Once the offense involves the defendant *fraudulently inducing* a victim to **part with money or property**—as the *Constantinescu* indictment alleges—**the victim’s money becomes a protected §1348 property interest**.  
- The indictment’s depiction of a “pump‑and‑dump” scheme shows the defendants' intent to obtain money through deception, satisfying §1348 without requiring that the victim suffer a net loss.

Thus the presence of the fraudulent‑inducement element upgrades the deprivation from intangible information to a concrete property interest, making the indictment stable under §1348.
