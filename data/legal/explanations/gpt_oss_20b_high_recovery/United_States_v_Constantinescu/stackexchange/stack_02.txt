Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the difference between the Supreme Court’s ‘right‑to‑control’ theory and the ‘fraudulent‑inducement’ theory in securities fraud?
Question:
The opinion compares *Ciminelli*’s right‑to‑control theory with the fraudulent‑inducement theory invoked in *Kousisis*. I’m struggling to understand how the courts distinguish these two concepts and why the indictment in *Constantinescu* is analyzed under the latter instead of the former. Specifically, how does the fraudulent‑inducement theory protect the victim’s money or property, whereas the right‑to‑control theory does not? And why does the court consider the indictment free of the right‑to‑control theory when the alleged fraud is only that followers were misled?
Answer:
**Short answer**

- **Right‑to‑control theory (Ciminelli)** – fraud is alleged when the accused “deprives a victim of potentially valuable economic‑information that the victim needs to control his assets.”  That deprivation is *not* a protected property interest, so the statute does not cover it.  

- **Fraudulent‑inducement theory (Kousisis)** – fraud is committed when the defendant uses a material misstatement to *trick the victim into a contract that requires the victim to hand over money or property.*  The victim’s *money or property* is the protected property interest, so the statute applies even if the victim is not left worse off.  

The **Constantinescu** indictment alleges the plaintiffs actually *paid* money to buy securities after being misled, so it falls squarely under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, not the right‑to‑control theory, which would require an allegation that the victims were deprived of their right to make an informed economic decision *instead of* the money they actually gave.

## 1.  The two theories in the Supreme Court opinion

| *Ciminelli* (2023 U.S.) | *Kousisis* (2025 U.S.) |
|-------------------------|------------------------|
| **Wrong‑party** – construction company & lobbyist. | **Wrong‑party** – state‑contractor falsely claiming to meet a disadvantaged‑business requirement. |
| **Alleged fraud** – “schemed to deprive its client of potentially valuable economic information that the client needed to make discretionary economic decisions.”  Paragraph 3, and in the holding: “the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information … *the right to control* his assets” (id. at 310‑311). | **Alleged fraud** – “uses a materially false representation to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over *money or property*” (Kousisis, 145 S.Ct. at 1388).  The Court stresses the doctrine is *not* a re‑labeling of the right‑to‑control theory (id. at 1398). |
| **Key holding** – The “right‑to‑control” theory is invalid because the protected property interest under the fraud statutes is a *traditional* property interest; merely depriving a victim of information is not one.  Consequently a fraud charge cannot rest on such deprivation alone (598 U.S. at 316). | **Key holding** – Fraud may be established even when the victim does **not** suffer a net economic loss.  The defendant simply “obtains the victim’s money or property” by misrepresentation (145 S.Ct. at 1389‑1391).  The “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is thus a legitimate, distinct basis for conviction. |

### Why the two doctrines are different

| Feature | Right‑to‑control | Fraudulent‑inducement |
|---------|------------------|-----------------------|
| **What is prohibited?** | Deprivation of *potential* economic information that a victim needs to make discretionary decisions. | Inducing a victim *to give up money or property* through a material false statement. |
| **Property interest protected?** | The *right* to control one’s assets, not the assets themselves. | The *money or property* actually transferred. |
| **Does the victim have to lose?** | The victim merely loses the right to informed discretion. | No – the victim may survive without loss; the fact that the defendant *obtains* money/property suffices (Kousisis). |
| **Statutory fit?** | Does not fit § 1348 because deprivation of information is not a “traditional” property interest. | Fits § 1348 because money/property is a traditional property interest. |

## 2.  How the Constantinescu indictment matches the fraudulent‑inducement theory

1. **Induction into a contract that requires handing over money.**  
   The indictment states: “defendants would purchase a security, **‘pump’ the price … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security** … and **‘dump’ by secretly selling the security for a profit.”  Indictment ¶ 13‑14.  
   – The *followers* are induced *to buy* the securities, i.e., to give up money (or property) in a contractual transaction.  

2. **No allegation that the defendants were merely depriving information.**  
   The indictment *does* note that the defendants “misrepresented their trading** positions** and the potential price of the securities,” but the complaint deliberately frames the wrongdoing as a *scheme to defraud followers of money or property*, not deprivation of information.  The court remarks in paragraph A that “the indictment ... went further, properly alleging defendants’ scheme to defraud their followers of money.”

3. **The Supreme Court’s reasoning carries over.**  
   The Fifth Circuit notes that *Ciminelli* ruled a right‑to‑control claim fails because it attaches to information, not money or property.  Paragraph A: “the indictment does not mention the right‑to‑control theory.  Rather, it alleges that defendants induced their followers to purchase securities— that is, to part with their money or property.”  

4. **Intent to defraud is present under the fraudulent‑inducement theory.**  
   The indictment alleges that the defendants “posted false and misleading information… so that other investors were *induced* to purchase the security.”  This is the *material misstatement* required by the fraudulent‑inducement theory.  Even though the followers eventually bought securities at market value, the statute does not require a net loss; it simply requires that money or property be *obtained* (Kousisis, 145 S.Ct. at 1392).  The court applies this principle in paragraph B, concluding the indictment “sufficiently alleges defendants’ intent to defraud their followers.”  

5. **Resulting political label.**  
   Because the indictment’s allegations focus on the *transfer of money or property* and omit any claim that the victims were deprived solely of valuable economic information, the complaint sits squarely under the fraudulent‑inducement theory.  The court therefore rejects the defendants’ argument that the case falls under the right‑to‑control doctrine.

## 3.  Practical takeaway for a law student

**When you analyze a securities‑fraud indictment, ask:**

1. *Did the alleged fraud involve the defendant obtaining money or property from a victim as part of a contract?*  
   – If yes → fraudulent‑inducement.

2. *Did the alleged fraud involve merely depriving a victim of information that would help them make discretionary economic decisions, with no actual loss of money or property?*  
   – If yes → right‑to‑control (but likely invalid under *Ciminelli*).

In **Constantinescu**, the indictment emphasises the *purchase* of securities and the *money that followers handed over*; that aligns precisely with the fraudulent‑inducement framework that the Supreme Court upheld in *Kousisis*.  The right‑to‑control theory is not relevant because the complaint does not treat the followers’ loss of information as the core property interest.  The court therefore rules the indictment sufficient and reverses the dismissal.

*Key quotations for reference*

- *Ciminelli* (598 U.S. at 316): “the right‑to‑control theory cannot form the basis for a fraud conviction.”
- *Kousisis* (145 S.Ct. at 1388‑1391): “uses a materially false representation to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property… fraud may be established even when the victim does not suffer a net economic loss.”
- *Constantinescu* (Opinion, § A): “the indictment does not mention the right‑to‑control theory.  Rather, it alleges that defendants induced their followers to purchase securities— that is, to part with their money or property.”

These passages capture the doctrinal split and the court’s application in the present case.
