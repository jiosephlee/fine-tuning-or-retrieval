Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Digital Wall Street: Social‑Media Influencers and the New Frontier of Securities Fraud

## Why This Matters

When the Fifth Circuit overturned a district‑court dismissal of a securities‑fraud indictment against a group of YouTube, TikTok, and Twitter personalities, it signaled that the old wire‑fraud framework can be applied to a new breed of “online shop‑keepers.” These aren’t bank‑tellers or licensed brokers; they are college‑grad‑level scroll‑ha‑makers with millions of followers who harbor modest hopes for a quick buck. The court’s decision underlines that an influencer who posts a boom‑tastic selfie about a penny‑stock and then sells it to the same audience can be prosecuted under the federal fraud statutes. The ruling also clarifies how the courts interpret the Supreme Court’s recently carved‑out “fraudulent‑inducement” theory— a theory that, until now, was largely limited to the world of contracting and government procurement. For the first time, that theory is being applied to the influencer arena, and it will be the touchstone for many future cases.

## A New Kind of Pump‑and‑Dump

The case, United States v. Constantinescu, revolves around eight defendants who had a combined follow‑count that swelled into the hundreds of thousands. They leveraged their asserted “trading acumen” to spark a “pump and dump” cycle on a handful of obscure securities. The indictment alleged that the defendants:

1. Purchased the stocks themselves.
2. “Pumped” the price by posting “false and misleading information” on social media.
3. “Dumped” the holdings after the price had risen, making a $114 million profit.

The defendants’ scheme fit the textbook definition of “pump and dump”: they induced their followers to “purchase the security … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” and then privately sold for profit. One might be tempted to dismiss the indictment as mere defamation or a case of reckless promises. The court saw no such naivete. Instead, it chose to test the indictment against the very old poisons of the federal fraud statutes— namely, that the defendants “deceived” and “obtained” money. It found the indictment sufficient.

## The Court's Indictment‑Sufficiency Analysis

Because the Fifth Circuit is required to view an indictment as having presented a “prima facie case,” it opens the case for normal criminal-constitution analysis. The court’s step through the indiction was methodical:

1. The court verified that every element of § 1348 (the “in connection with any security” fraud statute) was enumerated. 
2. The indictment’s misrepresentations were deemed a “material misrepresentation.”  
3. It addressed the defendants’ counter‑argument that the indictment merely claimed that the influencers “intended to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information,” which would fail if the fraud were based on a “right‑to‑control” theory as expounded in *Ciminelli*.

In a clear way, the court found that the indictment had more than the illusion of “deprivation of information” it had to have the “deprivation of money” requisite for fraudulent‑inducement.

## Fraudulent‑Inducement vs. Right‑to‑Control

The Supreme Court bifurcated the federal fraud statutes into two distinct paradigms in *Ciminelli v. State* and *Kousisis v. United States*. The former, the “right‑to‑control” theory, was abandoned because it sought to criminalize the mere proscription of an individual’s right to make decisions with their economic information. The latter, the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory, was preserved exactly because the fraud statutes are designed to protect tangible property interests— money, stock, contracts—rather than abstract knowledge.

The Fifth Circuit reminded the defendants that a “right‑to‑control” claim would fail because the defendants didn’t merely deprive the followers of information. The indictment asserted that they “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” That is the crux of the fraudulent‑inducement theory. The court made it clear that this theory does not require that the victim incur a net pecuniary loss; it only requires that the victim be tricked into parting with money or property. In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court clarified the same point—the victim’s bottom line may remain intact, but the fraud is still present if the defendant used a material misstatement to induce a contract.

This nuance matters because influencer marketing is fundamentally about inducement, not prohibition. A user may click a link and buy a stock even if it turns out to be worthless; the investor lost nothing. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, we can still prosecute the influencer if the investor was lured into a transaction through false statements. This expands the reach of federal fraud statutes into a digital marketing ecosystem that had previously seemed like a gray area.

## The Courage of *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*

While the Fifth Circuit relies heavily on the Supreme Court’s recent holdings, it also subtly signals that the reactionary step to the “right‑to‑control” theory, while long dead, is not dead yet. The court’s threat to scrutinize the “deprivation of economic information” claim indicates that regulators and prosecutors can beware. The stale case of *Ciminelli* will continue to serve as a cautionary tale for plaintiffs and civil litigants who craft their theories around “informed consent” to market “irrational” speculation.

The *Constantinescu* opinion also highlights that the federal fraud statutes still embody a “historic, white‑glove” credibility standard. Even in an algorithmic environment where the influencer may be posting an entire whole being on a five‑minute video, they bear the same burden of materiality and intent. The court reasserts that the indictment does not need to bind the course of a conceptual or abstract relationship; it can accommodate the real‑world digital praxis.

## Doctrine and the Big Picture

The Fifth Circuit’s decision will ripple across several doctrinal frontiers:

- **Circuit Court Interpretation of Securities Fraud**: In a circuit that has not yet produced a robust body of law on § 1348, this case becomes a pivotal building block. Future courts will likely borrow the same structure and threshold test for fraudulent‑inducement and for sufficiency of the indictment.
  
- **Implications for Digital‑Only Retaliation**: The court fits the case squarely under § 1348 and not under the mail/wire statutes, partly because the social‑media posts constitute wire transmissions. That signals that the statutes are still flexible enough to accommodate digital communications and, by extension, the new forms of advertisement.

- **Free‑Speech Prospects**: The dissenters would have argued that the influencers’ “speech” should be protected. The court sidestepped that argument. But the decision also leaves the door open for a future free‑speech solution: an influencer genuinely uninformed about the market may be protected if proof of “intent to defraud” is lacking. The evidence citation around the challengers’ “intent” claim underscores that the statutes require an intent to obtain money or property, not an intent to cause harm.

- **Jurisdictional Scope**: Because the indicators of fraud were distributed on *Twitter*, *TikTok*, and *Foundation*, the case affirms that the federal fraud statutes apply wherever a defendant transmits misleading information across interstate communications—even if the trade is purely online. That fits with the “wire” meaning of § 1348.

## What Comes Next? Regulatory and Policy Implications

### Oversight by the SEC

The SEC’s role in policing securities fraud is already well‑established. But the *Constantinescu* case signals that the SEC should consider new mechanisms to monitor “influencer‑driven” market manipulation. A potential tool is the SEC’s “Helpful‑Regulation‑Due‑Process” initiative, which targets large‑scale “pump‑and‑dump” coordinated through social media. The court’s emphasis on "inducement" is a boon in that the SEC can argue that the influencers’ posts are “material misrepresentations” that could "violate Rule A‑Business Regulation." It is still up for debate whether SEC enforcement can or should unilaterally target these misinformation campaigns, but the federal criminal case presents a strong precedent that such state‑level attempts are likely to be consistent with the securities fraud statutes.

### Platform Liability & Self‑Regulatory Measures

Platforms like Twitter, TikTok, and YouTube are not direct participants in the securities transactions by the influencers, but the business operations of each platform rely on user‑generated content. In the pre‑*Constantinescu* era, the court would have returned the question to the Second Circuit’s *Union of 2001* and *FTC v. Facebook* for platform liabilities. Now, however, the court’s decision indicates that platforms may need to consider new internal reporting obligations: if a 2025 influencer’s post is deemed to be a “material misrepresentation” that induces purchases in federal securities, the platform may be compelled to treat them as a “communications carrier” and may face liability for failing to police or flag misinformation. The regulatory policy may shift to require algorithmic transparency that can flag post‑pump indicators for rapid SEC review.

### Free‑Speech Defenses

Though the Fifth Circuit sidesteps a speech analysis, other courts may dial it in more sharply. If an influencer can prove that they had no “intent to defraud” and were simply misinformed, their speech might be spent under the First Amendment. The trial courts will need to weigh the available evidence that the Duke’s distinction between fraudulent‑inducement and deceptive persuasion has been overstated. The new context of “no net loss to investor” may even promote a “purportedly honest” defense. At present, the *Constantinescu* decision offers no obvious free‑speech safe‑harbor; however, the limited references to the “intent” element may encourage the defense to focus on "lack of intent to deceive" more strictly.

## Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of a dismissal in *United States v. Constantinescu* is the first time a federal appellate court has used the Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* framework to convict a digital influencer of securities fraud. In the language of a law‑blog and a legal printer house, the court has carved a new analogue of the securities‑fraud sheet: just admitting that an influencer posted false statements on a video, and that followers cashed on those statements by buying a security, is adequate to satisfy the indictment. No additional proof of a net loss or a protected property interest is necessary; the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is the new highway.

This decision is not a friendly invitation to the next online “Robert Kiyosaki.” Instead, it is a wake‑up call that the financing Web will not be exempt from the federal brutality when investors are swayed into bandito–style trades. The markets may be new, but the underlying doctrine is simple: if you lie to get money, you’re guilty—report no net loss needed. The regulatory gears are already turning; the SEC, the FTC, and the courts will now have a very concrete case to rely on. The remaining question—do we keep expanding the reach of federal fraud statutes into the social‑media zoo, or do we protect a digital culture that thrives on instant speech? For now, the court’s opinion tells us that time to reevaluate is long overdue.
