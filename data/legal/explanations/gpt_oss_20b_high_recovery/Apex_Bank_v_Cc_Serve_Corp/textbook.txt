Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Parties, Marks, and Procedural History

The route that brought *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* to the Federal Circuit is defined by the identities and aims of the two parties, the specific marks at issue, and the legal steps that unfolded in the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB).  Understanding these elements furnishes a foundation for the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis that will be examined in later chapters.

## Apex Bank  
Apex Bank is a Tennessee‑chartered retail bank that, at the time of the dispute, offered traditional banking products such as checking, savings, mortgages, and loans but did **not** provide credit‑card services.  The bank’s business model included 18 branch locations and was situated in the competitive consumer‑banking market.  Apex sought to expand by launching an internet‑banking brand.  In pursuit of that expansion, the company filed intent‑to‑use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) on August 2019 for the word mark “ASPIRE BANK” and a complementary design mark in connection with “banking and financing services.”  These applications were intended to secure trademark protection for the new online banking venture.  (See *J.A.* 7027‑7029.)

## CC Serve Corp.  
CC Serve Corp. is a credit‑card servicing provider that holds the federal registration (Reg. No. 2126948) for the standard‑character word mark “ASPIRE” with respect to credit‑card services.  The registration was issued in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996.  CC Serve’s business model revolves around arranging credit‑card issuance for banks, which then sell the cards to customers under the ASPIRE brand; the company handles account servicing, payment processing, counseling, and related credit‑card‑related activities.  The firm’s connection to the credit‑card industry hosts a strong commercial presence for the “ASPIRE” mark within that sector.  (See *J.A.* 6927‑6930.)

## ASPIRE BANK  
The marks at issue in Apex’s applications are the word mark **ASPIRE BANK** and an accompanying stylized design.  They were filed for “banking and financing services,” marking a distinct strategy to brand an internet‑banking platform.  Unlike the single‑word “ASPIRE” held by CC Serve, the new marks explicitly added “BANK,” thereby extending the linguistic and visual identifiers.  Apex sought registration of this new mark under the premise that the proposed “ASPIRE BANK” design would resolve any potential confusion with the existing “ASPIRE” mark used by CC Serve.  (See *J.A.* 5243, 7027.)

## Initial 2(d) Opposition filed by CC Serve in February 2020  
In February 2020, CC Serve exercised its right under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act and filed an opposition to the publication of Apex’s “ASPIRE BANK” application.  The opposition contended that the applicant’s marks were “confusingly similar” to CC Serve’s registered mark, citing similarity of appearance and the overlapping nature of the services offered.  The Board attributed the opposition’s premise to the similarity of the goods/services and the potential for consumer confusion.  The resulting TTAB action was guided by the likelihood‑of‑confusion test, a question of law with underlying facts distilled through the DuPont factors.  (See *J.A.* 2, 6931.)

## TTAB refusal based on likelihood of consumer confusion  
The TTAB, applying the 13‑factor DuPont framework, found that the “ASPIRE BANK” marks were likely to cause confusion with the established “ASPIRE” mark.  The Board considered the similarity of the services (credit‑card services vs. banking/financing services), the coexistence of CC Serve in the credit‑card field, and other factors before concluding that registration was barred.  Consequently, the Board refused registration of Apex’s marks, effectively preventing Apex from securing the intended protection for its new internet‑banking brand.  (See *J.A.* 63, 62–63.)

## Appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (2025)  
Unwilling to accept the TTAB’s refusal, Apex Bank filed an appeal with the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit on September 25, 2025, citing 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).  The court reviewed the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and the Board’s findings of fact for substantial evidence under the “two‑step” review standard set forth in *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Communications, Inc.* The Federal Circuit’s decision, penned by Judge Hughes, affirmed the Board’s factual finding that the services were highly similar (the second DuPont factor) but vacated the Board’s decisions on the sixth (similar marks) and first (totality of the marks) DuPont factors, remanding for reconsideration.  (See *J.A.* 103‑104.)  
This procedural trajectory—from opposition to refusal to appellate review—demonstrates the sequential stages of dispute resolution under the Lanham Act and sets the stage for a deeper analysis of the likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry.

# Legal Framework for Trademark Oppositions and Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Analysis

A trademark opposition is a common front‑line dispute in the U.S. trademark system.  The Opposition, drafted under the Lanham Act, is heard by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB); the Board applies the law and the facts to a close set of analytical rules, most famously the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion factors.  A party that disagrees with the TTAB’s decision may seek appellate review.  The appellate court scrutinizes the Board’s conclusions de novo, and evaluates the Board’s findings of fact for substantial evidence.  The case of *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* illustrates the hierarchical relationship between the statute, the TTAB’s discretion, the de‑novo standard of review, and the factual threshold that must support the Board’s decision.  In this section we break down each element of that framework, pointing to the statutory and case law that underpins it.

## Lanham Act § 1052(d): objective – refusal of registration when confusion is likely

Section 1052(d) of the Lanham Act is the statutory gate that authorizes opposition proceedings.  The statute imposes a *testing and refusal provision* that “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods and/or services.”  DuPont’s *evidence‑based* approach is rooted in this objective.  The statute is meant to prevent a party with no actual commercial success from gaining a monopoly over a mark that could mislead consumers into believing it originates from the same source.  As the *Apex* opinion demonstrates, the Board can refuse registration only after satisfying the requirement that the newer mark be likely to cause confusion with a preexisting right.

## TTAB as the first‑level decisionmaker in trademark opposition proceedings

The TTAB, seated within the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office, acts as the adversarial, quasi‑judicial body that adjudicates oppositions.  It has the power to issue decisions that are final except for appeal to the Federal Circuit.  The Board may obtain evidence, hear oral arguments, and apply the DuPont factors to produce a finding of likelihood of confusion.  Importantly, the Board’s term “confusion” is a *question of law* weighted against a body of *factual evidence*.  The *Apex* decision illustrates how the TTAB can transform a factual assessment of service similarities into a legal finding of likelihood.  Although the TTAB has discretion, that discretion is not unlimited; the Board’s conclusions must be grounded in the evidence presented and be legally sound.

## Appellate review: de novo review of legal conclusions, substantial evidence for findings of fact

When the Federal Circuit reviews a TTAB decision, it follows a bifurcated standard:

1. **Legal conclusions** are reviewed *de novo.*  The appellate court treats the TTAB’s wording of legal standards like a new case and applies the law independently, unbound by the Board’s reasoning.  *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc.* established that any limitation or interpretation advanced by the TTAB must be reconsidered.

2. **Facts** are reviewed for *substantial evidence.*  The Supreme Court’s definition in *Consolidated Edison Co. of N.Y.* (1938) and the Federal Circuit’s restatement in *M2 Software* continuingly hold that “substantial evidence is such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.”  An appellate court will not re‑compact the evidence but will check whether the Board’s factual findings are supported by at least that threshold.  In *Apex*, the Court affirmed the Board’s assessment of factors that were supported by substantial evidence (the second DuPont factor) but vacated other findings that lacked sufficient backing.

The statutory mandate that the Board not “consider every DuPont factor” (as long as the factors *relevant and on record*) is complemented by the appellate standard.  The Court therefore has the authority to dismiss conclusions that rely on an overly narrow factual basis.

## Introduction to the DuPont factors and focus on factors two, six, and one

The DuPont factors are thirteen factual questions that the TTAB, and by extension the appellate court, use to analyze likelihood of confusion.  They cover:

1. Similarity of the marks
2. Similarity of goods and services
3. Prior use
4. Strength of the mark, etc.

In *Apex*, the TTAB and the appellate court gravitated to three particular factors:

- **Factor 2 – Similarity of goods/services.**  This factor examines how closely the parties’ offerings overlap.  The Court in *Apex* reaffirmed that the Board correctly assessed a high degree of similarity, distinguishing the BTCC’s credit‑card services from the prospective bank’s “banking and financing” services.  A suggestion that the services were “not directly competitive” was deemed unsubstantiated.

- **Factor 6 – Number and nature of third‑party marks.**  The factor requires an appreciation of how crowded a marketplace is with similar-coded marks.  The Court found the Board's decision to restrict the market to “credit card services” too narrow, given the overlap that the Board had already found between the parties’ services.  The Board had to consider all third‑party marks that applied to similarly-situated goods and services.

- **Factor 1 – Similarity of the marks themselves.**  The visual, phonetic, and semantic kinship of the marks informs the overall commercial impression.  As the *Apex* decision showed, if the Court vacates factor 6, it may need to re‑evaluate factor 1, because the perceived strength or weakness of the marks themselves could shift.

The selection of these factors in *Apex* illustrates how litigants can zero in on particular DuPont factors that appear to hinge upon the Board’s methodological framework or in which the Board’s factual finding may require reinterpretation.

## The interplay between legal standards and factual findings in likelihood analysis

The DuPont factors are not a check‑list of *facts* alone; they are a *synthetic legal analysis* that blends findings into a unifying question: “What is the likelihood that a typical consumer will be confused as to the source of the derivative services?”  The Board’s findings are expressive of *both legal conclusions* (the test of “likelihood of confusion”) and *factual determinations* (quality of evidence).  The appellate court therefore must respect the *legal* underpinnings—such as the de novo standard—but still scrutinize the *vetted facts* of the Board.  This duality appears throughout the *Apex* judgment:

- The Court affirmed factor 2 because the Board’s findings were built on reliable evidence for service similarity.
- The Court found the Board’s analysis of factor 6 defective because it relied on a *narrow factual scope* that was inconsistent with the broader, factually similar services considered in factor 2.  The Court, therefore, vacated the fact‑based finding of a “normal scope of protection” awarded to CC Serve’s mark.
- Because factor 6’s outcome impacts the *commercial strength*, the Court remanded the Board to reassess factor 1.  The interplay shows how a misstep in one factual evaluation can cascade into a different legal consequence.

## Definition of substantial evidence per Supreme Court and appellate precedent

The *Consolidated Edison* standard remains the benchmark for appellate review of TTAB fact findings.  Within this framework, appellate courts consider whether the Board’s facts are on a “substantial” footing: that is, “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind would accept as adequate to support a conclusion.”  The *M2 Software* opinion reinforces that this standard is not “beyond a reasonable doubt” (which is a criminal standard), but it is more stringent than the “preponderance of evidence” needed in civil courts.  Failure to meet the standard permits the appellate court to issue a new finding or remand for new evidence.  *Apex* makes it clear that “substantial evidence” is both a *substantive* and *procedural* anchor: each factor’s factual support is evaluated against it before the appellate court forms a legal conclusion.

## Relevant case law establishing the application of DuPont factors

The Federal Circuit has repeatedly clarified how the DuPont factors are applied in opposition proceedings:

- **M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc.** (2006) – Bifurcated review, de novo legal standards, substantial evidence on fact findings, and the key role of the DuPont factor framework.
- **E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co. v. Daschot Tesoro Chemical Corp.** (1973) – The original source of the DuPont factor set, defining the overall approach to likelihood.
- **Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung… v. New Millennium Sports** (2015) – Clarified the use of third‑party marks (Factor 6) and the nature of “weak” marks as “thinly protected.”
- **Juice Generation, Inc. v. G.S. Enterprises** (2015) – Highlighted that a crowded field of similar marks may mitigate a consumer’s propensity for confusion.
- **Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc.** (1992) – Emphasized that “similar” goods in the sense of the sixth factor do not require identical categories; “similar” goods are judged by consumer perception in the relevant marketplace.
- **Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc.** (2002) – Reinforced that the Board need not evaluate *every* DuPont factor, only those relevant and on record.
- **Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC** (2012) – Emphasized the threshold that similarities need not be identical but can be “related in some manner.”

These cases, carried through to *Apex*, illustrate the practical application of DuPont factors, the conditions under which a Board can retreat from a similarity determination, and the mechanisms for appellate intervention.

---

In sum, the law governing trademark opposition draws on a statutory requirement to avoid consumer confusion, a TTAB that applies the DuPont framework, and an appellate court that reviews legal conclusions afresh while holding the TTAB to a substantial‑evidence standard on factual findings.  *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* demonstrates how appellate jurisprudence forces the Board to broaden its analytical scope—particularly in recognizing the range of third‑party marks and the intrinsic similarity between the parties’ services—when the board’s factual analysis is limited by an overly narrow definition.  Understanding this interplay equips law students to predict which factors a forum will focus on, how a Board may weave evidence into a legal decision, and how an appellate court may reform or uphold that decision.

# DuPont Factor 2: Similarity of Services and the Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Analysis  

In a trademark opposition, the second DuPont factor questions whether the goods or services offered by the parties are related or identical. The Board’s factual and legal determinations on this factor strongly influence whether a consumer is likely to mistake one source for another. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Board devoted considerable analysis to establishing that the services claimed by Apex and CC Serve were highly similar—a finding the Court ultimately affirmed as being supported by substantial evidence. The following discussion walks through the Board’s reasoning process, the evidence that underpinned it, and the Court’s review of the legal standard applied to the factor.

## Dictionary‑Based Definition of “Credit Card Services” and Its Expansion  

Because the Trademark ID Manual had deleted the entry “credit card services,” the Board first turned to a dictionary definition to clarify the scope of the services claimed by CC Serve. The Board determined that “credit card services” encompassed a wide array of activities: issuing credit cards for purchase financing; counseling about credit‑card debt; processing credit‑card payments and transactions; authorizing credit‑card use; monitoring card activity and providing alerts; managing card accounts; and offering access to credit‑score information. This interpretation was articulated in the analysis at J.A. 25‑28. The expansion of the definition was critical because it mapped CC Serve’s claimed services to functions that others might also consider “credit‑card related,” thereby opening the door to a comparative assessment with Apex’s proposed services.

## Comparison of “Banking/Financing Services” to Credit‑Card Issuance  

Having defined “credit card services,” the Board examined the dictionary definitions for the words “banking,” “bank,” and “finance.” It found that these terms historically include the act of extending credit or providing funds, even when the credit is delivered through a credit‑card product. Consequently, the Board reasoned that Apex’s banking and financing services—which involve issuing credit‑card‑like products under a new brand—were in legal overlap with CC Serve’s credit‑card services. The Board explained that the services were “legally identical, in part,” a conclusion it reached by citing the overlap in the core function of credit extension, as documented at J.A. 27‑28 and the subsequent discussion that followed. This legal identity was the linchpin for the similarity conclusion under factor 2.

## Finding That the Services Are “Legally Identical, in Part”  

The Board’s determination that the services were “legally identical, in part” is a pivotal factual finding. It translates the dictionary‑based definitions into the commercial reality of the market: that both parties are engaged in the same underlying financial activity—facilitating consumer credit products. The Board emphasized that such identical service lines fall squarely within the ambit of DuPont’s second factor, which is concerned with whether the goods or services are related or identical. The Board’s finding is captured in its conclusion that the services are highly similar, noted in J.A. 35. Because the factor is a question of fact, the Court’s de‑novo review focuses on whether the Board’s factual determination rests on substantial evidence, not on re‑deciding the legal conclusion itself.

## Use of Third‑Party Registrations to Support Similarity Assessment  

To substantiate the similarity analysis, the Board considered third‑party registrations that explicitly covered the same service categories—both credit‑card services and more broadly banking and financing services. The Board reviewed a group of marks that used the word “Aspire” (or derivatives) in connection with offering or facilitating credit‑card‑related services and, more generally, financial services. This evidence was presented to demonstrate that the market for these services is not isolated to the two parties but includes other users of similar marks faced with the same issues of source identification. The Board cited J.A. 33‑34 to signal that it used these third‑party registrations as corroborative data to argue that the services were indeed similar enough for a factual determination favoring likelihood of confusion.

## Heavy Weight Attributed to Factor 2 by the Board  

Factor 2 can carry significant weight because a close match between parties’ goods or services implores the entire likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis. The Board deliberately stressed that the similarity of Apex’s banking and financing services to CC Serve’s credit‑card services was a strong, affirmative indication that confusion is probable. This viewpoint was articulated in J.A. 35, where the Board noted that, given the high degree of similarity, factor 2 should weigh heavily in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. The Board’s judgement that the factor “weight heavily” reflects the prevailing legal principle that when the services are almost identical, the likelihood of confusion is heightened, thus reinforcing the opposition’s conclusion.

## Court’s Agreement: Substantial Evidence Backing the Board’s Similarity Conclusion  

The Court reviewed the Board’s legal conclusions on a de‑novo basis and facts for substantial evidence. With respect to factor 2, the Court found that the Board’s factual findings—particularly the broad dictionary‑based definition of “credit card services,” the recognition of overlap between banking/financing and credit‑card issuance, the legal identity conclusion, and the corroborating third‑party registrations—were adequately supported by substantial evidence. The Court noted that the Board had carefully considered the descriptions of both parties’ services and had concluded that they are “highly similar.” This finding was affirmed, as the Court held that the Board had not erred in its application of the standard, and that the evidence at hand was sufficient to support that conclusion. The Court therefore left that portion of the Board’s decision intact.

In sum, the Board’s multi‑step analysis of factor 2 in this case exemplifies how a trademark opposition must weave dictionary definitions, comparative service functions, and corroborative market evidence to determine whether the goods or services offered by the parties could be alike enough to create a likelihood of confusion. The appellate Court’s affirmation of the Board’s similarity finding confirms that, under the Lanham Act, a comprehensive assessment of service similarity is foundational to the likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry.

# DuPont Factors 6 and 1: Scope of Third‑Party Marks and Commercial Impression  

The appellate court’s opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* centers on the Board’s analysis of the sixth and first DuPont factors in a trademark opposition under § 2(d).  The Sixth factor requires consideration of the number and nature of third‑party marks in use on similar goods or services; the First factor calls for a comparison of the marks’ shared visual, phonetic, and commercial qualities.  In this case, Apex appealed that the Board had limited its scope of comparable third‑party marks solely to credit‑card‑service users and that it failed to reassess the marks’ commercial impression after vacating that scope.

## The Sixth DuPont Factor: Definition and Requirement  

Under the DuPont framework, the sixth factor assesses “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” (Apex, J.A. 51).  The Board’s analysis is limited to the relevant public and the goods or services that are “similar” to those of the parties.  The standard is purely factual, and the court examines whether the presence of numerous other marks shows the contested mark to be weak or whether the contested mark enjoys a broad scope of protection.  Importantly, the factor does **not** require that a competitor use the same exact goods; it must consider “similar” goods or services, as the court has underscored in previous precedent (see *Olde Tyme Foods*, 961 F.2d at 204).

## Board’s Narrow Definition of the Relevant Public  

In applying the sixth factor, the Board restricted the relevant public to “consumers of credit‑card services” (Apex, J.A. 47).  The Board justified this limitation by focusing its search on third‑party marks that used “Aspire” in the credit‑card arena.  This narrow universe excluded the 42 other registrations that employed “Aspire” for broader banking or financial services, such as checking or loan solutions.  The Board insisted that such marks were “essentially irrelevant” (Apex, J.A. 47), even though its own second‑factor analysis had already concluded that Apex’s banking and CC Serve’s credit‑card services were “highly similar” (Apex, J.A. 27‑28).  By truncating the universe for the sixth factor, the Board omitted similar services that the second factor had already identified as comparable.

## Exclusion of 42 Other Third‑Party Marks Using “Aspire”  

The Court held that the Board’s exclusion of the 42 other third‑party marks improperly narrowed the analysis.  The sixth factor demands that the board consider all marks that could plausibly be used on “similar” goods or services.  The appellate court reasoned that the Board’s prior factual finding of high similarity between the parties’ services should carry forward into the scope analysis for the sixth factor.  There was no evidence that any of the excluded marks were used on goods or services dissimilar enough to merit exclusion.  Accordingly, the Board had to broaden its search to include the full set of Aspire‑related marks employed in the broader financial‑services marketplace.

## Interdependence of Factor 6 with Factor 2’s Similarity Finding  

The Committee’s decision to vacate the sixth factor stems directly from the fact that the second factor already established the parties’ services as “legally identical in part” (Apex, J.A. 27‑28).  If the parties’ services are so closely aligned that they are “highly similar,” the UK, the same spectrum of comparable marks must be examined under other DuPont factors.  The Court stated that “when the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors” (*Apex*, J.A. 28).  Thus, the Sixth factor’s limited analysis rendered the Board’s conclusions unstable.

## The First DuPont Factor: Appearance, Sound, and Commercial Impression  

The First factor looks beyond the marks themselves to the “overall commercial impression” that arises from combining appearance, sound, connotation, and the commercial aspects of the services (Apex, J.A. 51).  Because the commercial strength or weakness of a mark shapes how consumers interpret that overall impression, any error in the Sixth factor’s assessment of third‑party marks can distort the First factor’s analysis.  The appellate court therefore vacated the Board’s First‑factor decision (“We must vacate the Board’s analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.” *Apex*, J.A. 52).  In other words, the commercial impression is tied to the mark’s contextual strength, which was already called into question.

## Vacating Factor 1 Due to Altered Factor 6 Analysis  

By vacating the Sixth factor, the Court signaled that the foundation of the First‑factor analysis was flawed.  The mark’s perceived commercial strength—whether it is seen as distinctive or weak—depends in part on how many similar marks exist in the relevant field.  If the field is broader than the Board initially tread, Apex’s mark could appear more distinctive, altering the commercial impression.  Because such a shift materially changes the outcome of the First factor, the appellate court ordered its remand.  The Court affirmed the Board’s Sixth‑factor vacatur and – noting the interdependence of the DuPont factors – remanded to reconsider both the scope of third‑party marks and the marks’ commercial strength under the First factor.

## Remand Instruction: Reconsidering the Scope of Third‑Party Marks and Reassessing Commercial Strength  

The remand requires the Board to:

1. **Include all third‑party marks that employ “Aspire” on services similar to those of Apex and CC Serve**—that is, those across the broader banking and financial‑services market; and  
2. **Reassess the First factor** after having a complete picture of the field’s saturation, which may affect the perceived distinctiveness and, consequently, the overall commercial impression the marks convey.

The decision reflects a principle that DuPont factors are not isolated; they inform one another, and a misstep in one factor may necessitate a reevaluation of the others.  The Court’s vacatur and remand underscore the importance of a uniform and comprehensive approach to the similarity and strength of marks wherever the parties’ services are substantially overlapping.

# Holding, Significance, and Practical Implications  

**Holding**  

The Federal Circuit affirmed the TTAB’s determination that Apex Bank’s and CC Serve’s services are “highly similar” under the second DuPont factor, a factual conclusion supported by substantial evidence. The Court vacated the Board’s findings under the sixth and first DuPont factors and remanded for reconsideration. The vacated findings were based on a misapplied scope of third‑party marks and an insufficient assessment of the marks’ commercial strength.  

**A. Final Disposition**  

1. **Second DuPont factor** – affirmed. The Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services (credit‑card services versus banking and financing services) are legally identical in part was supported by evidence and was reviewed de novo on a legal basis.  
2. **Sixth DuPont factor** – vacated. The Board limited its universe of “similar marks” to those used on credit‑card services only, despite its own finding that the services were highly similar. The appellate court held that the same breadth of services applies to factor 6.  
3. **First DuPont factor** – vacated. Because factor 1’s commercial‑impression analysis relies on the findings about similarity and strength, the Court remanded for reconsideration once factor 6 is properly evaluated.  

**B. Similar Goods Do Not Need to Be Identical**  

The Court reiterated that the standard for “similar goods” is a *relational* test: the goods or services must be related enough that consumers might reasonably assume they emanate from the same source. The factors do **not** require products or services to be identical; they may be related, overlapping, or merely “in the same field.”  The Court’s reference to *Coach Servs. v. Triumph* (Fed. Cir. 2012) clarifies that the context of marketing and the potential for mistaken source attribution are the controlling criteria.  

*Practical takeaway:* Opposition pleadings should show that, even if the goods or services differ technically, they occupy a related market (e.g., credit‑card services and financ­ing services). A narrow “identical” view can be foreclosed by evidence of overlapping activities, shared clientele, or joint marketing with banks.  

**C. Define the Universe of Third‑Party Marks in Factor Six**  

The appellate court found the Board’s refusal to consider marks beyond credit‑card services constituted a legal error. When the Board has established that the services are highly similar, it must preserve that same scope when identifying “similar marks on similar goods.”  Evidence of third‑party marks that use **Aspire** (or Aspire‑formative marks) in any banking or financing service—credit cards, mortgages, online banking, etc.—is relevant, not only those strictly in the credit‑card niche.  

*Practical takeaway:* Opposition draft must include a comprehensive survey of all third‑party marks sharing the same word/design in the broader field of banking/financing. The search should be systematic and include all classes, not just the narrow one where the applicant has filed.  Failure to do so risks an appellate remand.  

**D. Evidence of Commercial Strength or Weakness Governs Scope**  

The TTAB’s analysis of factor 6 hinges on evidence that a third‑party mark is either *commercially strong* (and thus merits a *normal* scope) or *commercially weak* (and thus limited). The court noted that CC Serve’s mark, being inherently distinctive, was entitled to the normal scope once the Board found no evidence of weakness. 

*Practical takeaway:* Opponents should gather:
- **Use‑in‑commerce** data (volume, sales, advertising spend) of third‑party marks;
- **Consumer surveys** indicating recognition or lack thereof; and
- **Registration data** showing prevalence of the mark among competitors.  

Such evidence can establish a mark’s strength, narrowing the strategic field. Conversely, corp. evidence of weak use (e.g., sporadic, geographically limited, or underused) may allow the applicant to argue a broader scope is unwarranted.  

**E. Appellate Review: De Novo vs. Substantial Evidence**  

The Court reaffirmed *M2 Software v. M2 Commc’ns* (Fed. Cir. 2006):  
- *Legal conclusions* (e.g., the application of DuPont factors) are reviewed **de novo**.  
- *Factual findings* (e.g., similarity of goods/services) require support by **substantial evidence**.  

In opposition proceedings, a party must present evidence that a reasonable mind could accept as adequate to support the factual finding. Mere inference or the “logical” judgment of the Board is not enough.  

*Practical takeaway:* Prepare a detailed evidence package that directly addresses each factual element:
- **Relatedness of services** – show overlapping functions, marketing channels, and target consumers.  
- **Number and nature of similar marks** – provide a full list of third‑party claims in the relevant service field.  
- **Commercial strength/weakness** – include quantitative sales data, advertising metrics, and expert testimony on consumer perception.  

**F. Drafting Opposition Arguments and Evaluating Likelihood of Confusion**  

1. **Begin with the factual context.** State that the services are highly similar, invoking the Board’s own findings under factor 2, and assert that the same breadth of services applies to all factors.  
2. **Broadly characterize third‑party marks.** Include a list of all matches to the applicant’s mark in the banking/financing field, not merely the niche where the applicant operates.  
3. **Supply strength/weakness evidence.** Illustrate whether the applicant’s mark has a standard (normal) or limited scope based on its industrial presence.  
4. **Counter alleged weakness.** If the opponent argues that the mark is weak, present contrary evidence of robust use or design distinctiveness.  
5. **Apply the de novo standard.** Show that the legal conclusions (e.g., application of the DuPont factors) are the correct analytical framework, and that the Board misapplied factor 6’s scope.  

*Practical take‑away:* If an opponent argues the Board’s analysis is flawed, tie the argument to the factual findings the appellate court has found to be reasonable. Contradict the Board’s restricted universe of marks by demonstrating that the same service context used for factor 2 applies to factor 6 and 1, thereby expanding the range of relevant third‑party marks that can influence the source‑identification analysis.  

---

**Key Lessons for Intellectual‑Property Students**

- **Do not conflate “identical” with “related.”** The DuPont factors accommodate overlapping services.  
- **Ensure a complete universe of comparative marks.** Narrow, discipline‑specific searches can lead to vacatur.  
- **Certify commercial strength.** A strong mark usually warrants the normal scope; lack of evidence of weakness preserves that default.  
- **Adhere to appellate factual‑finding standards.** Substantial evidence is not a “slip of the pen” – it dictates the TTAB’s conclusions.
