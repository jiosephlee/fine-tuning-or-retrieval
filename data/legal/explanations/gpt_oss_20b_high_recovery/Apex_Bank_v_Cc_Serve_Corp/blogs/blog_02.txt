Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Crowding the Field: Third‑Party Mark Evidence and the Sixth DuPont Factor  

## Why This Matters  

When the United States Patent and Trademark Office’s Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) hangs a “no” on a pending registration, the burden of proof shifts rapidly to the parties who wish to show that a similar mark is commercially weak or already in use in a crowded field. The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* turns that burden upside down: it forces the TTAB to evaluate a broader universe of comparable marks whenever it has already found that the services of the parties are highly similar. For attorneys drafting opposition briefs, or litigating infringement, the new doctrine means that your third‑party evidence must cover all services within the same commercial realm, not just your client’s precise niche.

The holding is not a radical doctrinal swing. It merely clarifies an old principle that the seven DuPont factors are fact‑dependent and that the “similarity” standard applied to the sixth factor should be consistent with the standard used to evaluate the second factor. The Court’s refocus on the broader field has a tangible impact: **the same set of third‑party marks that can tilt the analysis toward the accused applicant can also undercut the primary mark’s scope**.  In practice this pushes consultants, real estate attorneys, and branding specialists to inventory not only direct competitors but also “related” fin‑services players (e.g., fintech firms or personal‑finance apps using Aspire‑derivatives) when producing evidence of a crowded market.

## Recap of the Apex v. CC Serve Decision  

In 2019, Apex filed intended‑use applications for “Aspire Bank” word and design marks. CC Serve, already holding the inherently distinctive “Aspire” mark for credit‑card services, opposed under § 2(d).  The TTAB found likelihood of confusion on all thirteen DuPont factors.  The Board’s most consequential errors, as the Federal Circuit laid out, were (1) in limiting the universe of comparable marks to those used on credit‑card services for the sixth factor, and (2) proceeding with the first factor even after vacating the sixth.  

The Court affirmed the board’s conclusions on the second factor.  It held that “the services are highly similar…legally identical, in part,” a finding rooted in the fact that credit‑card issuance is effectively an extension of banking and financing.  The Third‑Party Evidence Problem: the TTAB passed over forty “Aspire‑derived” marks that used that word across broader financial or personal‑finance services.  By ignoring those marks, it effectively carved an arbitrary “credit‑card market” out of a much larger financial landscape.

## The Sixth DuPont Factor Under New Light  

The sixth factor examines “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.”  The Court reminded that similarity under the DuPont framework is measured **only** by institutional facts, not by the plaintiff’s precise service category.  *M2 Software v. M2 Commc’ns,* 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 applies the standard “finding of relevant underlying facts.”  When that underlying fact is “the services are highly similar,” the relevant universe of comparable marks must mirror that fact.

Thus, the Board’s  “credit‑card services” universe was inadequate.  The Court noted that the same market that sees “credit‑card services” and “banking and financing services” as “legally identical, in part” (Apex v. CC Serve 5) must be reflected in the *sixth* factor’s universe of third‑party marks.  The Fifth amendment to the sixth factor hinges not on a new legal standard but on a sovereign consistency in what “similar” means.

Practically, this means the TTAB must enroll every mark that uses “Aspire” on product or services that revolve around financial facilitation, whether that is a lending platform, a personal‑finance app, or a fintech partnership within a bank.  Those marks are “similar” insofar as the public perceives them all as connected to a “money‑related” service that might involve credit, loans, or accounts.  Ignoring them risks an overconservative claim of “normal scope” where a crowded field suggests *weak* protection.

## The Field of Aspire‑Derived Marks  

The eye‑catching part of the decision is how it lightens the conceptual load on trademark practitioners.  It says: **if your opposition, for example, claims a crowded field, be sure no one falls into the credit‑card bin alone.**  Suppose four marks read “Aspire Investments” on an investment‑advisory app, a “Aspire” powered app that helps with budgeting, and a “Aspire” fatigue‑tracking venture undefined by the Clerk Office.  Are these “similar” to “Aspire Bank” for purposes of confusion?  The Court says yes, because the shared public is a “consumer of ‘banking and financing services.’”

Likewise, should someone file a mark like “Credit‑Aspire,” the Board can consider it within the same sphere of “financial services” and not marginalize it to a niche credit‑card setting.  The implication is that the field of comparison now grows beyond lexical similarity; several discrete but adjacent services can be interrelated and their marks jointly inform commercial strength.

## Practical Implications for Practitioners  

1. **Broader Evidence Search.**  The testimony of two or three third‑party trademarks used in credit‑card services is no longer enough.  Vet the *entire* audience that navigates the financial umbrella: peer‑to‑peer loan networks, fintech products, cloud‑banking platforms, even credit‑worthiness portals.  Write exhaustive lists, and retrieve all registration or unregistered usage evidence that literally uses “Aspire” across those services.

2. **Keep the Scope of Similarity Consistent.**  Do not narrow the “consumer” definition after making a factual finding that the services are “highly similar.”  If the TTAB has concluded similar, keep that core set when evaluating the sixth factor.  Failure leads to a vacated decision and a costly remand.

3. **Weight of Weakness vs. Strength.**  Remember, a crowded field that indicates scarcity of distinctive use can reduce a mark’s monopoly scope.  The Court found CC Serve’s “Aspire” to enjoy a “normal scope of protection” because it is inherently distinctive.  But if a plaintiff’s mark is *deceptively* similar—like “Aspire“ under the guise of “personal finance”—the crowded field may undermine its strength and tip the balance toward refusal.

4. **First Factor Re‑analysis.**  The decision reminds us that the commercial impression evaluation is sealed to the board’s view of a crowded market.  If the sixth factor expands, the same words may now seem less distinctive, altering the visual or phonetic assessment tied to the first factor.  Ensure your first‑factor briefing anticipates that change.

5. **Strategic Redefinition is No Longer “Law and Order.”**  The Court’s tendency to preserve the actual unit of comparison sets a higher bar for derivation.  Claims to a broader territory will be denied if the court is consistent.  For both brand owners and challengers, the interplay of similarity and field should be central to any primetime launch.

## Bottom Line  

Far from being a purely theoretical footnote, *Apex* codifies a procedural alignment that will shape the way we gather, frame, and argue third‑party evidence in trademark oppositions.  The very essence of a “crowded field” changes from a collection of identical services to a mosaic of “financial services” that share a consumer base and a source.  Trademark attorneys should brace for this broader take on similarity: the same market reading that ends up in the second factor’s “high similarity” box automatically expands the universe of comparable marks.  In short, this is not just a new “field”—it’s a *crowd* that will get measured by the same yardstick.
