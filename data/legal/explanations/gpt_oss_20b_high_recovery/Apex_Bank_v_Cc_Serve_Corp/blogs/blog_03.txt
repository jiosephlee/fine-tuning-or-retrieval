Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Why Vacating the Sixth Factor Requires Revisiting the First: The Interdependence of DuPont Factors  

## Why This Matters  

Apex Bank’s appeal to the Federal Circuit serves as a warning of how a seemingly minor change in one portion of the DuPont framework can push the board to re‑examine an entirely different factor. The court affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor (similarity of goods and services) but vacated its treatment of the sixth factor (number and nature of similar marks) and, in turn, remanded for reconsideration of the first factor (overall commercial impression). The decision clarifies that the DuPont factors are not a tidy linear sequence but a tightly woven, interdependent network. For trademark oppositions, it means that a misapprehension of the marketplace can rip through the entire confusion analysis.

## The Facts in a Nutshell  

CC Serve holds the undeniably distinctive word mark “ASPIRE” for credit‑card services and licenses the brand to partner banks. Apex Bank, which has never issued credit cards, sought to register “ASPIRE BANK” for a forthcoming suite of online banking products. CC Serve opposed the registration on the ground that the marks were confusingly similar. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) examined all thirteen DuPont factors, found a likelihood of confusion, and refused the registration. Apex appealed, arguing that the board’s analysis of the second, sixth, and first factors was defective.  

## The Board’s Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Analysis  

### Second Factor: Similarity of Goods and Services  

The TTAB deliberated whether the two parties’ services were related in a way that could generate a mistaken belief of common source. By dissecting the dictionary definition of “credit‑card services” and linking it to the broader category of “banking and finance,” the board concluded that the two sets of services were “legally identical in part.” The court verified this conclusion as correct on appeal, affirming the board’s finding of substantial evidence that the services were highly similar and that the second factor weighed heavily in favor of finding confusion.  

### Sixth Factor: Number and Nature of Similar Marks  

This factor asks: How crowded is the marketplace? Does the presence of many similar marks on closely related goods or services dilute the distinctiveness of the mark at issue? The board narrowed its universe to only those third‑party marks that used “Aspire” in the credit‑card arena, ignoring the 42 other marks that engaged the broader banking‑financing space. Its conclusion that only nine Credit‑Card‑related marks existed and that none reached “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use was therefore limited.  

Because the board had already found the services highly similar in the second factor, it should have considered third‑party marks that overlapped that same service space, not just credit‑card‑specific ones. The court agreed, vacating the sixth‑factor finding and remanding for a more expansive review of comparable marks that cover the same *similar* goods and services.  

### First Factor: Overall Commercial Impression  

The first factor looks at the *collective* commercial impression—the totality of how the marks read, sound, and convey meaning to the relevant consumer base. When the commercial impression is assessed, the board must account for mark distinctiveness, the strength of the mark, the similarity of goods/services, and, crucially, the marketplace context. A change in the sixth factor’s scope—by widening the universe of comparable marks—can alter how “distinctive” a mark appears and the weight it carries. Because the court had vacated the sixth factor, the analysis of overall commercial impression must be redone. The court accordingly remanded the first factor to the board.  

## The Interdependence of the DuPont Factors  

The tower of confusion analysis is not a ladder of independent rungs. The DuPont factors interact in a way that resembles a silicate crystal—any crack trip‑activates adjacent bonds. The court emphasizes that if the second factor establishes a factual finding that the services are “legally identical in part,” the board must use that same factual baseline when judging both the sixth and first factors. Under this view:

1. **The Sixth factor depends on the scope defined by the Second factor.** If the board applies the wrong scope, it undermines the assumption that the same services are at issue and therefore misrepresents market crowding.  
2. **The First factor incorporates the Sixth factor’s market‑crowding judgment.** Distinctiveness, perceived strength, and overall commercial impression are all calibrated against how crowded the market appears. If the sixth factor is narrowed too much, the first factor may overstate the mark’s distinctiveness.  

The court’s wording underscores this interdependence: “We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.” It followed that the overall commercial impression must be reassessed in light of a potentially more crowded marketplace.  

## The Court’s Rationale in Detail  

The appellant’s main thrust was that the board’s narrowing of the universe made it impossible to gauge how “strong” or “weak” CC Serve’s mark was relative to similar marks. The court agreed that the board must consider analogous marks on *similar* goods/services whose *similarity* is defined by the board’s own assessment in the second factor. The court’s analysis—treading closely on past decisions such as *Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation*—showed that the *logical meaning* of the words involved forms the “floor” of the assessment.  

In quenching the sixth factor, the court said the board “not only vacated” the seventh but also “remanded for a reconsideration of the overall commercial impression” because the commercial impression can shift when the perceived *strength* or *weakness* of the mark changes. This demonstrates the threshold principle that the first factor cannot operate in isolation; it is the final, decisive factor only after the board’s weighing of similarity, strength, and crowding altogether.  

## Practical Implications for Opposition Litigators  

1. **Map the Entire Service Space First.** The battle begins with a granular delineation of the services offered by both parties—are they merely related or *legally identical*? The more precise the definition, the easier it is to anchor the universe of third‑party marks.  
2. **Collect Maximal Comparable Mark Evidence.** When the services overlap, bring in all third‑party marks that roam the same space, even if they are limited to broader categories (e.g., mortgages, savings lines). The sixth factor’s weight hinges on a full market crawl.  
3. **Avoid Narrowing Consumer Definitions.** As seen in Apex, restricting “relevant public” to only “credit‑card‑service consumers” can unduly narrow the analysis. Argue that the relevant public includes a broader group—those who think of banking and financing in a general sense.  
4. **Tie the First Factor to the Sixth.** When committing to a formal argument for overall commercial impression, highlight how the evidence of third‑party mark usage—if it indicates a crowded market—lowers the mark’s distinctiveness and strengthens the first factor’s claim to confusion.  
5. **Prepare for a Full Inter‑Factor Review.** If the board finds an error in any factor that rides on a factual finding of another, the entire sequence may be reassessed.  

In short, a holistic approach that ties every factor’s evidence together is your best shield.  

## Doctrinal Consequences  

Apex registers a potential shift in how the TTAB and appellate courts treat the DuPont factors, particularly the relationship between *market crowding* and *commercial impression*. The decision provides a concrete example of why the Sixth factor should be *parameterized* by the *same factual baseline* used in the Second factor. This harmonization may lead to:

- A *broader* statutory expectation that the scope of “similar goods/services” is always contingent on prior factual findings, tightening the board’s ability to narrow its universe arbitrarily.  
- Guidance for future opposition cases in *banking and finance*, where the range of services can be broad and overlapping, ensuring that weaker marks can still exert statutory influence.  
- A precedent that the first factor’s commercial impression is not purely *guilty of visual or phonetic similarity*. Instead, the logic that it is impacted by *how the product is marketed, its strength, and its market presence* may be formally entrenched.  

The ripple effect may spread into *trademark registration* (where applicants must anticipate the opposition proceedings) and injunctions (where courts may look to the cumulative DuPont analysis in determining the extent of declaration).  

## What to Watch Going Forward  

- **Further appellate decisions on the Sixth–First link.** Will federal courts adopt the same “one factual baseline governs all factors” approach, or will they attempt to differentiate?  
- **Evolution of the definition of “similar goods/services.”** A narrower approach may depress the ability of new entrants to secure marks that lie in a broad service spectrum.  
- **Potential impact on inherently distinctive marks**—in *ASPIRE* the court explicitly highlighted “normal scope of protection accorded to inherently distinctive marks.” This might alter how courts treat the *inherent distinctiveness* of a mark in relation to the crowded market analysis.  

The decision hints that the quantitative study of marketplace saturation—how many marks are truly used, how many are merely registered—could become central in unpredictable ways.  

## Conclusion  

Apex v. CC Serve does more than refine a single factor; it recasts the DuPont framework as a system where each factor is a piece of a larger equation. Vacating the Sixth factor made the first factor restate its claim, because the commercial impression they had envisioned hinged on an inaccurate view of the market’s crowdedness. For trademark practitioners, the message is clear: A precision‑first, evidence‑rich approach to both the marketplace and the overall commercial impression is not a convenient nicety—it is the only way to survive the now‑more tightly knit DuPont web. The decision reminds us that in trademark law, everything connects. Which factor you get wrong can send a domino reaction that ultimately has to be litigated in court— and that change is no longer confined to the factor itself but extends to the entire conclusion.
