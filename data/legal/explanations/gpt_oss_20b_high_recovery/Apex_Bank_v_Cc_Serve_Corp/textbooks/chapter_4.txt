Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 4: # DuPont Factors 6 and 1: Scope of Third‑Party Marks and Commercial Impression  

The appellate court’s opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* centers on the Board’s analysis of the sixth and first DuPont factors in a trademark opposition under § 2(d).  The Sixth factor requires consideration of the number and nature of third‑party marks in use on similar goods or services; the First factor calls for a comparison of the marks’ shared visual, phonetic, and commercial qualities.  In this case, Apex appealed that the Board had limited its scope of comparable third‑party marks solely to credit‑card‑service users and that it failed to reassess the marks’ commercial impression after vacating that scope.

## The Sixth DuPont Factor: Definition and Requirement  

Under the DuPont framework, the sixth factor assesses “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” (Apex, J.A. 51).  The Board’s analysis is limited to the relevant public and the goods or services that are “similar” to those of the parties.  The standard is purely factual, and the court examines whether the presence of numerous other marks shows the contested mark to be weak or whether the contested mark enjoys a broad scope of protection.  Importantly, the factor does **not** require that a competitor use the same exact goods; it must consider “similar” goods or services, as the court has underscored in previous precedent (see *Olde Tyme Foods*, 961 F.2d at 204).

## Board’s Narrow Definition of the Relevant Public  

In applying the sixth factor, the Board restricted the relevant public to “consumers of credit‑card services” (Apex, J.A. 47).  The Board justified this limitation by focusing its search on third‑party marks that used “Aspire” in the credit‑card arena.  This narrow universe excluded the 42 other registrations that employed “Aspire” for broader banking or financial services, such as checking or loan solutions.  The Board insisted that such marks were “essentially irrelevant” (Apex, J.A. 47), even though its own second‑factor analysis had already concluded that Apex’s banking and CC Serve’s credit‑card services were “highly similar” (Apex, J.A. 27‑28).  By truncating the universe for the sixth factor, the Board omitted similar services that the second factor had already identified as comparable.

## Exclusion of 42 Other Third‑Party Marks Using “Aspire”  

The Court held that the Board’s exclusion of the 42 other third‑party marks improperly narrowed the analysis.  The sixth factor demands that the board consider all marks that could plausibly be used on “similar” goods or services.  The appellate court reasoned that the Board’s prior factual finding of high similarity between the parties’ services should carry forward into the scope analysis for the sixth factor.  There was no evidence that any of the excluded marks were used on goods or services dissimilar enough to merit exclusion.  Accordingly, the Board had to broaden its search to include the full set of Aspire‑related marks employed in the broader financial‑services marketplace.

## Interdependence of Factor 6 with Factor 2’s Similarity Finding  

The Committee’s decision to vacate the sixth factor stems directly from the fact that the second factor already established the parties’ services as “legally identical in part” (Apex, J.A. 27‑28).  If the parties’ services are so closely aligned that they are “highly similar,” the UK, the same spectrum of comparable marks must be examined under other DuPont factors.  The Court stated that “when the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors” (*Apex*, J.A. 28).  Thus, the Sixth factor’s limited analysis rendered the Board’s conclusions unstable.

## The First DuPont Factor: Appearance, Sound, and Commercial Impression  

The First factor looks beyond the marks themselves to the “overall commercial impression” that arises from combining appearance, sound, connotation, and the commercial aspects of the services (Apex, J.A. 51).  Because the commercial strength or weakness of a mark shapes how consumers interpret that overall impression, any error in the Sixth factor’s assessment of third‑party marks can distort the First factor’s analysis.  The appellate court therefore vacated the Board’s First‑factor decision (“We must vacate the Board’s analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.” *Apex*, J.A. 52).  In other words, the commercial impression is tied to the mark’s contextual strength, which was already called into question.

## Vacating Factor 1 Due to Altered Factor 6 Analysis  

By vacating the Sixth factor, the Court signaled that the foundation of the First‑factor analysis was flawed.  The mark’s perceived commercial strength—whether it is seen as distinctive or weak—depends in part on how many similar marks exist in the relevant field.  If the field is broader than the Board initially tread, Apex’s mark could appear more distinctive, altering the commercial impression.  Because such a shift materially changes the outcome of the First factor, the appellate court ordered its remand.  The Court affirmed the Board’s Sixth‑factor vacatur and – noting the interdependence of the DuPont factors – remanded to reconsider both the scope of third‑party marks and the marks’ commercial strength under the First factor.

## Remand Instruction: Reconsidering the Scope of Third‑Party Marks and Reassessing Commercial Strength  

The remand requires the Board to:

1. **Include all third‑party marks that employ “Aspire” on services similar to those of Apex and CC Serve**—that is, those across the broader banking and financial‑services market; and  
2. **Reassess the First factor** after having a complete picture of the field’s saturation, which may affect the perceived distinctiveness and, consequently, the overall commercial impression the marks convey.

The decision reflects a principle that DuPont factors are not isolated; they inform one another, and a misstep in one factor may necessitate a reevaluation of the others.  The Court’s vacatur and remand underscore the importance of a uniform and comprehensive approach to the similarity and strength of marks wherever the parties’ services are substantially overlapping.
