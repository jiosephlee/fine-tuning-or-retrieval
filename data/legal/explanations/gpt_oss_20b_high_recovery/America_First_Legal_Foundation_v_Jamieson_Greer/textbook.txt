Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Case Background and Procedural History

The chapter below traces the arc of America First Legal Foundation’s (AFL) litigation, from its initial Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request to the appellate court’s ultimate determination that the claim raises no Article III subject‑matter jurisdiction. By situating the factual and procedural context that precedes the court’s reasoning, the discussion prepares the reader to engage with the standing and statutory challenges that follow.

## Identification of Participants: AFL, DOJ, OSC

- **America First Legal Foundation (AFL)** – a private non‑profit organization that, in this case, sought to compel the Department of Justice (DOJ) to disclose certain personnel‑email records. AFL’s claim hinges on alleged procedural violations by DOJ and, later, the Office of Special Counsel (OSC).

- **Department of Justice (DOJ)** – the federal agency targeted in the FOIA suit. DOJ retained agency records (email exchanges between an outside attorney and DOJ’s Civil Division) that AFL requested. DOJ denied the request, citing a policy that it does not screen employee‑email data for FOIA responsiveness absent the employee’s consent.

- **Office of Special Counsel (OSC)** – an investigative and prosecutorial body that enforces civil‑service rules. OSC’s statutory authority under 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214, 1215, 1216 and FOIA’s own enforcement provision (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)) situates it as an intermediary that might investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA‑withholding practices.

## Nature of the FOIA Request and DOJ’s Denial

AFL, in pursuit of transparent oversight, subpoenaed DOJ emails involving an external counsel and the DOJ Civil Division. 
- **FOIA provision** – In FOIA, a request for agency records is deemed “proceeding” if a court orders the agency’s production of records it improperly withheld; this triggers OSC’s obligation to investigate potential arbitrary or capricious withholding under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3).  
- **Denial rationale** – DOJ refused to supply the records on the grounds that it does not search employee e‑mail archives absent explicit consent, a policy AFL challenged as contrary to FOIA’s public‑interest mandate.

The refusal triggered AFL’s subsequent legal action under FOIA against DOJ.

## AFL’s Recourse: FOIA Litigation and Request to OSC

AFL filed a FOIA suit in the D.C. District Court, seeking:
1. **Court order** obligating DOJ to provide the requested emails, and
2. **Attorney‑fee award** in the event DOJ was found in violation of FOIA.

Parallelly, AFL directed OSC to investigate DOJ’s policy and conduct under § 1216(a)(3), arguing that OSC possessed a statutory duty to probe any act of “arbitrary or capricious withholding” of FOIA‑requested records.

However, OSC, after reviewing AFL’s petition, declined to investigate and “dismissed” the request. Its reasoning—that the statutory trigger requirements of the FOIA enforcement provision (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)) had not been satisfied—was contested by AFL as a misapplication of the law.

## OSC’s Internal Authority and Decision to Decline Investigation

OSC’s powers stem from statutory directives:
- **§ 1214** prescribes corrective‑action procedures for prohibited personnel practices, while
- **§ 1215** governs disciplinary action, and
- **§ 1216** extends OSC’s investigative reach to alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings.

Critically, OSC’s enforcement role under FOIA is conditional on the presence of at least three predicates: a court order targeting withheld records, an attorney‑fee award, and a question of whether the withholding was arbitrary or capricious. OSC’s refusal to investigate affirmed that it saw none of these predicates present in AFL’s case. The district court later concurred that OSC’s decision was legally erroneous under § 1216(a)(3). Nonetheless, it added that § 1216(c) places such investigative discretion within OSC’s purview, and therefore the court could not compel OSC to act. In sum, OSC’s internal authority was exercised in a manner that contravened the perceived statutory mandate, but the district court deemed OSC’s discretion unassailable without the FOIA trigger conditions.

## District Court’s Decision, Remand, and Subsequent Actions

1. **Partial victory for AFL** – The D.C. District Court granted AFL a partial judgment that OSC’s refusal violated § 1216(a)(3).  
2. **Remand to OSC** – Despite ruling incorrectly that OSC had a legal error in not investigating, the court remanded the case. The remand instructed OSC to reconsider its decision in light of the court’s interpretation.  
3. **OSC’s second refusal** – OSC again declined to open an inquiry under § 1216.  
4. **Motion for final judgment** – AFL, dissatisfied with OSC’s inaction, filed a motion for entry of final judgment. The district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims, citing lack of standing.  
5. **Appeal** – AFL appealed the district court’s judgment; that the appellate bench would review the case de novo under the standards set forth in *N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf*.

The procedural sequence culminated in the appellate court’s decision that AFL failed to meet Article III standing, a prerequisite for subject‑matter jurisdiction. The appellate order consequently vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded the case for dismissal.

## Appeal and Retrospective Direction for Review

The appellate court’s mandate to "review de novo" required examining the case anew, controlling the procedural posture and the standing question absented in the lower court. The court first affirmed that the “cases” and “controversies” requirements of Article III were indeed a function of standing jurisprudence, citing *Steel* and *Lujan*. AFL had not provided any injury “in fact” beyond a failed FOIA request and an unfunded investigative process. Because the appellate court was furnished with the same factual matrix but had to adopt its own factual determinations, the court concluded that AFL lacked the requisite concrete injury. Consequently, the appellate court ordered a vacatur of the district court’s judgment and remanded for the dismissal of AFL’s claims on the basis of lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

- **Key takeaway for the reader** – The procedural history illustrates how an ostensibly statutory mandate may be thwarted by a jurisdictional gatekeeper: Article III standing. The chain of events from FOIA request, through OSC’s internal discretion, to appellate judgment underscores that procedural violations must connect, via a clear causal link, to an injury that is concrete, particularized, and redressable in a judicial forum.

This chapter sets the stage for the next analysis: how the appellate court applied standing doctrines to reach its conclusion.

# Legal Framework Governing OSC’s Authority and FOIA Enforcement

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) occupies a unique position within the federal civil‑service system, tasked with safeguarding both personnel integrity and information transparency. Its authority is carved out primarily by the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) and the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). This chapter unpacks the statutory provisions that define OSC’s investigative powers, delineates the special FOIA enforcement mechanism, and explores the interplay between statutory mandates and OSC’s discretionary enforcement. By the end of the chapter, students will understand how §§ 1214, 1215, 1216 of the CSRA and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) of FOIA intersect, and how the courts have interpreted OSC’s competencies in the *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* case.

## Civil Service Reform Act Provisions: §1214, §1215, §1216

Section 1211 of the CSRA creates OSC and endows it with investigative authority over “prohibited personnel practices”—acts such as retaliation for whistleblowing or failure to provide procedural protections. Section 1214, the procedural core, requires OSC to see that an investigation proceeds fairly, efficiently, and lawfully, and that the complainant is kept informed of progress. 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D) prescribes notice obligations and procedural safeguards. 5 U.S.C. § 1215 then authorizes OSC to bring corrective action under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary action under 5 U.S.C. § 1215 against officers who violate prohibitive personnel rules. 5 U.S.C. § 1214(b)(3), 5 U.S.C. § 1215(a)(1) give OSC the power to seek remedial orders or punitive measures.

Section 1216 turns the OSC’s investigative lens toward FOIA compliance. Subsection (a)(3) commands OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). Importantly, § 1216(b) and (c) state that a threat of an FOIA violation may be handled “in the same way as a prohibited personnel practice.” The statutory language allows OSC to pursue corrective actions under § 1214 or disciplinary actions under § 1215, rendering § 1216 a conduit for FOIA enforcement that borrows the remedies of §§ 1214 and 1215.

Because the statutory language is intentionally generic, the courts have had to decide whether OSC’s mandate under § 1216 is *mandatory* or *discretionary* and whether it is limited by the trigger conditions of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). This has led to a patchwork of precedential interpretations, which are examined in the next section.

## FOIA’s Dedicated OSC Enforcement Mechanism – §552(a)(4)(F)(i)

FOIA’s enforcement framework is distinct from OSC’s general CSRA mandate. Section 552(a) establishes the rule that FOIA requests “shall… be answered according to the provisions of this Act.” Subsection (4) sets conditions under which a court may order production of records that an agency has wrongly withheld. Paragraph (F) specifies that a judgment “shall… order… that the Agency produce the requested records, award… attorneys’ fees, and, specifically, question whether the Agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously.”

The operative clause of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is multi‑pronged:

1. The court must order the production of records that the agency improperly withheld under FOIA.
2. The court must award the claimant attorneys’ fees against the Agency.
3. The court must specifically inquire whether the agency acted *arbitrarily* or *capriciously* in the withholding.

If all three predicates are satisfied, OSC is *statutorily required* to “promptly” initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). This statute imbues OSC with a *mandatory* investigative duty in the particular context of FOIA litigations that meet the three predicates, ensuring that agency non‑compliance is not plastered over through mere administrative non‑action.

In *America First Legal*, OSC was asked to investigate a FOIA‑alleged policy of DOJ that allegedly prohibited email searches without consent. OSC declined to do so, citing the absence of a court order, the award of fees, and the absence of an explicit arbitrage/caprice query—all the predicates of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). Thus, OSC’s choice to decline was grounded in the fact that the statutory trigger was incomplete, especially as the district court had not invoked the specific *arbitrary or capricious* question.

## OSC’s Discretionary Enforcement vs Mandatory Investigation

While § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) gives OSC a compulsory investigative role under exceptionally circumscribed circumstances (a presiding court’s FOIA order that meets all predicates), OSC’s broader authority under § 1216 is *discretionary*. The statute says OSC *shall* investigate but does not specify the conditions under which the investigation *must* occur. Rather, § 1216 allows OSC to *choose* to investigate an FOIA withholding as if it were a prohibited personnel practice, employing either corrective or disciplinary remedies under §§ 1214 and 1215.

The distinction is well illustrated by the jurisprudence. In *Frazier v. MSPB*, 672 F.2d 150 (D.C. Cir. 1982), the court affirmed OSC’s investigative powers under the CSRA, underscoring that OSC’s mandate was expansive, but practically oriented toward remedial action. *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487, 497 (6th Cir. 2011), dealt explicitly with OSC’s discretion to apply § 1214’s procedural requirements to a § 1216 investigation; the court concluded that OSC’s choice to adopt § 1214 notice procedures was discretionary, not compulsory.

In *America First Legal*, OSC relied on this discretionary framework to refuse to launch an investigation. The district court dissented, arguing that the OSC’s “legal error” in refusing the investigation was inconsistent with the statutory text. The appellate panel, however, concluded that the district court misinterpreted OSC’s discretionary power, even though it ultimately vacated the district court’s judgment for lack of standing. Thus, the *America First Legal* district court’s error had little bearing on the statutory architecture that the appellate court ultimately upheld: the combination of mandatory and discretionary trigger points remains intact.

## Relevant Case Law

### Frazier v. MSPB

In *Frazier v. MSPB*, the District of Columbia Circuit clarified OSC’s obligation to investigate prohibited personnel practices. The court recognized that OSC’s duties under § 1214, § 1215, and § 1216 were “integral to ensuring a fair, efficient, and lawfully‑conducted civil service.” 672 F.2d 150, 162. It reinforced that OSC’s investigative authority operates within a procedural framework; that is, OSC must notify the complainant about the progress of an investigation, thereby ensuring transparency about the inquiry and its potential remedial actions.

### Am. First Legal v. Kerner

*America First Legal v. Kerner*, 2023 WL 9546644 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023), was a pivotal district‑court decision that addressed the interplay between § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) and OSC’s § 1216 mandate. The court held that the *three predicates* of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) do not *limit* OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under § 1216. **"So, the court declared, OSC’s decision not to investigate was based on a legal error."** In *America First Legal*, the district court effectively said that OSC’s refusal to investigate, absent a court‑triggered FOIA order, would be erroneous, and the court remanded OSC for further consideration. Yet, it also declined to order an investigation because § 1216(c) preserved OSC discretion. The appellate court, in turn, vacated the district court’s judgment for lack of standing; nonetheless, the *kerner* line furnishes a vital interpretive precedent that OSC’s statutory duties are not automatically extinguished by the absence of a FOIA trigger.

### Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.

*Carson*, 633 F.3d 487, 495, has been the cornerstone case for OSC’s discretionary enforcement. The court held that OSC’s decision whether to adopt the notice and procedural rules of § 1214 within a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Consequently, OSC may or may not provide the *standard* procedural safeguards of a prohibited personnel practice investigation when evaluating a FOIA violation. This outcome dovetailed with the *America First Legal* district court’s reasoning that OSC—while empowered—was not compelled to take action absent a triggering FOIA order.

## Analysis of OSC’s Declination of Investigation Under §1216(a)(3)

In *America First Legal*, the OSC declined to investigate an alleged FOIA policy that DOJ apparently barred email searches without the employee’s consent. The court determined that the *predicates* of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not satisfied: no court had ordered production; no attorneys’ fees had been awarded; and the court had not specifically questioned whether DOJ’s withholding was *arbitrary* or *capricious*. Consequently, OSC refused to launch an investigation under § 1216(a)(3), invoking the discretionary nature of the statute.

The district court’s holding that OSC’s refusal “was a legal error” documents a perceived conflict between statutory text and the omission of the § 552 trigger. The appellate court, however, noted that § 1216(c) renders OSC’s enforcement under § 1214 and § 1215 *discretionary*. The appellate panel declined to rebuke OSC for its non‑action, focusing instead on the failure of AFL to meet standing requirements. The thesis from the appellate decision, though, concisely captures the statutory architecture:

- **Mandatory enforcement**: triggered by the full three predicates of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i); OSC must act.
- **Discretionary enforcement**: invoked at OSC’s judgment when the statutory trigger is incomplete; OSC may choose to investigate a FOIA violation as if it were a prohibited personnel practice, but it is not compelled.

Thus, *America First Legal* demonstrates the practical implications of this dual framework: when OSC declines an investigation, the litigation may still proceed in the courts with the expectation that the OSC will investigate *only if* the statutory container is fully activated. The court’s decision to vacate the district court’s judgment for lack of standing does not alter this structural reality = it merely curtails the ability of plaintiffs to assert the statutory redress of an alleged FOIA violation absent standing.

In sum, the interplay between § 1214, § 1215, § 1216, and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides a layered statutory architecture. OSC’s investigative authority is both *mandatory* (in FOIA’*80 order contexts*) and *discretionary* (in broader CSRA contexts). The appellate precedent further underscores that OSC’s discretionary decisions—such as declining to investigate a FOIA policy—are admissible and do not automatically trigger an appeal, unless the plaintiff establishes standing. This framework is essential for law students and practitioners navigating FOIA claims that implicate OSC.

# Article III Standing: Principles and Procedural Injury Doctrine  

Standing is the constitutional gatekeeper that limits federal courts to hearing only those “cases” and “controversies” contemplated by Article III.  Its purpose is not to decide whether a plaintiff is entitled to relief, but to determine whether a dispute is a “case” or “controversy” that falls within the federal courts’ jurisdiction.  Because the standing doctrine is a product of the judiciary’s own constitutional scrutiny, its components—injury in fact, causation, and redressability—have become the yardsticks against which any federal claim is measured.  When a plaintiff raises a procedural right, the Court applies a slightly more relaxed benchmark, allowing the plaintiff to show a concrete injury even though the alleged injury is rooted in a procedural violation.

---

## Constitutional Mandate: Article III Cases and Controversies Requirement  

Article III, Section 2, clause 1 endows the federal judiciary with jurisdiction over “cases” and “controversies.”  The Supreme Court has made clear that “standing” is not a separate remedy, but a constitutional prerequisite for subject‑matter jurisdiction itself (Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984)).  A dispute that is procedural in nature—such as a plaintiff’s claim that an agency failed to follow lawfully mandated procedures—does not automatically become a judicial “case” merely because the plaintiff seeks a court action.  The Court demands that the plaintiff satisfy the standing prerequisites so that the controversy falls within Article III’s purview.  

---

## Supreme Court Standing Prerequisites: Injury in Fact, Causation, Redressability  

The “injury in fact” prong requires that a plaintiff suffer an actual or imminent, concrete, and particularized injury that is more than an abstract or speculative harm (Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992)).  The causal connection between the alleged wrongful conduct and the injury must be direct and not merely attenuated (OF cases such as *Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack*, 808 F.3d 905 (D.C. Cir. 2015)).  Redressability asks whether a favorable judicial decision would likely eliminate or reduce the injury.  The Court has repeatedly emphasized that a claim can be dismissed for lack of standing if the plaintiff cannot show how a court order would bind the parties to effect redress (Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)).  These three tests form the backbone of the standing framework; failure on any element suffices to deny jurisdiction.  

---

## Distinction Between Procedural and Substantive Injuries  

A *substantive injury* arises when a plaintiff’s legally protected right is violated—such as the right to receive records under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA).  Substantive claims require the injury to be concrete and particularized, but the injury itself is often protected by constitutional or statutory provisions that grant the plaintiff standing.  A *procedural injury*, by contrast, is a harm rooted in the denial of a legally mandated process—such as the refusal of an agency to conduct an investigation mandated by statute or regulation.  Procedural injuries draw the Court’s attention to the manner in which a law is applied, rather than to the end result of that application.  Because procedural errors typically undermine the integrity of the legal system for many parties, courts are often reluctant to grant standing for them unless the plaintiff can demonstrate that the procedural violation directly affects a concrete, particularized interest.  

---

## Relaxed Standing Standards for Procedural Injury Claims  

When a plaintiff asserts a procedural injury, the Supreme Court has refused to create a separate doctrine.  Instead, appellate courts have relaxed the *imminence* and *redressability* component of the standard while retaining the injury‑in‑fact and causation prongs (Citizens for Law & Educ. v. Department of Education, 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005)).  Under that relaxed version, the plaintiff must show that the procedural right left unexecuted led to a concrete and particularized injury, but the Court need not prove that the injury is imminent or that a court order would automatically resolve it.  The Court also treats causation more stringently in procedural cases, demanding that the injury be a result of the defendant’s illegal conduct rather than a speculative chain of future events (Fla. Audubon Society v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658 (D.C. Cir. 1996)).  

This approach was applied in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 2025 U.S. 3rd Circuit No. 24‑5168 (Oct. 3, 2025).  The Court held that the AFL’s complaint that the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) failed to investigate a FOIA violation amounted to a procedural injury.  The Court found that AFL could not satisfy the relaxed standing test because the injury was not tied to a concrete, particularized interest and because a favorable judgment would not likely spur the OSC—or its downstream agencies—to conduct the investigation or compel DOJ to produce records.  The Court underscored that speculative causal chains (OSC taking no action → MSPB sanctions DOJ employees → DOJ releases records) cannot establish causation sufficient for standing, thereby concluding that AFL lacked Article III standing.  

---

---

**Take‑away for the practitioner:**  
In any federal challenge, the first analytical hurdle is to prove Article III standing.  The plaintiff must show a concrete, particularized injury arising from the defendant’s conduct.  When the injury is rooted in a procedural wrong, the pleading can only survive if the plaintiff shows that the procedural failure directly hurt a tangible legal interest and that a judgment would likely result in redress.  Speculative causal chains, tenuous ties to the plaintiff’s interests, or simply lacking a concrete injury will render the claim *unjusticiable* and will cause dismissal for lack of standing.

# Critical Examination of AFL’s Standing Theories

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the court, following its briefings, concluded that the plaintiff—America First Legal Foundation (AFL)—failed to satisfy Article III standing. The analysis hinged on whether AFL’s two claims provided a concrete injury that was both causally linked to the defendant’s conduct and likely to be redressed by a favorable court ruling. The following sections trace the court’s reasoning in detail, illustrating how each theory faltered on the constitutional requirements of injury, causation, and redressability.

## First Theory: OSC’s Failure to Investigate → Informational Injury to AFL

AFL’s first claim rests on the allegation that the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) declined to investigate the DOJ’s alleged improper withholding of employee e‑mail records under FOIA. AFL argues that this refusal deprived it of the information it sought in a FOIA request and thereby created an actionable injury. The injury is framed as a missed opportunity to obtain FOIA‑entitled documents, a “concrete, particularized informational injury.” The court accepted that informational injuries are viable in standing analyses (see *American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals* v. Feld Entertainment, 659 F.3d at 23). Yet, the claim failed on two fronts: the causal link between OSC’s inaction and AFL’s lack of records was attenuated, and redressability was speculative.

## Analysis of Causal Path and Speculation

To establish injury under Article III, AFL must show that the alleged statutory violation *caused* it the injury. The statutory framework at issue is 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to investigate arbitrary or capricious withholding of FOIA‑prohibited information. However, OSC’s authority “to investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 and disciplinary action under § 1215” is *discretionary* (see *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d at 495). Thus, the statutory language does not compel OSC to launch an investigation, nor does it guarantee that an investigation would coerce the DOJ to comply with a FOIA request.

AFL’s causal chain runs: (1) OSC should have investigated DOJ; (2) such an investigation could have led to corrective or disciplinary action under §§ 1214–1215; (3) the MSPB could have sanctioned DOJ employees; (4) DOJ would have been compelled to produce the records. Each step involves a decision by an independent agency that is *not* a party to the litigation. The Supreme Court has held that causation cannot rest on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (*FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367, 383). The courts do not decide the policy choices of the MSPB or DOJ, or the administrative discretion of OSC in a way that would render a favorable judgment here “likely to spur” the entire chain of enforcement. This speculative link places AFL’s injury beyond the reach of Article III. As the court noted, the chain “requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit” and about whether OSC would exercise its enforcement discretion.

## Rationale on Redressability and the ‘Will Provide Relief’ Standard

In cases involving alleged procedural injuries, the Supreme Court and the D.C. Circuit have relaxed the imminence and redressability tests but retained the injury‑and‑causation requirements (*Control for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d at 1157). Redressability must be grounded in the effect of a judgment *on the parties*, not in a hypothetical influence on third‑party conduct. The standard thus asks whether a favorable ruling “would *differentiate* the parties’ rights or obligations.” AFL’s claim lacks such a connection. A court order compelling OSC to investigate, even if it obliged OSC to provide notices or take disciplinary action, would not directly give AFL any additional right to obtain DOJ records; the right to the records remains with AFL through FOIA itself, not through OSC’s enforcement role. Accordingly, the court found that AFL’s injury was not redressable by a ruling on OSC’s discretion.

## Second Theory: Loss of Information From OSC Investigation

AFL’s second claim advances that, because OSC declined to investigate, it was deprived of the “notices and information” that would necessarily accompany a section 1214 investigation. The claim hinges on an alleged entitlement, under § 1214, to procedural notice and information when OSC explores a FOIA violation. AFL argues that the statutory necessity of OSC’s investigation under § 1216(a)(3) obliges OSC to grant the same procedural protections as it does in § 1214 cases.

## Entitlement to OSC Notices and 5 U.S.C. § 1214 Requirements

Section 1214 obliges OSC to provide notice of the complaint’s status and the right to a hearing. However, the statutory authority for OSC to investigate FOIA violations under § 1216(a)(3) does **not** contain an explicit requirement that OSC “shall” provide the same procedural notices. Rather, it affords OSC discretion to conduct the investigation “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” (i.e., as § 1214 and § 1215). The court held that OSC’s discretion is not compelled by statutory language (*Carson*, 633 F.3d at 495). Consequently, AFL does not obtain a *concrete, particularized* injury merely because it is denied the procedural notice under § 1214; that notice is a benefit, not an enforceable right.

## Discretionary Nature of Notice Provision Under § 1216

The discretionary nature of OSC’s enforcement discretion under § 1216 continues to be significant. Even if AFL had accessed a § 1214‑style investigation, OSC could lawfully choose not to provide notices. As the court recognized, the statutory text does not bind OSC’s conduct in that regard. Thus, a “remedy” that forces OSC to issue notifications is a procedural it can ignore. The legal consequence is that AFL’s claim lacks an injury that, if redressed, would alter the parties’ legal position in any way, satisfying the “specific injury” requirement. The court consequently rejected the argument that a favorable ruling would **obligate** OSC to furnish the information, noting that the discretionary nature allows OSC to continue to withhold the notices without any enforcement or judicial compulsion.

## Lack of Concrete Injury and Insufficient Redressability

Because the claim rests on a dispensation that is neither an enforceable right under existing civil statutes nor on a statutory obligation, AFL’s second theory fails to demonstrate a concrete injury. Moreover, a decree mandating OSC to follow § 1214’s notice protocol would not "redress" AFL’s perceived injury because the injury itself is – at best – a speculative procedural deficiency that may or may not be corrected by any judicial intervention. The standard for redressability requires a *clear* link between a judgment and a change in the party's status, which is absent here. Consequently, the court concluded that AFL’s second theory likewise fails to meet the standing threshold.

## Synthesis and Overall Failure to Meet Standing Threshold

In sum, the court’s de‑novo review found that AFL’s two standing theories both violate the Article III requirements. The first theory’s proposed injury is disconnected from OSC’s discretionary conduct and is unlikely to be remedied by any court action, while the second theory lacks an enforceable entitlement to OSC’s procedural notice. Both proposals falter on the combined injury‑and‑causation‑and‑redressability framework. Accordingly, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded for dismissal based on lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

# Holding, Disposition, and Broader Implications  

The Supreme Court of the United States hinges the decision of the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit on the constitutional question of standing. The appellate court finds that the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) cannot demonstrate a concrete, particularized injury that is redressable by a favorable ruling, and therefore lacks Article III standing. Because standing is a prerequisite for judicial power, the court vacates the district court’s judgment and remands the case for dismissal on grounds of lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

---

## Court’s Holding: AFL Lacks Article III Standing  

The court’s central holding is that AFL possesses no Article III standing to bring a claim that the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is obliged to investigate a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) withholding under § 1216(a)(3). The standard for standing, adopted from *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*, requires a concrete, particularized injury that is actual or imminent and redressable through the court’s judgment. AFL’s alleged injuries—failure of the Department of Justice (DOJ) to provide records and the absence of OSC’s investigatory notice—either trace to the agency that actually withheld the records (DOJ) or are only procedural in nature, and neither satisfies the “concrete injury” requirement. The appellate court therefore concludes that AFL’s claims lack Article III standing.

---

## Judicial Reasoning on IRC: Respective Standards and Final Conclusion  

The court applies the *Allen v. Wright* “case‑or‑controversy” framework to determine whether the district court had jurisdiction. AFL’s supplemental brief alleges a violation of a statutory procedural right under §§ 1216(a)(3) and (c). The court rejects this by treating the alleged procedural violation as a “procedural right in vacuo”; *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.* explains that a procedural right alone, absent a concrete interest in its performance, does not confer standing. The court further explicates the “relaxed” standard applied to procedural injuries in *Central for Law & Educ.* and *Fla. Audubon Soc’y*, where plaintiffs must still show that the procedural violation “resulted in injury” to a concrete, particularized interest. AFL’s injuries—lack of records and notices—are either not concrete (they depend on DOJ’s compliance) or insufficiently redressable. The speculative chain required to connect OSC’s inaction to DOJ’s non‑compliance violates the causation requirement found in *FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.*, where causation cannot rely on the unfettered choices of independent actors. The court therefore reaches the final conclusion that AFL cannot establish standing.

---

## Vacatur of the District Court and Mandated Remand  

Because standing is a prerequisite for Article III authority, the appellate court vacates the district court’s judgment that was based on the merits of the claims. The case is remanded with instructions that the district court dismiss AFL’s remaining claims for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction. The remand is not a question of the substantive merits of the FOIA claim itself but a procedural step that deletes any dismissal of the district court on standing grounds, affirming only that no action may be maintained in federal court when standing is absent.

---

## Impact on FOIA Plaintiffs Seeking OSC Enforcement  

The decision establishes that FOIA plaintiffs who depend on OSC’s private‑interest enforcement mechanisms—particularly the discretionary investigation under § 1216(a)(3)—cannot rely on a mere procedural right to compel OSC to act. A plaintiff must demonstrate a *direct* injury caused by the lack of OSC enforcement, not merely an abstract inconvenience with the agency’s records or the absence of OSC’s internal process. Consequently, litigants cannot simply file a FOIA claim that “burdens them” because an agency’s internal policy is not enforced by OSC; they must either challenge the agency directly under § 552 or marshal an injury stemming from OSC’s inaction that is concrete, particularized, and redressable. The ruling clarifies that OSC’s note‑and‑investigation process is discretionary and does not create a statutory entitlement to information. The court’s vacatur therefore removes the possibility that the district court could have entertained such a claim under the guise of FOIA litigation.

---

## Implications for OSC’s Discretionary Enforcement Decisions  

OSC retains its statutory enforcement discretion under § 1216. The court reaffirms that OSC’s decision whether to investigate alleged “arbitrary or capricious withholding” is not guided by a statutory trigger beyond the three predicates in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). OSC’s choice to decline an investigation does not itself create a complaintable injury for a plaintiff lacking a direct interest. The court’s refusal to compel OSC to act unless the mandatory conditions of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are met underscores that OSC’s discretion is an internal agency process not subject to constitutional challenge solely on procedural grounds. In effect, decisions that OSC makes—or does not make—are not “subject to judicial review” at the level of standing; only the end state of an agency’s conduct (e.g., the withholding of records) can be reliably challenged.

---

## Potential Pathways for Future Litigation Strategies  

FOIA litigants who cannot rely on OSC enforcement may pursue alternative strategies:

1. **Plain FOIA Litigation** – Plaintiffs can sue the agency that withheld records under § 552, citing the statutory requirement for prompt disclosure and remedies for non‑compliance, without invoking OSC.

2. **Intervention by a Third Party** – A party with a vested, concrete interest (for instance, a watchdog organization that relies on the information for advocacy or litigation) may assert standing if it can show that the FOIA retaliation harms its own separate interests.

3. **Agency‑to‑Agency Claims** – If the plaintiff can allege that OSC’s inaction has caused tangible harm to the agency’s other functions (e.g., delays in enforcement of other statutes), this may satisfy the injury requirement.

4. **Redress through Administrative Remedies** – While standing is not required to seek an administrative remedy, plaintiffs can request a hearing or appeal a decision directly in OSC or the Merit Systems Protection Board, then seek judicial review of those administrative decisions.

5. **Use of Alternative Statutes** – Plaintiffs might invoke whistleblower or civil‑rights statutes to establish that the agency’s failure to release records interferes with right‑to-information or the right to a fair hearing.

Whatever approach is chosen, the litigant must demonstrate that the injury is concrete, particularized, and redressable—ideally linking the harm directly to the agency’s refusal to comply with the FOIA request or to the agency’s own procedural practices, rather than to a speculative enforcement decision by OSC.

In sum, the appellate decision sets a high bar for standing in FOIA‑contingent OSC enforcement claims and clarifies that procedural rights under § 1216 cannot, by themselves, provide a basis for judicial review. This guidance will shape how FOIA plaintiffs construct their cases and underscores the independent discretion that OSC maintains in deciding whether to investigate alleged FOIA violations.
