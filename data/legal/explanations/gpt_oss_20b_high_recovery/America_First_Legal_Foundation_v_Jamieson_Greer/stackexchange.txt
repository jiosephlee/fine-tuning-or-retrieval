Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How do FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) and OSC § 1216(a)(3) interact, and can OSC investigate without meeting § 552's predicates?
Question:
I’m reading this opinion and am confused about the relationship between § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) and § 1216(a)(3). The court says OSC can pursue a FOIA investigation under § 1216 even though the three predicates of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are not satisfied. But my notes say § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) triggers OSC’s action. Does OSC have a ‘mandatory’ investigative duty under § 1216 independent of § 552? How does the statutory structure explain this relationship?
Answer:
**Short answer**

FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) makes OSC *mandatory* to investigate *only when* a court has ordered the release of records, has awarded the agency attorneys’ fees, and has specifically questioned whether the withholding was arbitrary or capricious.  
Section 1216(a)(3) gives OSC a *discretionary* authority to investigate any allegation of arbitrary or capricious withholding, regardless of whether those three FOIA predicates are met. In other words, OSC can choose to investigate under §1216(a)(3) even when §552(a)(4)(F)(i) is not triggered, but such an investigation is not required by law.

---

### 1.  What the statutes require

| Statute | Mandatory trigger | Discretion, or ‘may’? |
|---------|------------------|----------------------|
| **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** | *If* (1) a court orders production of an improperly withheld record, (2) the court awards the requester attorneys’ fees, and (3) the court expressly asks whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously” with respect to the withholding, **then** OSC **must** ‘promptly initiate a proceeding**. | – |
| **§ 1216(a)(3)** | OSC “shall conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under § 552”. | **Discretionary** – the policy statement in § 1216(c) (“may investigate and seek corrective action…in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved”) shows that the investigator’s decision to pursue an investigation is at OSC’s discretion. |
| **§ 1216(c)** | Gives OSC the procedural means to conduct the inquiry (notice, subpoena, etc.) **as if** it were enforcing a *prohibited personnel practice*.  | Providers of the *toolbox* for a discretionary investigation. |

> **Key point** – *§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not “limit” OSC’s power under § 1216(a)(3)*; rather, it provides a separate trigger that, when its predicates are met, turns OSC’s discretionary power into a **mandatory** action.

---

### 2.  How the Court explained the relationship

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the district court first incorrectly treated § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as if it restricted OSC’s authority under § 1216. The appellate court corrected that view:

> “… § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under § 1216 …” (see Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5).

The court recognized that OSC could investigate under § 1216(a)(3) *even when the three FOIA predicates were absent*, yet it also noted that § 1216(c) “committed that decision to OSC’s discretion” (see the same opinion, at *6*). Thus, the court’s own language shows that OSC **does not have a mandatory investigative duty under § 1216 independent of § 552**; the “shall” in § 1216(a)(3) merely establishes OSC’s *authority*, and the optional language in § 1216(c) confirms that the investigation is discretionary.

---

### 3.  Practical outcome

1. **If the three predicates of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are met** – OSC *must* investigate.  
2. **If the predicates are *not* met** – OSC *may* investigate under § 1216(a)(3); it can decide to proceed or to decline, and it is not legally required to do so.  
3. The *mandatory* part of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) cannot be used to compel OSC to investigate arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings in the absence of a FOIA court order meeting the three predicates.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for your note

| Original note | Revised note (based on the opinion) |
|---------------|-------------------------------------|
| § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) triggers OSC’s action **and** limits OSC’s authority under § 1216. | § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) triggers a *mandatory* OSC investigation *but does not limit* OSC’s overall authority under § 1216. OSC may investigate under § 1216(a)(3) at its discretion when the three FOIA predicates are absent. |

The statutory structure therefore explains the court’s view: the **FOIA warrants a mandatory investigation only under its own strict trigger**, while the **OSC law provides a broader, discretionary tool** that can be used independently of that trigger.

### Does OSC's enforcement discretion under § 1214(c) and § 1216(c) allow a court to compel it to investigate a FOIA violation?
Question:
The opinion notes that OSC disclaimed authority because the predicates of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were unmet and that § 1216(c) cements the decision to investigate or not as discretionary. I’m unsure whether a federal court can compel OSC to conduct a § 1216 investigation. If OSC has no ‘mandatory’ obligation, what limits the court’s ability to intervene? Is there any precedent that a court can force OSC to conduct a § 1216 investigation?
Answer:
**Answer**

No.  The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) does not have a statutory duty to investigate a FOIA‑violated withholding, so a federal court cannot compel it to do so.  The Court’s own analysis shows that the discretionary language of § 1216(c) “commits that decision to OSC’s discretion” (America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer, 2025 U.S. App. 4, *6).  Because the statute does not appoint a required investigation, a court is limited to reviewing whether OSC acted within its discretion, not to ordering an investigation.

---

### 1.  Statutory text

* **§ 1216(a)(3)** contends that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”  
  The Court notes that ◊ *id.* § 1216(c) follows, stating that OSC “may investigate … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”  The operative word in § 1216(c) is *may*—a textbook discretionary trigger.

* **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** is the “trigger” normally required for OSC to act, but the Court explains that OSC “disclaimed authority … because the three predicates for triggering an investigation … were not satisfied.”  The District Court’s earlier ruling that “section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under § 1216” (Am. First Legal Foundation v. Kerner, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5) was struck down and replaced by the Court’s own reading of § 1216(c) as discretionary.

---

### 2.  Court’s reading

> “the court declined to order OSC to investigate any FOIA policy **because, in its view, section 1216(c) committed that decision to OSC’s discretion**” (Id. at *6).  

The Court explicitly held that the statutory scheme does not create a mandatory investigative obligation.  It therefore found no grounding to compel OSC to conduct the investigation, even though OSC has the *authority* to do so.

---

### 3.  Limits on judicial intervention

The doctrine is straightforward: when a statute gives an agency *discretion* rather than a *requirement*, the judiciary may not compell the agency to exercise that discretion.  The Court’s pronouncement that § 1216(c) “commits that decision to OSC’s discretion” establishes the ceiling on judicial reach.  The appellate court did not have to consider stand‑and‑over‑demands for compulsion because the statute itself lacks the force of a duty.

---

### 4.  Absence of precedent forcing OSC to investigate

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the Court vacated the district court’s judgment, remanded, and never addressed the idea of ordering OSC to investigate.  The opinion contains no citation to a case that held a court can compel OSC under § 1216.  Thus, within the bounds of this case you find no precedent that a court can force OSC to conduct a § 1216 investigation.

---

**Bottom line**  
Because § 1216(c) assigns OSC discretionary authority, a federal court lacks the power to compel OSC to investigate a FOIA violation.  The opinion affirms that discretion, and there is no precedent in the case to overcome that limitation.

### Why does the court deny AFL standing based on an informational injury from DOJ’s non‑compliance with a FOIA request?
Question:
AFL claimed injury from DOJ withholding records, yet the appellate court says that doesn’t create standing. The district court found the FOIA request was denied. However, the appellate court states the injury is not concrete because any informational injury flows directly from DOJ, not OSC. I’m puzzled: if the relief seeks to compel OSC to investigate, how is the plaintiff’s injury linked to OSC? Did the court view the informational injury as too speculative or is there a residual standard for informational standing?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The court dismissed AFL’s standing because the “informational injury” AFL alleges comes from *Donald J. Utter* (the DOJ’s refusal to produce FOIA records), not from the Office of Special Counsel (OSC).  Under Article III standing, a plaintiff must show an injury that is *caused* by the defendant and that a favorable judgment would *likely* redress.  The chain of causation from OSC’s refusal to investigate to DOJ’s non‑compliance is too speculative, and even if OSC had to investigate, section 1216 does not give AFL a statutory right to the information that would be produced.  Hence there is no concrete, particularized injury traceable to OSC and no basis for redressability, so AFL lacks standing.

---

### 1.  To have standing, the plaintiff must

1. **Suffer an injury in fact that is concrete and particularized** – *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560‑61.  
2. **Show a causal link** between the injury and the defendant’s conduct – *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367, 383.  
3. **Demonstrate that a favorable ruling would likely redress the injury** – *Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC*, 126 F.4th 660, 667.

The appellate court applied these requirements even to “procedural injury” claims, noting that the standard is *relaxed*, but not abolished (see *Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d 1152, 1157).

---

### 2.  AFL’s first standing theory: *the injury comes from DOJ’s withholding*

AFL argues that OSC’s refusal to conduct a § 1216 investigation *caused* DOJ to withhold the emails it requested.  
The court found this theory “fails both causation and redressability.”

* **Causation:**  
  *The alleged causal chain is**:  
  1. OSC fails to investigate → 2. OSC might (but is discretionary) pursue § 1214/§ 1215 action → 3. MSPB sanctions the DOJ employee → 4. DOJ could then decide to produce the records.*  
  This chain relies on *speculation* about two independent agencies not before the court (MSPB and DOJ) and about OSC’s discretionary enforcement decisions.  The court quoted FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med. to stress that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (*FDA*, 602 U.S. 383).  Because the connection is speculative, AFL cannot establish that the injury was caused by OSC.

* **Redressability:**  
  Even if OSC had been bound to investigate, a court order compelling OSC to investigate would **not** directly make DOJ produce the requested records.  The court explained that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not on which *actors* might later comply.  Consequently, a favorable decision “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its injury is “too speculative” (*AF... v. Jamieson Greer*, 605 U.S.—actually the D.C. Circuit’s statement).

Because the injury was traceable to DOJ and not to OSC, AFL’s claim lacks the **causal link** required for standing.

---

### 3.  AFL’s second standing theory: *information that OSC would disclose*

AFL contends that if OSC undertook a § 1216 investigation, it would “confer an entitlement to *notifications* and *information* under § 1214” and thus could give AFL that information.

The court rejected this because:

* Section 1216 does **not** create a statutory entitlement to receive investigative findings or documents.  
  > “A section 1216(a)(3) *requirement that OSC conduct a § 1216 investigation* does not give AFL an entitlement to *receive information*.” (*AF... v. Jamieson Greer*, 605 U.S.)

* Even if OSC considered § 1214 notice requirements, the decision whether to follow them in a § 1216 investigation is **discretionary** (*Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487, 495‑96).  AFL acknowledges this discretion in its brief.

* Because no statutory right exists, AFL’s “informational injury” is not an **injury in fact** under Article III; informational injuries alone are insufficient unless tied to a right to that information (see *Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23; *FEC v. Akins*, 524 U.S. 11, 21).

Thus AFL does not satisfy even the *relaxed* criteria for procedural injury.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

- The alleged injury is **informational** and originates from DOJ’s actions, not OSC’s.  
- The causal link between OSC’s refusal to investigate and DOJ’s non‑compliance is speculative.  
- Section 1216 does not provide a statutory right to the information that would be disclosed, so AFL’s claim of an informational injury fails.

Because AFL cannot meet the three Article III requirements—injury, causation, and redressability—the court concluded AFL *lacks standing*.  Accordingly, the district court’s judgment was vacated and remanded for dismissal.

---

**Reference from the opinion**

> “*AFL’s first standing theory is that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has prevented AFL from receiving the records it requested under FOIA … any informational injury from DOJ’s refusal to disclose agency records is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.*” (*AF… v. Jamieson Greer*, 605 U.S.)  

> “*Given this speculative chain, AFL has not shown that a favorable judgment here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury.*” (*AF…*, 605 U.S.)  

> “*AFL also asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy … but section 1216 does not create an entitlement to receive information.*” (*AF…*, 605 U.S.)

These passages capture why the court rejected AFL’s standing.

### What distinguishes a procedural injury from a substantive injury under Article III standing, and how does that apply to AFL’s claim?
Question:
The opinion discusses ‘procedural injury’ and ‘procedural right in vacuo’ and mentions relaxed standing rules for procedural injury (e.g., Ctr. for Law & Educ.). I’m not clear on how the courts separate procedural vs substantive injuries. Does a violation of a procedural right automatically make the injury procedural, or does it depend on the nature of the impediment? And how does that determination affect AFL’s standing claim under § 1216?
Answer:
**Procedural vs. substantive injury under Article III**

Article III standing requires (1) *injury in fact*; (2) causation; and (3) redressability.  
When the injury alleged is an *infringement of a statutory procedural right*, courts do **not** treat the injury as a pure, discrete “procedural injury” that automatically satisfies the injury element.  Instead, they use a *relaxed* framework that takes the procedural right as a *protective* mechanism for a *threatened concrete interest* and asks whether the violation of that right has actually harmed that interest.

* “A procedural right in vacuo” – a right that protects no real or threatened interest – is *insufficient* for standing (see *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009)).  
* When a procedural right **does** protect a concrete, particularized interest, the court relaxes the *imminence* and *redressability* prongs but still requires a *real injury in fact* and a *causal link* between the violation and that injury (see *Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005)).  

Thus, a statutory violation is “procedural” **only if** it is limited to the mechanics of the process and the claimant’s interest is merely the *right to the process* (as in a denial of a hearing).  If the same statutory violation has the effect of depriving the claimant of a protected, non‑processual interest—such as ownership, property, or the ability to obtain information—it becomes a *substantive* injury, even though the wrong was committed in a procedural context.

---

### 1.  Was AFL’s §1216 claim a “procedural” injury?

AFL invoked §1216(a)(3), which says OSC “shall…conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552” and may “seek corrective action under § 1214 and disciplinary action under § 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”  

The court treated the §1216 obligation as a **procedural right** that *would* give AFL a *right to an investigation*.  The relevant passage is:

> *“Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.”* (cf. *Ctr. for Law & Educ.*, 396 F.3d 1157)

AFL’s claim, however, was that OSC’s *failure* to investigate deprived it of two types of information:

1. **The FOIA records DOJ had withheld.**  
2. **Notices and information that OSC would have produced under § 1214.**

Both are *informational* injuries.  Under the opinion, “any informational injury … is a concrete, particularized interest” (*Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23).  Therefore, the injury AFL points to is not merely the loss of a procedural right; it is the loss of the *content* the right is meant to preserve.  In effect, it is a *substantive* injury, even though the wrong was carried out through a procedural channel.

Because AFL alleges a *substantive* harm, the **relaxed procedural‑injury standard is not the useful framework**.  The court could not infer that the “procedural right” was the only element of the injury; it must still satisfy the conventional standing prongs.

---

### 2.  Why AFL’s procedural‑injury argument failed

The opinion explains that although the statutory wording does create a *procedural right*, AFL has not shown:

* **Injury in fact.**  
  The chain begins with OSC’s *non‑action*.  AFL then claims that because OSC does not investigate, DOJ will not produce the requested records.  This causation is *speculative*; the court notes the *attenuated* link and the independent choices of two agencies that are *not parties* to the suit:
  > *“…the causal connection between OSC’s failure to conduct a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s failure to comply with the FOIA request is too speculative and attenuated….”* (p. 35)

  The concrete informational injury is traceable only to DOJ, not to OSC.  Consequently, the injury is *not* an injury in fact caused by OSC’s alleged violation.

* **Redressability.**  
  Even if OSC were compelled to investigate, the court explains that the investigation could result in a corrective action or disciplinary action *only if OSC elects so* (see *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985)).  
  The decision whether OSC would actually sanction DOJ employees and whether that sanction would, as a *chain reaction*, compel DOJ to produce the records belongs to *other actors* (the MSPB and DOJ).  As the opinion puts it:
  > *“A favorable judgment…would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress…one cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.”* (p. 36)

  Therefore, AFL cannot demonstrate that a favorable ruling in this case would *likely* lead to the relief it seeks.  The *redressability* prong fails even under the *relaxed* standard for procedural injuries.

Because AFL fails the injury‑in‑fact and redressability prongs—both of which remain binding even for procedural‑injury claims—the court concludes that AFL has **no Article III standing**.

---

### 3.  Bottom line for AFL

- A **violation** of a statutory procedural right *does not automatically* make the injury procedural.  Whether or not the injury is treated as procedural depends on the *effect* of the violation on a protected interest.  
- In AFL’s case, the concrete deprivation of records is a **substantive informational injury**.  The procedural right to an OSC investigation did not produce a *direct, non‑speculative injury* to AFL.  
- Because AFL could not show a real injury in fact, nor a pressing need for the court’s intervention to redress that injury, the court declined to grant standing.

Thus, the distinction between procedural and substantive injury is not a mechanical classification but a question of *consequence*—and AFL’s § 1216 claim failed both *because the injury was substantive* and *because the causal link to OSC’s alleged procedural violation was too speculative*.

---

### How does the relaxed standing standard for procedural rights apply to § 1216 investigations, and why is it insufficient for AFL?
Question:
The appellate court notes that for procedural rights the court relaxes standards of imminence and redressability. Yet AFL still failed to satisfy ‘injury in fact’ and causation. How do we apply the ‘relaxed’ standard in a case where the alleged injury is protected by a procedural right? Why doesn’t the mere threat of losing procedural protections count as a concrete injury sufficient for standing?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Court’s “relaxed” standing rule only lightens the *imminence* and *redressability* hurdles for claims that allege a violation of a statutory procedural right.  It does **not** relax the *injury‑in‑fact* or *causation* requirements.  AFL’s alleged injuries were either non‑existent or too attenuated to be concrete, so the relaxation could never help it.  A mere threat that a procedural right might be lost is insufficient because a standing plaintiff must show an actual, particularized injury that can be remedied by a judgment *in this case*.

---

### 1. What the Court calls a “relaxed” standard

> “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of **imminence** and **redressability**, but not the issues of **injury in fact** or **causation**.”  
> **Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005);** see also **Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (en banc).**

Thus, in a prospective‑or‑procedural‑right case the court still requires:

1. The plaintiff’s interest be *concretely and specifically* harmed;  
2. The alleged breach of the right must *cause* that injury;  
3. The injury must be *actual or imminent* (but the Court applies a lesser test of imminence); and  
4. A favorable decision *really* has the prospect of remedying that injury (less stringent redressability).  

The powerful “relaxed” language applies only to *(2) imminence* and *(3) redressability*, not to *(1) injury* or *(2) causation*.

---

### 2. How the doctrine applies to § 1216

Section 1216 gives OSC the *authority* to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings, but it does **not** create a *statutory entitlement* to be investigated or to receive investigative notice.  The Court’s own analysis in this case states:

> “AFL’s first standing theory is that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has prevented AFL from receiving the records it requested through FOIA.”  
> … “[The] alleged causal chain is too speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing.”  
> **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer, 2025 U.S. App. O… (D.C. Cir.)**, 3128‑3129.

Because the statutory right is procedural and *discretionary*, the injury to AFL that comes from *not being investigated* is an abstract or “in‑vacuo” deprivation—something the Court has repeatedly ruled insufficient for standing (Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009)).  There is no concrete, subject‑matter injury that still dangles until a court order compels OSC.

---

### 3. Why a “threat of losing procedural protections” is not a concrete injury

AFL attempts to argue that OSC’s refusal to investigate *threatens* its future right to the FOIA records it can request or to investigative notice.  The Court underscores that this threat is *hypothetical*:

> “AFL’s second standing theory—based on its asserted informational interest in the fruits of the OSC investigation itself—fares no better.” … “AFL does not contend that OSC must engraft section 1214 notice requirements onto its section 1216 investigations.”  
> **…** “In the absence of an alleged entitlement to receive information under § 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory.”  
> **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer, 2025 U.S. App. O…, 3132‑3133.**

The “threat” remains a speculative future harm that depends on the *concurrent* actions of OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ—actors *outside* the case—making the injury *non‑concrete* and *imperfectly redressable*.

For procedural‑right cases the Court still requires that the alleged injury **happen** as a *result* of the defendant’s conduct.  A mere possibility that a future decision might favor the plaintiff is not enough (see *Ctr. for Law & Educ.*, 396 F.3d at 1157; *Fla. Audubon*, 94 F.3d at 664).

---

### 4. Bottom line

Because the Court never relaxed the *injury‑in‑fact* requirement, AFL had to show an actual, particularized harm that would result from OSC’s refusal to investigate.  It could not, and did not, show that such a refusal *directly* harmed the freedom of the FOIA records it sought or that OSC had to provide any notices or information.  Its causal chain was too speculative (involving DOJ’s independent FOIA‑production decision and the MSPB’s discretionary sanctions), and its claim of “informational standing” lacked an entitlement under § 1214.

In short, the “relaxed” standard cannot convert a *procedural‑rights claim that fails to show concrete injury* into a standing claim.  AFL’s injuries were either *not there* or *too speculative*, so the Court correctly found it lacked Article III standing.

### Does a procedural right in vacuo—such as OSC’s privilege to investigate—constitute a concrete injury, or must the plaintiff have an actual entitlement to information to have standing?
Question:
The court said a procedural right in vacuo is insufficient, citing Summers v. Earth Island. How does that principle operate? If AFL’s only claim is that OSC should investigate, does that itself constitute an injury, or does it need to lead to a tangible benefit (e.g., notice, information)? Are there any cases that clarify when a procedural right alone is enough for Article III standing?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
A “procedural right in vacuo” – e.g. OSC’s statutory duty to investigate – is *not* a concrete injury that alone satisfies Article III standing.  A plaintiff must show that the deprivation of that right actually deprived him of a legally protected interest – most often the right to receive the information the statute guarantees.  The court in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* made that plain by rejecting AFL’s two standing theories.  The first failed because the informative injury traceable to OSC’s inaction was *speculative*; the second failed because OSC’s “investigation” under § 1216 does **not** create an entitlement under § 1214 to receive notice or information, so there was no concrete injury at all.

---

## 1.  What the opinion says about a “procedural right in vacuo”

> “AFL claims that OSC violated its alleged procedural right to an investigation under section 1216.  But the *deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient* to confer Article III standing.” (¶ 70)

That is the legal maxim at the heart of *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488 (2009), which the court cites: a mere statutory entitlement, **if it is not tied to a particular, legal interest of the plaintiff, does not itself create standing**.

The court goes on to invoke the “relaxed standard” that applies to procedural‑injury claims:

> “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” (¶ 67)

The *relaxed standard* is a salve, not a bypass: the injury in fact question **remains**.  Even a procedural violation must “result in injury to some concrete, particularized interest.”

---

## 2.  AFL’s first theory – “OSC merely failed to investigate”

AFL’s first standing theory was that OSC’s refusal to conduct a § 1216 investigation *prevented* AFL from obtaining its FOIA‑requested DOJ records.  The court found this untenable because:

* The actual *informational injury* comes from DOJ withholding records, **not** from OSC’s inaction.  
  “Any informational injury from DOJ’s refusal to disclose agency records is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.” (¶ 78)

* The causal link between OSC’s non‑investigation and DOJ’s FOIA refusal is *speculative*.  
  “The alleged causal chain is…too speculative and attenuated.” (¶ 78–79)

Because the injury is neither *directly* caused by OSC nor *certain* to be remedied by a favorable ruling on OSC’s conduct, AFL failed the injury‑in‑fact element.  Hence, no standing.

---

## 3.  AFL’s second theory – “OSC must provide notice and information”

AFL tried to claim an injury to the *informational benefits* that a § 1216 investigation would provide under § 1214’s notice rules.  The court dismissed this theory for two reasons:

1. **No entitled right to receive the information.**  
   “Section 1216(a)(3) … does not create an entitlement to receive information under § 1214.” (¶ 83)

   A plaintiff can only suffer a concrete injury when the law gives him (or her) a *right* to a particular piece of information.  OSC’s discretion to follow § 1214's procedural requirements is *not* a statutory obligation; it is merely a discretionary choice.

2. **Lack of a concrete, particularized injury.**  
   “Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under § 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory.” (¶ 83)

Because no specific legal interest was lost, the plaintiff’s injury is *non‑existent*, not merely “speculative.”  The court therefore could not even contemplate redressability.

---

## 4.  Bottom line – when a procedural right is enough

Following *Summers* and the court’s application there, a procedural right turns into a *concrete injury* only when it:

1. **Creates a specific legal interest or entitlement** (e.g., the statutory right to receive records, to be notified, to sue, etc.).
2. **The deprivation of that interest can be plausibly linked** to the defendant’s conduct.
3. **A favorable judgment would actually redress the loss** (it would bind the parties or prevent the loss from occurring).

If the statutory right is merely *in‑vacuo* – a flair for the law that the plaintiff would enjoy *if* it were enforced – but the plaintiff’s success would require the actions of *other, independent* actors (another agency, a third‑party decision maker, etc.), then the injury is *speculative* and does not satisfy Article III standing.

---

### Take‑away for students

When you’re drafting a standing claim that hinges on a procedural right, you’re asking whether that right **actually touches a specific right you possess**.  If it does, you can meet the injury requirement; if it does not, the right remains an *in‑vacuo* formalism, and you’ll be stuck.  The *America First* opinion illustrates the two common pitfalls—speculation about causal chains and the absence of a statutory entitlement to the information you want.

So, in short: **A procedural right in vacuo is not enough**; the plaintiff must have a *real, entitled* interest (normally the right to the information) and must show that its deprivation is concrete and particularized.

### How do courts analyze causation and redressability when an agency’s discretionary decision could indirectly affect a plaintiff’s FOIA right?
Question:
The appellate court’s reasoning was that the causal chain from OSC’s lack of investigation to DOJ’s eventual release of records involved speculation about two independent agencies. I’m confused about the test for causal connection here. Is speculation about what other agencies might do unacceptable, and what standard determines whether redressability is satisfied? How does the court decide if a favorable judgment here would ‘very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress’ the plaintiff’s injury?
Answer:
**Answer – How courts treat causation and redressability in a FOIA‑standing case**

The D.C. Circuit in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* (2025) makes it clear that an agency’s discretionary refusal to investigate does not automatically confer standing when the plaintiff’s alleged injury is an informational one that depends on another agency’s future conduct.   The opinion rests on the two classic standing predicates – **injury in fact + causation** – and on the *redressability* test that ties a favorable judgment to a realistic change in conduct by the parties before the court.

---

### 1.   Causation

* The court says that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (p. 4‑2,1, citing **FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.**).  
* In this case, the alleged injury is DOJ’s withholding of records.  OSC’s refusal to investigate was *not* the proximate cause of that withholding; rather, it would only *possibly* influence two separate post‑decisional actions:

  1. OSC might direct the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) to sanction DOJ employees.  
  2. The MSPB might, in turn, compel DOJ to comply with the FOIA request.

* The court calls this “speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit” and judges it too speculative to satisfy Article III causation (p. 4‑3,3).  

Hence, a plaintiff cannot rely on an agency’s **discretionary “enforcement”** decision or on a chain of independent actions that are not compelled by the very court that is considering the standing claim.  The causal link must be a *direct* or *proximate* effect of the defendant’s conduct.

---

### 2.   Redressability – “Very likely spur the next steps”

* The redressability standard in standing “requires that a favorable judgment **very likely spur** the next steps necessary to redress the injury” (p. 4‑3,3).  
* The court points out that redressability is judged on **binding effect on the parties before the court**, not on whether the judgment would “persuade actors who are not before the court” (p. 4‑3,4; citing **Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC** and **Haaland v. Brackeen**).  
* In this case, even if OSC had been compelled to investigate, the court notes:

  * OSC itself has no statutory obligation to provide notices or follow 1214 procedural rules in a section 1216 investigation (**Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.**).  
  * A court order directing OSC to investigate would **not be reasonably likely to compel** the MSPB or DOJ to act, because those agencies’ steps are not bound by the court’s decision.

Therefore, a favorable judgment in the standing action would not *very likely* bring about the DOJ’s records release or the MSPB’s sanctioning – the necessary intermediate steps hinge on independent actions beyond the court’s reach.

---

### 3.   Bottom line for the plaintiff

* **Injury in fact**: AFL’s claims that OSC’s refusal caused its FOIA injury is insufficient. The injury is attributable to DOJ, not OSC.  
* **Causation**: The chain OSC → MSPB → DOJ is speculative.  
* **Redressability**: A favorable ruling does not bind MSPB or DOJ, so it would not spur the needed steps to redress AFL’s information‑seeking injury.  

Because causation and redressability fail, AFL lacks Article III standing under the *relaxed* procedural‑injury doctrines applied here.

---

#### Take‑away citations (from the opinion)

| Issue | Opinion Reference | Key Holding |
|-------|-------------------|-------------|
| Causation | p. 4‑3,3 (FDA v. All) | Speculative chains to unspecified actors do not satisfy causation |
| Redressability | p. 4‑3,3 (Hecate Energy, Haaland) | Redressability requires binding effect; cannot rely on persuading outside actors |

The court therefore vacates the district court’s judgment and remands for dismissal for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.
