Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Standing with FOIA: The D.C. Circuit’s Tightening of the Procedural Injury Standard

The D.C. Circuit’s opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* (No. 24‑5168, Oct. 3, 2025) may seem, on its face, to be a simple standing boot‑strap: the court refuses to let a plaintiff sue because it has no Article III standing. Yet the decision quietly reshapes how courts treat procedural‑rights claims under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The appellate court’s blank‑check interrogation of the “informational injury” standard clears the way for FOIA litigants — especially activist groups and public‑interest NGOs — to know that a “right to an investigation” on its own is no longer sufficient to survive a standing challenge. Instead, courts will demand that a plaintiff demonstrate a concrete, particularized injury that is causally tied to the agency action in question. The implications ripple beyond FOIA, into the very interface between statutory enforcement mechanisms and plaintiff claims that skirt pure procedural inquiry.

## Why This Matters

FOIA is one of the grandest constitutional tools for ensuring that government activity remains subject to public scrutiny. For decades, litigants have turned to the Act to compel agencies to produce documents that, by the letter of the law, must be released. Alongside the statutory requirements to prove a good‑faith request and to pay reasonable fees, a separate body of enforcement has—through the Office of Special Counsel (OSC)—been envisioned as a guardian against procedural wrongs, including FOIA withholdings. The OSC’s statute specifically calls for it to investigate “arbitrary or capricious withholding of information” when a court orders production. In *Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit went back to the very bedrock of those statutory safeguards and asked, if a plaintiff cannot show an injury that is the result of a procedural right that is harmful in itself, can it still pursue litigation on the merits? The answer, doctrine is clear: no. 

The overt refusal to regard a procedural right — even one carved in statute — as baring an injury that satisfies the federal standing test, marks a turning point. Prior to this case the “procedural‑rights doctrine” for FOIA could, in theory, allow plaintiffs to rely on claims that a statutory enforcement mechanism simply did not act. In practice, however, *Jamieson Greer* demonstrates that courts pause to weigh the actual material injury that such inaction may inflict on a plaintiff’s legal interests. The result clears a broader doctrinal space for FOIA plaintiffs to assess their standing in the face of OSC inaction. The decision, like the trajectory of FOIA litigation in the 1990s and 2000s, tells litigants that a formal right to a statutory hearing is, by itself, uncertain ground to foreclose agency discretion.

## The Case in Brief

America First Legal Foundation (AFL), a public‑interest group, sought to force the Department of Justice (DOJ) to release emails between an outside attorney and DOJ’s Civil Division, claiming that DOJ had improperly withheld records. In a FOIA suit, the DOJ produced the documents (and continued to produce). AFL also asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s policy of not searching employee e‑mail for responsive records without that employee’s explicit consent, as mandated by § 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act. OSC refused, citing that the statutory trigger under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) was not met. AFL then sued OSC, alleging a violation of a procedural right to a Section 1216 investigation.

A federal district judge partially ruled for AFL, but on remand rejected the claim for lack of standing, deeming the procedural right to an investigation insufficient. AFL filed for final judgment and was dismissed. The D.C. Circuit, reviewing de novo, found AFL had not satisfied Article III standing and set aside the judgment. The court affirmed that OSC’s decision not to investigate was not a misstep under Section 1216, yet emphasized that, still, AFL had no concrete injury to argue.

## The Procedural‑Rights Doctrine Re‑examined

AFL’s legal narrative hinged on the jurisprudence that procedural safeguards, when denied, reveal a concrete, particularized injury. The “procedural‑rights” standard, as articulated in *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.* and *Citizens for Law & Education v. Dep’t of Educ.*, relaxes the “injury in fact” and “imminence” prongs: once a plaintiff shows a denied procedural right that protects a victimized interest, the injury, causation, and redressability analyses become more palatable. However, the D.C. Circuit comes in with a counter‑argument that a procedural right in vacuum, one devoid of a protected interest, does not “tokenless” injury; it must be tied to a concrete and particularized harm. The judge reminded that a procedural injury “does not convert a mere abstract right into concrete harm” without a “legally protected” interest being jeopardized.

AFL advanced that the 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) investigation warranted an information injury because the requested emails were missing. The court held the instruction to “enquire” was a *discretionary*, not mandatory, activity—section 1216(c) implicitly allowed OSC to choose whether or not to conduct a Section 1216 investigation. In the absence of such a duty, a plaintiff lacks the object of the procedural right. Moreover, even if OSC had been mandated to investigate, the court found that AFL did not present a concrete injury: the factual claim that DOJ had withheld the requested records did not derive from OSC’s inaction but from DOJ’s own refusal. Thus the chain of causation was too speculative for the purpose of securing standing.

Consequently, the D.C. Circuit clarified that a procedural‑rights claim under FOIA can only succeed if the injury is directly tied to the statutory recourse that is breached. Without that connection, the claim is fractionally void. This is no distant reading. Prior cases — *Hefner v. Jenkins*, *UMC v. Stahl* — have recognized that a plaintiff must show that an agency’s refusal to comply with FOIA constitutes a deprivation of a statutory right that the court can remedy. *Jamieson Greer* reinforces that reasoning and extends it to procedural‐rights claims with respect to OSC.

## Causation and the Speculation Problem

At the heart of the court’s decision is the insistence that the causal chain between a defendant’s conduct and a plaintiff’s injury must be *direct*, not a series of “unfettered choices” by third parties. The D.C. Circuit’s critic, meriting attention, pointed out that any informational injury arising from DOJ’s failure to comply with FOIA is plainly traceable to DOJ, not to OSC. If the plaintiff wants a remedy that stems from an OSC investigation, they must prove it irreversibly leads to an outcome that corrects the DOJ’s conduct — “that a favorable judgment here would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury. Inferences across multiple bureaucratic layers—OSC ∟ MSPB ∟ DOJ—were deemed “speculative" and barred by precedent in *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* and *Hecate Energy v. FERC*.

The decision underscores the judicial caution against opening the floodgates of wide‑ranging speculation. A plaintiff seeking to rely on a procedural claim against the OSC will now confront a higher hurdle: regardless of how the statutory text may motivate OSC to investigate, the plaintiff must show that by coalescing the OSC’s investigation, the flow of duties in the chain will collapse in their favour. The court cannot "compel" or speculate about the conduct of independent agencies. Judges cannot determine that the MSPB would impose sanctions or that DOJ would produce the documents if OSC had conducted an inquiry. They can only base that on concrete, factual, concrete injury, not on the theoretical interplay of independent actors.

## OSC Enforcement and the FOIA Process

The ruling brings OSC’s discretion to the fore. Under § 1216(a)(3), OSC “shall…conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under Section 552.” Yet, as the D.C. Circuit made clear and reinforced by the court’s remand instructions, that statutory text does not compel OSC to investigate unprompted. OSC’s disavowal of authority “because the three predicates for triggering an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not satisfied” stands. This decision therefore ultimately reaffirms that the OSC's investigative powers are discretionary, not compelled by mere statutory receipt of a FOIA claim. As an administrative agency, OSC remains a footnote in FOIA litigation unless the Court actually orders an investigation, which itself is barred without a standing plaintiff.

In a broader administrative context, the decision resonates with *United States v. Teitelbaum*, wherein the Supreme Court held that the mere presence of statutory enforcement mechanisms is insufficient where the plaintiff has no concrete injury. In the FOIA realm, the decision suggests that FOIA enforcers – OSC, Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) – must be anchored in real, actionable claims before they can be engaged as a forum for relief.

## What This Means for NGOs and Public‑Interest Plaintiffs

The takeaway for activist groups, public‑interest litigants, and even ordinary FOIA users is sharp: a procedural‑rights claim on the premise that OSC “should” investigate will likely fail standing if that claim does not connote a concrete injury that flows from that specific investigation. To succeed, a plaintiff may now need either (1) to establish that their FOIA request was denied or unfulfilled, and that OSC’s investigation would directly subject DOJ to potential disciplinary action that would rectify that denial; or (2) to align the procedural right to an investigation with a statute that offers a remedy that is specific to the plaintiff’s injury.  

In practice, this might mean that a FOIA roadblock, even if rooted in a procedural misstep by the OSC, may not constitute an actionable injury unless that procedural step directly halts the agency’s withholding of documents. NGOs will probably have to retool their litigation strategies: focus on the agency’s refusal rather than OSC intermediation; bring FOIA claims with heavier reliance on statutory and constitutional arguments about the entities themselves; or seek administrative remedies directly from the not‑enforced agency.  

The decision also signals to those who view FOIA as a litigation-agnostic shield that the agency’s ability to enforce discipline is not automatically loophole‑proof. OSC’s discretion creates an additional layer of uncertainty. But that uncertainty clarifies that the criminal or civil graft of FOIA is still the weapon of choice, not the procedural whim of the OSC.

## Redressability Treads the Narrow Road

An advanced point in the opinion is the court’s choice not to evaluate redressability as a separate claim. Since AFL failed to articulate a concrete injury, the D.C. Circuit deemed it meaningless to test whether a favorable judgment would “likely spurn” the chain of events needed to remedy that injury. This echoes *Haldane v. Brackeen*: the host of “redressability” is contingent upon the injury and not on a speculative rule of the Court. In everyday litigation terms, “redressability” is a rails‑murder law—without an injury, the question of whether a judgment could fixed it is moot. The decision is an efficient exercise of judicial economy, but it also places doctrine squarely with the injury-first test; a procedural‑rights claim that cannot bind a concrete injury defaults to ‘no standing.’ 

## The Broader Consequence for FOIA and Administrative Law

In the long run, the D.C. Circuit may be nudging a shift from *procedural injury* to *factual injury* for FOIA plaintiffs. The decision will signal that, like in *Visking v. Cramer* or more recent *Haiti v. U.S.*, a statutory procedural right alone is insufficient. Instead, the demonstrable effect of that right being withheld is pivotal. In court‑statistical terms, the decision re‑establishes the Gordian knot between the *right* to a hearing and the *ability* of the plaintiff to *use* that hearing to overcome an agency’s refusal. When the lien on that right is not weighty enough, the courts will require a proof of impact, which makes FOIA litigation more fact‑heavy and less “procedural” and more about the content of the docket. This may dissuade frivolous claims but can also deter legitimate public‑interest litigation that currently could have relied on the procedural‑rights doctrine for initial standing.

The decision also has potential downstream effects on administrative adjudication. The OSC’s refusal to investigate FOIA withholdings under the costy condonation associated with § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) suggests that future litigants will need to push through the evaluation under ordinary FOIA statutes, perhaps with a new tailoring to the managerial spirit rather than the procedural compromise. It may encourage a new generation of FOIA strategists who understand the boot‑strapped, case‑by‑case nature of the S.C. and the lack of an automatic procedural remedy in the face of any denial or involuntary withholding by an agency.

## How to Move Forward

For those trailing the *Jamieson Greer* line after the decision, a clear path emerges: document explicit processing issues, exemplify the way the agency’s actions produce a concrete injury (e.g., failure to produce record, potential reputational or litigation harm, or financial loss), and then tie the OSC’s role directly to that injury. Present any existing statutory causation that would require OSC to find proper corrective action. The courts will still scrutinize whether an OSC investigation after a FOIA claim would materially change the outcome.

Furthermore, NGOs might consider adopting a strategic approach that emphasizes FOIA requests’ *immediate spiny liquor*—the withheld records themselves—rather than procedural setbacks. A well‑drafted FOIA request that clearly demonstrates the necessity of the documents and the probable harm if withheld will survive the standing hurdle. Meanwhile, as the law remains uncertain around discretionary enforcement by the OSC, litigators may counsel clients to seek remedies directly from agencies or from the Merit Systems Protection Board, understanding the difference between a “check‑in” and a “check‑out” in the investigative statute.

## Conclusion

*America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* reaffirms the doctrine that a procedural right in glyphic statute does not automatically equate to a concrete injury. The D.C. Circuit’s insistence on a causal and specific injury for standing is an affirmation of the two‑step process embodied in *Lujan* and *Summers*. The decision shifts the FOIA landscape: a procedural‑rights claim that rides on OSC’s statutory leash no longer suffices to open a door into office, unless that leash connects to a tangible, personalized injury. For activists and FOIA litigants, the take‑away is clear: subpoenas and FOIA provisions remain powerful, but the path to enforce them often requires a more granular showing of injury than the procedural‑rights doctrine would traditionally offer.  

The real lesson is that public‑interest defendants cannot salute procedural infirmity as a remedy in itself; they must have injuries that are not illicit, not vague, but firmly anchored in the present—right now—point of a Government that refuses to produce the information it ought to share. As the lilt of FOIA continues to sing, the *Jamieson Greer* note will be heard as a tightening of the procedural injury requisite, ensuring that only those with a truly concrete claim are granted the right to compel an investigation.



# OSC’s Enforceability Puzzle: Mandatory vs. Discretionary Power Under § 1216(a)(3)

## Why this case matters

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the D.C. Circuit confirmed that the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) does not automatically have a duty to investigate any alleged FOIA violation, even when Congress set up a “brisk” investigative mechanism in the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA). Instead, OSC’s role is shallow and conditional: it only must step in when a FOIA‑enforcement trigger—specifically a court order under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—closes the door on statutory discretion. For FOIA litigants seeking new evidence (like the AFL’s attempt to compel the DOJ to search employee e‑mail), that distinction means there is no “automatic” corridor for an independent agency watchdog; the litigation has to be the press‑ganged limiter of OSC’s powers, or, failing that, nothing happens.

The take‑away is straightforward: the statutory maze does not give OSC a “claw” that can yank up an agency’s hidden e‑mail treasure under CTA. If you want OSC, you must first get a FOIA decision that forces OSC’s hand. The court’s opinion doesn’t make OSC a soft‑target, but it does make OSC a soft little duck in the FOIA pond.

## The statutory maze: § 1216(a)(3) vs. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

The CSRA created OSC to enforce “prohibited personnel practices” (5 U.S.C. § 1214) and to enforce a limited array of “other” misconduct, including FOIA violations (5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)). The text says, roughly, that OSC must “conduct an investigation” of any allegation of arbitrary or capricious withholding of FOIA‑prohibited information and may pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215 as if it were dealing with a personnel violation.

Actorally, that reads as *mandatory*. Yet the FOIA statute itself (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)) expressly limits OSC’s obligation to *three* specific predicates: (1) a court orders the agency to produce records wrongfully withheld; (2) the court awards attorneys’ fees to the applicant; and (3) the court narrowly asks whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with regard to the withholding. Only when all three strand be true does OSC have a *mandatory* investigative duty, according to the statutory text. For the rest of the time, OSC’s “investigate” turns into *discretion*.

The miss‑reading came from a district court that, on the face of it, treated OSC’s Section 1216 powers as a blanket collar on any FOIA dispute. The B‑section in the appellate brief was “if OSC disclaimed authority to investigate, the district court found that the three predicates for triggering an investigation under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not satisfied,” implying that OSC would have to investigate irrespective of FOIA’s own enforcement clause. The panel had to repair that oversight.

## The Court’s analysis of the “must‑investigate” seizure

The D.C. Circuit, in a de‑novo spelling out, decided that § 1216(a)(3) does not force OSC to step in under any circumstance. The critical is the word *shall*: it is not a “shall” bound to all FOIA violations, but an instruction inviting action *in the same way as a prohibited personnel practice* *if* such a practice is alleged. The underlying exam is the way the statute unfolded: OSC’s investigatory role is discovered only when a federal court first slides the FOIA remedy across the desk.

The court carefully parsed FOIA’s own enforcement clause to isolate it as the only dispatch for OSC, and found that without a court order meeting the three‑point rubric, OSC can decline to investigate. The underlying policy in this scenario was that Congress wanted OSC to stand ready only when someone—having gone through the arduous FOIA gridlock of litigation—demonstrated the need for a more targeted investigation.

The underlying *stand‑up* threatens a corrective one: when the court found *no* Article III standing for the AFL, the issue did not even surface. That is perhaps a bit embarrassing, because the court could have reached the substantive question of OSC power, but the procedural skeleton slid away until a later past or a different case team would need to wrestle that tool into the mix.

## Why the AFL is dead‑on for standing, and what that means for FOIA lawyers

Stand‑up hinges on injury. The court carefully weighed the two alleged injuries: 1) the AFL’s FOIA request was denied, 2) the OSC dismissed investigating.

The litigation was protected by the loud roar of the “injury must be concrete.” Two separate injuries that appear immediate are, in theory, fine. The problem is causation. The court points out that the injury inflicted by a FOIA denial belongs to the *doer* of the denial: the DOJ, not the OSC. The chain of causation flounders. The speculation becomes a *chain of speculations*, something the court evinces “caution” against when assessing redressability.

The same in the second theory: the AFL may have thought that a “mandatory” OSC investigation would produce its own “notice” and “information” under § 1214. However, the law makes the OSC’s *exercise of section 1214's notice requirements in that scenario a matter of discretion, and there is no statutory right to information just for being in the system. The AFL, by making a false assumption that the existence of a dispute automatically entitles it to a tracing of the investigation, missed the real bar of standing.

The court's footnote is that the key current appellate doctrine stating that “a procedural right in vacuo does not suffice to grant standing” (Summers v. Earth Island Inst.) is still in play, so the mere clip of a procedural statute does not add a new front to the "concrete interest" test. That means FOIA litigants—unless they can somehow tie themselves to an actual FOIA proceeding that forced OST to schedule a punitive investigate—cannot wield an external watchdog to restrain an agency.

## Doctrinal implications: the tabletop of FOIA enforcement

1. **Contraction of OSC’s policy‑probing scope**  
   By consigning OSC’s investigatory role to the second half of FOIA (when the court, through § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), signals a “mandatory” crack), the ruling narrows OSC’s ability to act as a *self‑correcting* agency. The earlier expectation that OSC could rear willow its arm for organizational culture or policy missteps is shown to be overstated. That contracts the “insider” voice of the law enforcement model.

2. **Stuckness of FOIA as the primary gatekeeper**  
   The delineation strengthens the primacy of the FOIA judicial process itself. The solitary trigger for OSC involvement is that very process. That means, at least in principle, FOIA decisions are even more critical for triggering any independent review, for likely effect the budget of the “get involved” stage is shifted towards the courts instead of OSC more readily.

3. **Ill‑fated interpretive ambition**  
   Some have argued that the thread between the CSRA–FOIA interface was fabricating an “umbrella” that implicitly required OSC to probe every FOIA violation. The court’s decision invalidates those claims, but also calls into question devoted remains of the civil service system creating oversight structures that do not always deliver on their promises. The consequence is that Congress may have to re‑think that oversight tether at a statutory level, or delegate more power to an informal oversight board.

4. **Implications for standing and procedural problems**  
   The holding didn't reach a question of *how* the injury was redressed or the analysis of the *procedure*. But it reasserted that *Article III standing* remains a barrier to civil‑administrative challenges that are manifestly “discretionary” and not tied to an explicit FOIA case. That echoes the tradition that FOIA's sanctioning arm is not a secretive watchdog that can wrest details from the agency on its own volition.

## What this means for the downstream: FOIA litigation, OSC policy, the e‑mail czar

The stakes for FOIA litigants are curved. If you want to force an agency to admit your documents—especially corporate or email data you suspect are hidden—then the usual FOIA path is the only route. To get OSC involved, you need a **court order** that triggers § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) after plaintiff takes the long FOIA procedure, obtaining *sources* for retaliation or signed letters.

Because OSC can decline to investigate absent that order, the drafters of policy for the Court under FOIA advisories may realize that the official enforcement can be circumvented by not going to court or may decide to align the CAD on the proper enforceability path.

In practice, the decision leaves the policy enforcement very much in the hands of the Merit Systems Protection Board and the DOJ’s own disciplinary processes, rather than an independent OSC oversight that once could step in and question the fairness of an agency's handling of public records. This should be a holy‑stagger for FOIL Scholars and a grim reminder that power in the FOIA architecture is sometimes congealed in the NSO of the litigation system.

## Concluding thoughts

OS C’s “enforceability puzzle” is essentially a “must‑investigate vs. discretionary” conundrum resolved in favor of the latter. This decision, while procedural in nature, validates an Organic Boilerplate that puts FOIA's own arbitration at the center of all such investigations. It signals that the statutory infrastructure was never meant to carry an autonomous watchdog that under any circumstance will look into an agency’s alleged FOIA sting (the missing e‑mail search policy). The CSRA's lofty vision is tempered by FOIA's call for accountability only in the presence of a discrete judicial requirement.

For attorneys and litigants in FOIA cases, the functional take‑away is that OSC will rarely be an ally unless you trigger FOIA’s own enforcement clause. For policy makers, it is a reminder that the interplay of statutory texts needs to be deliberately layered: a “mandatory” engagement might better be written explicitly, or mock‑resilience to conundrums might be included in corporate policy. And for the audience of this blog, the lesson is simply: These statutory labyrinths can literally rearrange the role of agencies from introspective to indifferent, and the election to go down the FOIA road is the only way to remind OSC that it has a job to do.



# Using OSC as a Trump Card? Practical Lessons for FOIA Litigants in the Wake of the Ruling  

## The Case at a Glance  
On October 3, 2025 the D.C. Circuit delivered a brief but striking opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* that will reverberate through every FOIA‑litigant on the brink of a “fine‑print” remedy. The federal court held that the plaintiff lacked Article III standing to sue the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate a FOIA policy it claimed was arbitrary. In doing so, the judge clarified that OSC’s investigatory authority is strictly bounded by § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) and that a plaintiff’s “procedural injury” alone is insufficient to trigger a standing analysis that ultimately fails.

In short, PACs and other public‑interest groups can no longer lean on OSC as a back‑stabbing “trump card” in their FOIA battles. The decision inspects the statutory framework, the procedural posture, and the constitutional leash that keeps administrative agencies from shifting court‑made claims onto one another. It leaves FOIA litigants with a hard‑looked requirement to structure their claims around what the law actually provides, rather than the intuitive “let OSC investigate and force the agency to comply.”

## OSC’s Statutory Mandate Revisited  
OSC, established under the Civil Service Reform Act, is authorized to probe “prohibited personnel practices” (5 U.S.C. § 1214) and, more pertinently for FOIA cases, to investigate “arbitrary or capricious withholding” of records (5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)). The statute, however, is not a blank check. As the district court observes, OSC’s investigative mandate activates only when the FOIA itself has issued a judicial order: (1) a court commands the agency to produce, (2) the court awards attorney’s fees, and (3) it explicitly questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not grant OSC a blanket authority to investigate every alleged FOIA misstep. The D.C. Circuit consistently treats OSC as a discretionary enforcer—only engaged when the statute’s own triggers flash green.  

Prior to *AFL v. Greer*, the district court in *AFL v. Kerner* (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023) had suggested that OSC must investigate when it “is warranted.” That reading was effectively dead‑bolted by the D.C. Circuit: OSC’s role is *not* “mandatory” but firmly tethered to § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). This clarification removes a convenient “redress” option that many FOIA litigants tacitly counted on.

## Standing—The Gatekeeper  
The Court’s analysis turns to Article III standing, a perennial hurdle in administrative litigation. The plaintiffs’ first procedural claim—that OSC had a “deprivation of a procedural right”—falls short because procedural rights in vacuo do not rise to concrete, particularized injuries. *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488; *Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau*, 115 F.4th 618—citing the same rule. The plaintiffs then pivot to two alleged informational injuries: that DOJ denied the records requested under FOIA and that OSC failed to provide notices and information prescribed by § 1214.

The Court points out a fatal causation gap. Any informational injury from DOJ’s non‑compliance is attributable to DOJ, not OSC. Speculation that an OSC investigation would trigger a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) corrective action, which in turn would influence DOJ’s conduct, is “too speculative” and “attenuated.” In *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) the Court rejected speculative causation tied to independent agency decisions. The same reasoning applies here: unless a plaintiff can tie the refusal to investigate directly to the deprivation at hand, the chain dissolves.  

Even if the injury were real, the court applies the “relaxed” standard for procedural injuries (*Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d 1152) and finds that the plaintiffs still cannot satisfy the concrete injury requirement. Lacking standing, the case is dismissed.

## Implications for FOIA Litigation  
For practitioners, the message is blunt: OSC is not a convenient enforcer in FOIA disputes unless the statutory triggers in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are satisfied. That rules out the idea that an FOIA “policy” merely refusing to search employee emails without consent can be pursued through OSC. The Court is explicitly wary of plaintiffs leaning on OSC to police agency policies that never touch in‑court contests.  

Consequently, FOIA litigants must now widen the lens. The “cheap” overlay of OSC disappears, leaving the core FOIA causes of action: refusal, partial compliance, delay, and bad‑faith requests. Cases such as *Center for Law & Education v. Dept. of Educ.*, 2022 F. App’x 122‑125 illustrate that FOIA litigation still thrives on direct agency refusal or ineffective compliance, without the crutch of OSC’s discretionary investigation.

The strategic sway is double‑pronged. First, plaintiffs must rehearse the evidentiary bar for a § 552(b)(1)(A) claim. The agency’s denial and the plaintiff’s “relevant interest” (e.g., a member of the public, a whistleblower, a political actor) remain the mainstay. Second, plaintiffs need to anticipate the possibility that the agency’s conduct is *arbitrary or capricious* but not *subject to OSC*. A refusal to investigate that is purely discretionary does not yet recognizable as a statutory injury that must be remedied through OSC.

## Other Remedies – FOIA and MSPB Payloads  
### FOIA Bad‑Faith and Delay Claims  
FOIA’s Bad‑Faith action is a distinct, albeit narrower, cause of action. It applies when an agency refuses or fails to comply in bad faith, i.e., “severely and intentionally.” The plaintiff bears a heavy burden, but courts in *Burke v. Citizens for a Better Env.*, 104 F.3d 1237 have affirmed that a pattern of bad‑faith refusals, coupled with a clear legal entitlement to the records, can bring a damages suit. Since DOJ, in this case, eventually produced the materials, a Bad‑Faith claim would likely collapse.

Delay claims under § 552(b)(1)(A)(E)(4) cover “unefficient or unreasonable” responses. The complaint would need to show a failure to comply within the statutory time frame (two business days for non‑complex requests, 20 days for complex requests). The strategic nuance is that a delay claim is not automatically winable; the plaintiff must carry a “more severe” or “pattern” burden.

### MSPB Enforcement as a Parallel Route  
Although § 1216(a)(3) cites OSC’s parallel enforcement under § 1214 and § 1215, the Court emphasizes that OSC’s decision to investigate is discretionary. A plaintiff may still use the MSPB to pursue a “prohibited personnel practice” claim, but only if the alleged FOIA non‑compliance is tied to a personnel action that affects the plaintiff – e.g., an employee’s reassignment or demotion for their FOIA request. This vehicle is distinct from OSC’s investigative route but can be effective when a personal‑harm angle is present.

### Judicial Remedy – Direct Court Order  
If a FOIA dispute reaches the courts, the most straightforward remedy is for the court to order production under § 552(a)(4)(F)(ii). The magistrate or district judge can compel the agency to comply, circumventing the reliance on OSC entirely. After the court issues such a subpoena, IFC and the plaintiff can argue that the investigative as well as non‑compliance elements satisfy the statutory mandate, thereby potentially forging an OSC investigation *post hoc*. This route respects the statutory “trigger” of OSC’s authority and preserves standing for any subsequent claim.

## Strategic Takeaway  
The D.C. Circuit’s ruling has trimmed the toolbox of FOIA plaintiffs. Instead of a “secondary” agency that might step in to investigate and correct a policy, OSC is now firmly a *reactive* tool, only activated by a court’s order. The key tasks for litigants:

1. **Assert the Agency’s Guilt under FOIA’s Core Claims**: Confine the lawsuit to the agency’s direct conduct. Demonstrate an actual and actionable refusal, delay, or bad‑faith misbehavior that directly affects the plaintiff’s legally protected interest.

2. **Leverage the DOJ or the Agency Rather Than OSC**: Use DOJ’s or the agency’s refusal to act as the basis for a court order. The court can compel OSC *after* the agency has been forced to comply through FOIA or to produce the records it was supposed to produce.

3. **Explore MSPB as an Alternative “Personnel” Fix**: If the FOIA request triggers personnel repercussions (e.g., retaliation), the plaintiff may file a MSPB complaint to pounce on the prohibited personnel practice element.

4. **Keep OSC’s Scope in Mind for Future Litigation**: OSC’s discretionary power remains significant but limited. An FOIA request that triggers OSC’s investigatory mandate will require a judicial order to compel.

This shift doesn’t render OSC irrelevant. The Office will still be a vital watchdog for personnel abuses and for FOIA violations that are clearly “arbitrary or capricious” when a court has already brought the case forward. It simply shifts the onus onto the plaintiff’s ability to get to that court stage—a stage the Court now requires to be initiated under the “order, attorney’s fees, and a factual review” GPL.

## Final Thoughts  
The 2025 decision is a conservative reaffirmation of the boundary between statutory enforcement and judicial power. OSC can investigate a FOIA misstep only when the statute itself has been triggered; it cannot be pillaged as a stand‑in defendant in a FOIA suit. For practitioners, the lesson is straightforward: don’t count on OSC to hold the agency accountable unless a court has already called for a review. Instead, fire the FOIA claim at its roots—whether through a refusal, delayed compliance, or a procedural anomaly that burdens the plaintiff’s legally protected interest.

The policy undercurrent is also clear: the Court is unwilling to let a plaintiff’s “right to be investigated” substitue for an injury grounded in the agency’s actual conduct. The friction between institutional autonomy and individual relief has never been so pronounced. As FOIA litigation continues to evolve, public‑interest litigators must adapt to the space where the administrative procedure meets the civil‑rights threshold—where standing is the final gatekeeper and OSC is no longer the wonder‑weapon.



# The Interagency Chain: OSC, MSPB, and DOJ – How an Office’s Decision Fuels or Blocks Redress  

## Why This Matters  
America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is not so much a doctrinal tour‑de‑force of FOIA as it is a momentary stoplight in the federal administrative highway. The Decision confirms that a party can’t simply ask a court to force the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to launch an investigation and then count on a downstream chain of actions—MSPB discipline, DOJ record release—to cure its injury. That chain, the court says, is too speculative. For ordinary litigants, the ruling spells out one more reason to hit the paper trail directly for the agency that holds the files, rather than an obscure intermediary. For the administrative law community, it clarifies the validity limits of the “procedural‑right” entry for FOIA claimants and the boundaries of judicial compulsion of independent agencies.

## The Facts in Brief  
The case is built on a very simple FOIA exchange: AFL, a conservative watchdog, requested DOJ’s email exchanges that might illuminate the Department’s treatment of an outside attorney. DOJ denied the request on the (somewhat questionable) ground that it does not search employee e‑mail without consent. The same e‑mails had already been produced during a separate FOIA suit, bruising the claim that an informational injury existed. AFL next directed OSC to investigate the alleged executive policy (no e‑mail searches without consent) under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). OSC recused itself, citing the truism that § 1216 requires a triggering FOIA court order and not an arbitrary directive. AFL, in turn, sued OSC, alleging a procedural injury and two concrete informational losses: a failure to produce the records and the omission of section 1214‑style notice during a potential investigation.

The district court did a “partial win/loss” that froze the process with a CR that called for a remand, leaving AFL essentially stuck. AFL appealed, and the appellate court—treating its review de novo—found AFL lacking Article III standing and remanded it back to the district court, which was to dismiss the suit for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

## Standing Re‑played: Who Gets to Ask Who’s Doing What?  
Standing is a narrow legal sieve. The D.C. Circuit starts from the foundational recognition that a plaintiff must suffer an “injury in fact” that is concrete and particularized, substantiated by a causal link to the defendant’s conduct, and that a favorable judgment would likely bring about a redress. In a FOIA context, some courts have given a “relaxed” standard for procedural injuries, a concession that an alleged violation of a statutory procedural right will hurt the plaintiff’s ability to obtain information. AFL tried to ride that band on two legs: the “lack of OSC investigation” and the “proposed section 1214 notice.” The court, however, rejected even that clemency.

### First Leg: No OSC Investigation, No Records  
AFL’s narrative is: without OSC’s inquiry, DOJ will not “show up” the requested e‑mails. The court, leaning on *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, warned that a procedural right “in vacuo” — a right without a corresponding protected interest — does not fulfill the injury requirement. AFL tried to substitute the “two concrete, informational injuries” as a bridge, but the chain was already too long. The connection between an OSC investigation and DOJ’s actual compliance involved not just one but two independent agencies—a stretch that the court considered a doctrine‑friendly “speculative” sequence, per *FDA v. All for Hippocratic Med.*.

### Second Leg: Section 1214‑Style Notice from a Section 1216 Investigation  
Even if OSC had conducted an investigation, AFL’s demand that OSC deliver the same procedural notice it would give during a conventional “prohibited personnel practice” inquiry was unfounded. The court reminded the reader that OSC’s authority under § 1216 is “discretionary” and does not compel the agency to emulate the notice prerequisites of a different statutory context. In other words, a Plaintiff cannot simply demand procedural particulars where none exist under the statute at issue.

The court’s verdict: AFL fell short on both fronts. There was no injury linked directly to OSC, no compelling link between OSC’s actions and DOJ’s compliance, and no statutory entitlement to OSC’s notice. Hence, standing faltered even before the court could address the substantive question of OSC’s duty.

## The Causal Chain – Speculation or Substantiality?  
The appellate court’s core contribution is a confirmatory pause on interagency enforcement like this: the procedural logic that a “missing investigation” should spark a corrective cascade is too head‑long. In a typical FOIA compliance chain, OSC initiates the probe; the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) moves to correct the record; DOJ responds. Each hop in this process is a decision by a separate, autonomous agency not party to the litigation. The court points out that holding a plaintiff responsible for the thoroughness of another agency’s internal processes would be a step beyond the court’s role, reminiscent of the “tub-rule” in *Heckler v. Chaney* and the modern “no‐speculation” standard of *Hecate Energy v. FERC*.

In sum, the court held: “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” The fascia between OSC’s procedural mandate and DOJ’s record release is a postponed trust that cannot be enforced through a FOIA litigation unless that trust is concretely germinated into a statutory obligation.

## Doctrinal Ripples – Where the Law Leans  
1. **OSC’s Duty Is Contextual, Not Automatic**  
   Section 1216(a)(3) in isolation does not oblige OSC to act when a FOIA claim is filed absent a court order. The statute pairs “investigation of arbitrary or capricious withholding” with the opportunity for § 1214 or § 1215 enforcement *only* if a court explicitly orders it. By reaffirming this, the court gives OSC leeway, it says, in a sphere that is often expected to be reactionary, especially when a FOIA request has no direct FOIA court order.

2. **The “Procedural‑Right” Tetrad Has a Bent**  
   The analyst’s eye sees that *CTV v.* the Court’s decision dims the procedural‑right exception often invoked by FOIA plaintiffs. It underscores the reality that the exception is not a blanket extension of procedural accountability; rather, it is a shaded corner that demands a tangible, statutory entitlement to some procedural benefit. In the absence of such entitlement, a procedural argument collapses.

3. **Speculation vs Causation Is a Litigator’s Hallmark**  
   The court’s “speculation” analysis draws a wider signal: not only is the chain under examination suspicious, but a petitioner cannot proceed on future rumor wars. In administrative law, the standard is sense that a Supreme Court decision will hold that “speculative causation” is insufficient. The *Socr. <…>* reading stands.

## Downstream Effects – What Is at Stake for Litigants and Agencies?  
1. **Litigants Must Anchor Pedestals to Data**  
   More FOIA plaintiffs will likely pivot from “hue of policy” to concrete evidence of withholding. If hearings or internal investigations are secondary, the baseline of the FOIA suit must start with actual nondisclosure or refusal to produce. Legal practice may see a shift in focus toward DOJ compliance actions directly, rather than sub‑reporting.

2. **OSC’s Portfolio Might Shrink**  
   The ruling clarifies that OSC is not a default regulator of every FOIA “policy.” Without a court order, OSC’s activation remains at the discretion of the agency’s internal promotion of the particular enforcement path. Consequently, OSC could narrow its focus to serious, vetted complaints, reinforcing a “trial‑by‑error” approach that still speeds up many enforcement actions but without a guaranteed pipeline from any arbitrary FOIA claim.

3. **MSPB’s Role Remains Unchanged, but Its Accessibility is Limited**  
   The Merit Systems Protection Board keeps its corrective and disciplinary toolbox intact. But because OSC can’t order it to investigate a policy unless a court forces the action, the board’s docket will be tinged with fewer unsolicited FOIA‑related cases, potentially altering the board’s public perception of civil service protection.

4. **Congressional Opportunities**  
   If stakeholders are unsatisfied with the narrow scope of OSC’s statutory authority, a congressional amendment to clarify that OSC must investigate every FOIA denial could widen the enforcement channel. That would albeit restrain OSC’s discretion but could increase FOIA compliance. The court will likely watch that with interest.

## A Mini‑Green‑Flag for the Judiciary  
While the Decision primarily reaffirms the limits of standing, it does a good job of demonstrating the judiciary’s capacity to keep agency autonomy in check. It is a cautionary note to litigants who might think invoking the “two‑party” chain is a shortcut, and a reminder that the courts are not supposed to pro‑actively dictate what one agency will do. That seems, for now, the best compromise between protecting citizens’ access to information and preserving the independence of federal procedural and corrective systems.

## Final Thoughts  
America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is a tidy lesson in FOIA mechanics and standing doctrine. It is clear: litigants cannot rely on a cascade of agent decisions that are too disconnected from the immediate wrong. When the chain is too speculative, the court steps back and says “no.” For those navigating FOIA enforcement, the message is unmistakable: get the records from DOJ directly, or structure a claim that pinpoints a concrete, statutory right to OSC’s investigation — something that the Court found absent here. In the quiet permutations of this case, the interagency chain remains as slippery as ever, but now the scholarly waters are a little clearer.
