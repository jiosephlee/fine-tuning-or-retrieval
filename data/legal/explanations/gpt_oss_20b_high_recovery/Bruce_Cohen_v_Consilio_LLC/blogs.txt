Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Collecting Penalties From the Top: The 8th Circuit’s Confirmation That Only the Commissioner Can Seek Wage‑Law Penalties  

## Introduction  

On October 6, 2025, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s summary judgment against Bruce Cohen in a case that began as an ordinary wage‑serious claim but ended up raising an under‑sung issue: who may recover statutory penalties under Minnesota’s wage laws?  In Cohen v. Consilio, the court concluded unequivocally that, for the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), it is the state commissioner—not the employee—who may seek and recover penalty amounts that the statutes authorize.  That ruling may seem technical, but it has a surprisingly sweeping reach.  It sets a clear line between the civil remedies an employee can sue for and the penalty enforcement that is blunted to the state.  The effect is two‑fold: it constrains individual wage‑law litigants from pursuing what might otherwise look like punitive redress; and it anchors enforcement within the public agency that administers the statutes.

## The Statutory Landscape  

Minnesota’s wage statutes converge in one tidy principle: statutory penalties are expressly earmarked for the state.  The MPWA’s § 181.171(1) explicitly adds the commissioner to the “civil action” clause.  In fact, § 181.101(a) is so narrowly worded that it requires the commissioner to “serve a demand for payment” before any penalty can be imposed.  In *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.* the Minnesota Supreme Court noted that when statutory language is “plain and unambiguous” the legislature’s intent is apparent, and the law must be given effect as written.  The MPWA’s penalty framework therefore forces a “state‑first” approach: employers breach wage provisions, the commissioner demands payment, and if the employer does not comply within ten days the commissioner can impose a penalty that must be paid back to the employee.

The MFLSA, Minnesota’s analogue to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, mirrors that structure.  The same language that prevails in the MPWA over the wage‑payment sections applies to penalty provisions in the MFLSA; it requires that the penalty be payable to the commissioner, not to the employee, and that the commissioner’s action be the only vehicle for enforcing it.  The MWTA, meanwhile, focuses on notification requirements for policy change but likewise does not provide for civil penalties that individual employees may recover.

## Court’s Analytical Path  

The Eighth Circuit’s panel proceeded “de novo,” mindful of the fundamental question: does an employee have standing to recover the statutory penalty?  Reviewing district‑court filings, the panel first rejected Cohen’s MPWA claim—that average‑daily‑wage penalties were available to him.  The court’s language was lucid: “only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101.”  The court also recalled *Lagasse v. Horton*, which explained that the legislature had, in a prior statute, intentionally left enforcement to the commissioner.  By this reading, the employee cannot take the “penalty” prize to the courtroom; the state’s enforcement apparatus is the sole delegate.

The court then turned to the MFLSA claim.  Although the parties had noticed a “stipulation that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages,” Cohen still sought statutory penalties.  The court, noting Pepper’s *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* line on citizen standing for punitive remedies, decided that the MFLSA penalties constituted a **government** action, payable to the commissioner.  Therefore, Cohen’s claim suffered a jurisdictional check: “the requisite personal interest must continue throughout the litigation.”  Without continuing policy violation, the claim could be moot.  The panel thus vacated the summary judgment on the MFLSA penalty request, remanding the district court to decide the mootness question.

With regard to the MWTA, the court’s review was swift.  Because the statute does not provide a civil penalty for failing to notify employees of policy changes, the claim “lacked enough meaningful argument to warrant revision” and was dismissed in its original form.

Finally, the court noted a procedural issue with Cohen’s injunctive relief request, which had evolved in the briefing to a withdrawal of the 2019 policy.  In light of *InfoDeli v. C.* standards, the court declined to consider the change and defers that question to the district court.

A concurring opinion, while endorsing the MPWA reasoning, cast doubt over the MFLSA penalty request, noting that the courts were not yet convinced the plaintiff had standing under the penalty scheme.

## Analytical Perspective  

The court’s construction overwrites a common misconception that private wage claimants can tap the statutory penalty belt as a form of punitive redress.  The Monday‑morn‑state‑commissioner theme rings through the MPWA’s language: attendance at the “law’s harm” is for the state, not for the employee.  This is not a surprising reading.  By reserving enforcement to the commissioner, the legislature effectively placed the penalty mechanism in the hands of the department that administers wage policy, ensuring that enforcement is subject to administrative oversight, pleadings, and due process.  A unilateral plaintiff claim for a penalty would invert that design and potentially open the door for “penalty chases” that ignore the broader regulatory compliance program.

From a policy standpoint, the line is clear: the penalty is a “public” remedy aimed at correcting systemic wage violations.  Employees, while entitled to overtime payment and liquidated damages, are not the intended recipients of punitive money; they merely receive restitution for lost wages.  The commissioner’s role as a “prime mover” in penalty enforcement thus preserves the wage statutes’ spirit as a regulatory instrument rather than a private enforcement tool.

### Standing and Mootness  

The tricky part is the MFLSA claim, which the court left unsettled.  The underlying question—do employees have standing to recover penalties that are earmarked for the state—remains a standing‑vs‑public‑interest debate.  The court in *Roy et al.* would recall that a plaintiff must show a **direct** injury; if the penalty is to be paid to the commissioner, the employee’s injury is indirect, mediated through the state, which raises a traditional standing concern.  In *Town of Chester*, the Supreme Court emphasized that a plaintiff must sustain a “continuing interest” to maintain jurisdiction.  Here, if Consilio’s new overtime policy is already repealed or changed, Cohen’s prospective interest is gone, and the claim may be moot even if ESL.

### Potential Downstream Shifts  

By clarifying the penalty do‑not‑claim rule, the 8th Circuit nudges wage‑law litigants toward two distinct paths: (1) seek ordinary damages (compensatory, liquidated, or statutory double damages) that directly benefit the employee; or (2) rely upon the state commissioner to enforce penalties.  This bifurcation has tangible effects.

For class actions, the absence of a private penalty claim limits a potential lever for a binding class remedy. The class members can still seek collective damages, but cannot aggregate a “penalty fund” among themselves.  This may make class certification easier or harder, depending on the policy and the commonality of injury.  Employers will find the picture more predictable: the “penalty threat” still exists, but it is the state’s game, not the employee’s.

## Doctrinal and Practical Implications  

1. **Clarification of the “payment of penalties to the state” paradigm** – The 8th Circuit’s ruling confirms a literal statutory reading.  This informs precedent explainers such as *People v. Gintshall* and *Kobes v. Cupps*, clarifying that wage penalty enforcement is separable from civil restitution.  
2. **Standing in wage‑law penalty claims** – The decision accelerates analysis of whether private plaintiffs can recover penalties.  Future cases may adopt a two‑step test: (a) is the penalty payable to the plaintiff, or (b) does the plaintiff have a “direct” interest?  Similar to *Business v. State*, the test will hinge on where the penalty is routed.  
3. **Litigation strategy** – For wage‑law litigator, the focus must shift to making a convincing **damage** argument.  The inability to seek penalty amounts forces a rigorous cooperative approach with the commissioner if the plaintiffs want to limit double or multiplier penalties.  
4. **Class action dynamics** – The absence of a private penalty mechanism may alter class certification standards under MBIA and the Collier doctrine.  Plaintiffs may need to emphasize the broader impact of the employer’s misconduct rather than the penalty itself.  
5. **Policy reforms** – States that desire a hybrid approach will need to explicitly authorize the employee to recover penalties, perhaps by amending the wage statutes or drafting a new administrative nuisance penalty rule.  The 8th Circuit’s stance signals that such changes would be necessary because the current statutes preclude it.

## Policy Implications and Future Outlook  

The panel’s holding underscores a fundamental principle: wage statutes are regulatory, not punitive, in the sense of personal recovery.  This provides a commitment that penalties will be administered uniformly by the state, preventing a flood of individualized penalty suits that could overload the system.  However, the decision also accentuates a potential gap in employee recourse.  In places where employers regularly slip through the penalty net—if the commissioner is slow or unwilling to enforce—employees may feel that the punitive engine is dampened.  A future trend might see states choosing to tweak the statutes, giving private parties a chance to recover penalties, thereby increasing the “stick” enforcement.  

From a broader perspective, the decision puts the 8th Circuit in line with other states that treat wage violations under the umbrella of administrative enforcement.  It also reinforces a doctrine that accepts that penalties are, by nature, a public tool to maintain compliance—a tool that falls outside the purview of private litigation.  The ripple effect could include more precise drafting of state wage statutes, or the introduction of clear mechanisms for employees to invoke penalties under a *wage‑law‑class* statute.

## Conclusion  

The 8th Circuit’s ruling in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio* has delivered a clean and authoritative connection between statutory text and enforceable rights.  By reasserting that only the commissioner may seek penalties under the MPWA, MFLSA, or MWTA, the court has drawn a bold bar: employees cannot harvest penalty money in suit.  Instead, they must turn to ordinary damages or rely on state‑designated enforcement. The decision thereby clarifies the contours of wage‑law claims, shapes the litigation strategy for plaintiffs, and delineates the line between state‑run regulatory enforcement and the private right to recover from wage theft.  

For wage‑law practitioners, the takeaway is unambiguous: when framing a complaint, focus on wage‑payment and compensatory damages. When penalties are the target, coordinate with the commissioner and consider how the state's enforcement regime prioritizes, registers, and administers those recoveries.  And for employers, understand that the penalty risk still looms—just not through a private civil action. The decision may be an administrative nicety to a taxpayer, but it is a decision that reshapes the punitive landscape of wage law in Minnesota—and, by extension, in other states that follow a similar statutory pattern.



## Mootness, Standing, and the Future of Wage Penalties: Lessons from Cohen v. Consilio

When a company swipes a new overtime policy into its handbook, the question often becomes: “What happens to the penalties employees were fighting for?” The 8th Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* (No. 24‑2079, 8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) offers a clear view of how a policy change can truncate both the availability of civil penalties and the court’s jurisdiction over those penalties. The ruling cleanly separates state‑level MPWA penalties, federal MFLSA penalties, and the now‑irrelevant MWTA claim, and it delineates the tenuous line between “no continuing injury” and “no continuing interest.” Below, we unpack the Court’s reasoning, explore the standing doctrine it invokes, and consider how this decision molds the future of wage‑law litigation.

### Why the Case Matters

At first glance, the case appears to be routine overtime litigation – an aggregate demand for back pay and liquidated damages. Yet a sizeable payment of over $256,000, followed almost immediately by a settlement stipulation that the employee “had been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed,” left the real question outside the ordinary: could the plaintiff still recover *statutory* penalties after the employer stopped the alleged violation? The answer depends as much on Congress’s statutory language as on the particular facts of the overtime policy’s demise. The 8th Circuit’s willingness to vacate the MFLSA penalty claim on mootness grounds sets a template for future cases where an employer’s policy shift may “absolutely clear” the unlikelihood of recurrence.

### MPWA Penalties: The Commissioner’s Gatekeeper

The Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) is a classic example of a law that intertwines employer obligations with a quasi‑regulatory enforcement mechanism. Section 181.101(a) is blunt: only the Commissioner may “serve a demand for payment of wages” and, if the employee does not comply within ten days, the Commissioner may charge and collect a daily penalty. The statute is unambiguous in its “plain language and unambiguous meaning” – “When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31). Thus, the 8th Circuit rightly held that an employee who has been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages cannot demand the daily‑average wage penalty that the MPWA affords; the penalty is a statutory right of the Commissioner, not of the employee. The district court did not err on that front.

### MFLSA Penalties: Standing Collapses When the Policy Dims

Directing our attention to the federal claim, the situation becomes more nuanced. The Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) allows a plaintiff to recover statutory penalties if an employer violates the Act. However, pursuant to *Friends of the Earth* v. Laidlaw, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), the plaintiff must show that the alleged injury (the threatened risk of ongoing unlawful conduct) hath indeed a continuing element – that the violation is likely to recur. The Ninth Circuit recognized that a voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not strip a court of jurisdiction: *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.,* 455 U.S. 283 (1982). Yet the *8th* Circuit adds a subtle twist: a claim may become moot if the facts that give rise to the alleged misconduct no longer exist. The Court noted that “both statutes do not provide a direct statutory right for an employee to recover penalties; the penalty itself is a penalty on the employer prior to the settlement of wages.” (Cohen v. Consilio, 8th Cir. 2025, 10‑11). Thus, the employee’s standing to seek penalties hinges on two factors: (i) the existence of a concrete injury – a clear waiver or policy that will likely continue to result in unpaid wages; and (ii) an event that leaves a continuing interest – the policy’s status.

Because the parties agreed – and the court noted – that Consilio openly communicated a new premium overtime policy in 2019 and that the policy may still be in force, the Court could not yet decide whether the claim was moot. After triaging obvious issues (the MPWA claim and the MWTA claim), the Court vacated the district court's summary judgment on the MFLSA penalty claim and remanded the case. The remand order instructs the district court to examine whether any “continuing injury” remains, thereby forcing the defendant to either still practice the allegedly wrongful overtime rule or face the possibility of a penalty.

### Standing for Penalties: Is an Employee’s Claim a Personal Injury?

Standing in a penalty claim is at once procedural and substantive. “A plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief” (Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., 581 U.S. 433). The 8th Circuit’s emphasis on *continuing interest* underscores a crucial issue: a plaintiff might accept the settlement of wages without a residual legal injury; but the statutory penalty is a regulatory remediation that must affect the defendant moving forward. *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F.4th 1181, considers the “continuing interest” proximate to the original injury. Thus, mere receipt of back pay does not preserve a cause of action if the underlying policy no longer continues.

### MWTA Penalties: The Letter of the Law

The Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) attempts to widen the statutory net for employers’ unearned wages by mandating notice of overtime policy. However, the 8th Circuit determined that the MWTA contains no provision for civil penalties, nor does it provide an employee the means to recover a penalty from the employer directly. The Court simply noted that the allegation of “failure to provide required notice” has no statutory remedy in the form of a penalty. Consequently, the district court’s dismissal of the MWTA claim was affirmed without further ado.

### Injunctive Relief: When Procedural Missteps Matter

Cohen’s request for an injunction to rescind Consilio’s overtime policy is another lesson on the importance of procedural propriety. The Court reminds us that a new relief request must be introduced via an amended complaint or a separate pleading, not a paper‑back summary‑judgment brief. *InfoDeli, LLC*, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6. This omission, while minor, illustrates that even in the context of wage‑law disputes, the procedural rails remain firmly in place.

### Doctrinal Implications: A New Weight on Penalties

The decision clarifies several doctrinal points relevant beyond Minnesota:

* **Penalties are not an employee’s individual right** – only the state or the federal government can enforce them, which limits the circumstances in which an employee can pursue them.  
* **The “continuing injury” or “continuing interest” standard applies** to any statutory penalty claim. Even a partial policy change that removes the risk of non‑payment may convert an active claim into a moot one.  
* **State statutes may mirror federal standards** or diverge. The MPWA’s “Commissioner‑only” focus demonstrates that an employee cannot simply litigate a penalty that is meant for the intermediary.

### Future of Wage‑Law Litigation: A Policy‑Driven Landscape

The 8th Circuit’s ruling tethers wage‑law enforcement to the prevailing policy settings at the time of litigation. Employers can now lean on a policy change (within reason) to curtail claims for penalties; plaintiffs must anticipate that the success of a penalty claim hinges on a policy that is still active. This puts a premium on *evidence* about the status of the policy at a hearing. For litigants, it will be imperative to secure proof of the *persistent presence* of an alleged violation. If a defendant can demonstrate that a policy change has resolved the alleged non‑payment, the claim’s redundancy becomes persuasive, potentially leading to dismissal.

Congress may weigh additional clarification. Viewers might note that the *MFLSA* was drafted with the intention that employers could be penalized for a pattern of non‑payment, not merely for one isolated overpunch. But the *Credibility* of the penalty hinges on the *continuance* of non‑compliance. In anticipating future legislation, lawmakers might consider tightening the statutory language to preserve penalty rights independent of ongoing policy issues or clarifying that penalties are automatic upon a violation, irrespective of policy changes.

### Conclusion

*Cohen v. Consilio* reminds us that wage‑law litigation is as much a question of *how* a policy ends as of *what* the policy was. The decision underscores that the statutory world of penalties is slotted to the particularities: the MPWA’s Commissioner‑driven penalties, the MFLSA’s injury‑based penalties, and the MWTA’s lack of penalty at all. Moreover, the Court’s treatment of mootness and standing presages a future in which many would-be penalty claims may be cut short by a pragmatic change in policy. For employers, this creates a new incentive to review policies proactively before they run afoul of the law; for employees, a clarion call to capture the precise footprint of a violation before it disappears into policy update. In a legal landscape that blends regulatory oversight with contractual proceedings, *Cohen* will undoubtedly be the touchstone for debate on whether the law can still protect individuals when the very threat that gave rise to the claim has gone out of existence.



# No Penalties, No Problem? Why Minnesota’s Wage Theft Act Provides No Enforcement Remedy in Wage‑Laws Cases  

## The Case That Restated “There Are No Penalties”

In October 2025 the Eleventh Circuit heard Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC, a case that, on the surface, looked like a routine wage‑law suit. A plaintiff alleging he had been deprived of overtime and that his employer had changed its overtime policy without giving proper notice. The district court stripped any claim for statutory penalties under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) and held that none existed. The Fifth Circuit confirmed that dismissal, but clarified that *only* the MWTA’s notice‑and‑correct window was operative, not a punitive‑damages mechanism.  

Why did the appeals court say so? Because the MWTA, once read in its plain language, does not supply employees with the power to enforce a penalty or a specific monetary penalty. The only consequence of violating the Act’s single notice provision is the recovery of unpaid wages, not the punitive damages that employees habitually rely on under similar federal statutes. That distinction is important not only for those working in Minnesota but for anyone who has ever treated a state wage‑law penalty as a de‑facto deterrent.  

## A Closer Look at § 181.032

Section 181.032 of the Minnesota Statutes sets out a single requirement: employers must provide notice to employees of any change to their wages, and the employee may seek remedy if the employer fails to do so. The statute’s language enumerates the notice provisions and a “notice‑and‑correct” window. It does not, however, contain any of the classic statutory‑penalty phrasing—“civil penalty,” “penalties,” or “punitive damages.” Under the general rule of statutory interpretation, a statute does not create a remedy where the text is silent. When a legislative intent is unclear, courts give the plain, ordinary meaning to the words, and when words are absent, they stay absent.  

The Eleventh Circuit leaned on this principle. It pointed out that the MWTA’s executive branch, through the Department of Labor, has the authority to compute wage adjustments and charge wages, but the statute never says that any amount collected is a penalty paid to the state or to the employer. The “penalty” language is simply gone missing, and a penalty can’t magically appear as an implicit consequence of a notice failure. The court therefore felt it was reaching beyond the law to let plaintiffs seek statutory penalties under the MWTA.

## Injunctions versus Penalties

Even though the MWTA had no statutory penalties, the question still floated on the surface: could Cohen compel Consilio to rescind the policy that he alleged violated the Act? The answer lay in the distinction between injunctive relief and punitive damages. An injunction is a form of equitable relief that stops ongoing conduct. In wage‑law cases it is often available when an employee argues that the employer’s course of action is illegal and that monetary damages alone are inadequate. But the MWTA did not explicitly grant injunctive relief. It only talked about the right to recover unpaid wages if a notice failure occurred.  

Legal scholars and courts have long held that when a statute expresses a specific remedial desire—such as monetary damages—it is limited to that remedy unless the text says otherwise. The Eleventh Circuit noted that while its counterpart in the MFLSA case granted statutory penalties (because the text explicitly allowed “civil penalties”), the MWTA did not. Thus, Cohen’s claim for an injunction was, in effect, a non‑issue: the doctrine would have required an amendment or a new pleading to raise an injunctive claim that was not part of the original complaint.

While some might argue that injunctive relief ought to be available through state labor boards or administrative penalties, the MWTA simply did not grant such powers. The statute was limited to notice and wage payment, not to coercive orders against an employer.

## Where Employees Rely on Punitives

Historically, when employees have confronted wage‑theft suits, they have leaned on the punitive damages provisions of federal statutes such as the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) or its state equivalents like the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). These statutes expressly authorize statutory penalties—often double the unpaid wages—thus giving an incentive to employers to comply with wage rules. Employees find these penalties crucial because they signal that the state will not tolerate wage violations, providing an external deterrent beyond the private right to recover unpaid wages.  

In Cohen’s case, the MWTA’s silence on penalties meant that employees would have had to rely on either federal law or the MFLSA. The court’s dismissal of the MWTA claim underscored that the state’s wage‑law enforcement strategy may inadvertently leave employers with a less punitive deterrent if they are not subject to the MFLSA. For a business based primarily in Minnesota, missing the MFLSA’s full cutoff, employers may now have a smaller risk profile if they can skirt the state’s wage‑theft laws while complying with minimal notice standards.

## Doctrinal and Practical Consequences

The decision, while narrow, signals broader doctrines in labor law. First, it reinforces that a state can delegate “notice” rights to employees but cannot grant them “penalties” without explicit statutory language. Second, it clarifies that the modern “duty of care” to prevent wage theft is enforced through monetary damages, not through solidarity‑based deterrents. Third, it invites lawmakers to reconsider whether the MWTA’s notice requirement is a sufficient deterrent in the absence of financial penalties.  

One consequence is that state labor boards, which already have limited investigatory powers, will continue to rely on wage‑lawsuits to enforce compliance. Without penalty provisions, the primary leverage remains the threat of civil action for unpaid wages. Employers may respond by tightening internal policies to avoid the two‑tier risk structure—maintain the new policy but in full compliance with the MFLSA as well, thereby avoiding any claim for penalties.

The court’s decision also has downstream effects on litigation strategy. Plaintiffs must now weigh the merits of pursuing the MFLSA’s statutory penalties versus relying on state wage‑law provisions. In states where statutes mirror Minnesota’s MWTA layout—notice gamely existing but penalties absent—the pattern may repeat: employees must bring separate litigation under federal statutes if they seek punitive damages.

## Where We Go From Here

Do employees need a state‑level penalty to deter wage theft? Minnesota has answered “no” for its MWTA. The decision is clear: the MWTA is a notice statute, not a penalty statute. Addressing the gap may require legislative change. State lawmakers might add a penalty clause, or they might simply let employers rely on existing federal penalties. Either way, what matters now is that employees and attorneys in Minnesota must game the system, leaning heavily on federal claims where punitive damages are paramount. Meanwhile, the Eleventh Circuit’s ruling will be cited in future cases, ensuring that state statutes without explicit penalty language will not be glossed over by courts hungry for deterrence.

Ultimately, Wisconsin, Texas, or Ohio may learn from Minnesota’s example: The law’s effectiveness hinges on its words. If lawmakers wish to hold employers hostage for wage violations, they must write the statutes to be strict, with explicit penalties; otherwise, employers will thrive with minimal risk of punitive judgment. For now, Cohen’s case is a quiet, bureaucratic affirmation: maybe appoint a penalty; the law can make it happen.



# Policy Changes in the Midst of Litigation: How Consilio’s New Overtime Premium Rule Undermined Cohen’s Claims

## Why this matters

In an era where wage disputes routinely play out in federal court, *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* offers a front‑row seat to the friction that emerges when a company’s overtime policy shifts while an employee’s lawsuit is pending. In October 2025 the Eighth Circuit reviewed a case in which an employer, Consilio, a Minnesota‑based document‑review firm, had eliminated a “premium overtime” policy that had previously trimmed employees’ overtime earnings. The company settled with one defendant, paying alleged overtime wages and liquidated damages, yet the litigation’s demise hinged on whether it could still compel the employer to pay statutory penalties. The Court’s split decision—affirming the district court’s absence of penalties under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA); vacating the summary judgment on the *minimum wage* penalty provision of the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) on mootness grounds; and dismissing a Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) penalty claim because no statutory basis existed—has practical reverberations for employers, defendants in class actions, and employees seeking full redress.

## The overtime policy shift

Consilio’s July‑2019 email to its Minnesota‑based hourly licensed attorneys promised an “elimination” of its overtime premium—an apparently convoluted way of stating that all hours, including overtime, would be paid at the employee’s base rate. The company’s earlier practice likely had adhered to the federal overtime premium requirement of 1.5× the basic rate under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). The change struck a direct chord with workers who “worked over the threshold without the promised premium,” forming the kick‑off for Cohen’s complaint. When Cohen sued in August 2020, he alleged that the new policy had created a breach of the MPWA, the MFLSA, and the MWTA, seeking compensation, liquidated damages, and multiple tiers of statutory penalties.

The crux of the moment was the settlement that followed: Consilio paid a lump sum of $256,010.01—which included overtime and liquidated damages—and the parties stipulated that Cohen was fully satisfied with his wages and liquidated damages. Still, Cohen pressed that he was still entitled to penalties under the three statutes. When earmarked back to the court, the parties agreed that a summary judgment on penalties would likely swing the case toward a clean finish, thus averting further litigation.

## MPWA penalties – “Commissioner‑only” enforcement

Under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, a violator of the MPWA can be liable for “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” The statute, however, is explicit: only the Commissioner may *serve a demand*, *collect*, and *apportion* penalties. The language teaches that penalties are not an employee‑direct remedy.  In *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, the Minnesota Court of Appeals held that an unambiguous statutory phrase—such as the Commissioner’s exclusive power over penalties—cannot be overridden by legislative intent or policy.

Judge Kelly, writing for the 8th Circuit, accepted that the MPWA demands the Commissioner's enzymatic involvement and thus precluded Cohen’s direct claim for penalties. The Court affirmed the district court’s summary judgment, holding that the statue’s “penalties” are not employee‑accessible but must flow through the Commissioner’s administrative apparatus, and that the settlement had neutralized any unilateral wage dispute.

This outcome dovetails with the doctrine that Congress (here, the Minnesota legislature) can set up a “compliance‑based” penalty system that channelizes relief strictly to a state agency, thereby shielding the executive from direct punitive pursuit. The admission that the MPWA’s penalty mechanism is *not* available to a private party pre-emptively galvanized the settlement’s conclusion: once the employer paid the due wages, the MPWA penalty channel was effectively sealed.

## MFLSA penalty claim and the mootness conundrum

The MFLSA sets out clear statutory penalties for unpaid overtime, yet the employee claims could still haunt the employer if the alleged violation persists. The 8th Circuit recognized that the MFLSA does indeed authorise a penalty refund, but the grant of that remedy is contingent on a continuing injury—ergo, the claim’s standing and continued interest. This is a classic “standing‑then‑mootness” sequence: a plaintiff must first have a continuing harm; if the defendant stops the wrongful conduct, the claim may lapse. Referencing *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, the Court noted that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation … does not deprive a court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” but that “the presence or absence of a continuing injury” is still a fact‑checking hurdle.

While the settlement promised pay and liquidated damages, nothing in the parties’ stipulation said the new policy was irrevocably corrected or that the policy had been sufficiently amended to make the alleged violation impossible to recur. Under *Cohen*, the appellate court turned the stand‑the‑bats on this question, vacating the summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanding to decide *jurisdiction*—standing, specifically—once a concrete record has sorted whether the policy remains in effect. The practical upshot is that an employer may, by altering or tightening an overtime policy, effectively “muzzle” an employee’s penalty claim if the court is convinced that the unsafe conduct has ended; otherwise, the employee retains standing but the court must still decide the statute’s penalty provisions.

The MR. 8th Circuit’s treatment of the MFLSA claim underscores a subtle, but important nuance: *states can prevent federal statutory penalties by hastening policy changes that stop the violation.* This provides a legal lifeline for employers invited to remediate in the face of an alleged wage violation, but it also encourages injury recipients to review whether the policy at issue is still the same or whether new claims have arisen.

## MWTA penalties – the “no‑penalties” result

Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f) speaks to “mandatory and written notice of changes to overtime policy,” while § 181.032(d)(4) mandates that the employer should provide “reasonable notice” to employees. The MWTA does not create a penalty scheme like the MPWA does. The Eighth Circuit, having followed precedent and noting that no statutory penalty is attached to the MWTA, dismissed Cohen’s claim as lacking any “meaningful argument” to reverse the district judge’s ruling. This outcome has a clear doctrinal implication: employers may not rely on the MWTA to harbor penal provisions; instead, litigants must pool under the MPWA or another statutory vehicle. Courts will consider any MWTA claim that looks to rely on a penalty as a misreading absent any statutory provision.

## Settlement stipulations and class‑action viability

In corporate grievance litigation, the interplay between settlement stipulations and the viability of a class action is critical. Cohen’s alleged putative class comprised “similarly situated” employees—hour‑based licensed attorneys. The settlement’s words that “no claims will remain” if the court grants the summary judgment directly interfered with the very basis of class certification: a defined and common injury as nineteen hours per week. A settlement that declares “no claims remain” undermines that injury—if employees are paid, the common injury evaporates. The Eighth Circuit did not contemplate injunctive relief in the case; even commenting on the lack of injunctive relief in the brief, the court cited *InfoDeli* and *Fed. R. Civ. P. § 15(a)*, emphasizing that new claims must be introduced through amending complaints, not post‑summary‑judgment briefs.

In simple terms, once an employee’s wage deficit disappears, the commonality prerequisite for class certification is lost. Employers must, therefore, tread carefully: if a policy change clears a single employee’s wage deficiency, the whole class may lose standing, unless the policy change itself is a new, separate, and continued violation pertinent to the larger group.

## Doctrinal implications

The *Cohen* decision crystallises several broader legal doctrines:

1. **State‑level penalty architecture**—The MPWA shows that a statutory penalty can be constructed to flow solely through a state commission, preventing private suits from capturing the penalty.

2. **Mootness and continuing interest in wage claims**—The contrast between *Friends of the Earth* and the 8th Circuit’s vacated MFLSA claim illustrates that *a defendant’s cessation of unlawful conduct does not automatically kill a statutory claim,* but that continuing injury must be proved first.

3. **Severability of advice on policy enforcement versus class foundation**—A settlement eliminating “all claims” may sever the common injury that glassfully undergirds a class, exposing the employer to a potential class‑action lien if the settlement is challenged.

4. **Notice‑only statutes lacking penalty provisions**—The MWTA, like many administrative statutes, demands compliance but offers no residual penalty for violation; attempts to tack on a penalty at the private‑party level would be doomed.

These doctrines together reinforce a “policy‑change‑caution” for employers: submitting an early settlement or a sweeping policy amendment can squander the employee’s remaining right to recover statutory penalties; Conversely, failing to address potential penalty claims may expose the employer to an opaque, lingering liability.

## Practical guidance for employers

1. **Do not “unilaterally erase” penalties by shifting an overtime policy**. Under the MPWA, penalties are not recoverable by the employee, even if the employer later adjusts policy—unless the state Commissioner specifically charges them. Reason:** The employee’s claim can be regulated by the state, not by the employer’s action alone.

2. **If you settle for unpaid wages and liquidated damages, explicitly exclude penalty claims in the settlement unless you want the government to be involved**. A clean settlement that acknowledges all “wages” often precludes the employee from pursuing these penalties. A settlement that leaves penalties open may be used to preserve the employee’s right to claim them through the Commissioner.

3. **Keep a detailed audit trail of any policy modifications that could be construed as continuing or returning: pay stubs, internal memos, and training documents can be turned against you if the “wrongful conduct” is not truly eliminated**.

4. **Guard class‑action prospects**. If a policy change affects a potential class, be sure the change is truly *and* *permanently* correct. Otherwise, the class may lose its private cause of action, yet the collective may still pursue a claim under the class‑action rule (e.g., the unit thereof). Use litigation support to differentiate “one employee” from “the class.”

5. **Mind the MWTA’s notice provisions**. Though no criminal or civil penalty arises, it remains a statutory requirement to *give reasonable notice* whenever a policy changes. Failure to do so can give rise to breach claims from employees and can also add a layer of risk on top of the wage law bet.

6. **Analyze the “continuing interest”**. If each subsequent overtime policy change cuts a different slice of the employee’s wage, you may transform a good complaint into a mere procedural one. If the policy still flourishes after a settlement, you owe statutory penalties contingent on the MFLSA, after all.

**Bottom line:** A policy shift during litigation is a double‑edged sword: it can patch a breach and thereby avoid future penalties, yet it can also extinguish the common injury essential for class action viability. The *Cohen* decision calls for a careful, statutory‑compliant approach to wage policy management—avoid ambiguous gaps, document every shift, and align settlement terms with the statutory landscape, lest an otherwise satisfied employee still burns a case file to recover statutory penalties.
