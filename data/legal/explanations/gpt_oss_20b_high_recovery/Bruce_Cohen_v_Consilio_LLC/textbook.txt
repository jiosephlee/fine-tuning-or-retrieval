Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Ch. 1 Background, Parties, and Procedural History

## Identification of Plaintiff (Bruce Cohen) and Defendant (Consilio, LLC) and their respective roles in the legal document‑review industry  

Bruce Cohen is a licensed attorney who served as an hourly document reviewer for Consilio, LLC, a national law‑service company that specializes in contract review and due‑diligence support for corporate clients. Consilio’s business model relies on a large contingent workforce of attorneys who prepare legal drafts and analyze contracts for billing and audit purposes. Cohen’s employment began in 2018, during which he performed the same responsibilities as other Minnesota‑based reviewers.

## Chronology of the 2019 policy change: email notice, elimination of overtime premium, and subsequent request for base‑rate payment  

In August 2019, Consilio communicated a new overtime policy to its Minnesota document reviewers by email. The policy eliminated the previously offered premium premium‑pay schedule and clarified that all hours worked, regardless of number, would be compensated at the employee’s base hourly rate. The company’s message also announced that, effective August 2019, overtime hours would not be reimbursed at a rate above the base. This change suddenly removed an extra incentive that had previously been earned by reviewers who exceeded the standard workload.

## Filing of the 2020 lawsuit: alleged MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA violations and the sought remedies (overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, injunctive relief)  

On August 2020, Cohen filed a complaint on behalf of himself and a putative class of similarly situated employees. The complaint alleged that Consilio’s 2019 policy violated the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Cohen sought, as the primary remedy, owed overtime pay and liquidated damages under the MFLSA. He also requested statutory penalties under each implicated statute, amounting to $172,080 in MPWA penalties, $42,000 in MFLSA penalties, and $42,000 in MWTA penalties. In addition, Cohen petitioned for injunctive relief, specifically an order requiring Consilio to rescind the improper overtime policy and return to the prior premium scheme.

## Settlement and stipulations: $256,010.01 payment to Cohen and other reviewers, specific amounts received, and the parties’ agreement that all wages and liquidated damages had been paid  

During litigation, Consilio and Cohen negotiated a settlement that involved a contingent payment of $256,010.01 to Cohen and the other Minnesota licensed reviewers. As part of this payment, Cohen received $3,225.21 in overtime compensation and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties entered into a stipulation that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit” and “does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the MFLSA.” The parties could not agree on the statutory penalties, however. The settlement did not address these penalties, leaving Cohen’s claims for them unresolved.

Following the settlement, both parties agreed that the most expedient resolution would come from a summary‑judgment ruling on the disputed claims. They acknowledged that a full grant by the court would effectively end the case.

## The district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on all claims and the basis for that ruling (no genuine issues of material fact, entitlement as a matter of law)  

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims. The court concluded that there were no genuine issues of material fact and, as a matter of law, Consilio was entitled to judgment. The ruling asserted that the wage payments and liquidated damages settled the claims for pay, that the statutory penalties were not authorized under the relevant statutes, and that injunctive relief was not warranted because the defendants complied with the policy change. The court’s decision was rendered on the basis that the evidence presented—including the settlement and stipulations—demonstrated factual conclusiveness and legal entitlement to dismissal.

## The appellate court’s petition and the specific arguments Cohen seeks to revive on appeal  

On October 6, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit heard Cohen’s appeal. The appellate petition challenged the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on three fronts: (1) the MPWA claim, to argue that the statutory penalties were not limited exclusively to the Commissioner; (2) the MFLSA claim, to revive the question of whether the statutory penalties could be awarded to a plaintiff even if the employer’s unlawful conduct ceased; and (3) the MWTA claim, to seek a remedy for the alleged lack of required notice of the overtime policy change. 

In his brief, Cohen argued that the appellate court should reconsider whether statutory penalties should be accessible to employees under the MPWA and MFLSA, and whether the alleged policy change satisfied the procedural notice requirements of the MWTA. He also sought to invalidate the district court’s summary‑judgment findings that deferred to the settlement stipulations, contending that the evidence did not conclusively eliminate every material dispute.

The Eighth Circuit, after reviewing the record de novo, affirmed the district court’s judgment on the MPWA, vacated the MFLSA portion for remand due to a potential mootness and jurisdictional question, and permitted the dismissal of the MWTA claim. This decision reflected the appellate court’s evaluation of the statutory language, the parties’ agreements, and the procedural posture preceding the dismissal of the case.

# Ch. 2 Federal and State Penalty Theory under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act

## Statutory language of Minn. Stat. § 181.171 and § 181.101(a): the role of the Commissioner and the conditions for demanding and collecting penalties

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the appellate court examined the statutory framework that gives rise to the “average‑daily wage penalties” language litigated by Cohen. Section 181.171 authorizes a civil action for violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) and specifies that, “when an employer is found to have violated such section, it will be liable for compensatory damages and … other appropriate relief.”  Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Crucially, the statute does not by itself grant employees the right to procure penalties; that right is confined to the Commissioner.  Section 181.101(a) states that a penalty “may be charged and collected … if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand.”  In that same subsection the Commissioner’s authority to “serve a demand for payment of wages” and to “charge and collect” a penalty is explicitly outlined.  Consequently, plaintiffs must first obtain a writ or referral from the Commissioner before any penalty can be taken; the Commissioner is the exclusive agent that may inaugurate the penalty process, and any money recovered is held for the employee rather than the plaintiff.  

In *Cohen*, the court emphasized that the MPWA’s penal scheme is administrative in nature: the employee’s claim for penalties is not a direct contractual or statutory recovery from the employer; it is a recoverable interest conferred solely by the Commissioner.  Therefore, an employee who seeks such penalties must go through the Commissioner’s channels, not directly against the employer.  The statutory language—unambiguous in its allocation of enforcement power—serves as the foundation for the appellate court’s ultimate rejection of Cohen’s claim.

## Key case law shaping the Commissioner’s exclusive enforcement power: Allan v. R.D. Offutt, Burkstrand, Minn. Stat. § 181.13(a) and § 181.14

The court relied heavily on precedents that give weight to the plain meaning of the MPWA and to other Minnesota statutes that grant the Commissioner a comparable remedy.  In *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, the bench distilled the rule that “‘when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect” (Minn. 2015).  This rule, later echoed in *Burkstrand v. Burkstrand* (Min. 2001), informs the court’s reading of § 181.101(a): the Commissioner’s authority is clear and unambiguous.  

The court also turned to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) and § 181.14, statutes that prescribe a procedure for a discharged or voluntarily terminated employee to recover unpaid wages or commissions.  Those provisions grant employees the right to “charge and collect a penalty” after a Commissioner‑issued demand.  Though structured differently, the statutes show that the Legislature intended penalties to be imposed and collected by the Commissioner, not directly by the aggrieved employee.  By referencing these statutes, the appellate court confirmed that the MPWA follows a similar pattern, reinforcing the Commissioner’s exclusive enforcement tenure.

## Analysis of the plain‑meaning argument advanced by Cohen and the court’s rejection of that line of reasoning

Cohen argued that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning” of § 181.101 authorized employees to pursue average‑daily wage penalties directly.  His plain‑meaning claim rested on the literal reading of the phrase “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.”  The appellate court, however, carefully contrasted that argument with the statutory scheme’s explicit allocation of enforcement actions to the Commissioner.  The court observed that the statutory provision does not merely state that an employer is liable for “penalties” when the MPWA is violated; it further specifies that the penalty is bound to the Commissioner’s demand and collection process.  The ordinance forever limits the employee’s right to seek penalties through the Commissioner.  

Where Cohen’s argument converged on the statutory text, the court found that the text already contains a narrower, more precise regulation of penalty enforcement.  The plain‑meaning approach suggested by Cohen would have implied employees could bypass the Commissioner, but the statute’s own structure contradicts that.  The court emphasized that given the Legislature’s clear language in both the MPWA and related wage statutes (181.13(a) and 181.14), the intent was to centralize penalty enforcement within the Commissioner's purview.  Thus the plain‑meaning argument was rejected.

## Determination that only the Commissioner may charge and collect penalties, and any proceeds go to the employee concerned, not to the plaintiff directly

From the statutory and case‑law analysis, the appellate court concluded that (1) the Commissioner is the exclusive agent entitled to “charge and collect” the penalty and (2) the statute compels that any money collected by the Commissioner “must be paid to the employee concerned.”  Because the penalty requirement is restricted to the Commissioner’s mediation, Cohen’s return to a direct claim against Consilio was barred.  The court found that the Montana enforcement channels were not engaged in the complaint, and even though Cohen had received back wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA, those payments did not satiate the MPWA penalty provision that is earmarked to the employee via the Commissioner.  The proceedings, as configured, did not allow Cohen’s relief pursuit.

## Conclusion that the district court correctly denied summary judgment on the MPWA penalty claim

The district court had previously granted summary judgment to Consilio on all three claims as a result of a stipulation that Cohen had been paid the wages and liquidated damages it sought.  Because the statutory framework distinctly bars an employee from seeking average‑daily wage penalties outside the Commissioner’s process, there was no genuine issue of material fact regarding whether Consilio could have been held liable for such penalties.  The appellate court agreed with the district court’s conclusion that Cohen’s MPWA penalty claim should have been dismissed, affixing that the MPWA merely offers a penality that cannot be enforced by a private plaintiff without the Commissioner’s intervention.  The district court’s denial of Cohen’s penalty claim was therefore proper and affirmed.

# Ch. 3 MFLSA Penalties, Standing, and Mootness under Federal Law

## Overview of Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act statutory penalties and enforcement mechanics

The Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) is modeled on the federal Fair Labor Standards Act.  It provides that an employer who fails to pay overtime wages may be subject to civil penalties and statutory damages.  The Act sets out a two‑step enforcement process: first, a worker files a complaint, and the Department of Labor (or the Commissioner under state law) may investigate and, if negligence or willful misconduct is found, assess a penalty.  In Minnesota, the statutory language makes clear that any penalty imposed under the statute becomes the property of the Commissioner, not of the aggrieved employee.  This is not a peculiarity of the MFLSA; the Minnesota statute outlines that, “the Commissioner is the ultimate beneficiary of any penalty.”  Consequently, the employee’s remedy for unpaid overtime wages is the recovery of the wages themselves (plus liquidated damages) while the penalty is an end‑game payment to the state.

In Cohen’s suit Consilio paid him $256,010.01 in overtime wages and liquidated damages, and the parties agreed that any penalties, if awarded, would go to the Commissioner.  The Committee examined whether Cohen could, even knowing this fact, pursue a claim for statutory penalties under the MFLSA in a federal district court.

## Stipulated payment of overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA and its impact on the plaintiff’s claim for penalties

Because the parties stipulated that Cohen had received all wages and liquidated damages that the MFLSA entitiled him to, the district court had no factual dispute on the existence of a monetary award.  The only remaining question was whether Cohen was entitled to an additional statutory penalty.  The MFLSA text does not preclude a penalty, but it does not require that a worker seek them.  Rather, the statute allows the Commissioner to assess a penalty when the employer is found liable under the remedial portion of the Act.  The appellate court therefore treated the penalty claim as dependent on the district court’s determination that, even though Cohen was fully compensated, he could still seek a penalty to prevent future violations.

Because the penalty is paid to the Commissioner rather than to the employee, the court had to look at whether Cohen had the requisite personal stake to sue for the penalty.  The MFLSA does not expressly grant an employee a statutory right to recover a penalty, but it also does not forbid it.  The central question emerged: could a private plaintiff recover a civil penalty that, by its nature, is awarded to the state?

## Standing for federal civil penalties: requirement to demonstrate a personal injury linked to the enforcement action and the role of the Commissioner’s collection

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services* clarified the standing requirement for white‑collar, “citizen” plaintiffs who seek civil penalties under federal statutes.  The Court held that the plaintiff must have a “personal injury” arising out of the defendant’s ongoing unlawful conduct, and that the penalty must be tailored to protect that injury.  The ability to recover a penalty, even for a private party, depends on evidence that the injury will continue if the conduct persists.

In *Cohen*, only the MFLSA was relevant; thus the court applied the *Friends of the Earth* rationale as a framework for standing.  Cohen asserted that the alleged policy would continue to affect similar employees, thereby persisting a personal injury.  However, consilio had already compensated all overtime and liquidated damages owed.  The question became whether the alleged violation had been *sustained* or had been “voluntarily ceased” with the payment and lapse of the particular overtime policy.

Because the penalty was to go to the Commissioner, Cohen’s injury was indirect.  The Court recognized that the penalty’s purpose is to deter future violations, not to compensate the employee for a past injury.  Hence, standing needed to rest on an ongoing threat of injury, which is inextricably tied to the conduct itself.  If that threat has been eliminated, the plaintiff lacks a campaign for a penalty.

## Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw and the distinction between ongoing unlawful conduct and a “voluntary cessation” of the challenged practice

The court explained that *Friends of the Earth* distinguishes between (1) persistent unlawful conduct that, in a state of being under an ongoing scheme, creates a continuing wrong for non‑enforcement, and (2) a voluntary cessation that effectively eliminates the wrong and therefore removes the interest in preventing future harm.  The Court held that a defendant’s “voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality” of that practice.  Yet it also noted that the plaintiff’s claim for a penalty might become moot if the wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur.

In Cohen’s case, the district court had accepted that pay had been made, but the court could not definitively conclude whether Consilio remained in practice of the 2019 overtime policy.  If the policy were still operative, the employee could be subject to future violations and thus incur additional injuries.  Conversely, if the policy had ended and the employer had ceased the conduct, the alleged threat was gone.

The appellate court emphasized that, for the penalty claim to survive muster, the Mesa of *Friends of the Earth* required showing that the conduct remained in place and that serious injury to the plaintiff was probable.  The evidence in *Cohen* did not support conclusive proof of continued wrongful conduct or an ongoing risk of repeated injury.  Because indemnity for future injuries is the hallmark for granting a penalty, the court could not decide based on incomplete evidence.

## Mootness analysis: whether Consilio’s policy remained in force and if future violations could still be prevented by penalties

The district court’s summary‑judgment ruling relied on the parties’ stipulation and the absence of evidence that the policy remained.  Since the payment of wages and liquidated damages resolved the substantive question of who was owed money, the appellate court treated any remaining penalty claim as a withdrawal.  However, the Standing doctrine for civil penalties cast doubt on whether the trial court had authority to award such sanctions when the relevant law directs them *to* the Commissioner.

The court therefore turned to mootness.  The MFLSA authorizes statutory penalties that are meant to discourage ongoing violations.  If the alleged violation is no longer available to recur, the penalty becomes an abstract, non‑injurious remedy.  The court’s discussion of *Friends of the Earth* conveyed that a claim for penalties may be moot if the underlying conduct is truly no longer a threat.  Conversely, if the policy could still be in effect, the penalty would still be relevant.  Because the parties’ stipulation did not rule out that the policy might continue, the appellate court could not resolve the mootness question and noted that it would be a jurisdictional issue to decide on the remand.

In short, the appellate court recognized that the district court had not conclusively shown that the policy had been permanently abolished or that the risk of future violations was null.  Without such a finding, the plaintiff’s claim for a penalty could be moot, and the court lacked jurisdiction to decide it.  The remand therefore allowed further inquiry into the policy’s status, any continuing hazard, and the mistaken assumption that wages and liquidated damages foreclosed the possibility of penalties.

## Result: the appellate court vacated the summary judgment for the MFLSA penalty claim and remanded to resolve jurisdictional and mootness questions

After reviewing the elements above, the appellate court concluded that the district court had erred in granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim.  The court held that the claim was likely moot if Consilio’s overtime policy had ceased, or that the district court lacked jurisdiction to award penalties that are directed to the Commissioner rather than the employee.  As a remedy, the court vacated the summary judgment and remanded the case for the district court to:

1. Determine whether Consilio’s 2019 overtime policy remains in force or whether it has been definitively abolished at the time the payment was made, and
2. Decide whether, pending the status of the policy, Cohen has a continuing personal injury that could support a claim for statutory penalties under the MFLSA.

In reframing the issue as a jurisdictional, mootness question, the appellate court reinforced the procedural boundary: only when a plaintiff can demonstrate a continuing injury and purposefully targeted penalties to that injury can a federal court entertain a claim for civil penalties when those penalties are earmarked for the Commissioner.  The remand therefore preserved the litigants’ opportunity to resolve these issues, but it also illustrated the narrow window in which claims for statutory penalties can survive and ultimately be deemed honest requests for enforcement rather than mere claims for additional monetary award.

# Ch. 4 MWTA and Injunctive Relief: Final Holding and Significance  

## Scope of Minnesota Wage Theft Act § 181.032(f) and § 181.032(d)(4): notification requirement and absence of penalty authority  

Section 181.032(f) of the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) and the related subsection (d)(4) impose a notification duty on employers: if a policy affecting overtime wages is altered, the employer must advance written notice to all affected employees. The statutory language under those subsections focuses expressly on the obligation to inform, not on any civil penalties for non‑compliance. In the added factual context, Consilio’s 2019 email notified employees that overtime premiums would be eliminated, thereby satisfying the MWTA’s notice obligation. Because the text of § 181.032(f) and (d)(4) contains no words permitting the imposition of penalties, the MWTA offers no statutory footing for a claim in punitive damages against an employer that merely fails to issue notice. This statutory conclusion forms the basis of the appellate court’s statement that the MWTA “does not provide a mechanism for civil penalties” and that an employee cannot invoke such penalties under the Act.  

## The appellate court’s decision not to revivify the MWTA claim due to lack of substantive argument and sign of waiver  

The appellate court noted in paragraph C that Cohen’s brief contained “little to no discussion” of the MWTA claim and that the only argument presented was that Consilio’s delay in notifying employees justified a penalty. Because the MWTA itself does not authorize penalties, the brief failed to supply a substantive legal basis for reversal. In the absence of an argument that the district court erred on the merits, the higher court declined to reopen the case. The court applied a standard that a claim must survive a “waiver” of motion to dismiss only if the litigant cited at least a legal authority supporting their position; here, the claim was effectively waived when the court dismissed it. As a result, the appellate court affirmed the district court’s dismissal of the MWTA claim.  

## Treatment of Cohen’s evolving injunctive relief requests (from rescind policy to withdraw 2019 policy) and the requirement to amend a complaint, not to address in the brief  

In the controversy over injunctive relief, Cohen initially demanded an “affirmative injunction” to rescind Consilio’s alleged illegal overtime policy. When the case proceeded, he altered the request, dropping the rescind demand and instead pressing for withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The appellate court, citing its procedural rules and the principle enshrined in *InfoDeli, LLC* and *Fed. R. Civ. P.* 15(a), observed that “new claims are properly raised through an amended complaint, not an opposition brief.” Because Cohen’s evolving injunctive request was presented only in the brief, the court had no jurisdiction to evaluate it, and it consequently left the issue unresolved. The court effectively required Cohen to amend his pleading to assert the injunctive relief.  

## Summary of the final holding: affirmed summary judgment for MPWA and MWTA claims, vacated MFLSA penalty claim, and remanded for jurisdictional determination  

The appellate court’s final order reflected a bifurcated outcome. It affirmed the district court’s summary judgment on the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) claim, concluding that only the Commissioner may pursue daily‑average‑wage penalties under § 181.101(a). It affirmed the dismissal of the MWTA claim because the Act offered no penalty authority and the brief failed to provide contrary argument. Conversely, the court vacated the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim for statutory penalties, citing a mootness concern related to the continued existence of Consilio’s overtime policy and the necessity of a jurisdictional determination. The case was remanded to the district court for that purpose, with the instruction that any further proceedings must first address whether the MFLSA penalty claim remains viable.  

## Implications: reinforcement of the Commissioner’s exclusive role, clarification of standing for statutory penalties, and guidance for plaintiffs when policy changes have already remedied wage claims  

This decision signals a stringent reaffirmation of the Commissioner’s sole authority to collect civil penalties under wage‑payment statutes. Plaintiffs seeking punitive damages must therefore prove that the statute itself authorizes them, as is the case with the MPWA’s § 181.101(a) but not with MWTA’s notification provisions. The court’s emphasis on standing—particularly in *Friends of the Earth* and *Town of Chester*-type considerations—demonstrates that a plaintiff must sustain a continuing interest in the alleged unlawful conduct to sue for penalties. The ruling further teaches litigants that once an employer rectifies a wage‑payment issue, claims premised on evidence of past malfeasance can become moot, potentially eliminating the right to penalty damages. Finally, by underscoring the procedural requirement to amend a complaint for new claims, the court provides a procedural roadmap for plaintiffs who wish to adjust their relief demands in the wake of evolving policy changes.
