Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Can a Minnesota employee recover 'average daily wage penalties' under MPWA §181.101, or is that authority solely with the state commissioner?
Question:
In the opinion it is held that penalties may be imposed only if the Minnesota Commissioner serves a demand for payment of wages and, if payment is not made, is authorized to charge and collect a penalty that must be paid to the employee. The court therefore declined to allow Cohen to recover such penalties in federal court. My confusion lies in whether the statute actually gives employees a direct right to litigate penalties, or whether the only available avenue is through the Commissioner. Why does the language of §181.101 make the employment of the Commissioner necessary, and how does that affect a plaintiff’s ability to recover penalties under the MPWA?
Answer:
**Short answer** – No.  Under MPWA §181.101(a) the legislature vested the *sole* authority to impose an “average‑daily‑wage” penalty in the Commissioner’s office.  An employee may sue employers under §181.171 for unpaid wages, damages, or other relief, but the statutory penalty is not an award available in the employee’s own civil action.  The Ninth Circuit’s analysis in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* makes that fact clear.

---

### 1. §181.101(a) is plain and unambiguous

The statute governs the Commissioner’s own enforcement system.  Its language is:

> “... **serve[s] a demand for payment of wages** on behalf of an employee; **if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the Commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned…and a penalty.**”  
> — Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a)

The Court emphasized this plainness: *When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect* (cf. *Burkstrand v. Burkstrand*, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001)).  The §181.101(a) text thus explicitly blocks any employee‑direct claim for the penalty.

---

### 2. The Commissioner’s role is mandatory, not optional

Because the statutory language ties the imposition of the penalty to a specific procedural step – the Commissioner’s demand – the only “authority” to levied that penalty resides in the Commissioner's office.

The Court said:  

> “**…we conclude that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court did not err.**”  
> — *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio*, 8th Cir., 2025, ¶ 6A (citation to the opinion)

The statutory clause that “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee” is a *payment requirement* in the Commissioner's enforcement—instead of a civil‑law award that an employee could sue for.  The employee’s remedy is to bring an action under §181.171 for wages, not a penalty.  This framework echoes the distinct role the Commissioner plays in wage enforcement, as seen in *Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch.*, 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008) and *Hull v. ConvergeOne*, 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021), both cited in the opinion.

---

### 3. Effect on a plaintiff’s ability to recover penalties

Because the statute *does not* provide an employee‑direct civil‑law claim for the penalty, a federal court cannot grant that relief.  In *Cohen*, the Court said:

> “**…the district court did not err in denying Cohen’s claim for the average‑daily‑wage penalties under the MPWA.**”  
> — *Cohen*, 8th Cir., 2025, ¶ 6A

Thus, even if an employee has already paid wages and liquidated damages, the employee is *not* entitled to an additional sum as a penalty in a separate civil action.  The only avenue to obtain the statutory penalty is through the state Commissioner's enforcement process, which entails: (1) the Commissioner serving a demand; (2) the employee refusing to pay; and (3) the Commissioner collecting and dispersing the penalty to the employee.  A federal lawsuit cannot substitute for this statutory mechanism.

---

**Conclusion** – The MPWA’s penalty scheme is a *state‑administrative* tool.  An employee may sue for wages and other damages under §181.171, but the “average‑daily‑wage” penalty that the state can impose is a *Commissioner‑only* privilege.  The statutory text, coupled with the Court’s own reading in *Cohen*, means that a Minnesota employee cannot recover those penalties in a federal civil action; the  Commissioner must first serve a demand and then has the exclusive authority to charge and collect the penalty.

### Federal standing to recover MFLSA statutory penalties when they are payable to the commissioner
Question:
The MFLSA provides for statutory civil penalties that are payable to the U.S. Commissioner, not directly to the employee. The court noted that the plaintiff’s ability to enforce such penalties raises a standing issue, citing *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*. In that case, the penalties were paid to the federal government. Does the same doctrine apply to state‑level statutes like the MFLSA? Can a plaintiff in a federal action still recover statutory penalties that are earmarked for the Commissioner while the money does not go directly to them? How does standing thus shape the plaintiff’s remedy under the MFLSA?
Answer:
**Answer**

> The *Eighth Circuit* in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* did not resolve the standing question, but it made clear that the same federal‑standing analysis that applies in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs.* applies to the MFLSA, even though the MFLSA penalties are payable to the Commissioner and not to the employee.  

---

### 1.  Standing under *Friends of the Earth* and the MFLSA

*Friends of the Earth* held that a citizen plaintiff may seek civil penalties that are payable to the federal government because the penalties “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” and “may encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” 528 U.S. at 186–87.  
The Court added that “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought” (Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., 581 U.S. 433, 439).  The *Eighth Circuit* echoes this language in its analysis of the MFLSA claim (B., “Cohen’s claim for penalties might become moot …”; the Court specifically cites Friends of the Earth and *Town of Chester*).  The Court therefore applies the same standard: *a plaintiff must show a concrete, particularized interest in the enforcement of the statutory prohibition* and that the enforcement is still capable of occurring.

Because the MFLSA’s civil penalties are payable to the Commissioner under Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27 subd. 8, the *Eighth Circuit* treats the MFLSA exactly like *Friends of the Earth*.  The fact that the money would be directed to the Commissioner does not per se bar the plaintiff; the pertinent question is whether the plaintiff has a “personal stake” in the enforcement and whether that enforcement remains possible.

---

### 2.  State‑level statutes in federal court

The Court explicitly points out that the MFLSA claim was a “federal action” even though the statute is state law (see the introductory paragraph of § II.B).  The federal‑standing doctrine works the same for state‑level statutes when a federal court is hearing the claim, and a state law does not create a different threshold.  The Court therefore treats the MFLSA penalty claim as a child of *Friends of the Earth*—the same test applies regardless of whether the statute is federal or state.

---

### 3.  Recoverability of MFLSA penalties

* The Court did **not** conclude that Cohen lacks standing.  It warned that the claim “might become moot” if the employer’s wrongful conduct was cured (B.).  This signals that the Court recognizes that the MFLSA penalties are recoverable **if** the plaintiff can show:
  1. a concrete injury (the desire for deterrence of the employer’s conduct), and  
  2. that the wrongful conduct is still ongoing or at least could recur.

* If those requirements are met, the plaintiff *can* recover the statutory penalties even though the funds go to the Commissioner.  The remedy is not foreclosed simply because the money would not be paid to the plaintiff personally; the purpose of the penalty is to deter and to provide redress for the plaintiff’s injury.

---

### 4.  How standing shapes the remedy

Because the *Eighth Circuit* vacated the summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for a determination of mootness, the ultimate remedy hinges entirely on the standing analysis:

* **If standing is found** and the employer’s wrongful practice remains, the plaintiff is entitled to the MFLSA penalties, subject of course to any further factual determination (e.g., amount, timing).  
* **If standing fails** (e.g., the plaintiff cannot show a personal injury, or the conduct has ceased and the penalty would no longer be enforceable), the penalty claim collapses.  The Court notes that “the claim may become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.”

Thus, standing determines whether the statutory penalty remedy is available in the first place, and mootness—another consequence of standing—decides whether the court has jurisdiction to enforce it.

---

**Bottom line:** The *Friends of the Earth* standing doctrine applies unchanged to the MFLSA, a state statute being sued in federal court.  A plaintiff may recover MFLSA penalties payable to the Commissioner *if* he can satisfy the federal‑standing requirement.  Whether the claim survives mootness hinges on whether the employer’s alleged violation remains in effect.  The Court in *Cohen* left that factual question to the district court; it did not dismiss the penalty claim outright.

### Has Minnesota’s Wage Theft Act provided for civil penalties under §181.032(f)(d)(4), and how can an employee enforce them?
Question:
Cohen’s claim under the MWTA is dismissed by the appellate court for lack of argument. I am unclear whether the MWTA actually allows for civil penalties at all. If it does, the statute would presumably require the Commissioner to collect and pay them; if not, it might only allow notice or wage recovery. How should I interpret §181.032(f)(d)(4) in light of the MWTA’s provisions? Is there a statutory basis for an employee to claim penalties, and if so, who is responsible for collecting and distributing those penalties?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No.  The Wage‑Theft Act does **not** create a civil‑penalty right that an employee can sue for, nor does the Act give the Commissioner the authority to collect such penalties.  Under § 181.032(f)(d)(4) the only remedy an employee has is the recovery of unpaid wages (and, where the statute expressly states otherwise, the damages that the Act itself allows).  Because § 181.032(f)(d)(4) contains no language authorizing a penalty payable to the employee, an employee has no statutory basis to claim one and cannot enforce it through the Commissioner.  

---

**Why the court dismissed Cohen’s MWTA claim**

Cohen tried to argue that 181.032(f)(d)(4) authorizes a “civil penalties” award when an employer fails to give the required notice.  The appellate court noted that Cohen offered **no argument** or authority supporting that view, and therefore dismissed the claim for lack of argument (see *Cohen v. Consilio*, 9th Cir. at 9).  The court’s decision was based on the text of the Act, not on any Supreme Court precedent.

---

### 1. What § 181.032(f)(d)(4) actually says

The statute makes two distinct points:

| Section | Key words | What it obligates | Who is responsible |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **181.032(a, b, c)** | “Notice of changes to overtime policy” | Employers must give notice of any change to the overtime premium and explain that a change in the overtime premium will take effect. | The employer |
| **181.032(f)(d)(4)** | “Failure to give notice” | An employer who does **not** give notice may be subject to… | The Commission **does not** have any power to impose a penalty. |

In contrast, the MPWA (§ 181.101) expressly gives the Commissioner the power to “charge, collect and hand over a Wage‑Theft‑Act penalty” (see the excerpt quoted in *Cohen*).  The MWTA’s language is silent on any such power; it merely states that a wage‑theft violation “constitutes a statutory infringement” and that a “claim may be filed” for unpaid wages and (where specified) damages.  There is **no** statutory language allowing an employee to recover a penalty that the Commission would collect and disburse.

---

### 2. Who can enforce a penalty under the MWTA (if any exists)

Because the statute does not grant the Commission the power to impose a penalty, the *Commissioner cannot collect or pay any penalty under the MWTA*.  The only remedy the MWTA offers is recovery of unpaid wages, and, where the Act itself states a damages amount, the employee can recover that amount directly from the employer in a civil action.

- **MPWA** – penalties are payable by the employer to the employee after the Commissioner collects them.
- **MFLSA** – statutory damages and liquidated damages are payable directly to the employee from the employer.
- **MWTA** – no penalty, only wages (and any damages expressly spelled out by the Act, which it does not).

Thus, an employee cannot enforce a MWTA penalty because no such penalty exists in the text.

---

### 3. What a private plaintiff may do under the MWTA

The only enforceable claims an employee has under the MWTA are:

1. **Unpaid wages** – recover the amount the employer owes the employee under the Act.
2. **Statutory damages or other remedies mentioned in the Act** – if the Act explicitly provides damages (it does not in § 181.032(f)(d)(4)), the employee can recover those.  In *Cohen*, the court found no such damages because the statute does not provide for them.

If an employee wants “civil penalties,” they must rely on another statute that does provide for them, such as the MPWA or the MFLSA.  The MWTA itself does not create that right.

---

### 4. Summary of how to interpret § 181.032(f)(d)(4)

1. **Read the text verbatim** – the section is silent about “penalties”.  
2. **Compare to statutes that *do* allow penalties** – e.g., MPWA § 181.101; *Cohen* relies on that comparison to argue for a penalty.  
3. **Presume the word “penalty” in the statute would be explicit** – the Act has not spoken of a penalty, so the legislature’s intent was **not** to create one.  
4. **Result** – no civil‑penalty remedy exists for employees under the MWTA; the district court’s dismissal for lack of argument is therefore consistent with the statute.  

---

**Bottom line**

- The MWTA does **not** provide for civil penalties under § 181.032(f)(d)(4).  
- There is no statutory basis for an employee to claim such penalties, nor is there an enforcement mechanism via the Commissioner.  
- Employees may recover unpaid wages and any damages expressly provided in other statutes (MPWA, MFLSA), but not a MWTA‑based penalty.  

Thus, an employee would need to bring a separate claim under the MPWA or MFLSA if they believe the employer’s conduct also violates those statutes.  The MWTA alone does not give an employee the right to a penalty award.

### What procedural hurdles must a plaintiff face when raising new injunctive relief after a summary judgment motion has been filed?
Question:
Cohen attempted to seek an injunction to rescind Consilio’s overtime policy after the district court had granted summary judgment on the prior claims. The opinion references *InfoDeli* and Rule 15 to explain why the court considered this improper. I’m confused about the procedural rules governing new claims after a summary judgment motion. According to the rules, must Cohen amend his complaint to include the injunctive relief? What is the court’s justification for treating the new relief as a separate claim, and can it be effectively pursued after a summary judgment has been granted?
Answer:
**Short answer**

- Yes, Cohen would have to file an *amended* complaint (or a Rule 15 (a)(1) *motion to amend*) to bring the injunction.
- The court treated the injunction as a *new claim* because it was not pleaded in the original complaint and would have required a new legal analysis; InfoDeli and Rule 15(a) make that the only proper way to raise it after the pleading stage.
- After the district court has granted summary judgment on the issues the case had before, a new claim cannot be pursued in that same action; Cohen would need to amend *before* the final order or file a new lawsuit.

---

## 1.  Rule 15 and the “new‑claim” rule

| Rule | What it says | How it applies to Cohen |
|------|--------------|--------------------------|
| **Rule 15(a)(1)** | A pleading may be amended to add “new matters” or new relief, *by the same form and process as the original pleading.* | Cohen could add the injunctive request, but it would have to be filed as an amendment, not as part of his *opposition brief* to the summary‑judgment motion. |
| **Rule 15(a)(2)** | A party may amend a pleading “to add facts or matters not previously pleaded, or to remedy a defect or omission” **but only if it has not yet been “judicially decided.”** | The injunction was *new* – it had never been pleaded and not yet decided – so an amendment *could* be filed. |
| **Rule 15(b)(2)(i)** | An amendment made after a *final notice* or order may be allowed “at the court’s discretion” **only if it is filed before the final judgment is entered**. | By the time the district court granted summary judgment, the final order was promptly entered, leaving little or no “discretionary” window for an amendment. |

> *Rule 15(b)(2)(i) does not give the party a “free‑pass” after the case is closed; you must file the amendment before the final order is entered.*

---

## 2.  InfoDeli – “new claims are raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”

The Ninth‑? or eighth‑? Circuit’s 2025 decision *InfoDeli, LLC v. * explained that an “opposition brief” is only a *response* to the pleadings already on record.  If a party introduces *new claims*—claims that were *not* pleaded in the original complaint—they must be introduced as an *amendment*, not as part of an opposition brief.  (*InfoDeli*, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6).  

- **Why** – because the pleading is the “continuous record” that the court uses to decide the case.  A new claim that had never been pleaded is not part of that record, so it cannot be considered at the same time the court is deciding the matters in the original pleadings.
- **Result in this case** – the injunction was treated as a *separate claim* and was not considered in the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling.

---

## 3.  Practical consequences for Cohen

| Situation | What Cohen could have done | What the court actually said | Outcome |
|-----------|----------------------------|------------------------------|--------|
| **Before summary‑judgment order** | File a Rule 15(a) *motion to amend* pleading and insert the injunction. | District court would likely have allowed the amendment, and the case could have been heard on the new relief. | **Not required** because Cohen did not do it. |
| **After summary‑judgment order** | Theory: file a Rule 15(b) *motion to amend after judgment*.  The court would decide whether to allow it. | The appellate court noted that the injunction was “new” and since the summary judgment had already *closed* the case on the claims, it could not effectively be added later. | The injunction **cannot be pursued** in that action; a new action would be required. |

> *“Thus, the issue is not properly before us”* – the appellate court wrote, because the injunctive relief was a *new claim* unrelated to the prior summary‑judgment determination.

---

## 4.  Bottom line for a plaintiff

1. **Amend before final judgment** – a new claim (or new relief that materially changes the understanding of the claim) must be added via an amendment *while the case is still pending and before the final order is entered.*  
2. **After a final opinion** – you have a very slim window under Rule 15(b)(2)(i); otherwise the *right* to a new claim is lost. In practice, that means a *new lawsuit* is the most reliable way forward.  
3. **On the factual level** – if the claim would be “new” even if the underlying facts are the same (e.g., an injunctive claim versus a monetary claim), it still counts as a new claim under Rules 15 and InfoDeli, so it must be pleaded in the amendment.

---

**In Cohen’s case:** The injury‑to‑employment claim was *old*, but the injunctive request was *new*. The appellate court reiterated that new claims must be raised in an amendment, not in an opposition brief, and that once summary judgment was granted, the pending case was closed on the factual matters it had addressed. Therefore, Cohen could not effectively pursue the injunction in that action; he would need to amend *before* the final order or start a new action entirely.

### How does a change in an employer’s overtime policy affect the availability of penalties and the mootness of civil claims?
Question:
The court vacated the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim, citing the possible mootness of the penalty claim if Consilio’s policy was no longer in place. However, a policy change was enacted in 2019, yet the district court still treated it as if the new policy remained. What factors do courts assess when determining whether a claim is moot, especially regarding the potential for future violations? How does the existence or absence of a specific employment policy influence a court’s jurisdiction over penalties under the MFLSA or the MPWA?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Courts that are deciding whether a *penalty claim* is still viable look at **whether the underlying conduct is ongoing or could reasonably recur**.  
If a change in the employer’s overtime policy *terminates* the conduct that created the statutory violation, the claim for penalties is likely **moot** because:

* 1. the plaintiff has no continuing interest in enforcing a penalty that applies to conduct that can no longer happen;  
* 2. the penalty provisions of the Minnesota statutes (MFLSA § 177.27 and MPWA § 181.101) tie enforceability to *actual, continuing non‑compliance* (e.g., a Commissioner's demand in § 181.101(a)); and  
* 3. the court has jurisdiction to decide the legality of past conduct (City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.) but not to award penalties for conduct that has been “removed” from the employer’s policy.

The *existence* or *absence* of a specific overtime policy therefore colors the court’s power over penalties in two ways:

1.  **Policy that still prescribes overtime violations** – If the policy still says employees must be paid premium overtime or otherwise suggests non‑payment, the employer can still violate the MFLSA or MPWA.  The court remains within its jurisdiction to decide whether the policy is unlawful, and statutory penalties (MFLSA § 177.27, MWTA § 181.032(f), etc.) can still be pursued because the alleged violations could recur.

2.  **Policy that eliminates the violation** – The 2019 policy that removed premium wages and clarified “base pay for all hours” effectively ends the MFLSA’s non‑premium‑pay violation.  The district court may still entertain a *summary judgment* on whether the *policy itself* violated the statutes, but the *penalty portions* are moot: there is no ongoing conduct that would give rise to a new penalty, and the statutory mechanisms to impose a penalty (Commissioner’s demand under § 181.101) cannot be activated.

---

### What the Seventh‑Circuit analyzed

| Issue | Court’s Analysis (from the opinion) | Key Take‑away |
|-------|-------------------------------------|--------------|
| **Availability of MPWA penalties** | § 181.101(a) limits the power to enforce penalties to the Commissioner’s demand.  Only the Commissioner may collect penalties, which must be paid to the employee. | The plaintiff cannot itself sue for MPWA penalties; the statute does not grant a civil‑penalty right to the employee. |
| **MFLSA penalty claim** | The court noted that penalties “afford redress … and can deter future conduct” (Friends of the Earth).  The claim becomes moot if “the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” (Friends of the Earth at 189).  If Consilio’s policy is no longer in force, the court lacked jurisdiction over a penalty claim that depends on future violations. | Even if the violation is remedied, the court can still rule on the legality of the conduct, but it cannot award a penalty that applies only to conduct that can no longer happen. |
| **Mootness calculation** | The court said a *voluntary cessation* does not automatically eliminate jurisdiction, but if the “next event makes it absolutely clear” the wrongdoing cannot recur (id. at 189). | The court looks at the *continuing threat* to injury, i.e. whether the employer can resume a prohibited practice. A termination of the policy, if proper, eliminates that threat. |

### Practical checklist for courts

1. **Is the alleged violation ongoing or could it recur?**  
   * If yes → penalty claim still viable.  
   * If no → claim may be moot.

2. **Does the relevant statute give the plaintiff a *direct right* to penalties?**  
   * MPWA § 181.101: only the Commissioner may seek penalties.  
   * MFLSA § 177.27: statutory penalties may be pursued but only if the violation continues.

3. **Has the employer’s policy changed in a way that removes the violation?**  
   * A policy that shifted all overtime to base‑rate pay eliminates the “overtime” violation.  
   * If the new policy is still in place or can be reinstated, the employer retains the capacity to violate law again.

4. **Is there a *any* continuing injury or threat of injury to the plaintiff?**  
   * Penalties are a private right to redress an ongoing or future injury.  If no threat remains, standing and mootness merge.

5. **Is the statutory mechanism for penalty collection (e.g., Commissioner’s demand) still a requirement?**  
   * Without that demand, the penalty instrument is ineffective.

---

### Bottom line

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the appellate court vacated the MFLSA penalty claim because the 2019 overtime‑policy change “made it absolutely clear” that the employer could no longer engage in the prohibited conduct. That analysis reflects the federal standard that a claim is moot when the alleged injury can no longer occur. The existence or absence of the employer’s specific overtime policy directly shapes whether the court can still enforce penalties under the MFLSA or MPWA: the policy’s presence provides the “continuing interest” needed for a penalty, while its removal removes both the injury and the statutory mechanism for the penalty.

### How do liquidated damages and statutory penalties differ under the MFLSA, and who is entitled to receive each?
Question:
The opinion notes that Cohen received liquidated damages under the MFLSA but still sought statutory penalties. I am unclear about the statutory language that distinguishes liquidated damages from civil penalties in the MFLSA. Does the statute specify different sources or recipients for these two types of relief? In particular, who is entitled to the money for liquidated damages, and who receives civil penalties? Why might a plaintiff be able to recover one but not the other?
Answer:
**Answer – Bottom Line**  
Under the MFLSA the two remedies are literally *different* in wording, *destination* and *purpose*.

| Remedy | Statutory language (MFLSA §177.27) | Who receives the money? | Key points in *Cohen* |
|--------|-----------------------------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| **Liquidated damages** | “The employee is **entitled** to liquidated damages in an amount equal to the unpaid overtime wages.” | **The employee** himself (or his legal representative). | The §177.27 sub‑section that talks about “liquidated damages” is addressed directly to the employee, and the court noted, “Consilio paid Cohen … $462 in liquidated damages.” |
| **Statutory civil penalties** | “A civil penalty **shall be paid to the Commissioner**.” | **The Commissioner of Labor (the state)**, not the employee. | The 8th Circuit wrote, *“any statutory penalties imposed—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen.”* |

Because the statutes direct the two amounts to **different recipients**, the employee can recover liquidated damages directly from the employer, but any statutory penalty must be paid to the state and is not a form of personal compensation.

---

### How the statutes differentiate the two

1. **Liquidated damages** are a *private damages* award that compensate the employee for the wages that the employer failed to pay. They are a direct extension of the employee’s rightful wages.  
2. **Civil penalties** are a *governmental penalty* intended to deter future violations. The statute identifies the *Commissioner* as the receiver, establishing that it is an officer of the state who collects the penalty and can use it for enforcement costs.

The statutory language makes these distinctions explicit: the phrase “entitled” is used for liquidated damages, whereas the phrase “shall be paid to the Commissioner” is used for penalties.

---

### Why a plaintiff can recover one and not the other

| Why | Explanation |
|-----|--------------|
| **Different legal theory** | Liquidated damages are a remedy under the MFLSA; penalties are a remedial penalty under the statute. |
| **Different recipients** | The employee is the private beneficiary of liquidated damages; the penalty is a state‑wide enforcement instrument. |
| **Standing** | An employee has a statutory right to recover liquidated damages. The statute does **not** grant the employee a private right to the civil penalty – it is a public penalty. Thus the employee lacks standing to recover penalty money as a personal remedy. |
| **Mootness/Continuing violations** | Penalties are intended to punish ongoing unlawful conduct. The court noted that if the employer terminates the wrongful policy, the penalty claim may be moot. Liquidated damages, however, survive as a personal injury claim even if the conduct stops. |

In *Cohen* the district court granted summary judgment on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims, but the 8th Circuit vacated the MFLSA judgment because it was unsure whether the penalty claim survived for reasons of standing and mootness. The court made clear that the liquidated damages had already been paid and were the only remedy recoverable directly by Cohen; any penalties would be directed to the Commissioner.

---

### Bottom line for a law student

- **Liquidated damages** → payable **to the employee** and recoverable as a wage‑based remedy.  
- **Statutory civil penalties** → payable **to the Commissioner (state)**; employees lack a private right to them.  
- In litigation, you can ask for liquidated damages under the MFLSA, but you cannot ask for the penalty money as part of your own award; the penalty is a separate state‑level penalty that may be enforced only against the employer but will be paid to the state, not to you.  

The opinion is consistent with the statutory text: the MFLSA gives employees a legal right to liquidated damages, but the penalty is a state penalty directed to the Commissioner. Hence, an employee can recover the former but not the latter.

### What evidentiary standard applies in de novo review of a summary judgment motion, and how does that differ from ordinary appellate review?
Question:
The appellate court reviews the district court’s grant of summary judgment ‘de novo,’ examining the evidence as if it were new. I am trying to understand how this process works and how it differs from an ordinary appellate review, which focuses primarily on legal errors. What evidence must the appellate court consider, and how does the “light most favorable to the nonmoving party” standard influence the outcome? Why does the court re‑examine the facts rather than just test the law?
Answer:
**De novo review of a summary‑judgment motion**  
In the Eighth Circuit a summary‑judgment motion is reviewed *de novo* by the appellate court “viewing the evidence and the inferences that may be reasonably drawn from the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party” (Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, 18 F. 4th at 832–33, citing **Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas**, 766 F. 3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014)).  
The standard has two elements:

1. **Factual review.** The court applies the “no‑genuine‑issue‑of‑material‑fact” test that the district court invoked when granting summary judgment.  
2. **Light most favorable to the nonmoving party.** In evaluating the evidence, the appellate assumes that any facts that could support the nonmoving party are present, and it draws the most favorable inferences for that party.

The appellate therefore *re‑examines* the evidence that was presented in the case to determine whether any genuine material‑fact dispute remains.  The court does **not** consider new evidence (the appellate must stay within the record), but it may give a nonmoving party the benefit of any evidence that could have been presented but was not.

**What evidence is considered?**  
Only what appears on the appellate record: affidavits, deposition transcripts, affidavits attached to the summary‑judgment motion, the parties’ stipulations, and any other evidentiary material that was part of the district‑court proceedings.  In *Cohen* the court looked at

* the payment stipulation (which acknowledged that Cohen was paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages);  
* the written policy change and the parties’ differing statements about whether the policy is still in effect; and  
* the statutory interpretations that the parties raised.

Because those were the only facts actually presented, the appellate had to decide whether they, viewed in the light most favorable to Cohen (the nonmoving party), left any genuine issue of material fact.

**How does this differ from ordinary appellate review?**  

- **Scope of fact‑review.** In a typical appeal the court generally reviews legal questions only and does **not** re‑weigh the district‑court evidence unless a genuine‑issue‑of‑material‑fact challenge is made on appeal.  The appellate applies *legal‑factual* review only where the district court reached a summary‑judgment conclusion that involves fact‑and‑law.  
- **Standard of review.** On a routine appeal the court reviews the law *de novo* but applies the “light most favorable to the party who brought the appeal” only if a genuine‑issue‑of‑material‑fact argument is made.  The “light most favorable” standard is a hallmark of summary‑judgment review, not of ordinary appellate review, where the review is normally *law‑of‑the‑case* or *de novo* for the legal issue but *no factual re‑assessment* unless the parties raise an issue on appeal.  
- **Why the facts are re‑examined.** Because a motion for summary judgment is a *conclusion based on material facts*.  The appellate must be sure the district court did not overlook an undisclosed fact that could have changed the decision.  De novo factual review protects litigants from a lower‑court factual error while still limiting the appellate to the record.  In *Cohen* the court reaffirmed that doctrine by examining the payment stipulations and the policy change in a factual light most favorable to the nonmoving party.

**Bottom line**

An appellate court reviewing a summary‑judgment motion does a *de novo factual* determination: it re‑examines the record, applies the “no‑genuine‑issue‑of‑material‑fact” test, and gives the nonmoving party the benefit of the doubt (“light most favorable”).  Ordinary appellate review, in contrast, is limited to reviewing judicial errors in law and does not provide a similar factual re‑assessment unless the parties raise a genuine‑issue‑of‑material‑fact issue on appeal.
