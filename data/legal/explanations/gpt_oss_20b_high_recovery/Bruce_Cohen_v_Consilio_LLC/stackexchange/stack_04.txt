Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What procedural hurdles must a plaintiff face when raising new injunctive relief after a summary judgment motion has been filed?
Question:
Cohen attempted to seek an injunction to rescind Consilio’s overtime policy after the district court had granted summary judgment on the prior claims. The opinion references *InfoDeli* and Rule 15 to explain why the court considered this improper. I’m confused about the procedural rules governing new claims after a summary judgment motion. According to the rules, must Cohen amend his complaint to include the injunctive relief? What is the court’s justification for treating the new relief as a separate claim, and can it be effectively pursued after a summary judgment has been granted?
Answer:
**Short answer**

- Yes, Cohen would have to file an *amended* complaint (or a Rule 15 (a)(1) *motion to amend*) to bring the injunction.
- The court treated the injunction as a *new claim* because it was not pleaded in the original complaint and would have required a new legal analysis; InfoDeli and Rule 15(a) make that the only proper way to raise it after the pleading stage.
- After the district court has granted summary judgment on the issues the case had before, a new claim cannot be pursued in that same action; Cohen would need to amend *before* the final order or file a new lawsuit.

---

## 1.  Rule 15 and the “new‑claim” rule

| Rule | What it says | How it applies to Cohen |
|------|--------------|--------------------------|
| **Rule 15(a)(1)** | A pleading may be amended to add “new matters” or new relief, *by the same form and process as the original pleading.* | Cohen could add the injunctive request, but it would have to be filed as an amendment, not as part of his *opposition brief* to the summary‑judgment motion. |
| **Rule 15(a)(2)** | A party may amend a pleading “to add facts or matters not previously pleaded, or to remedy a defect or omission” **but only if it has not yet been “judicially decided.”** | The injunction was *new* – it had never been pleaded and not yet decided – so an amendment *could* be filed. |
| **Rule 15(b)(2)(i)** | An amendment made after a *final notice* or order may be allowed “at the court’s discretion” **only if it is filed before the final judgment is entered**. | By the time the district court granted summary judgment, the final order was promptly entered, leaving little or no “discretionary” window for an amendment. |

> *Rule 15(b)(2)(i) does not give the party a “free‑pass” after the case is closed; you must file the amendment before the final order is entered.*

---

## 2.  InfoDeli – “new claims are raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”

The Ninth‑? or eighth‑? Circuit’s 2025 decision *InfoDeli, LLC v. * explained that an “opposition brief” is only a *response* to the pleadings already on record.  If a party introduces *new claims*—claims that were *not* pleaded in the original complaint—they must be introduced as an *amendment*, not as part of an opposition brief.  (*InfoDeli*, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6).  

- **Why** – because the pleading is the “continuous record” that the court uses to decide the case.  A new claim that had never been pleaded is not part of that record, so it cannot be considered at the same time the court is deciding the matters in the original pleadings.
- **Result in this case** – the injunction was treated as a *separate claim* and was not considered in the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling.

---

## 3.  Practical consequences for Cohen

| Situation | What Cohen could have done | What the court actually said | Outcome |
|-----------|----------------------------|------------------------------|--------|
| **Before summary‑judgment order** | File a Rule 15(a) *motion to amend* pleading and insert the injunction. | District court would likely have allowed the amendment, and the case could have been heard on the new relief. | **Not required** because Cohen did not do it. |
| **After summary‑judgment order** | Theory: file a Rule 15(b) *motion to amend after judgment*.  The court would decide whether to allow it. | The appellate court noted that the injunction was “new” and since the summary judgment had already *closed* the case on the claims, it could not effectively be added later. | The injunction **cannot be pursued** in that action; a new action would be required. |

> *“Thus, the issue is not properly before us”* – the appellate court wrote, because the injunctive relief was a *new claim* unrelated to the prior summary‑judgment determination.

---

## 4.  Bottom line for a plaintiff

1. **Amend before final judgment** – a new claim (or new relief that materially changes the understanding of the claim) must be added via an amendment *while the case is still pending and before the final order is entered.*  
2. **After a final opinion** – you have a very slim window under Rule 15(b)(2)(i); otherwise the *right* to a new claim is lost. In practice, that means a *new lawsuit* is the most reliable way forward.  
3. **On the factual level** – if the claim would be “new” even if the underlying facts are the same (e.g., an injunctive claim versus a monetary claim), it still counts as a new claim under Rules 15 and InfoDeli, so it must be pleaded in the amendment.

---

**In Cohen’s case:** The injury‑to‑employment claim was *old*, but the injunctive request was *new*. The appellate court reiterated that new claims must be raised in an amendment, not in an opposition brief, and that once summary judgment was granted, the pending case was closed on the factual matters it had addressed. Therefore, Cohen could not effectively pursue the injunction in that action; he would need to amend *before* the final order or start a new action entirely.
