Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties Involved: George Santos, Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, Disney  
The plaintiff is former United States Representative **George Santos**, who brought federal copyright and state‑law claims against **Jimmy Kimmel** (host of ABC’s *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* “JKL”), **ABC Television** (the network that airs JKL), and **The Walt Disney Company**, the corporate parent of ABC. Santos asserts that Kimmel, ABC, and Disney used his copyrighted videos without permission, thereby infringing his exclusive rights.

## Cameo Requests and Santos’s Video Production  
Through the online platform Cameo, Kimmel contacted Santos under fictitious pseudonyms and asked him to produce a series of personalized video messages. Santos complied with each request, filming the videos that became the subject of the dispute. These videos were the copyrighted works that Santos claimed were protected under the Copyright Act.

## Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” Segment and use of the videos  
Kimmel subsequently incorporated the videos into a recurring segment on JKL titled **“Will Santos Say It?”** The segment aired the videos for comedic, satirical commentary, positioning them as mockery of Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. The use was public and broadcast, and Kimmel asserted that it conferred First‑Amendment protection.

## Claims Raised by Santos: copyright infringement and state‑law claims (breach, implied contract, fraudulent inducement)  
Santos’s complaint alleged that Kimmel’s broadcast constituted **copyright infringement**, accusing the defendants of using his video messages without consent. The complaint also asserted three independent state‑law claims:  
1. **Breach of contract** under the Cameo Terms of Service;  
2. **Breach of an implied contract**; and  
3. **Fraudulent inducement**, alleging that Kimmel misrepresented the nature of the videos for financial gain.  
Santos contended that the defendants violated these contractual obligations and deceived him.

## District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal and fair‑use rationale  
The U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York dismissed the complaint entirely under **Rule 12(b)(6)**, finding that Santos’s copyright claims were barred by the **fair‑use doctrine**. The court analyzed the four statutory factors:  
* **Purpose and character** – the court regarded Kimmel’s use as *transformative*, employing the videos for satirical commentary rather than reproducing them wholesale.  
* **Nature of the copyrighted work** – the original videos were factual (in the sense that they captured Santos’s likeness and performance) and thus less protected for this purpose.  
* **Amount and substantiality** – the use was limited to the portions relevant for the satire.  
* **Effect on the market** – the segment did not serve as a substitute for the original works and did not harm their market potential.  

On these grounds, the court concluded that a fair‑use defense was adequate at the pleading stage. The court also dismissed the state‑law claims, holding that either they were *preempted by the Copyright Act* or that Santos failed to plead a foundation for them.  The district court entered a **final judgment of dismissal**.

## Appeal filing, docket, and review procedure  
Santos appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit on **October 6 2025**, docket number **No. 24‑2196‑cv**. The appellate court applied the **de‑novo** jurisprudence set forth in *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.* and reviewed the pleadings and the district court’s fair‑use analysis. Judge Lohier, writing for the panel, found no reversible error. The Court affirmed the district court’s dismissal, holding that Santos’s claims failed to meet the pleading standard and that the fair‑use defense was sufficiently established in the record.

# Statutory and Common‑Law Foundations  

The jury of a lawsuit rests on whether a complaint overtly shows that a legal wrong has occurred. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Court examined a rich tapestry of federal and state doctrines—fair‑use under § 107 of the Copyright Act, preemption under § 301, the pleading standard of Rule 12(b)(6), and state‑law contract and fraud rules from Illinois and New York—before deciding the merits of each claim. The following sections outline the statutory and common‑law underpinnings that frame the Court’s analysis, exposing the precise nexus between statutory language or common‑law rule and the Court’s factual findings.  

## Copyright Act §107 – Fair‑use factors  

Section 107 of the Copyright Act enumerates four non‑exclusive factors that together guide a court’s determination of whether a particular use is “fair.” These factors are: the purpose and character of the use; the nature of the copyrighted work; the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and the effect of the use on the market for the original. In *Santos*, the Court applied the first factor, the “purpose and character of the use,” with primacy, because fair use is “a flexible concept that varies with context.” The district court had already held that the use—including Kimmel’s broadcast of Santos’s Cameo videos—was melodic or idiosyncratic enough not to be a mere supersession, but “transformative,” adding commentary. The appellate Court agreed, elaborating that transformation is judged “by a reasonable observer, not by the user’s subjective intent.” It cited *Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith* and *Hachette Book Grp. v. Internet Archive* to support the idea that the Court may find fair use single‑handedly, even at the pleading stage.  

The second factor, the “nature of the copyrighted work,” was implicitly satisfied, as the Statute’s language encourages protection for expressive works but also recognizes that facts, facts, and ideas are not protected—though the Court noted that the videos’ excerpts were “published on the same day they were created,” implying the original work’s publication under 17 U.S.C. § 101.  

The third factor, the amount and substantiality of the excerpt, was treated under the standard that “the mere fact that a work is longer does not automatically defeat a fair‑use claim.” The court looked at the excerpt’s compilations of a figure and a sound bite, concluding that the portion used was not a “substantial part” in the administrative sense.  

The fourth factor, the effect upon the market, was explained with reference to *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose* and *Hachette Book Grp.* The Court found no demonstrable market harm, noting that the plaintiff’s complaint failed to allege a “potential substitution market.” Thus, in *Santos*, all four factors combined to support the court’s finding that Kimmel’s use was fair and, at least at the pleading stage, barred the plaintiff’s copyright claim.  

## Copyright Act §301 – Potential preemption of state‑law claims  

Section 301 of the Copyright Act provides that a federal copyright law “shall not be construed so as to preclude … the enforcement of any other ... copyright ... law” and that the Act “does not preclude... use of discounts or federal court.” Importantly, the statute also includes the “preemption clause,” which states that a state law “cannot similarly create or protect an exclusive right for an author that is protected by the Federal Copyright Act.” In *Santos*, the district court dismissing the breach‑of‑contract claims on the ground of § 301 preempted the state‑law claims because the breach claims were, in fact, allegations that the defendants had unilaterally restricted the plaintiff’s “commercially valuable right.” The Court, however, found that the claims’ nature was not a copyright right at all—a “qualitatively different” contract claim—so the dismissal was based not on preemption but on failure to state a claim. Nonetheless, the Court reaffirmed the statutory text, repeating that the Copyright Act preempts “the creation or protection of exclusive rights” that coincide with a federal right.  The paragraph in the opinion where the Court recalls that **§ 301 only preempts “exclusively, in their nature and subject matter, the copyrighting or exclusion of copyright, or encompasses the entire scope of the right conferred by the Copyright Act”** anchors the importation of federal preemption into the state‑law arena.  

## Rule 12(b)(6) – Failure to state a claim standard  

Federal Rule 12(b)(6) requires a complaint to present a statement of facts that would supply the elements of a claim “if proven true.” The Court uses this standard uniformly, first reviewing the factual allegations in the complaint, the exhibits attached, and any documents referenced. In *Santos*, the Court follows *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable* in examining the complaint de‑novo. The Court found that Santos’ copyright claims satisfied Rule 12(b)(6) because the complaint presented a complete story: the contractor’s creation of the videos, the producers’ appropriation into the show, and the contested terminology.  But in contrast, the state‑law claims lacked the requisite pleadings. The bankruptcy’s argument that the breach of contract claims were preempted was *adequate*, but not the breach's element. The fraudulent inducement claim failed because the complaint did not allege the plaintiff’s out‑of‑pocket loss, a required element for New York law. The Court’s analysis demonstrates how Rule 12(b)(6) serves as the gatekeeper—if the complaint lays out the facts sufficient for a particular cause of action, the court may allow the claim to proceed; otherwise, it is dismissed.  

## Illinois contract law – presumption against non‑contracting third‑party benefits  

Illinois recognizes a strong presumption that a contract does not confer rights or responsibilities upon a third party unless an express declaration conveys such intent. *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.* applies in *Santos*: it establishes that an implied‑beneficiary dispositive must be “practically an express declaration.” The plaintiff alleged that the Cameo Terms of Service, a contractual instrument between Kimmel and the platform, afforded Santos a “beyond‑reasonable” benefit. The Court explained that because Santos is not, per Illinois law, a party to the Terms of Service, and the Terms themselves do not address that interpretation, the Court could adopt Illinois’ presumption and dismiss the claim for failure to plead eligibility. Therefore the Illinois contract law doctrine prevented the plaintiff from extracting the terms of an agreement that did not create a contractual relationship to begin with.  

## New York contract law – essentials of implied‑in‑fact contracts and meeting of minds  

New York requires that an implied‑in‑fact contract be supported by the parties’ intent to be bound—a “meeting of minds.” The Court cited *Rocky Point Props. v. Sear‑Brown* and *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.* to explain that a plaintiff must allege the essential terms: consideration, the parties’ intent, and a sufficient description of the terms. In *Santos*, the complaint did not plead the exact terms of the Course of the Conspiracy or the technical details of the alleged breach. Without that, New York law precludes the court from acting on a slip‑shod allegation. The Court reiterates that it is not sufficient to assert the existence of an agreement; a statutory “essential” approach demands that at the complaint stage the plaintiff supply fact that establishes the parties’ agreement and the particulars of the contract terms. The Court concluded that Santos’ breach of contract claim did not contain these elements and was dismissed accordingly.  

## New York fraudulent inducement – out‑of‑pocket loss requirement  

Under New York law, a fraudulent inducement claim requires that the plaintiff allege an actual out‑of‑pocket loss in addition to deceit. The Court’s reference to *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney* underscores that a mere “unfair bargain” is insufficient; there must be some measurable loss paid due to the defrauded figure. In *Santos*, the complaint failed to allege any concrete loss incurred by the plaintiff, such as a payment for the video or damages resulting from losing a contract.  Because the pleading never indicated a loss, the court determined that the claim was lacking an element Apex; it fixed the out‑of‑pocket loss requirement thereby appropriately applying New York’s rule.  

## Supreme Court guidance on good‑faith in fair‑use analysis  

The Supreme Court in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Entertainments* asserted that ‘good faith and fair dealing’ underpins the fair‑use defense. However, the issue has since been clarified by the Court, which in *Hitch* and other decisions pronounced that a good‑faith basis is not automatically required for fair use, especially when the defendant’s conduct is “... motivated by criticism and commentary,” or when “the parties… have no particular expectation of the use.” In *Santos*, the Court clarified that the alleged “bad faith” representations by Kimmel do not blunt the fair‑use defense. The Court noted that the Supreme Court has “distanced itself from its earlier statements,” and that the plaintiff’s own crate of fallacious allegations would, in fact, support the fair‑use outcome.  

In conclusion, the statutory and common‑law doctrines evidently shape each claim in *Santos v. Kimmel*. The Court’s reliance on these doctrines—fair‑use under § 107, preemption under § 301, Rule 12(b)(6), Illinois non‑party presumption, New York implied‑contract rules, the out‑of‑pocket loss requirement, and Supreme Court guidance—provides a compact roadmap of the legal principles in a complex multi‑jurisdictional case.

## Copyright Infringement and Fair‑Use Analysis

The Second Circuit’s review of Santos v. Kimmel focused on whether the District Court properly dismissed Santos’s copyright‑infringement claim at the pleading stage on the affirmative defense of fair use. The court’s analysis was structured around the four statutory fair‑use factors, supplemented by an examination of alleged bad‑faith conduct. The findings concluded that each factor favored a finding that the First‑party use—Kimmel’s broadcast of the videos—was a fair use, and therefore the claim should be dismissed.

### Purpose and Character – The Transformative Nature of Kimmel’s Satirical Critique

The district and appellate courts applied the Supreme Court’s standard that the “purpose and character” of a use is judged on its *transformative* nature (Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F. 4th 26). The court explained that "the purpose must add something new … with a further purpose or different character." The claim’s *transformative* element was clear: Kimmel’s segments titled “Will Santos Say It?” were not merely reproductions of Santos’s original videos; they were embedded in a broader satirical program that critiqued the former congressman’s willingness to speak absurdly for monetary gain. Santos “does not dispute the District Court’s finding that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was ‘to comment on the willingness of Santos…to say absurd things for money.’” The court noted that a reasonable observer, not the parties’ subjective intent, dictates transformative status. As the complaint itself acknowledged, “a reasonable observer … would think the videos conveyed feelings of hope, … positivity,” not merely a platform for Kimmel’s commentary. Thus, the first factor overwhelmingly supported fair use.

### Nature of the Work – Creative Status of the Videos and Simultaneous Publication

For the “nature of the work” factor, the judges referenced the statutory definition that a copyright protects “original works of authorship” (17 U.S.C. § 101). The court observed that the videos were “self‑published” on Cameo the same day they were created, thereby satisfying the originality requirement. The videos were also registered with the Copyright Office at the time of publication; the opinion cited the defendants’ exhibits and noted that “the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created.” Since the works were fully original, the second factor did not lean against fair use.

### Amount and Substantiality – Inclusion of the Entire Video and Copyright Registration

The third factor examines how much of the original work was used. The appellate court acknowledged that Kimmel broadcast the *entire* video. While “the use of the entire work normally disfavors a finding of fair use,” the court held that the amount was *necessary* for the purpose of commentary, fitting the “required and appropriate amount” test. In addition, the court noted that the videos were “copyright‑registered” in a single registration covering the entire work; thus, the second factor influences the third. Because the defendant’s use was integral to the commentary, the court considered the use justified under the amount factor.

### Market Effect – No Sign that Kimmel’s Segment Usurps Santos’s Market

Under the fourth fair‑use factor, the court required evidence that the defendant’s use adversely affected the original’s market. The district court’s finding that Kimmel’s use did not supplant the videos “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute” was affirmed. The court explained that “the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market” (Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569). The fact that the videos were shown as part of a satirical series, not a marketable product, meant there was no plausible market harm.

### Bad‑Faith Allegations – Questionability under Supreme Court Evolution and Case Law

Santos attempted to defeat fair use by raising Kimmel’s deceptive conduct on Cameo. The court traced the Supreme Court’s history: while Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises stated that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” (though the Court has since “distanced itself from this statement”), subsequent decisions have limited the reach of bad‑faith as a defense. The appellate court rejected Santos’s claim that Kimmel’s “false representations demonstrate bad faith and thus nullify the fair‑use defense.” The court viewed that Santos’s complaint lacked evidence of Kimmel’s intent to supplant the videos, and that “the complaint paints a portrait of defendants motivated by criticism, not commercial appropriation.” Accordingly, the bad‑faith argument could not defeat the first‑factor finding.

### Precedential Support – Andy Warhol, TCA Television, Harper & Row, and Others

The court drew heavily on try‑but‑not‑quite‑clear precedent. Andy Warhol found it “so clearly established by a complaint … to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim at the pleading stage” (TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F. 3d 168). The appellate court noted that “the first factor strongly supports a finding of fair use” and that this resolution is consistent with the standard approach in earlier cases, such as Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F. 4th 163. These authorities underlined the shift toward a flexible, context‑dependent analysis, guiding the court’s conclusion that the claim was dismissed without further fact‑finding.

### Court’s Finding – Fair Use Bars the Infringement Claim at the Pleading Stage

In sum, the appellate court affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of the copyright‑infringement claim. The court found that each of the four statutory fair‑use factors – transformation, nature, amount, and market effect – supported a conclusion that Kimmel’s use was protected. Bad‑faith allegations offered no substantive basis to rebut the first factor. Consequently, the court held that the claim failed at the pleading stage and was rightly dismissed.

# State‑Law Claims – Contract & Fraudulent Inducement

## Direct Breach of Cameo TOS – Non‑contracting third‑party beneficiary presumption  

In Santos’s direct breach claim, the plaintiff alleged that Jimmy Kimmel breached the Cameo Terms of Service when he produced the “Will Santos Say It?” segments and later aired them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (JKL). The core issue is whether Santos, a person who never entered the Cameo agreement, can invoke the Terms of Service as the basis for a remedy. Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service because they are a contract made in Illinois, provides a strong presumption against conferring benefits on third parties who are not expressly named in a contract. The appellate court therefore pointed to Barry v. St. Mary’s Hospital, which establishes that a non‑contracting party must rely on an express declaration that the contract’s terms are intended to benefit him. Santos offered no such declaration or any provision in the Terms of Service that expressly or implicitly designates him as a beneficiary. Consequently, the court held that the plaintiff could not bring a third‑party‑beneficiary claim, and the district court’s dismissal on this ground was correct.

## Implied‑Contract Claim – Missing essential terms and lack of meeting of minds  

Santos also pursued an implied‑contract claim directly against Kimmel and the involved broadcasting companies. Under New York law, which governs this action, a plaintiff must plead, in non‑conclusory language, the essential terms of the alleged contract—price, subject matter, and the promises of each party. In addition, the claim requires proof of a meeting of the minds, i.e., mutual assent to those terms. The court found that Santos’s complaint failed to identify or describe any of the alleged contract’s essential terms. He also did not allege any conduct, communication, or agreement that would indicate a mutual assent between the parties. Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Group illustrates that a claim lacking such essential terms does not state a cause of action. Accordingly, the appellate court affirmed the district court’s dismissal of the implied‑contract claim for failure to state a claim.

## Preemption Discussion – Copyright preemption raised but not pursued  

During the lower‑court proceedings the district court considered that Santos’s contract claims might be preempted by the Copyright Act. The appellate court noted that the issue of preemption is one that the court would ordinarily analyze if the plaintiff’s claims survived the pleading analysis. However, because the plaintiff’s contractual claims were found deficient by violation of both contract doctrine and pleading standards, the appellate court elected not to resolve the preemption question. The court’s statement that it “does not need to resolve preemption because each of the claims fails to state a claim” reflects the principle that a claim that is already deemed defective does not merit further examination on a secondary ground such as federal preemption.

## Fraudulent Inducement – Requirement of out‑of‑pocket loss not alleged  

Santos also asserted a fraudulent‑inducement claim under New York law, arguing that Kimmel’s false representations about the videos induced him to pay for them. New York’s fraudulent‑inducement causes require the plaintiff to plead an actual out‑of‑pocket loss incurred as a result of the defendant’s acts. The court cited Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney and Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill to emphasize that a fraud claim must allege that the plaintiff suffered a concrete, direct loss—whether monetary or otherwise. Because Santos’s complaint did not specify any loss or financial harm, the court found the claim insufficient. This matched the district court’s dismissal and was likewise affirmed on appeal.

## Case Law Illustrations – Barry, Rocky Point Props, Lama Holding, Connaughton  

The appellate court strategically invoked four key cases to support its conclusions. Barry v. St. Mary’s Hospital clarified the presumption against third‑party beneficiaries; Rocky Point Props. underscored the necessity of pleading essential contract terms and a meeting of minds; Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney and Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill illustrated the out‑of‑pocket‑loss requirement for fraud claims. By applying these precedents to the facts in the Santos case, the court illustrated how established doctrines either preclude the claims or bar them for failure to allege required elements.

## Summary – Claims do not state a prima facie cause of action  

For all of the reasons discussed, the state‑law claims that Santos brought—direct breach of a non‑contracting third‑party, implied contract, and fraudulent inducement—each failed to plead a viable cause of action. The distinct legal problems in each category required specific elements—beneficiary status, essential contract terms and mutual assent, and out‑of‑pocket loss—that Santos did not provide. The court’s analysis, grounded in Illinois and New York contract doctrines and in the statutory and case‑law requirements for fraud, explains why each claim was dismissed and why the appellate court reversed the lower court’s determinations on no other legal ground.

# Holding, Significance, and Future Lessons

## Final Holding – All claims dismissed; fair use prevails; state‑law claims fail to state  
In the Ninth‑day opinion, Judge Lohier affirmed the Southern District of New York’s dismissal of entirely the copyright infringement claim and, likewise, of each state‑law claim filed by former congressman George Santos.  The appellate court held that the videos streamed on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* constituted a transformative use that fell within the fair‑use defense, and it found that Santos had not complied with the pleading requirements necessary to sustain any of the state‑law remedies.  The decision, therefore, confirms that the district court’s final judgment stands in its entirety.  

## Implications for Fair‑Use Doctrine – Expansion to television parody and satire  
Santos v. Kimmel illustrates the continued expansion of fair use to the realm of televised parody.  The court reiterated that the key test is whether a *reasonable observer* would perceive the new work as adding “new expression or meaning” rather than merely superseding the original.  Because Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segments were “commentary and criticism” rather than an attempt to replace the original videos, the first fair‑use factor was deemed favorably.  The decision confirms that satirical television programs may air short excerpts of copyrighted material so long as the use is clearly transformatively framed and the other fair‑use factors (nature, amount, and market impact) do not negate the defense.  

## Third‑Party Beneficiary Clarification – Conditions for benefit under service contracts  
In addressing Santos’s breach‑of‑contract allegation against the Cameo Terms of Service, the court made clear that a non‑contracting third party cannot invoke a contract that was drafted strictly between the platform and the content creator.  Under Illinois law, the court applies a *strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties* unless an express or very clear notwithstanding provision exists.  New York’s third‑party beneficiary test likewise demands the pleadings contain the essential terms of the alleged contract, an element Santos did not furnish.  The opinion thereby clarifies that a mere request for a personalized video does not automatically create a contractual relationship with the platform, and that courts will not infer such a relationship absent explicit contractual language.  

## Preemption Effects – Interaction between copyright and state contracts  
Santos’s claims for breach of contract and fraudulent inducement were dismissed partially on the basis of preemption.  The court reiterated that the Copyright Act preempts any state‑law claim that duplicates the protections afforded by federal copyright (see *Melendez*).  Because the alleged breach involved the transmission of a copyrighted performance, the state‑law claim was effectively barred by the Copyright Act’s preemptive authority.  Appellate judges should therefore scrutinize any state‑law counter‑claims that, even when couched in contract or tort language, recast a federally protected right.  

## Pleading Strategy – Necessity of stating essential elements and damages  
The *Santos* decision serves as a cautionary example of how failure to plead as an essential element can derail a claim.  For breach of contract, the plaintiff must allege the existence of an *express* or *implied* agreement and provide its specific terms; any absence of the latter renders the claim defective at the pleading stage.  For fraudulent inducement, New York law requires the complaint to allege *actual out‑of‑pocket loss*; Santos’s omission precluded liability.  These standard pleading requirements, grounded in precedent (e.g., *Caniglia* and *Lama Holding*), are as critical in copyright cases as they are in broader civil litigation.  

## Practical Take‑aways for Copyright Litigation  
1. **Fair‑use analysis is anchored in the observer test for transformation**; craft pleadings that articulate the new purpose of the use in plain language.  
2. **Avoid relying on implied third‑party benefits** in service‑contract disputes; secure an explicit contractual element or a clearly stated third‑party beneficiary clause.  
3. **Be mindful of preemption**: any claim that attempts to address a federal copyright right must demonstrate a unique state‑law remediable policy not covered by the Copyright Act.  
4. **State‑law claims must be fully developed**: plead essential contract terms, and when alleging fraud, clearly describe actual damages or loss.  
5. **Keep the oral and textual evidence documented** so that the transformation and market‑impact factors can be convincingly asserted.  

By internalizing these principles, law students and litigants can craft more robust pleadings and anticipate how appellate courts will evaluate such claims in the evolving tapestry of copyright law.
