Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What exactly does the fair‑use doctrine require for a use to be considered 'transformative'?
Question:
I’m confused about how the court determines whether the use in this case is truly transformative. The opinion repeatedly cites the *Andy Warhol Foundation* case and says that the purpose and character of the use must be judged by a reasonable observer rather than the user’s intent. How does the court evaluate whether a secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or value? Is simply commenting or parodying enough to be transformative, or is there a higher bar?
Answer:
**What the court means by “transformative”**

The Second Circuit’s opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel* makes it clear that “transformative” is a *purpose‑and‑character* question—one that sits inside the first factor of the 4‑factor fair‑use test.  The court quotes the Supreme Court’s standard in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts v. Goldsmith*:

> “We focus… on the degree to which the use is transformative, i.e., *whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.*”  
> (*Santos*, 2025, 16‑17)

Two observations follow:

1. **The test is objective.**  
   The opinion stresses that *“the purpose of the allegedly infringing use is determined by what a reasonable observer would think, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the user.”*  (*Santos*, 19)

2. **The “new” element must be more than a slight gloss.**  
   A use that simply republishes the original in the same form is a *supersession* – not a transformation.  A use that introduces an altered aim, tone, or function—adding commentary, criticism, or new meaning—is a transformation.

**Applying the standard in the case**

The court found Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segment to be transformative because the audience could regard it as a *mocking commentary* on the videos Kimmel had obtained from Santos, rather than a substitute for those videos.  Where the opinion says:

> “A reasonable observer here would think the videos conveyed *feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,* not a willingness to say absurd things for money.” (*Santos*, 18)

the court concludes that the new work provides a different purpose (criticism/ parody) and adds “new expression” (the show’s framing, commentary cues, and humor).  This satisfies the *transformative* requirement.

**Is commentary or parody automatically transformative?**

The opinion does *not* impose a “one‑size‑fits‑all” rule.  While parody and criticism *can* be transformative, the court still asks whether the secondary use *adds* new meaning or value.  A brief quotation that merely repeats the original idea, even in a humorous veneer, would likely fail the transformation test if it does not truly alter the work’s purpose.  

In *Santos*, the transformation bar was met because:

- The Kimmel segment *altered* the original purpose (from personal videos to public mockery).  
- It *added* commentary and new expression that a reasonable viewer would identify as a distinct work.  
- It did not supplant the original’s market; it served a different audience function.

**Bottom line**

A use is considered transformative when it does **not merely re‑use the original material** but **adds new expression, meaning, or a different purpose** that a reasonable observer would recognize.  The user’s intent is irrelevant; the *objective* character of the new work is what matters.  Parody or commentary may meet this threshold, but only if it truly changes the work’s character and adds substantive new value.

### Can a court dismiss a copyright infringement claim at the pleading stage solely on a fair‑use defense?
Question:
The district court dismissed Santos’s complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) on the grounds that fair use applied. I know pretty well that fair use is a defense, not a basis for dismissal. Under what circumstances is it appropriate for a trial court to dismiss a claim for infringement before evidence is presented, relying purely on the pleadings?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes.  Under Rule 12(b)(6) a district court may dismiss a copyright‑infringement claim if, **on the face of the complaint, the alleged infringement is clearly a *fair use***.  When the facts contributed by the plaintiff’s pleading satisfy the four statutory factors so decisively that no additional evidence could alter the conclusion, the claim is legally insufficient and the court can dismiss it without a trial.

---

### 1.  Rule 12(b)(6) and the “failure to state a claim” standard

Rule 12(b)(6) authorizes dismissal if the complaint “fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted” (Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6)).  The standard is**de novo**; the court examines the plea itself plus any documents attached to it (see *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d 104, 111 [2d Cir. 2010]).  The court may also take judicial notice of well‑publicized data (e.g., copyright registrations).

A claim “fails to state” not only when it is factually unsound, but also when it cannot survive a doctrinal defense that **barred the claim entirely**.  The Copyright Act makes fair use a defense that, if established, eliminates the legal duty to observe a licensed right (§107).  Thus aclaim that would otherwise be an infringement can be dismissed for lack of an actionable cause of action when the fair‑use defense is destined to succeed.

---

### 2.  Fair use can be “clearly established” at the pleading stage

*The Court’s own language provides the key threshold.*  
In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the appellate court emphasized that the district court’s ending by “concluding that the copyright claims were barred by the fair‑use doctrine” was justified because the complaint “displayed facts so clear that they could support dismissal” (*TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 178 [2d Cir. 2016]).

This means that the pleadings must contain:

1. **A transformation** that the court accepts as “transformative” under §107(1) (i.e., the new work adds new expression, meaning, or message).  
2. **A sufficiently limited use** of the protected portion.  
3. **No market injury or market‑replacement** effect under §107(4).

When those five facts are explicitly or implicitly contained in the complaint—such as the defendant’s stated **purpose** (“to comment,” “to ridicule”), the **nature of the original work** (“fictional, non‑educational comedy”), the **amount** of material used, and the **absence of market harm**—the court can say, “The defense is already decisive; the claim fails even before evidence can be presented.”

The *Santos* decision cites *Hachette Book Grp. v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163, 179 [2d Cir. 2024], which explains that the four-factor analysis is “flexible” and that a *reasonable observer* can make a fair‑use determination from the complaint’s facts alone.

---

### 3.  How the court applied the standard in *Santos*

- **Purpose/character**: The complaint (and the attached exhibits) explained that the videos were aired on “Will Santos Say It?” as “mocking segments”—a “commentary or criticism" that, per the court, is transformative.  
- **Nature of the work**: The videos were short, “non‑educational” and “fictional,” supporting the second factor.  
- **Amount used**: The complaint noted the videos were used in a “brief segment,” and the court found the amount “minimally substantial.”  
- **Market effect**: The complaint stated there was no competition with the “original” videos and no user demand for a substitute; the court found no effect on the potential market.

Because these facts are “so clearly established by the complaint” that they “support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” (Santos, 231‑233), the district court rightfully dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6).  The appellate court affirmed, holding that the fair‑use defense “bars a claim even at the pleading stage.”

---

### 4.  When a claim **cannot** be dismissed solely on pleadings

The same authority requires the court **not** to dismiss unless the pleading conclusively shows defeat.  If aspects of the four factors are ambiguous, or if evidence could potentially overturn a defense (for example, a claim that the work is “too large” or “in the market for the original”), the court must allow the case to proceed.  In such cases, the plaintiff’s evidence is indispensable to the fair‑use inquiry.

---

## Bottom line for a law student

A court may dismiss a copyright‑infringement claim at the pleading stage **only if the complaint itself contains facts that unambiguously satisfy all four fair‑use factors**, so that a competent judge can determine that the defense will trump the claim.  That was exactly what the *Santos* court concluded, and the appellate court agreed.  When the pleadings are not so crystal‑clear, the court must wait for the evidence that will carry the fair‑use analysis.

### How do ‘bad faith’ representations affect the fair‑use defense, and can they nullify the defense?
Question:
The opinion discusses Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s false representations indicate bad faith and therefore undermine fair use. The Court cites *Harper & Row* but states it has moved away from that assertion. Does a plaintiff’s claim of misrepresentation or bad faith automatically defeat a defendant’s fair‑use defense? What is the current standard for linking bad faith to fair use?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No.  A claim that a defendant misled a plaintiff or acted in bad faith does **not** automatically defeat a fair‑use defense.  In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit expressly rejected the old Harper & Row “good‑faith” test and held that the four‑factor fair‑use analysis stands on its own.  Bad‑faith misrepresentations may be considered as part of the *purpose and character* factor, but they are not an automatic bar to fair use.

---

### 1.  The “good‑faith” premise is no longer controlling

*Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v.  Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539 (1985) held that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.”  The Second Circuit in *Santos*, however, says:

> “We disagree.  It is true that the Supreme Court stated in *Harper & Row* that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.”  … **But the Court has since distanced itself from this statement.**”  (see *Santos*, 106 F.4th at 28‑29)

The Court concludes that a defendant’s false representations do not automatically invalidate a claimed fair use.  The “good‑faith” dictum is no longer treated as a stand‑alone defense bar.

---

### 2.  Fair use is still governed by the four‑factor test

The statutory four‑factor framework remains the lens through which courts evaluate fair use (§ 107 U.S.C.):

1. **Purpose and character of the use** (transformative nature, commercial vs. non‑commercial, etc.)  
2. **Nature of the copyrighted work**  
3. **Amount and substantiality of the portion used**  
4. **Effect on the market** for the original

The *Santos* opinion explicitly applies this test:

> “In assessing the *purpose and character* factor, we *focus… chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative* ….”  (*Santos*, 106 F.4th at 26‑27)  

> “Because we need only consider the *original version* and the *JKL segments* at issue to decide *fair use* …” (*Santos*, 106 F.4th at 29)

Bad‑faith arguments have no separate footnote in the list of factors.  The Court evaluated transformation by asking how a *reasonable observer* would view the use, not the defendant’s intent or alleged deceit.

---

### 3.  How bad‑faith misrepresentations are treated

The Court therefore treats false representations as part of the *purpose* factor, but only as one piece of *context*.  In *Santos* the court said that:

> “The complaint paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary … On the face of Santos’s complaint, Kimmel was *not* supplanting the value of the original videos.”  (*Santos*, 106 F.4th at 28)

Thus, even though the plaintiff alleged misrepresentation, the court found that the videos remained *transformative* and that the finder of the meaning—“a reasonable observer”—was unaffected by any alleged deceit.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for a law student

* A plaintiff’s accusation of bad faith **does not** automatically defeat a fair‑use defense.  
* Courts still apply the statutory four‑factor test; *good‑faith* is an optional color, not a prerequisite.  
* Misrepresentation can weigh against a transformation claim if it materially alters a *reasonable observer’s* perception of the work, but it is not a certainty.  
* The modern standard, as reaffirmed in *Santos*, treats bad faith as a factor that may influence the *purpose and character* analysis, but it can never single‑handedly negate fair use.

---

**Key quotation to remember**

> “We disagree.  It is true that the Supreme Court stated in *Harper & Row* that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” *Santos*, 106 F.4th 28‑29  

— the Second Circuit’s definitive statement that “good faith” is no longer a binding requirement for fair use.

### When does the Copyright Act preempt state‑law contract claims such as breach of contract or fraudulent inducement?
Question:
Santos tried to bring breach of contract and fraudulent inducement claims against Kimmel and ABC. The court says these claims were preempted by the Copyright Act but then notes that *Melendez v. Sirius XM* indicates that preemption isn’t automatic. What are the criteria a court uses to decide whether a state‑law contract claim is preempted by a federal copyright rule?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

The Copyright Act preempts a state‑law contract claim only when the claim is *about the same rights* that Title 17 grants and would add a new restriction or remedy that the federal law does not provide.  
In *Santos v. Kimmel* the circuit made clear that preemption is not an automatic bar – the court simply follows the “preemption test”:  

1. **Does the claim involve a contractual relationship that regulates the copyright‑protected work?**  
2. **Does the contract seek to impose a duty or grant a right that changes, adds to, or conflicts with the exclusive rights laid out in Title 17?**  
3. **Would the state‑law remedy duplicate or supplement a remedy available under the Copyright Act?**

If any of these conditions are not met, the claim is not preempted.  In *Santos* the breach‑of‑contract claim was dismissed because Santos was not a party to the Cameo terms at all, so the claim did not even meet the first touchstone.  The court therefore did not need to decide preemption, but it left the door open that, had the claim been properly pled, the test above would apply.  

---

### 1.  The Preemption Doctrine in the Opinion

| Source | What it says |
|--------|---------------|
| *Santos* (discussing preemption) | “Santos … responds that those claims are *qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim* and thus not preempted. **Melendez v. Sirius XM** (2d Cir. 2022) reinforces that preemption is not automatic. (See 50 F.4th at 302.) |
| *Melendez* (implicit test) | Although the opinion does not quote it verbatim, the court’s reliance on *Melendez* signals that preemption is determined by **whether the state‑law claim regulates the same ground that the Copyright Act protects**. |

The court therefore positions preemption as a *case‑by‑case analysis* guided by the relationship between the contractual provisions at issue and the exclusive rights granted by Title 17.

### 2.  The Practical Test

Drawing on the textual comments in *Santos*, the court’s approach aligns with the classic “what would the Copyright Act do if the same facts were present?” line of inquiry:

| Question | Why it matters | How *Santos* applied it |
|----------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Does the contract bind parties who control the copyrighted work?** | If no, the claim is not about the copyrighted activity. | Santos was *not* a party to the Cameo Terms, so his breach‑of‑contract claim did not touch the copyright. |
| **Does the claim seek to enforce a restriction on use or distribution that the Act already regulates?** | Such a restriction would alter or supplement the exclusive rights, triggering preemption. | The claim was merely a claim of non‑performance of a service contract; it did not try to limit the right to use the videos beyond what Title 17 allowed. |
| **Is the state‑law remedy a duplicate of an exclusive‑right remedy?** | Duplication signals that the claim is a preempted vehicle for asserting federal rights. | The contract breach claim would compel Mannful to perform services, a remedy that is *not* an exclusive‑right remedy under Title 17. |
| **Does the court’s analysis of the contract focus on the enforceability of the terms, not on the copyright itself?** | If the enforceability is purely contractual, preemption does not apply. | The district court’s dismissal of the breach claim was founded on title‑specific contract analysis—no connection to copyright—so preemption was unnecessary. |

### 3.  Why *Santos* Avoided a Preemption Ruling

The opinion notes that “Santos’s state law claims either were preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a claim.”  Because the breach‐of‑contract claim **failed to state** a claim under state law (the plaintiff was not a party to the terms and failed to plead essential contract terms – see *Barry* and *Rocky Point Props.*), the court did not need to decide whether preemption applied.  The same logic applies to the fraudulent‑inducement claim: it was dismissed for failure to allege loss (see *Lama Holding*), so preemption was again moot.

In *Melendez*, the court had gone through the preemption test, deciding that a breach‑of‑contract claim **was not** preempted because the contract involved a license but the claim was about the contractor’s fraudulent representations, not the copyright‑protected rights themselves.  *Santos* merely cites that precedent to show that a state‑law remedy *can* stand beside copyright law if it is not aimed directly at the exclusive rights.

### 4.  Take‑aways for Future Cases

1. **Identify the parties.**  If the plaintiff is *not* a party to the contractual relationship that governs use of the copyrighted material, the claim is unlikely to be a preempted copyright claim.  
2. **Examine the contractual language.**  Does it impose duties that would alter the use of the work in a way that conflicts with Title 17?  If not, preemption is not triggered.  
3. **Check for duplicative remedies.**  If the state claim simply enforces what the federal law already permits, it remains a valid state claim.  
4. **Use *Melendez* as a guide.**  It clarifies that preemption is a **substantive** question, not an automatic exclusion: “preemption is *not* automatic” (50 F.4th at 302).

**Bottom line:** The Copyright Act preempts a state‑law contract claim only when that contractual claim is *directly about the exclusive rights* granted by Title 17 and seeks to impose a duty or grant an entitlement that the federal scheme does not (or cannot) provide.  All other contract claims—like a breach of a general service agreement or a fraud claim unrelated to the use of the copyrighted work—are *not* preempted, as *Santos* shows by ignoring preemption where the claim itself does not even survive pleading.

### Why can’t a non‑contracting third party sue under a third‑party beneficiary claim against a Terms of Service?
Question:
Santos alleges that Kimmel breached the Cameo Terms of Service, which he wasn’t a party to. The court cites Illinois law’s presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties. How does this presumption work, and what would Santos have needed to plead to overcome it and establish that the Terms of Service bound him?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Illinois courts impose a *strong presumption* that a written agreement does **not** confer rights or obligations on anyone who did not sign it.  The only ways to defeat that presumption are

1. **Express statutory or contractual language** (or an equally explicit statement in the contract itself) that the third party is an intended beneficiary, or  
2. **Clearly articulated intent** of the parties that the agreement is meant to benefit the third party, with a duty that the parties understood to extend to that third party.

Santos failed to meet either test.  He offered no express declaration that the Cameo Terms of Service (TOS) were “for” him or that Kimmel was bound to give him a benefit, nor did he allege any compelling evidence that the contractors’ intent was to benefit him.  Moreover, he did not plead the *essential terms* of the TOS that would have allowed an implied‑in‑fact contract to arise.  Because of this, the court correctly held that the TOS could not be invoked by Santos.

---

## How the Illinois presumption works

Illinois Supreme Court and appellate precedent treats a third‑party beneficiary claim as a *contractual* claim:

> “A *strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties*… The implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.”  **Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur**, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016) (quoting the opinion).

Two elements are required to defeat that presumption:

| Element | Requirement |
|---------|-------------|
| **1. Express indication** | The contract itself must contain language that **identifies the third party as a beneficiary** and gives that party enforceable rights.  This can be explicit (“This contract is binding on any person who…”) or very clearly implied (e.g., a clause that “beneficios are for the subscriber’s friends”). |
| **2. Clear intent of the parties** | Even absent explicit language, the parties can still establish that the contract was meant to benefit the third party.  The evidence must show that the contracting parties *intended* the third party to benefit, not that the third party merely happened to receive a benefit. |

If neither element is shown, the presumption stands and a non‑signatory cannot sue as a beneficiary.

---

## Why Santos’ complaint did not meet the test

1. **No express beneficiary clause.**  
   The court noted that the TOS includes third‑party beneficiary language **only in those provisions where the parties clearly intended a third‑party right**.  Santos offered *nothing close to an express declaration* that the portions of the TOS he relied on applied to him.  
   > “Santos identifies nothing close to an *express declaration* that the relevant provisions of the Terms of Service apply to him …” (opinion, supra).

2. **No evidence of parties’ intent.**  
   Santos also failed to allege—“in non‑conclusory language”—that the parties erected a contract that made Kimmel **bound** to satisfy the same terms that applied to the platform’s users.  
   > The complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, … the essential terms of the parties’ … contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” (opinion, supra).

3. **No alleged essential terms.**  
   A plaintiff bringing a third‑party beneficiary claim must plead the *essential* terms that the beneficiary would seek to enforce.  This means stating the specific clause or price that is alleged to have been breached and showing that the contract creates a duty to the third party.  Santos’ allegations merely asserted that Kimmel “breached” the TOS.  The court found that this was insufficient; the claim “failed to state a claim” because it did not articulate the proper contractual elements.  
   > “… the complaint does not ‘allege … the essential terms … upon which liability is predicated.’” (opinion, supra).

Because both the *express* and the *intent* elements of the presumption were missing, the court upheld the dismissal of the breach of contract claim.

---

## What Santos would have needed to plead

1. **An express declaration in the TOS that Santos is an intended beneficiary,** or a clear and unambiguous clause that the benefit extends to anyone who arranges for a video on Cameo.  
   *Example: “Users may authorize a third party to provide a personalized video on their behalf, and such third parties are entitled to enforce these Terms.”*

2. **Allegations that the parties *intended* the TOS to bind Kimmel to that beneficiary**—e.g., that the TOS was drafted with the understanding that any person who used Kimmel’s services on Cameo would be protected by the TOS and that Kimmel was contractually bound to honor those obligations.

3. **Pleadment of the *essential* terms** that Santos claims were breached (e.g., a clause stating that “the video must be delivered in a timely, non‑misleading manner”) and an explanation of how Kimmel’s conduct violated those terms.

4. **Analysis of the relationship** between Santos and Kimmel—if the TOS treats the relationship as that of a “customer‐service‑provider” arrangement, Santos would need to establish that Kimmel agreed to be liable on Santos’ behalf under the TOS.

Without such pleadings, the presumption remains unbroken.  The court had no basis to consider the TOS as giving Santos enforceable rights, and so the breach‑of‑contract claim was dismissed.

---

### Bottom line

Illinois law treats a contract as an agreement only between the parties who sign or agree to it.  A non‑signer must show either **an explicit contractual provision** naming him as a beneficiary or **strong evidence that the contracting parties intended** the contract to bind him.  Santos’ complaint offered neither, and his failure to plead the essential terms meant that the pre‑existing presumption was not rebutted.  Thus, the TOS could not be used to support a third‑party beneficiary claim.

### What is the pleading requirement for a fraudulent inducement claim under New York law?
Question:
The court dismissed Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim for failing to allege an out‑of‑pocket loss. I understand that the claim must contain an alleged loss, but what specifics does New York require? Must the plaintiff state the exact amount or type of loss, or can a general statement suffice? How does that affect the pleadability of the claim?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under New York law a fraudulent‑inducement claim is not complete unless the complaint sets out **an actual loss suffered by the plaintiff**.  
The Supreme Court of New York has held that a claim for fraudulent inducement “requires the plaintiff to show he incurred an out‑of‑pocket loss” (see **Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996)**).  This is the loss that connects the fraud to the damages the plaintiff claims.

### What the pleading must contain

| Element | What the complaint must allege | Court’s guidance |
|---------|-------------------------------|------------------|
| **Loss type** | The plaintiff must identify the loss as an *out‑of‑pocket* loss—money, property, or a direct expense that was paid. | “Santos failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud.” (Santos v. Kimmel, 2d Cir. 2025, § II, final paragraph.) |
| **Loss amount** | A specific dollar figure is **not required** at pleading stage.  The complaint may simply state “$X was lost” or “Santos suffered a loss of funds.” | The 2d Cir. opinion affirms the standard in *Lama* and *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017)* that the amount does not have to be exact. |
| **Connection to fraud** | The loss must be shown as a direct result of the defendant’s misrepresentation. | “…Santos’s fraudulent‐inducement claim for failure to allege any out‑of‑pocket loss as required under New York law.” |
| **Omitted or vague loss** | A statement such as “I was harmed” or “I suffered a loss” without any reference to a tangible, out‑of‑pocket loss is *insufficient* and can be struck under Rule 12(b)(6). | **Santos did not allege any type or amount of loss.** The appellate court therefore dismissed the claim as failing to state a cause of action. |

### Practical effect on pleadability

* **Missing the loss element is fatal.** A fraudulent‑inducement claim that does not allege an actual out‑of‑pocket loss falls outside New York’s statutory definition of the claim and is dismissed.  
* **A general “loss” can work if it is specific enough.** If the complaint says, “Santos lost $5,000 when the defendant misrepresented X,” the claim may stand. The exact number need not be perfected; the amount may be refined at trial.  
* **Intangible or speculative losses do not satisfy the requirement.** Failure to show a concrete out‑of‑pocket loss—e.g., “lost reputation” or “felt cheated”—would not survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.

**Bottom line:** In New York a fraudulent‑inducement claim must name an *actual* out‑of‑pocket loss. The amount may be left imprecise, but the complaint must identify the loss as a specific, tangible injury incurred because of the fraud. The ruling in *Santos v. Kimmel* demonstrates that a complaint that merely says the plaintiff was deceived, without further alleging a concrete loss, is insufficient and will be dismissed.

### How does the court interpret the market effect factor when the ‘second work’ offers a competing substitute?
Question:
In the fair‑use analysis, the court refers to the dictum that a second work must not 'usurp the market' for the original. Santos argued that the Kimmel videos might have some effect on the market for his videos. How do courts evaluate whether a derivative or derivative‑like use competes with the original, especially when no direct competition exists?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the court explains the market‑effect test in § I (4):

> “The fourth factor asks *not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute” (Court, ¶ ??).  

The court also notes that for a derivative use to “usurp” the market, it must **present a product that a consumer could purchase or consume in place of the original**. If the second work merely comments on, critiques, or lampoons the original and is not offered, marketed, or purchased as an alternative, it does **not** usurp the market.

In the instant case the Kimmel‑aired segments were a parody‑style critique of Santos’s videos. The court found that:

1. **No substitutive market existed.**  
   *“Santos has not suffered market harm … because we ask not … whether it usurps the market by offering a competing substitute.”* The segments were not sold or presented as an alternative to Santos’s own videos.

2. **Derivative works seldom create a licensing market.**  
   The court cited *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose* – “the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.” (Court, ¶ ??)

Because the Kimmel segments neither fill a market for Santos’s videos nor offer a substitute product, the fourth factor favors fair use. The court therefore concluded that Santos’s claim that the Kimmel videos “might have some effect on the market for his videos” is unpersuasive.

**Bottom line:** Courts assess the market‑effect factor by asking whether the second work *could* provide an alternate product that would replace the original. When there is no viable substitute—i.e., when the derivative is purely transformative, commentary, or critique—the factor leans toward fair use. This is precisely how the Second Circuit evaluated the Kimmel videos in *Santos*.
