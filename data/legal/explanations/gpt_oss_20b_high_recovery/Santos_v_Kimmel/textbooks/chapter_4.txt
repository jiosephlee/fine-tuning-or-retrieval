Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 4: # State‑Law Claims – Contract & Fraudulent Inducement

## Direct Breach of Cameo TOS – Non‑contracting third‑party beneficiary presumption  

In Santos’s direct breach claim, the plaintiff alleged that Jimmy Kimmel breached the Cameo Terms of Service when he produced the “Will Santos Say It?” segments and later aired them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (JKL). The core issue is whether Santos, a person who never entered the Cameo agreement, can invoke the Terms of Service as the basis for a remedy. Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service because they are a contract made in Illinois, provides a strong presumption against conferring benefits on third parties who are not expressly named in a contract. The appellate court therefore pointed to Barry v. St. Mary’s Hospital, which establishes that a non‑contracting party must rely on an express declaration that the contract’s terms are intended to benefit him. Santos offered no such declaration or any provision in the Terms of Service that expressly or implicitly designates him as a beneficiary. Consequently, the court held that the plaintiff could not bring a third‑party‑beneficiary claim, and the district court’s dismissal on this ground was correct.

## Implied‑Contract Claim – Missing essential terms and lack of meeting of minds  

Santos also pursued an implied‑contract claim directly against Kimmel and the involved broadcasting companies. Under New York law, which governs this action, a plaintiff must plead, in non‑conclusory language, the essential terms of the alleged contract—price, subject matter, and the promises of each party. In addition, the claim requires proof of a meeting of the minds, i.e., mutual assent to those terms. The court found that Santos’s complaint failed to identify or describe any of the alleged contract’s essential terms. He also did not allege any conduct, communication, or agreement that would indicate a mutual assent between the parties. Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Group illustrates that a claim lacking such essential terms does not state a cause of action. Accordingly, the appellate court affirmed the district court’s dismissal of the implied‑contract claim for failure to state a claim.

## Preemption Discussion – Copyright preemption raised but not pursued  

During the lower‑court proceedings the district court considered that Santos’s contract claims might be preempted by the Copyright Act. The appellate court noted that the issue of preemption is one that the court would ordinarily analyze if the plaintiff’s claims survived the pleading analysis. However, because the plaintiff’s contractual claims were found deficient by violation of both contract doctrine and pleading standards, the appellate court elected not to resolve the preemption question. The court’s statement that it “does not need to resolve preemption because each of the claims fails to state a claim” reflects the principle that a claim that is already deemed defective does not merit further examination on a secondary ground such as federal preemption.

## Fraudulent Inducement – Requirement of out‑of‑pocket loss not alleged  

Santos also asserted a fraudulent‑inducement claim under New York law, arguing that Kimmel’s false representations about the videos induced him to pay for them. New York’s fraudulent‑inducement causes require the plaintiff to plead an actual out‑of‑pocket loss incurred as a result of the defendant’s acts. The court cited Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney and Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill to emphasize that a fraud claim must allege that the plaintiff suffered a concrete, direct loss—whether monetary or otherwise. Because Santos’s complaint did not specify any loss or financial harm, the court found the claim insufficient. This matched the district court’s dismissal and was likewise affirmed on appeal.

## Case Law Illustrations – Barry, Rocky Point Props, Lama Holding, Connaughton  

The appellate court strategically invoked four key cases to support its conclusions. Barry v. St. Mary’s Hospital clarified the presumption against third‑party beneficiaries; Rocky Point Props. underscored the necessity of pleading essential contract terms and a meeting of minds; Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney and Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill illustrated the out‑of‑pocket‑loss requirement for fraud claims. By applying these precedents to the facts in the Santos case, the court illustrated how established doctrines either preclude the claims or bar them for failure to allege required elements.

## Summary – Claims do not state a prima facie cause of action  

For all of the reasons discussed, the state‑law claims that Santos brought—direct breach of a non‑contracting third‑party, implied contract, and fraudulent inducement—each failed to plead a viable cause of action. The distinct legal problems in each category required specific elements—beneficiary status, essential contract terms and mutual assent, and out‑of‑pocket loss—that Santos did not provide. The court’s analysis, grounded in Illinois and New York contract doctrines and in the statutory and case‑law requirements for fraud, explains why each claim was dismissed and why the appellate court reversed the lower court’s determinations on no other legal ground.
