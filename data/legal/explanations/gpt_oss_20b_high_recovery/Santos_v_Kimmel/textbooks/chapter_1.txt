Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties Involved: George Santos, Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, Disney  
The plaintiff is former United States Representative **George Santos**, who brought federal copyright and state‑law claims against **Jimmy Kimmel** (host of ABC’s *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* “JKL”), **ABC Television** (the network that airs JKL), and **The Walt Disney Company**, the corporate parent of ABC. Santos asserts that Kimmel, ABC, and Disney used his copyrighted videos without permission, thereby infringing his exclusive rights.

## Cameo Requests and Santos’s Video Production  
Through the online platform Cameo, Kimmel contacted Santos under fictitious pseudonyms and asked him to produce a series of personalized video messages. Santos complied with each request, filming the videos that became the subject of the dispute. These videos were the copyrighted works that Santos claimed were protected under the Copyright Act.

## Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” Segment and use of the videos  
Kimmel subsequently incorporated the videos into a recurring segment on JKL titled **“Will Santos Say It?”** The segment aired the videos for comedic, satirical commentary, positioning them as mockery of Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. The use was public and broadcast, and Kimmel asserted that it conferred First‑Amendment protection.

## Claims Raised by Santos: copyright infringement and state‑law claims (breach, implied contract, fraudulent inducement)  
Santos’s complaint alleged that Kimmel’s broadcast constituted **copyright infringement**, accusing the defendants of using his video messages without consent. The complaint also asserted three independent state‑law claims:  
1. **Breach of contract** under the Cameo Terms of Service;  
2. **Breach of an implied contract**; and  
3. **Fraudulent inducement**, alleging that Kimmel misrepresented the nature of the videos for financial gain.  
Santos contended that the defendants violated these contractual obligations and deceived him.

## District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal and fair‑use rationale  
The U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York dismissed the complaint entirely under **Rule 12(b)(6)**, finding that Santos’s copyright claims were barred by the **fair‑use doctrine**. The court analyzed the four statutory factors:  
* **Purpose and character** – the court regarded Kimmel’s use as *transformative*, employing the videos for satirical commentary rather than reproducing them wholesale.  
* **Nature of the copyrighted work** – the original videos were factual (in the sense that they captured Santos’s likeness and performance) and thus less protected for this purpose.  
* **Amount and substantiality** – the use was limited to the portions relevant for the satire.  
* **Effect on the market** – the segment did not serve as a substitute for the original works and did not harm their market potential.  

On these grounds, the court concluded that a fair‑use defense was adequate at the pleading stage. The court also dismissed the state‑law claims, holding that either they were *preempted by the Copyright Act* or that Santos failed to plead a foundation for them.  The district court entered a **final judgment of dismissal**.

## Appeal filing, docket, and review procedure  
Santos appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit on **October 6 2025**, docket number **No. 24‑2196‑cv**. The appellate court applied the **de‑novo** jurisprudence set forth in *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.* and reviewed the pleadings and the district court’s fair‑use analysis. Judge Lohier, writing for the panel, found no reversible error. The Court affirmed the district court’s dismissal, holding that Santos’s claims failed to meet the pleading standard and that the fair‑use defense was sufficiently established in the record.
