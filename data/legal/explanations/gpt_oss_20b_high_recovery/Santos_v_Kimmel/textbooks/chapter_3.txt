Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: ## Copyright Infringement and Fair‑Use Analysis

The Second Circuit’s review of Santos v. Kimmel focused on whether the District Court properly dismissed Santos’s copyright‑infringement claim at the pleading stage on the affirmative defense of fair use. The court’s analysis was structured around the four statutory fair‑use factors, supplemented by an examination of alleged bad‑faith conduct. The findings concluded that each factor favored a finding that the First‑party use—Kimmel’s broadcast of the videos—was a fair use, and therefore the claim should be dismissed.

### Purpose and Character – The Transformative Nature of Kimmel’s Satirical Critique

The district and appellate courts applied the Supreme Court’s standard that the “purpose and character” of a use is judged on its *transformative* nature (Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F. 4th 26). The court explained that "the purpose must add something new … with a further purpose or different character." The claim’s *transformative* element was clear: Kimmel’s segments titled “Will Santos Say It?” were not merely reproductions of Santos’s original videos; they were embedded in a broader satirical program that critiqued the former congressman’s willingness to speak absurdly for monetary gain. Santos “does not dispute the District Court’s finding that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was ‘to comment on the willingness of Santos…to say absurd things for money.’” The court noted that a reasonable observer, not the parties’ subjective intent, dictates transformative status. As the complaint itself acknowledged, “a reasonable observer … would think the videos conveyed feelings of hope, … positivity,” not merely a platform for Kimmel’s commentary. Thus, the first factor overwhelmingly supported fair use.

### Nature of the Work – Creative Status of the Videos and Simultaneous Publication

For the “nature of the work” factor, the judges referenced the statutory definition that a copyright protects “original works of authorship” (17 U.S.C. § 101). The court observed that the videos were “self‑published” on Cameo the same day they were created, thereby satisfying the originality requirement. The videos were also registered with the Copyright Office at the time of publication; the opinion cited the defendants’ exhibits and noted that “the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created.” Since the works were fully original, the second factor did not lean against fair use.

### Amount and Substantiality – Inclusion of the Entire Video and Copyright Registration

The third factor examines how much of the original work was used. The appellate court acknowledged that Kimmel broadcast the *entire* video. While “the use of the entire work normally disfavors a finding of fair use,” the court held that the amount was *necessary* for the purpose of commentary, fitting the “required and appropriate amount” test. In addition, the court noted that the videos were “copyright‑registered” in a single registration covering the entire work; thus, the second factor influences the third. Because the defendant’s use was integral to the commentary, the court considered the use justified under the amount factor.

### Market Effect – No Sign that Kimmel’s Segment Usurps Santos’s Market

Under the fourth fair‑use factor, the court required evidence that the defendant’s use adversely affected the original’s market. The district court’s finding that Kimmel’s use did not supplant the videos “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute” was affirmed. The court explained that “the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market” (Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569). The fact that the videos were shown as part of a satirical series, not a marketable product, meant there was no plausible market harm.

### Bad‑Faith Allegations – Questionability under Supreme Court Evolution and Case Law

Santos attempted to defeat fair use by raising Kimmel’s deceptive conduct on Cameo. The court traced the Supreme Court’s history: while Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises stated that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” (though the Court has since “distanced itself from this statement”), subsequent decisions have limited the reach of bad‑faith as a defense. The appellate court rejected Santos’s claim that Kimmel’s “false representations demonstrate bad faith and thus nullify the fair‑use defense.” The court viewed that Santos’s complaint lacked evidence of Kimmel’s intent to supplant the videos, and that “the complaint paints a portrait of defendants motivated by criticism, not commercial appropriation.” Accordingly, the bad‑faith argument could not defeat the first‑factor finding.

### Precedential Support – Andy Warhol, TCA Television, Harper & Row, and Others

The court drew heavily on try‑but‑not‑quite‑clear precedent. Andy Warhol found it “so clearly established by a complaint … to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim at the pleading stage” (TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F. 3d 168). The appellate court noted that “the first factor strongly supports a finding of fair use” and that this resolution is consistent with the standard approach in earlier cases, such as Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F. 4th 163. These authorities underlined the shift toward a flexible, context‑dependent analysis, guiding the court’s conclusion that the claim was dismissed without further fact‑finding.

### Court’s Finding – Fair Use Bars the Infringement Claim at the Pleading Stage

In sum, the appellate court affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of the copyright‑infringement claim. The court found that each of the four statutory fair‑use factors – transformation, nature, amount, and market effect – supported a conclusion that Kimmel’s use was protected. Bad‑faith allegations offered no substantive basis to rebut the first factor. Consequently, the court held that the claim failed at the pleading stage and was rightly dismissed.
