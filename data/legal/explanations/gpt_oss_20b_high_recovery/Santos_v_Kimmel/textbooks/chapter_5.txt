Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 5: # Holding, Significance, and Future Lessons

## Final Holding – All claims dismissed; fair use prevails; state‑law claims fail to state  
In the Ninth‑day opinion, Judge Lohier affirmed the Southern District of New York’s dismissal of entirely the copyright infringement claim and, likewise, of each state‑law claim filed by former congressman George Santos.  The appellate court held that the videos streamed on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* constituted a transformative use that fell within the fair‑use defense, and it found that Santos had not complied with the pleading requirements necessary to sustain any of the state‑law remedies.  The decision, therefore, confirms that the district court’s final judgment stands in its entirety.  

## Implications for Fair‑Use Doctrine – Expansion to television parody and satire  
Santos v. Kimmel illustrates the continued expansion of fair use to the realm of televised parody.  The court reiterated that the key test is whether a *reasonable observer* would perceive the new work as adding “new expression or meaning” rather than merely superseding the original.  Because Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segments were “commentary and criticism” rather than an attempt to replace the original videos, the first fair‑use factor was deemed favorably.  The decision confirms that satirical television programs may air short excerpts of copyrighted material so long as the use is clearly transformatively framed and the other fair‑use factors (nature, amount, and market impact) do not negate the defense.  

## Third‑Party Beneficiary Clarification – Conditions for benefit under service contracts  
In addressing Santos’s breach‑of‑contract allegation against the Cameo Terms of Service, the court made clear that a non‑contracting third party cannot invoke a contract that was drafted strictly between the platform and the content creator.  Under Illinois law, the court applies a *strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties* unless an express or very clear notwithstanding provision exists.  New York’s third‑party beneficiary test likewise demands the pleadings contain the essential terms of the alleged contract, an element Santos did not furnish.  The opinion thereby clarifies that a mere request for a personalized video does not automatically create a contractual relationship with the platform, and that courts will not infer such a relationship absent explicit contractual language.  

## Preemption Effects – Interaction between copyright and state contracts  
Santos’s claims for breach of contract and fraudulent inducement were dismissed partially on the basis of preemption.  The court reiterated that the Copyright Act preempts any state‑law claim that duplicates the protections afforded by federal copyright (see *Melendez*).  Because the alleged breach involved the transmission of a copyrighted performance, the state‑law claim was effectively barred by the Copyright Act’s preemptive authority.  Appellate judges should therefore scrutinize any state‑law counter‑claims that, even when couched in contract or tort language, recast a federally protected right.  

## Pleading Strategy – Necessity of stating essential elements and damages  
The *Santos* decision serves as a cautionary example of how failure to plead as an essential element can derail a claim.  For breach of contract, the plaintiff must allege the existence of an *express* or *implied* agreement and provide its specific terms; any absence of the latter renders the claim defective at the pleading stage.  For fraudulent inducement, New York law requires the complaint to allege *actual out‑of‑pocket loss*; Santos’s omission precluded liability.  These standard pleading requirements, grounded in precedent (e.g., *Caniglia* and *Lama Holding*), are as critical in copyright cases as they are in broader civil litigation.  

## Practical Take‑aways for Copyright Litigation  
1. **Fair‑use analysis is anchored in the observer test for transformation**; craft pleadings that articulate the new purpose of the use in plain language.  
2. **Avoid relying on implied third‑party benefits** in service‑contract disputes; secure an explicit contractual element or a clearly stated third‑party beneficiary clause.  
3. **Be mindful of preemption**: any claim that attempts to address a federal copyright right must demonstrate a unique state‑law remediable policy not covered by the Copyright Act.  
4. **State‑law claims must be fully developed**: plead essential contract terms, and when alleging fraud, clearly describe actual damages or loss.  
5. **Keep the oral and textual evidence documented** so that the transformation and market‑impact factors can be convincingly asserted.  

By internalizing these principles, law students and litigants can craft more robust pleadings and anticipate how appellate courts will evaluate such claims in the evolving tapestry of copyright law.
