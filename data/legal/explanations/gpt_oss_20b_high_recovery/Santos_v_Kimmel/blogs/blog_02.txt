Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The Lost Loss: Why Santos’s Fraudulent Inducement Claim Failed

The Second Circuit’s October 6, 2025 decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* may at first glance seem like another routine dismissal of a high‑profile plaintiff’s misdirected grievances. Yet, tucked among the standard APA‑style discussion of fair‑use parody and contract preemption, the Court delivers a dose of procedural clarity that will reverberate for anyone who pens a complaint against a celebrity, a platform, or a public figure on grounds that a “false statement” induced them to enter into a bargain. The Court’s insistence that a fraudulent‑inducement claim must allege a specific out‑of‑pocket loss is the fulcrum of the ruling, and it signals a stubbornly literal reading of New York’s tort codification that will humble fan‑based litigation for years to come.

## Why This Case Matters

Santos, a former congressman with a season of political theater on his record, sued Jimmy Kimmel and ABC after Kimmel hijacked a series of Cameo videos that Santos authored for “personalized” requests posed under altered names. While the seemingly dramatic heart of the case concerns whether Kimmel’s televised use of the videos violated Santos’s copyrights, the Court’s decision on the fraudulent‑inducement claim teaches a much stricter rule for tort plaintiffs: it is not enough to show that a defendant’s misrepresentation embarrassed or offended a plaintiff. A green‑lit cause of action hangs on an unequivocal out‑of‑pocket loss, a standard that New York law—and the Second Circuit—take very seriously. The ruling therefore provides the next benchmark for litigious fans who fear that a cameo, meme, or viral skit could be a form of fraud or bait‑and‑switch.

## New York's Fraudulent Inducement Doctrine

Fraudulent inducement in New York is not a mere abstract nuisance; it is a strict tort that demands a concrete loss. The jurisprudence is contained in *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney, Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996) and *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Both cases emphasize that the alleged fraud must result in a “specific out‑of‑pocket loss,” translating into a demonstrable dollar figure that the plaintiff actually suffered. The standard explicitly excludes reputational or mental‑health harms unless those pains can be tied to a direct economic upset—such as a missed contract, a declined opportunity, or an explicit cost incurred in the fraud’s wake.

In the *Santos* case, the defendant Kimmel’s false representations were framed by Santos as “misleading” requests that induced him to create the videos. He alleged, briefly, that he was “induced to supply these videos” on the basis of deceptive leads from the host. The complaint, however, stops short of articulating any dollar loss. No money was spent on production fees beyond slotted video consultants. No licensing revenue was sacrificed. No concrete contract was broken by Kimmel’s demands; instead, the harm he declared was chiefly humiliation and emotional distress at being mocked on national television.

The Second Circuit’s notice, “Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim failed to allege any out‑of‑pocket loss,” shows full compliance with the New York statute. The Court does not reach a second‑level analysis of the alleged misrepresentation itself; it simply notes that a prudent view of the pleadings reveals a failure to satisfy the headline prerequisite. As the Court states, “We agree with the District Court’s conclusion that Santos failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud.”

## The Court’s Analytical Path

The appellate court guided its decision through the factual matrix possessed in the complaint and the procedural requirements of Rule 12(b)(6). Judge Lohier, writing for the panel, underscored the Court’s adherence to New York’s statutory demands. In a nutshell, the Court’s reasoning stops at the pleading stage and refuses to “reverse an engineering of the claim” that would let a plaintiff’s intangible picture of frustration become a tort remedy.

Two points deserve attention. First, the Court notes that *Santos’s* allegations do not plainly describe a failure to pay for an alleged service, a refusal to accept an offer, or a loss of a bank deposit that would have resulted from Kimmel’s misrepresentations. Second, the Court explicitly cites *Connaughton v. Chipotle*, pointing out that a “failure to allege actual out‑of‑pocket loss” is a legal bar to rescue a claim. In short, the argument boiled down to a pleading‑failure.

Had the complaint had included, for instance, that Santos “paid a $350 fee to a filmmaker to produce the custom cameos” or that “his performance would have reached 500,000 viewers—forever on the set—to know that he “lost that exposure” and thus for the first time “his market value,” the court may not have been able to deny the fraud claim at this juncture. The point is that the underlying law does not consider intangible losses as adequate.

## Implications for Upcoming Fan‑Based Misrepresentation Litigations

The case has an outsized effect for several classes of litigants:

1. **Emerging Influencers and Micro‑Creators**  
   Many young creators rely on short‑form videos and paid personal messages as a hobby, not an income stream. When a celebrity misrepresents a brand or a platform misleads them about the terms of a contract, the affected party often does not have a clear money trail. Under *Santos*, such a plaintiff would find themselves sinking in the sea of the “out‑of‑pocket loss” exception.

2. **“Bait‑and‑Switch” Claims Against Brands**  
   A fan may sue a sponsor that promised free gifts or paid discussions but delivered something entirely different. The plaintiffs would have to prove that they were out‑of‑pocket when they signed up, that the sponsor’s misstatement cost them money, or that they paid for the opportunity under false pretenses that later led to lost revenue. Without an expressive loss, the claim will likely fall flat at the pleading stage.

3. **Public‑Figure Satire and the “First Amendment” Shield**  
   The person whose frivolous complaint was based on humiliating parody does not get luxury. The *Santos* ruling codifies the flat out‑of‑pocket requirement, thus limiting scope of franchise that satirists enjoy. A reflection: the result is a “soft pad” for First Amendment rights because a pure, unmitigated failure to claim a putative loss means even highly damaging ads are unactionable.

4. **SNS/Video‑Streaming Platforms' “Terms of Service” Claims**  
   The *Santos* Court’s refusal to treat a Cameo Terms of Service breach as a contracted obligation reinforces the point that third parties to a platform do not automatically step into the contract. The requirement to prove out‑of‑pocket loss becomes a double hurdle: first you must show you are a party (or a third‑party beneficiary) to a terms agreement; second you must prove out‑of‑pocket loss under fraud. The combination significantly deters litigants who are operating outside the platform’s commerce.

In sum, the ruling filters out a wave of potential suits that, while conceptually attractive, lack an actionable loss metric. These lawsuits are more likely to be dismissed at the pleading stage, precisely because, in the absence of a dollar loss, the court has no legitimate basis to interpret the alleged misrepresentation as tortious fraud.

## The Intersection of Copyright, Contract and Tort

Although the fraudulent‑inducement claim is the sort of claim that is glaringly dominated by your loss, the *Santos* decision illustrates a broader theme: lower‑court dismissals of specialized ground claims are often enmeshed with doctrines that reach beyond their narrow focus. In the same opinion, the Court simultaneously affirmed the dismissal of the copyright claim (which had been collection of non‑transformative use) as a fair‑use defense and of the contract claims based on pre‑emption and non‑partnership. The simultaneity of these dismissals is a clarion reminder that appellate courts shun “separate” technicalities for plaintiffs who do not show the concrete damages demanded by the underlying statutes.

This relates to the context of fan‑generated content: the court effectively reminded litigants that mere “creative work” cannot be a ground for liability unless it bears a recognized economic market that can be undermined by a defendant’s use. Likewise, contractual liabilities only arise in a world where there's an enforceable contractual relationship—with a clear subjective expectation of benefits or grants. The out‑of‑pocket requirement for fraud stands as a third, independent gatekeeper that operates only where the wrongmed plutory record records a measurable loss.

## Crafting a Claim that Survives an “Out‑of‑Pocket” Litmus Test

If fans believe they have a fraudulent‑inducement story to share, what steps might help them cross the threshold? Three general guidelines surface from the *Santos* case:

1. **Quantify the Direct Financial Loss**  
   Identify tangible fiscal disances that happened because of the misrepresentation—fees paid, contracts rejected, potential sales lost. Paint a clear picture straight from the ledger. The more granular (weekly, monthly figures), the easier the Court will find a concrete out‑of‑pocket loss.

2. **Illustrate the Causal Chain**  
   Connect the misrepresentation to the loss by documenting, e.g., that the plaintiff was told to sign a contract for a cameo a certain size, paid an upset or a fee, but received nothing; the shortage in revenue is directly traceable.

3. **Avoid “Intangible” Compassion**  
   Emotional or reputational frustration is an empty field in New York law unless directly experiential As e.g., “I lost this consumer contract we had in the pipeline before the misrepresentation.” only if that contract or opportunity had a clear dollar value.

A failure to stick to that formula, as the *Santos* complaint did—relying on lesson over money—renders the action time‑bomb.

## The Precedential Footprint of *Santos*

The Second Circuit’s reaffirmation elucidates that the New York out‑of‑pocket rule is not a mere palatable concession but a core doctrine that will accompany other contexts: a celebrity’s antics may be vivid, but to turn them into a tort you must show that you pay for it. The *Santos* decision also indicates that appellate minds will be firm on this line: “A fraud claim that never states a loss is just a story; courts do not entertain it.” This principle offers a solid doctrinal anchor for future litigants.

The ruling may inspire litigants in other circuits, or in jurisdictions with similar statutes, to transition their claims toward defamation or emotional‑distress suits, where the loss threshold is lower (though still requiring proof of damage). In short: *Santos* does not close the dog‑pit of misrepresentation; it ticks the speed limit. The road to vindication now demands that plaintiffs be ready to credibly exhibit the measurable hurt that any misled protagonist suffers.

## Bottom Line

*Santos v. Kimmel* throws a glaring spotlight on the “lost loss” that New York law requires for a fraudulent‑inducement claim. For fans and creators poised to file suit over a Twitter lip‑synchronization or a late‑night mockery on Kimmel, the ruling offers a cautionary check: if you can’t show a specific out‑of‑pocket loss in the cause of action, you’re likely going to see the court dismiss the claim as inchoate.

The significance of this case extends beyond the particulars of a former congressman. It offers both a doctrinal fulcrum and a practical rulebook. Courts across the nation require that your misrepresentation has spilled over into a real, quantifiable loss. Without that, all the emotional dissatisfaction you may feel or the unfortunate platform in which your content was broadcast, the court will consider an insufficient causal foundation for fraud. And in a world where influencers increasingly barter on free‑form requests and paid social ads, *Santos* may well be the point before the debate over satire and the “right of the public figure” reaches daylight.
