Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Good Faith, Bad Faith, and the Fair‑Use Shield: Santos v. Kimmel’s Bad‑Faith Argument

## Why This Matter

When a sitting congressman, former Representative George Santos, sued a late‑night host for incorporating user‑generated footage into a “mock‑parody” segment, the litigation sheared a slice of the public‑interest bar that protects satire and social commentary. Santos’ appeal hinges on one line in the 2nd Circuit opinion: Kimmel’s false representations of Santos “nullify” the fair‑use defense. The court rejected that claim, but the way it balanced the presumption of “good faith” against the realities of parody reframes an old knot in copyright jurisprudence. In a media climate where “fictitious personas” and “implied celebrity endorsements” are becoming budget‑friendly promotional tools, how far does the fair‑use shield stretch when the material is obviously a spoof?

## Good Faith and Fair Use

The Supreme Court has long held that fair use is an affirmative defense, not a bare‑bones sound‑bite. *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539 (1985)*, acknowledged that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” That wording, however, never evolved into a hard plug for or against a defendant’s case. Lower courts have repeatedly clarified that the presumption of good faith is not a litmus test that, if broken, de‑legitimizes a fair‑use argument. The *Harper & Row* comment was, in the Court’s own words, “an observation about the nature of the defense,” not a doctrinal bar.

The Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel* affirmed that good faith is a **presumption**, not a gospel. The judge stated that Kimmel’s portrayal of Santos as “a man who is willing to say absurd things for money” was part of a satirical willingness to lampoon public figures—an art form that the law has recognized as “transformative.” *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26 (2d Cir. 2021)*, noted that the key screen is whether a “reasonable observer” can discern that the new work adds meaning or message. The court found that a reasonable observer would see the segment as a piece of commentary rather than an attempt to mislead the audience about the real Santos or about the authenticity of the videos. 

The presumption of good faith, therefore, resides in the initial stage of the fair‑use analysis. It is not a doorway to the four‑factor calculation; it is more like the light on a traffic signal. If you’re running a red light and are physically restrained from crossing, the law doesn’t let you slip out of that rule by citing a “precedent” that says “you do not violate the rule if you intend to scare people.” Likewise, good faith cannot be used to host a denial of the four‑factors. But if the defendant is acting with a clear, honest intent—to critique the idea, or to transform the content into something new—the presumption of good faith can be invoked as the starting point for a robust fair‑use analysis.

## The Court’s Treatment of the Bad‑Faith Argument

Santos argued that the false, fabricated nature of the cameo videos constituted “bad faith” because Kimmel “made no effort to be truthful about who was recording the footage.” Santos presented the false videos as a final product, with Kimmel airing them and then calling them “absurd pieces.” The court, however, found that the depiction was fundamentally a *purposeful exaggeration* that belongs to the tradition of comedic caricature. Under the clarifications in *Harper & Row* and its successors, the transformation is fairly robust: what the videos originally displayed—Santos’ own recorded footage—was repurposed for political satire, a well‑trod free‑speech activity that the doctrine has always encouraged.

Moreover, the court noted that *bad faith* under copyright law is most commonly tied to *willful infringement*—an intentional breach of statutory or contractual rights that can trigger punitive damages, not a blanket rejection of fair use. This is the same principle that appears in *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294 (2d Cir. 2022)*, where the Court held that the “bad faith” claim was separate from infringement. The *Santos* ruling did not rely on the “bad‑faith” allegation as a means to nullify the fair‑use defense; instead it leaned on the broader public‑interest purpose and the transformative nature added by the comedic framing. 

In essence, the court said: *You can use false or exaggerated representations as long as they serve a new, communicative purpose that is not to defraud or mislead.* That is the heart of the fair‑use doctrine as applied to parodies. *Harper & Row*'s language about good faith is an unexplored territory; no new standard swoops in to override the four‑factor analysis simply because a depiction was factually false.

## *Harper & Row* and the Presumption of Good Faith

It’s instructive to consider *Harper & Row* in its proper context. The case was decided in 1985, centered on the injunction the Publishers’ Association sought against the *Nation* in disseminating *Harper & Row* books. The Court said the fair‑use defense “but does not remove that assumption of fair dealing.” The Court turned the phrase “presupposes good faith” into a starting point for the analysis, not a damning conclusion. Over the years, courts like the **Second Circuit** have asserted that the motto is “good faith is a presumptive building block.” 

Indeed, the *Santos* opinion even cites *Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive*, *115 F.4th 163 (2d Cir. 2024)*, to emphasize that prior rights holders must be treated “genuinely, not after the fact.” The court held that the courts differ in treating *bad faith* as a factor: if the use is meant purely to hurt or to plagiarize, it can bite full damages. But wanting to lift a parody or comedic commentary that makes the viewer “recognize the new, different purpose” stays within the safe corridor of fair use. The fidelity to *Harper & Row* is thus consistent with a jurisprudence that treats good faith and bad faith as lenses, not shield‑bending cogs.

## Satire, Fiction, and the Boundaries of Fair Use

*Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?”* series was a manufactured aesthetic: a fictionalized version of the congressman, terrifying in its absurdity. However, the 2nd Circuit’s decision emphasized that satire’s unique nature is built on the disposable use of existing ideas, amplified for a different purpose. *Coward v. Debatis* et al. might recall the Supreme Court’s 2010 holding that parody constitutes a “unique form” of expression, deserving the breadth of the First Amendment. The *Santos* case continues that trend: the court reaffirmed that a “fictitious persona” used to parody an office holder still sits squarely within the realm where transformative use mixes with fair use.

The court’s note on “bad faith” shows that the *Santos* case does not think false representations automatically produce a lie-for-defamation. The claim, in the eyes of the court, was that Santos was misrepresented by the new material. However, the *Santos* case also said a second factor, “the nature of the copyrighted work,” was historical: a series of improperly cultivated, presumably lean, videos that also became the object of Kimmel’s parody. *Santos'* interpretation recognized that these videos were “the primary material used in parody,” and that the claim was that they were not used to mislead but to poke fun. Thus, the only presumption that could come into play was for *good faith* at the beginning of the analysis, which in turn positioned the four‑factor race to continue on a safe path.

## Wider Implications for Platforms That Lean on Fictional Personas

“So what does all this mean for YouTubers, meme‑makers, and the next generation of “influencer comedians”?” Some who alleged was that the case simply re‑affirmed that one can *fake* a person or a brand as long as the portrayal is commentary and *not* an attempt to con the audience into believing a false fact. 

From a **copyright** perspective, the decision signals that the “additional room afforded to a creator to alter or transform content,” whether the transformation is simply news commentary or a fictional comedic narrative, enjoys unsurpassed flexibility. It also sets a precedent that the *transformation test* will still apply even when the subject is a fictionalized version of a real person. That allows platforms to build “customized clips” around public figures without having to fear a “bad faith” flag that might bar fair use.

In a practical sense, the ruling means that online platforms can keep offering customized joke videos for a fee, because a violin of deep‑fake or accumulated out‑of‑character clips still falls under the transformative ambit if the content is a satirical commentary. And because *Santos* made clear that good faith remains a presumptive notion, the onus remains on any litigant who thinks their use was a false representation to show otherwise. This nuance cleverly preserves the **defensive pitch** of the fair‑use defense while not abandoning the mechanisms that help preserve the economic welfare of the original creators, a delicate balance that copyright courts have long favoured.

Of course, it won’t be a vacation for all content creators. The *Santos* decision does not say that simply blowing a paper balloon around a real person makes a free‑speech statement immune to all claims. If a user professional or a platform tries to misrepresent a person in a way that harms that person’s reputation or a brand’s commercial viability, the analysis may still weigh all four factors differently, and look for *bad faith* or *defamation*. Yet as far as the Supreme Court’s own desire to keep transformations alive in the digital age goes, this case looks like a sign that the law is content to let people remix and parody even if the “facade” is false.

### Defamation, Fact, and the Role of Fiction

There are legitimate concerns about conflating *defamation* with *copyright*. *Santos* does not address defamation directly, but it underscores that the “false representation” argument is not “enough” to destroy a fair‑use defense. The only proper way to bring a defamation claim is to show that the denial of the present reality was *factual*, not comedic. That said, the law still reserves the opportunity to hold a defamed figure liable when the depiction is on their behalf, especially if the portrayal goes beyond the permissible “joking” category.

If we look at *Windham v. Castronovo*, a case that conjoined defamation and copyright, we see how the courts keep the two prongs distinct: a broadcast that truly misrepresents a factual statement is not found to be in the realm of fair use. The *Santos* court made it clear: it is not Kimmel’s verbal misrepresentations that break the law; it is *the expectation* that the audience understand the piece as a parody. If the audience is misled into thinking the fictional persona is the real Santos, a defamation concern may swirl. But that would be a distinct claim; *Santos* did not then ask for a defamation remedy—though, in the future, this could be a fighting point.

## Bottom Line

Santos’ attempt to have false representations of a "fictional" Trump-like figure eliminate the fair‑use defense is consistent with how the *Harper & Row* pronouncement has been interpreted: the “presumption of good faith” is a helpful assumption but not a blanket sedation. The 2nd Circuit emphasizes that transformative use stands on its head when you incorporate an original piece into a new, commentary‑driven format—even if the ending comments say the woodwork was done “for pay.” The court’s ruling fortifies the principle that a satirist can legitimately misrepresent or exaggerate a public figure without tripping a copyright blue‑card, so long as the mockery is transformative.

What this means for content creators is that, when building a persona or a comedic skit around a real person, the transformed work can stand firmly in the safe harbor of fair use, even when the portrayal is radically fabricated. It also highlights the continuing flexibility of the four‑factor framework: the law is not a conservative engine that will stop the press of a comedic knockout if it is unrelated to *trademark*, *fraud*, or *defamation*. 

In the end, *Santos* illustrates that the moral compass guiding *fair use* has always been held not by the smallest actors but by the broader questions of **transformation, public‑interest purpose, and repeated commentary**. It doesn't, however, rule out that *true bad faith*—for example, the fraudulently misaligned use of a copyrighted piece with an intent to sabotage the rights holder—can still be a relevant factor, especially when the misuse is pointed beyond art and satire. But for those looking to keep their brand on a frayed but protective “fair‑use” rope, a good‑faith, transformed parody will always be a viable path—regardless of whether the target admittedly embraced the role.
