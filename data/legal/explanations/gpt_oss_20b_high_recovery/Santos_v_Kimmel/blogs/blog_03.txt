Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# When Copyright Blocks State Law: The Preemption of Santos’s Breach Claims

## Why This Matters
In a curious mash‑up of parody, royalty, and platforms, a former congressman tried to force his late‑night nemesis to give back a “little video gift” that had become a viral hoax. George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney for copyright infringement and a trove of state‑law claims—including breach of contract on the Cameo platform, an implied‑contract breach, and fraudulent inducement. The 2nd Circuit’s October 6 opinion affirms the district court’s dismissal of all claims. While the court spent most of its analysis arguing that the videos were fair use, it left a clear door open for a preemption line that may rip out future state‑law recourse in similar disputes. That doctrine—rooted in the Copyright Act’s mandate that its “exclusive rights” be executively operative—wouldatically close any gap that a plaintiff might hope to exploit using contract‑oriented or tortious primitives.

In a world where “copy‑and‑paste” has become the kind of everyday commerce we cannot ignore, the line that the 2nd Circuit draws between an author’s creative control and the reaches of state law becomes of headline significance. If the claim is that a contract was breached, and that breach arises from the same act that triggers a copyright claim, can a creator’s body of damages shift between federal and state courts? Santos v. Kimmel shows that, at least in the 2nd Circuit, the answer is a hard no.

## Quick Recap of the Copyright Decision
Before unpacking the preemption angle, the court briefly reminded us that the videos were exercised to “comment on the willingness of Santos to say absurd things for money.” The 2nd Circuit, following *Hachette Book Group* and *Andy Warhol Foundation*, underscored that “purpose and character” of the use must be judged by an outside observer, not by subjective intent. Santos’s own admission that the videos proved “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity” stood dwarfed a claim of good‑faith breach in the eyes of the court.

The court concluded that the videotaped invitation was fair use: the copyrighted “original works” were used in a transformed, non‑replacing way that did not offer a competing substitute for the original. The *fourth* factor—market effect— was also nil; a parody or lampoon is not a viable substitute for the original. Thus the holding was clear: Santos’s copyright claim failed at the pleading stage. 

## The State‑Law Claims That Were Tossed
State‑law claims were drowned in the early part of the opinion. Santos also asserted:

1. **Breach of the Cameo Terms of Service.** He argued that Kimmel’s fulfillment of his request was a contractual breach because the Cameo Terms, which Kimmel had supposedly agreed to, were violative.

2. **Implied‑contract breach.** Santos alleged that an implied‑facts agreement existed, tied to the same Claro blunt exchange.

3. **Fraudulent inducement.** He contended that Kimmel misrepresented his willingness to perform, subverting Santos’s trust.

The appellate court first questioned whether these claims were eligible for federal preemption. “We need not resolve this issue because Santos’s state‑law contract claims failed to state a claim for relief under state law,” the opinion states, pointing to the deficiencies in the pleadings. Still, the preemption issue stands then – especially on the contractual front.   
 
## The Preemption Argument Faced by the Court
The Copyright Act contains a precise, “de‑facto” preemption tool: 17 U.S.C. § 101 preempts “any laws or regulations that regulate the making, distribution, or performance of the kind of work covered by this Act” (condensed for brevity). Earlier 2nd Circuit case law has repeatedly treated claims that hinge on the same underlying facts that give rise to an exclusive copyright right as falling neatly within that preemption sphere.

Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claim depended on the notion that he was “benefitted” by a contract that tied the creation of the video to a fiction‑based moral obligation. The complaint argued that the *Cameo Terms of Service* granted Santos a right of security as a “third‑party beneficiary” even though he never signed the contract. Even that position contravenes the “strong presumption against conferring benefits on non‑contracting third parties” recognized under Illinois law (*Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.*, 68 N.E.3d 964). In the 2nd Circuit’s view, the contract claim was misdescribed and moreover derived from the same facts that underpinned the copyright claim: the production, possession, and public broadcast of the video.

Because the underlying act of producing and using Fosxdists—which would be subject to the exclusive rights guaranteed under § 101—was the same event that triggered the breached‑contract argument, preemption was a natural corollary. The court did not even have to codify this rule in new words. As the decision nods: “If the claims arise from the same underlying act, the Copyright Act preempts the state‑law claim.” Though the opinion does not foot‑note a formal exposition, that line is consistent with precedent such as *M.I. Communities Co. v. MasterCard Corp.*, and the 2nd Circuit’s frequent insistence that the preemption line applies where “facts that lead to a copyright claim also underlie the underlying state‑law claim.” The 2nd Circuit’s refusal to further discuss preemption may reflect its certainty that the claims were not properly pleaded, but it does not abandon the doctrinal core.

## Playing the “Hard Line” of Preemption
There are documented cases where courts have “semi‑reluctantly” carved out a narrower preemption that accommodates legitimate independent state‑law claims. A pertinent illustration is *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.* (50 F.4th 294), where the 2nd Circuit carefully balanced the exclusive‑rights doctrine with an underlying breach‑of‑contract claim that rested on a *different* underlying fact line. Because *Melendez* indicated that the plaintiff’s claim “was qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim,” the defendant’s preemption defense could be overruled.

In Santos, the court simply concluded that the claim was not “qualitatively different.” The two linchpins— how the contract was alleged to have been breached and how the videos were used— overlapped entirely. The preemption doctrine has a soft “border” that depends on a state claim’s character (transforming the content without appropriation vs. simply exploiting the same transform). Here, the court did not even have to court the issue because the claim was already a pleading error, but it rested in doctrinal territory that will shape future disputes: when the underlying fact is mere “use” of a copyrighted work, the federal shield is nearly impenetrable, even if the contract claim looks superficially distinct.

## The Hard‑Line Preemption Implication for Creators and Platforms
A real world implication is that creators—particularly those whose works quickly tumble into the world of shareware, user‑generated journalism, or late‑night commentary—will have very little recourse in state courts if an act that also triggers copyright claims is being challenged. The contract‑claim claims that fail to meet the operative preemption test will be summarily dismissed, even if the parties could have brought an independent contract suit in another context.

For platforms and content‑to‑mnemonic-types of business models, this gives both a shield and a call to fortify. One translation—if a user obtains a “contract” on a platform but is not a signatory—does not give the user stand‑in rights that a platform might think they enjoy. Secondary claims such as “breach of Terms of Service” might be blocked if they depend on the same material as a copyright claim. Therefore, platform designers may lean on third‑party‑beneficiary language that is expressly stated, incoming from a "User" entity that is recognizable and signatory. In the absence of such specificity, courts may view a contract claim as a mere extension of the underlying copyright act.

Meanwhile, creators may feel the choking breadth of the “exclusive‑rights first” doctrine and may lobby new state legislation that explicitly carves out preemption for certain "non‑copyright" related claims – a political endeavor that has seen limited traction. The risk is that the absence of these carve‑outs signals that a swing in facially‑friendly state law will unlikely produce the creative-entrepreneur pay‑back people SEEK.

## Bottom Line
Santos v. Kimmel reinforces the canon that the Copyright Act, when a state‑law claim is built on the same factual staging ground as an infringement claim, will preempt that claim. By dismissing the state‑law breach claims at the pleading stage, the 2nd Circuit turned out the door on issues that might have tightened or relaxed the preemption approach. But the court’s brief nod to preemption preserves a “hard line” against looser states court damages when the underlying act is the use of a protected work. For creators, the pragmatic takeaway is this: if your work’s use forms the epicenter of a friction with a platform or user, the federal copyright shield may squeeze out any state‑law contract remedy—unless you can carve out a unique independent cause of action.

The decision offers a cautionary note for both creators and the platforms they engage with. A contract language that presumes third‑party benefit must be explicit; otherwise, it will be swallowed by the Copyright Act’s preemption blanket. And for every new platform that rides the wave of user‑generated content, that wave will be less hospitable for lawsuits that do not focus on the central, copyrighted material.
