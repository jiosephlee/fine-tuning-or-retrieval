Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a court dismiss a copyright infringement claim at the pleading stage solely on a fair‑use defense?
Question:
The district court dismissed Santos’s complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) on the grounds that fair use applied. I know pretty well that fair use is a defense, not a basis for dismissal. Under what circumstances is it appropriate for a trial court to dismiss a claim for infringement before evidence is presented, relying purely on the pleadings?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes.  Under Rule 12(b)(6) a district court may dismiss a copyright‑infringement claim if, **on the face of the complaint, the alleged infringement is clearly a *fair use***.  When the facts contributed by the plaintiff’s pleading satisfy the four statutory factors so decisively that no additional evidence could alter the conclusion, the claim is legally insufficient and the court can dismiss it without a trial.

---

### 1.  Rule 12(b)(6) and the “failure to state a claim” standard

Rule 12(b)(6) authorizes dismissal if the complaint “fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted” (Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6)).  The standard is**de novo**; the court examines the plea itself plus any documents attached to it (see *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d 104, 111 [2d Cir. 2010]).  The court may also take judicial notice of well‑publicized data (e.g., copyright registrations).

A claim “fails to state” not only when it is factually unsound, but also when it cannot survive a doctrinal defense that **barred the claim entirely**.  The Copyright Act makes fair use a defense that, if established, eliminates the legal duty to observe a licensed right (§107).  Thus aclaim that would otherwise be an infringement can be dismissed for lack of an actionable cause of action when the fair‑use defense is destined to succeed.

---

### 2.  Fair use can be “clearly established” at the pleading stage

*The Court’s own language provides the key threshold.*  
In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the appellate court emphasized that the district court’s ending by “concluding that the copyright claims were barred by the fair‑use doctrine” was justified because the complaint “displayed facts so clear that they could support dismissal” (*TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 178 [2d Cir. 2016]).

This means that the pleadings must contain:

1. **A transformation** that the court accepts as “transformative” under §107(1) (i.e., the new work adds new expression, meaning, or message).  
2. **A sufficiently limited use** of the protected portion.  
3. **No market injury or market‑replacement** effect under §107(4).

When those five facts are explicitly or implicitly contained in the complaint—such as the defendant’s stated **purpose** (“to comment,” “to ridicule”), the **nature of the original work** (“fictional, non‑educational comedy”), the **amount** of material used, and the **absence of market harm**—the court can say, “The defense is already decisive; the claim fails even before evidence can be presented.”

The *Santos* decision cites *Hachette Book Grp. v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163, 179 [2d Cir. 2024], which explains that the four-factor analysis is “flexible” and that a *reasonable observer* can make a fair‑use determination from the complaint’s facts alone.

---

### 3.  How the court applied the standard in *Santos*

- **Purpose/character**: The complaint (and the attached exhibits) explained that the videos were aired on “Will Santos Say It?” as “mocking segments”—a “commentary or criticism" that, per the court, is transformative.  
- **Nature of the work**: The videos were short, “non‑educational” and “fictional,” supporting the second factor.  
- **Amount used**: The complaint noted the videos were used in a “brief segment,” and the court found the amount “minimally substantial.”  
- **Market effect**: The complaint stated there was no competition with the “original” videos and no user demand for a substitute; the court found no effect on the potential market.

Because these facts are “so clearly established by the complaint” that they “support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” (Santos, 231‑233), the district court rightfully dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6).  The appellate court affirmed, holding that the fair‑use defense “bars a claim even at the pleading stage.”

---

### 4.  When a claim **cannot** be dismissed solely on pleadings

The same authority requires the court **not** to dismiss unless the pleading conclusively shows defeat.  If aspects of the four factors are ambiguous, or if evidence could potentially overturn a defense (for example, a claim that the work is “too large” or “in the market for the original”), the court must allow the case to proceed.  In such cases, the plaintiff’s evidence is indispensable to the fair‑use inquiry.

---

## Bottom line for a law student

A court may dismiss a copyright‑infringement claim at the pleading stage **only if the complaint itself contains facts that unambiguously satisfy all four fair‑use factors**, so that a competent judge can determine that the defense will trump the claim.  That was exactly what the *Santos* court concluded, and the appellate court agreed.  When the pleadings are not so crystal‑clear, the court must wait for the evidence that will carry the fair‑use analysis.
