Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How do ‘bad faith’ representations affect the fair‑use defense, and can they nullify the defense?
Question:
The opinion discusses Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s false representations indicate bad faith and therefore undermine fair use. The Court cites *Harper & Row* but states it has moved away from that assertion. Does a plaintiff’s claim of misrepresentation or bad faith automatically defeat a defendant’s fair‑use defense? What is the current standard for linking bad faith to fair use?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No.  A claim that a defendant misled a plaintiff or acted in bad faith does **not** automatically defeat a fair‑use defense.  In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit expressly rejected the old Harper & Row “good‑faith” test and held that the four‑factor fair‑use analysis stands on its own.  Bad‑faith misrepresentations may be considered as part of the *purpose and character* factor, but they are not an automatic bar to fair use.

---

### 1.  The “good‑faith” premise is no longer controlling

*Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v.  Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539 (1985) held that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.”  The Second Circuit in *Santos*, however, says:

> “We disagree.  It is true that the Supreme Court stated in *Harper & Row* that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.”  … **But the Court has since distanced itself from this statement.**”  (see *Santos*, 106 F.4th at 28‑29)

The Court concludes that a defendant’s false representations do not automatically invalidate a claimed fair use.  The “good‑faith” dictum is no longer treated as a stand‑alone defense bar.

---

### 2.  Fair use is still governed by the four‑factor test

The statutory four‑factor framework remains the lens through which courts evaluate fair use (§ 107 U.S.C.):

1. **Purpose and character of the use** (transformative nature, commercial vs. non‑commercial, etc.)  
2. **Nature of the copyrighted work**  
3. **Amount and substantiality of the portion used**  
4. **Effect on the market** for the original

The *Santos* opinion explicitly applies this test:

> “In assessing the *purpose and character* factor, we *focus… chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative* ….”  (*Santos*, 106 F.4th at 26‑27)  

> “Because we need only consider the *original version* and the *JKL segments* at issue to decide *fair use* …” (*Santos*, 106 F.4th at 29)

Bad‑faith arguments have no separate footnote in the list of factors.  The Court evaluated transformation by asking how a *reasonable observer* would view the use, not the defendant’s intent or alleged deceit.

---

### 3.  How bad‑faith misrepresentations are treated

The Court therefore treats false representations as part of the *purpose* factor, but only as one piece of *context*.  In *Santos* the court said that:

> “The complaint paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary … On the face of Santos’s complaint, Kimmel was *not* supplanting the value of the original videos.”  (*Santos*, 106 F.4th at 28)

Thus, even though the plaintiff alleged misrepresentation, the court found that the videos remained *transformative* and that the finder of the meaning—“a reasonable observer”—was unaffected by any alleged deceit.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for a law student

* A plaintiff’s accusation of bad faith **does not** automatically defeat a fair‑use defense.  
* Courts still apply the statutory four‑factor test; *good‑faith* is an optional color, not a prerequisite.  
* Misrepresentation can weigh against a transformation claim if it materially alters a *reasonable observer’s* perception of the work, but it is not a certainty.  
* The modern standard, as reaffirmed in *Santos*, treats bad faith as a factor that may influence the *purpose and character* analysis, but it can never single‑handedly negate fair use.

---

**Key quotation to remember**

> “We disagree.  It is true that the Supreme Court stated in *Harper & Row* that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” *Santos*, 106 F.4th 28‑29  

— the Second Circuit’s definitive statement that “good faith” is no longer a binding requirement for fair use.
