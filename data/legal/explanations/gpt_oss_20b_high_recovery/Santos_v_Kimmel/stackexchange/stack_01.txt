Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What exactly does the fair‑use doctrine require for a use to be considered 'transformative'?
Question:
I’m confused about how the court determines whether the use in this case is truly transformative. The opinion repeatedly cites the *Andy Warhol Foundation* case and says that the purpose and character of the use must be judged by a reasonable observer rather than the user’s intent. How does the court evaluate whether a secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or value? Is simply commenting or parodying enough to be transformative, or is there a higher bar?
Answer:
**What the court means by “transformative”**

The Second Circuit’s opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel* makes it clear that “transformative” is a *purpose‑and‑character* question—one that sits inside the first factor of the 4‑factor fair‑use test.  The court quotes the Supreme Court’s standard in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts v. Goldsmith*:

> “We focus… on the degree to which the use is transformative, i.e., *whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.*”  
> (*Santos*, 2025, 16‑17)

Two observations follow:

1. **The test is objective.**  
   The opinion stresses that *“the purpose of the allegedly infringing use is determined by what a reasonable observer would think, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the user.”*  (*Santos*, 19)

2. **The “new” element must be more than a slight gloss.**  
   A use that simply republishes the original in the same form is a *supersession* – not a transformation.  A use that introduces an altered aim, tone, or function—adding commentary, criticism, or new meaning—is a transformation.

**Applying the standard in the case**

The court found Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segment to be transformative because the audience could regard it as a *mocking commentary* on the videos Kimmel had obtained from Santos, rather than a substitute for those videos.  Where the opinion says:

> “A reasonable observer here would think the videos conveyed *feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,* not a willingness to say absurd things for money.” (*Santos*, 18)

the court concludes that the new work provides a different purpose (criticism/ parody) and adds “new expression” (the show’s framing, commentary cues, and humor).  This satisfies the *transformative* requirement.

**Is commentary or parody automatically transformative?**

The opinion does *not* impose a “one‑size‑fits‑all” rule.  While parody and criticism *can* be transformative, the court still asks whether the secondary use *adds* new meaning or value.  A brief quotation that merely repeats the original idea, even in a humorous veneer, would likely fail the transformation test if it does not truly alter the work’s purpose.  

In *Santos*, the transformation bar was met because:

- The Kimmel segment *altered* the original purpose (from personal videos to public mockery).  
- It *added* commentary and new expression that a reasonable viewer would identify as a distinct work.  
- It did not supplant the original’s market; it served a different audience function.

**Bottom line**

A use is considered transformative when it does **not merely re‑use the original material** but **adds new expression, meaning, or a different purpose** that a reasonable observer would recognize.  The user’s intent is irrelevant; the *objective* character of the new work is what matters.  Parody or commentary may meet this threshold, but only if it truly changes the work’s character and adds substantive new value.
