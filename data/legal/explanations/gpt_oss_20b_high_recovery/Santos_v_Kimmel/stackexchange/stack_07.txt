Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How does the court interpret the market effect factor when the ‘second work’ offers a competing substitute?
Question:
In the fair‑use analysis, the court refers to the dictum that a second work must not 'usurp the market' for the original. Santos argued that the Kimmel videos might have some effect on the market for his videos. How do courts evaluate whether a derivative or derivative‑like use competes with the original, especially when no direct competition exists?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the court explains the market‑effect test in § I (4):

> “The fourth factor asks *not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute” (Court, ¶ ??).  

The court also notes that for a derivative use to “usurp” the market, it must **present a product that a consumer could purchase or consume in place of the original**. If the second work merely comments on, critiques, or lampoons the original and is not offered, marketed, or purchased as an alternative, it does **not** usurp the market.

In the instant case the Kimmel‑aired segments were a parody‑style critique of Santos’s videos. The court found that:

1. **No substitutive market existed.**  
   *“Santos has not suffered market harm … because we ask not … whether it usurps the market by offering a competing substitute.”* The segments were not sold or presented as an alternative to Santos’s own videos.

2. **Derivative works seldom create a licensing market.**  
   The court cited *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose* – “the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.” (Court, ¶ ??)

Because the Kimmel segments neither fill a market for Santos’s videos nor offer a substitute product, the fourth factor favors fair use. The court therefore concluded that Santos’s claim that the Kimmel videos “might have some effect on the market for his videos” is unpersuasive.

**Bottom line:** Courts assess the market‑effect factor by asking whether the second work *could* provide an alternate product that would replace the original. When there is no viable substitute—i.e., when the derivative is purely transformative, commentary, or critique—the factor leans toward fair use. This is precisely how the Second Circuit evaluated the Kimmel videos in *Santos*.
