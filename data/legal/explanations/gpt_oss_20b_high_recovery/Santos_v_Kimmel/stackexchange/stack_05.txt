Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why can’t a non‑contracting third party sue under a third‑party beneficiary claim against a Terms of Service?
Question:
Santos alleges that Kimmel breached the Cameo Terms of Service, which he wasn’t a party to. The court cites Illinois law’s presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties. How does this presumption work, and what would Santos have needed to plead to overcome it and establish that the Terms of Service bound him?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Illinois courts impose a *strong presumption* that a written agreement does **not** confer rights or obligations on anyone who did not sign it.  The only ways to defeat that presumption are

1. **Express statutory or contractual language** (or an equally explicit statement in the contract itself) that the third party is an intended beneficiary, or  
2. **Clearly articulated intent** of the parties that the agreement is meant to benefit the third party, with a duty that the parties understood to extend to that third party.

Santos failed to meet either test.  He offered no express declaration that the Cameo Terms of Service (TOS) were “for” him or that Kimmel was bound to give him a benefit, nor did he allege any compelling evidence that the contractors’ intent was to benefit him.  Moreover, he did not plead the *essential terms* of the TOS that would have allowed an implied‑in‑fact contract to arise.  Because of this, the court correctly held that the TOS could not be invoked by Santos.

---

## How the Illinois presumption works

Illinois Supreme Court and appellate precedent treats a third‑party beneficiary claim as a *contractual* claim:

> “A *strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties*… The implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.”  **Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur**, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016) (quoting the opinion).

Two elements are required to defeat that presumption:

| Element | Requirement |
|---------|-------------|
| **1. Express indication** | The contract itself must contain language that **identifies the third party as a beneficiary** and gives that party enforceable rights.  This can be explicit (“This contract is binding on any person who…”) or very clearly implied (e.g., a clause that “beneficios are for the subscriber’s friends”). |
| **2. Clear intent of the parties** | Even absent explicit language, the parties can still establish that the contract was meant to benefit the third party.  The evidence must show that the contracting parties *intended* the third party to benefit, not that the third party merely happened to receive a benefit. |

If neither element is shown, the presumption stands and a non‑signatory cannot sue as a beneficiary.

---

## Why Santos’ complaint did not meet the test

1. **No express beneficiary clause.**  
   The court noted that the TOS includes third‑party beneficiary language **only in those provisions where the parties clearly intended a third‑party right**.  Santos offered *nothing close to an express declaration* that the portions of the TOS he relied on applied to him.  
   > “Santos identifies nothing close to an *express declaration* that the relevant provisions of the Terms of Service apply to him …” (opinion, supra).

2. **No evidence of parties’ intent.**  
   Santos also failed to allege—“in non‑conclusory language”—that the parties erected a contract that made Kimmel **bound** to satisfy the same terms that applied to the platform’s users.  
   > The complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, … the essential terms of the parties’ … contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” (opinion, supra).

3. **No alleged essential terms.**  
   A plaintiff bringing a third‑party beneficiary claim must plead the *essential* terms that the beneficiary would seek to enforce.  This means stating the specific clause or price that is alleged to have been breached and showing that the contract creates a duty to the third party.  Santos’ allegations merely asserted that Kimmel “breached” the TOS.  The court found that this was insufficient; the claim “failed to state a claim” because it did not articulate the proper contractual elements.  
   > “… the complaint does not ‘allege … the essential terms … upon which liability is predicated.’” (opinion, supra).

Because both the *express* and the *intent* elements of the presumption were missing, the court upheld the dismissal of the breach of contract claim.

---

## What Santos would have needed to plead

1. **An express declaration in the TOS that Santos is an intended beneficiary,** or a clear and unambiguous clause that the benefit extends to anyone who arranges for a video on Cameo.  
   *Example: “Users may authorize a third party to provide a personalized video on their behalf, and such third parties are entitled to enforce these Terms.”*

2. **Allegations that the parties *intended* the TOS to bind Kimmel to that beneficiary**—e.g., that the TOS was drafted with the understanding that any person who used Kimmel’s services on Cameo would be protected by the TOS and that Kimmel was contractually bound to honor those obligations.

3. **Pleadment of the *essential* terms** that Santos claims were breached (e.g., a clause stating that “the video must be delivered in a timely, non‑misleading manner”) and an explanation of how Kimmel’s conduct violated those terms.

4. **Analysis of the relationship** between Santos and Kimmel—if the TOS treats the relationship as that of a “customer‐service‑provider” arrangement, Santos would need to establish that Kimmel agreed to be liable on Santos’ behalf under the TOS.

Without such pleadings, the presumption remains unbroken.  The court had no basis to consider the TOS as giving Santos enforceable rights, and so the breach‑of‑contract claim was dismissed.

---

### Bottom line

Illinois law treats a contract as an agreement only between the parties who sign or agree to it.  A non‑signer must show either **an explicit contractual provision** naming him as a beneficiary or **strong evidence that the contracting parties intended** the contract to bind him.  Santos’ complaint offered neither, and his failure to plead the essential terms meant that the pre‑existing presumption was not rebutted.  Thus, the TOS could not be used to support a third‑party beneficiary claim.
