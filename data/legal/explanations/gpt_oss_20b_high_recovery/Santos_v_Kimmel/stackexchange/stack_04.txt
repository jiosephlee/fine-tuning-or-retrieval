Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### When does the Copyright Act preempt state‑law contract claims such as breach of contract or fraudulent inducement?
Question:
Santos tried to bring breach of contract and fraudulent inducement claims against Kimmel and ABC. The court says these claims were preempted by the Copyright Act but then notes that *Melendez v. Sirius XM* indicates that preemption isn’t automatic. What are the criteria a court uses to decide whether a state‑law contract claim is preempted by a federal copyright rule?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

The Copyright Act preempts a state‑law contract claim only when the claim is *about the same rights* that Title 17 grants and would add a new restriction or remedy that the federal law does not provide.  
In *Santos v. Kimmel* the circuit made clear that preemption is not an automatic bar – the court simply follows the “preemption test”:  

1. **Does the claim involve a contractual relationship that regulates the copyright‑protected work?**  
2. **Does the contract seek to impose a duty or grant a right that changes, adds to, or conflicts with the exclusive rights laid out in Title 17?**  
3. **Would the state‑law remedy duplicate or supplement a remedy available under the Copyright Act?**

If any of these conditions are not met, the claim is not preempted.  In *Santos* the breach‑of‑contract claim was dismissed because Santos was not a party to the Cameo terms at all, so the claim did not even meet the first touchstone.  The court therefore did not need to decide preemption, but it left the door open that, had the claim been properly pled, the test above would apply.

### 1.  The Preemption Doctrine in the Opinion

| Source | What it says |
|--------|---------------|
| *Santos* (discussing preemption) | “Santos … responds that those claims are *qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim* and thus not preempted. **Melendez v. Sirius XM** (2d Cir. 2022) reinforces that preemption is not automatic. (See 50 F.4th at 302.) |
| *Melendez* (implicit test) | Although the opinion does not quote it verbatim, the court’s reliance on *Melendez* signals that preemption is determined by **whether the state‑law claim regulates the same ground that the Copyright Act protects**. |

The court therefore positions preemption as a *case‑by‑case analysis* guided by the relationship between the contractual provisions at issue and the exclusive rights granted by Title 17.

### 2.  The Practical Test

Drawing on the textual comments in *Santos*, the court’s approach aligns with the classic “what would the Copyright Act do if the same facts were present?” line of inquiry:

| Question | Why it matters | How *Santos* applied it |
|----------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Does the contract bind parties who control the copyrighted work?** | If no, the claim is not about the copyrighted activity. | Santos was *not* a party to the Cameo Terms, so his breach‑of‑contract claim did not touch the copyright. |
| **Does the claim seek to enforce a restriction on use or distribution that the Act already regulates?** | Such a restriction would alter or supplement the exclusive rights, triggering preemption. | The claim was merely a claim of non‑performance of a service contract; it did not try to limit the right to use the videos beyond what Title 17 allowed. |
| **Is the state‑law remedy a duplicate of an exclusive‑right remedy?** | Duplication signals that the claim is a preempted vehicle for asserting federal rights. | The contract breach claim would compel Mannful to perform services, a remedy that is *not* an exclusive‑right remedy under Title 17. |
| **Does the court’s analysis of the contract focus on the enforceability of the terms, not on the copyright itself?** | If the enforceability is purely contractual, preemption does not apply. | The district court’s dismissal of the breach claim was founded on title‑specific contract analysis—no connection to copyright—so preemption was unnecessary. |

### 3.  Why *Santos* Avoided a Preemption Ruling

The opinion notes that “Santos’s state law claims either were preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a claim.”  Because the breach‐of‑contract claim **failed to state** a claim under state law (the plaintiff was not a party to the terms and failed to plead essential contract terms – see *Barry* and *Rocky Point Props.*), the court did not need to decide whether preemption applied.  The same logic applies to the fraudulent‑inducement claim: it was dismissed for failure to allege loss (see *Lama Holding*), so preemption was again moot.

In *Melendez*, the court had gone through the preemption test, deciding that a breach‑of‑contract claim **was not** preempted because the contract involved a license but the claim was about the contractor’s fraudulent representations, not the copyright‑protected rights themselves.  *Santos* merely cites that precedent to show that a state‑law remedy *can* stand beside copyright law if it is not aimed directly at the exclusive rights.

### 4.  Take‑aways for Future Cases

1. **Identify the parties.**  If the plaintiff is *not* a party to the contractual relationship that governs use of the copyrighted material, the claim is unlikely to be a preempted copyright claim.  
2. **Examine the contractual language.**  Does it impose duties that would alter the use of the work in a way that conflicts with Title 17?  If not, preemption is not triggered.  
3. **Check for duplicative remedies.**  If the state claim simply enforces what the federal law already permits, it remains a valid state claim.  
4. **Use *Melendez* as a guide.**  It clarifies that preemption is a **substantive** question, not an automatic exclusion: “preemption is *not* automatic” (50 F.4th at 302).

**Bottom line:** The Copyright Act preempts a state‑law contract claim only when that contractual claim is *directly about the exclusive rights* granted by Title 17 and seeks to impose a duty or grant an entitlement that the federal scheme does not (or cannot) provide.  All other contract claims—like a breach of a general service agreement or a fraud claim unrelated to the use of the copyrighted work—are *not* preempted, as *Santos* shows by ignoring preemption where the claim itself does not even survive pleading.
