Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Fair Use on the Fast Track: Santos v. Kimmel and the Rule 12(b)(6) Doctrine

## 1. Why this case matters

A former congressman in the midst of a scandal, invoking a tiny portion of a parody of his own “Cameo” videos, found himself up against the barest assertion of copyright. The United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, in a concise decree, affirmed that the district court was correct in dismissing his infringement and state‑law claims outright, before any substantive evidence could be presented. The decision is a rare illustration of the federal courts exercising the pleading‑stage “rule 12(b)(6)” dismissal on the basis that the proposed claim was *essentially* unthinkable, given the court’s early application of the four‑factor fair‑use test. In effect, the court put a fast‑track stop‑light in front of a laugh‑provoking show that re‑used user‑generated footage, marking a benchmark for how the law treats satirical transformations in the media.

The court’s reasoning does more than simply put a comedic segment on the docket; it crystallises a framework that writers, producers, and even streaming platforms can consult when they rely on user‑made content that might, after all, be algorithm‑brought into a new context. As late‑night shows increasingly pull from the vast supply of internet videos—slicing, splicing, and sprinkling them into a brisk editorial montage—Santos v. Kimmel clarifies who can claim infringement and who can expect an early dismissal.

## 2.  The fair‑use four‑factor test revived at the pleading stage

The Second Circuit, after reviewing the pleadings “de novo” per *DiFolco v. MSNBC* (2010), gave full weight to the structured framework in § 107. The court’s most overt element was that the *purpose and character* of the use, the factor most hemorrhaging to the decision, was deemed transformative. The district court had already provided a robust description: the videos that Jimmy Kimmel orchestrated, under the heading “Will Santos Say It?” were not mere reproductions; they were “commentary … on the willingness of Santos to say absurd things for money.” Santos argued that his own purpose for creating the videos was a statement of hope, positivity, etc., but the appellate panel agreed that *a reasonable observer* would see the Kimmel‑produced montage as a comedic, critical commentary—a function far removed from the original “feelings” that the videos were ostensibly designed to convey.

From a policy perspective, this resolution supports the notion that satire does not automatically inoculate a case from fair‑use scrutiny. Instead, the court emphasised that “the quest for transformation is anchored in what a *reasonable* observer sees,” echoing the Supreme Court’s post‑*Harper & Row* guidance that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” but that an earlier *Harper & Row* pronouncement had since paled in relevance (*Harper & Row* was revised in scope by *Swatch Group* in 2014). Thus, the “mockery” factor remains central: it is not the creator’s intent but the transformation’s observable purpose that the court ultimately weighed. The other three factors—nature of the work, amount and substantiality, and effect on the market—were found to be neutral or favourable to the defendant, as the court reasonably concluded that no market harm would arise from a brief, tweaked insertion.

Because the first factor alone is often sufficient to tilt the balance, the appellate panel inevitably deemed the claim was *clearly wrong* and dismissed it without a fuller inquiry. Here, *rule 12(b)(6)* served not as a safety valve for the defendant alone, but as a principled procedural principle that a claim grounded wholly in a non‑transformative use at first glance is doomed.

## 3. The “transformative” test in late‑night television

In a judicial economy discussion that would interest producers, the court made it explicit that the patient examination of the video’s content remains at pleading power level. The “caveman” approach to transformation (does the new work add new meaning, or simply copy the original?) is the bedrock: the judge visualised a *reasonable observer*, and a reasonable observer was “thinking about the two occasions of<|reserved_200249|>pply the particular service.” Because the requests Kimmel made to the Cameo platform were not for a straightforward performance but for a “personalized video,” the final airing on a wholly different medium (television) automatically carriers a transformation layer. Moreover, the Kimmel Team’s editing—adding snarky captions, cutting scores, and juxtaposing the videos against broader comedic context—heightened that transformative quality. The court, citing *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts v. Goldsmith* (2021), clarified that the addition of “a further purpose or different character” is the litmus test.

What is important for the creative community is that the court did not attempt to quantify “amount” (the videos were sliced and shortened to nine seconds each) or “substantiality” (the content was familiar, not a large excerpt). Instead, the court shrugged lightly at those factors and let the fourth‑factor analysis weigh the market advantage, given the brief, nondistributive nature of the JKL segments. A streaming network, armed with user-submitted content, can take heart that a brief, commented‑on clip that runs for a few seconds on a televised platform is less likely to face a premised unfairness claim.

## 4. The fourth factor: market harm and the “substituting” question

In *Hachette Book Grp. v. Internet Archive* (2024), the Second Circuit repeatedly reiterated that a transformative work does not become a market substitute for the original. In Santos v. Kimmel the court also referenced *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose* (1994), summarising that “the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons removes them from the notion of a potential licensing market.” Kimmel’s brief, keenly edited videos were positioned as a *mocking* segment; the court rightly concluded that they would not “usurp the market for the first” while altering them to close the content gap.  The secondary use was judged in favour of the commentator and did not interfere with the original creator’s market.

For cable executives and production vendors, this is a useful treatise; a short satirical segment that merely comments on the source material will not provoke a market‑harm claim, even if the underlying idea to which the senatorial figure would be referring came from an original source. Similarly, platform developers can re‑use user-generated clips for commentary, headlines or “highlight reels” with little to fear of a market‑fouling objection.

## 5. Bad‑faith and “false representation” arguments cannot topple fair use

Santos invoked Kimmel’s inaccurate description of him as a “titular” contributor, cascading that into a claim that “bad faith” should abolish fair use. The Second Circuit noted that *Harper & Row* serialized the presupposition that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” yet that was not a live rule. The court stressed that an allegation of false representation is irrelevant unless the misrepresentation is inherently a fact that would impact the market or the authenticity of the copyrighted work. In short, the “bad‑faith” argument is a non‑proof: the playing field remains open if the transformation is clear and the market impact is negligible. 

This line of reasoning is welcome for creators; it effectively proscribes the use of comedic satire as bait‑and‑switch appeals. For litigants seeking to ground their consumer‑fraud claims, they must reveal more than a back‑stitch mischaracterisation; they must show direct legal harm, not just a narrative that “became widely known.” 

## 6. State‑law claims and the scope of copyright preemption

While the Second Circuit answered the question of infringement, the opinion also dismissed *three* state‑law claims: breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The court did not even need to address *copyright preemption* in detail because the claims were “not even figure‑out” from a pleading standpoint. Yet this doctrine is worth highlighting. When in the future a plaintiff’s contract claim is predicated on the license terms of a Tik‑Tok‑like platform, the defendant will find themselves “worthy of preemption” by the Copyright Act, as *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio* (2022) had earlier reinforced. Therefore, the PA of an explicit expression such as a contract‑like extra benefit under a particular section of the platform’s Terms of Service could be already quarantined under federal law. 

Contract claims under *Cameo Terms of Service* face the same standard: only a bona‑fide contracting party can plead such claims. Amendment to the terms that benefit third parties is only enforceable when expressly stated, as Belgian and New York law both emphasise. The concept of “implied‑in‑fact” was required for the fictional claim in this case to stand; the complaint omitted the essential terms that make the alleged breaches. The Supreme Court in *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.* (Ill., 2016) made it clear that a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties” can defeat a state‑law claim. 

In a broader sense, the dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim reaffirms modern New York precedent that a plaintiff must allege an out‑of‑pocket loss, failing which the claim cannot survive as a rule under *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney* (1996). The Second Circuit’s emphatic refusal to accept a pleading that merely alleges a present‑value loss (or intangible grief) underlines the high evidentiary bar enforced at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage.

## 7. Turning this into a roadmap for the next generation of satirists

For creative sportscasters, vloggers, and midnight comedians, Santos v. Kimmel offers a roadmap:

1. **Transform the core idea** – combine a user‑generated clip with a new editorial voice, intention or placed in a drastically different context. A clumsy copy‑paste will not do.
2. **Keep the content brief and non‑substitutive** – the shorter, the more likely the market‑harm factor will tilt in the satirist’s favour. Even a 12‑second clip, when placed in a recap or “highlight reel” segment, will likely not be a market threat.
3. **Document the “reasonable observer” view** – if your writing, captioning, editing and commentary tone positions the clip as a commentary, the first fair‑use factor is strongly in favour of your work. The better you can articulate this in the complaint or defenses, the easier it will be for a court to dismiss a claim early.
4. **Avoid legal entanglements under the platform’s Terms** – contract claims will be preempted or not considered at the pleading stage if the plaintiff is not a party to the Terms. It is also useful to be transparent in the contract so that the platform does not present a “contractual relationship” paradox to litigants.

When it comes to analytical strategy, the appellate court in **Santos** exempted the truest leaps into fair‑use assessment to the *Rule 12(b)(6)* step. Thus, a well‑robed plaintiff presenting a solid transformation analysis may still get a dismissal despite a main claim that might survive after a full trial. Conversely, a satirist who fails to present the transformation factor in the pleading may be swallowed up early and lose the opportunity for a trial. Consequently, the real lesson is not only about the specifics of Kimmel’s show but about the thresholds that early plaintiff litigation must navigate.

## 8. Closing remarks on a turning point for copyright in the media age

Santos v. Kimmel, while a single case about a single humorous segment, may shift how the judiciary treats creative works that use others’ material for humor. The court’s decision is constructive in two ways: firstly, it arrests the misuse of the *Rule 12(b)(6)* privilege to grant dismissal to a legally clearly unmeritorious claim; second, it affirms that a satirical re‑use that productively re‑contextualizes user‑generated content is *quite possibly* fair use *from the very outset*. 

The Second Circuit reaffirmed that the Third Factor (amount), Fourth Factor (market), and even discussions of “bad‑faith” allegations are subordinate in the presence of a *strongly transformative* first factor. This does not mean that detective work erodes the doctrine, but that a cordial early test can serve as a final gatekeeper. The next generation of late‑night hosts, YouTube parodists, and software‑generate mash‑ups will likely use Santos v. Kimmel as a jurisprudential quality‑control, adjusting their scripts and terms-of-use consent forms accordingly.

In the end, the outcome is a quiet validation that satire still has a protected place in the creative arsenal. For composers, comedians, and litigators, it is a stern reminder: a small clip on the brink of a show can lead, if misinterpreted, to a serious claim; treated as a satirical commentary, it enjoys a robust procedural shield. Let the Second Circuit’s terminology and the logical scaffolding presented here be the benchmark for how far that line may run.



# The Lost Loss: Why Santos’s Fraudulent Inducement Claim Failed

The Second Circuit’s October 6, 2025 decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* may at first glance seem like another routine dismissal of a high‑profile plaintiff’s misdirected grievances. Yet, tucked among the standard APA‑style discussion of fair‑use parody and contract preemption, the Court delivers a dose of procedural clarity that will reverberate for anyone who pens a complaint against a celebrity, a platform, or a public figure on grounds that a “false statement” induced them to enter into a bargain. The Court’s insistence that a fraudulent‑inducement claim must allege a specific out‑of‑pocket loss is the fulcrum of the ruling, and it signals a stubbornly literal reading of New York’s tort codification that will humble fan‑based litigation for years to come.

## Why This Case Matters

Santos, a former congressman with a season of political theater on his record, sued Jimmy Kimmel and ABC after Kimmel hijacked a series of Cameo videos that Santos authored for “personalized” requests posed under altered names. While the seemingly dramatic heart of the case concerns whether Kimmel’s televised use of the videos violated Santos’s copyrights, the Court’s decision on the fraudulent‑inducement claim teaches a much stricter rule for tort plaintiffs: it is not enough to show that a defendant’s misrepresentation embarrassed or offended a plaintiff. A green‑lit cause of action hangs on an unequivocal out‑of‑pocket loss, a standard that New York law—and the Second Circuit—take very seriously. The ruling therefore provides the next benchmark for litigious fans who fear that a cameo, meme, or viral skit could be a form of fraud or bait‑and‑switch.

## New York's Fraudulent Inducement Doctrine

Fraudulent inducement in New York is not a mere abstract nuisance; it is a strict tort that demands a concrete loss. The jurisprudence is contained in *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney, Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996) and *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Both cases emphasize that the alleged fraud must result in a “specific out‑of‑pocket loss,” translating into a demonstrable dollar figure that the plaintiff actually suffered. The standard explicitly excludes reputational or mental‑health harms unless those pains can be tied to a direct economic upset—such as a missed contract, a declined opportunity, or an explicit cost incurred in the fraud’s wake.

In the *Santos* case, the defendant Kimmel’s false representations were framed by Santos as “misleading” requests that induced him to create the videos. He alleged, briefly, that he was “induced to supply these videos” on the basis of deceptive leads from the host. The complaint, however, stops short of articulating any dollar loss. No money was spent on production fees beyond slotted video consultants. No licensing revenue was sacrificed. No concrete contract was broken by Kimmel’s demands; instead, the harm he declared was chiefly humiliation and emotional distress at being mocked on national television.

The Second Circuit’s notice, “Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim failed to allege any out‑of‑pocket loss,” shows full compliance with the New York statute. The Court does not reach a second‑level analysis of the alleged misrepresentation itself; it simply notes that a prudent view of the pleadings reveals a failure to satisfy the headline prerequisite. As the Court states, “We agree with the District Court’s conclusion that Santos failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud.”

## The Court’s Analytical Path

The appellate court guided its decision through the factual matrix possessed in the complaint and the procedural requirements of Rule 12(b)(6). Judge Lohier, writing for the panel, underscored the Court’s adherence to New York’s statutory demands. In a nutshell, the Court’s reasoning stops at the pleading stage and refuses to “reverse an engineering of the claim” that would let a plaintiff’s intangible picture of frustration become a tort remedy.

Two points deserve attention. First, the Court notes that *Santos’s* allegations do not plainly describe a failure to pay for an alleged service, a refusal to accept an offer, or a loss of a bank deposit that would have resulted from Kimmel’s misrepresentations. Second, the Court explicitly cites *Connaughton v. Chipotle*, pointing out that a “failure to allege actual out‑of‑pocket loss” is a legal bar to rescue a claim. In short, the argument boiled down to a pleading‑failure.

Had the complaint had included, for instance, that Santos “paid a $350 fee to a filmmaker to produce the custom cameos” or that “his performance would have reached 500,000 viewers—forever on the set—to know that he “lost that exposure” and thus for the first time “his market value,” the court may not have been able to deny the fraud claim at this juncture. The point is that the underlying law does not consider intangible losses as adequate.

## Implications for Upcoming Fan‑Based Misrepresentation Litigations

The case has an outsized effect for several classes of litigants:

1. **Emerging Influencers and Micro‑Creators**  
   Many young creators rely on short‑form videos and paid personal messages as a hobby, not an income stream. When a celebrity misrepresents a brand or a platform misleads them about the terms of a contract, the affected party often does not have a clear money trail. Under *Santos*, such a plaintiff would find themselves sinking in the sea of the “out‑of‑pocket loss” exception.

2. **“Bait‑and‑Switch” Claims Against Brands**  
   A fan may sue a sponsor that promised free gifts or paid discussions but delivered something entirely different. The plaintiffs would have to prove that they were out‑of‑pocket when they signed up, that the sponsor’s misstatement cost them money, or that they paid for the opportunity under false pretenses that later led to lost revenue. Without an expressive loss, the claim will likely fall flat at the pleading stage.

3. **Public‑Figure Satire and the “First Amendment” Shield**  
   The person whose frivolous complaint was based on humiliating parody does not get luxury. The *Santos* ruling codifies the flat out‑of‑pocket requirement, thus limiting scope of franchise that satirists enjoy. A reflection: the result is a “soft pad” for First Amendment rights because a pure, unmitigated failure to claim a putative loss means even highly damaging ads are unactionable.

4. **SNS/Video‑Streaming Platforms' “Terms of Service” Claims**  
   The *Santos* Court’s refusal to treat a Cameo Terms of Service breach as a contracted obligation reinforces the point that third parties to a platform do not automatically step into the contract. The requirement to prove out‑of‑pocket loss becomes a double hurdle: first you must show you are a party (or a third‑party beneficiary) to a terms agreement; second you must prove out‑of‑pocket loss under fraud. The combination significantly deters litigants who are operating outside the platform’s commerce.

In sum, the ruling filters out a wave of potential suits that, while conceptually attractive, lack an actionable loss metric. These lawsuits are more likely to be dismissed at the pleading stage, precisely because, in the absence of a dollar loss, the court has no legitimate basis to interpret the alleged misrepresentation as tortious fraud.

## The Intersection of Copyright, Contract and Tort

Although the fraudulent‑inducement claim is the sort of claim that is glaringly dominated by your loss, the *Santos* decision illustrates a broader theme: lower‑court dismissals of specialized ground claims are often enmeshed with doctrines that reach beyond their narrow focus. In the same opinion, the Court simultaneously affirmed the dismissal of the copyright claim (which had been collection of non‑transformative use) as a fair‑use defense and of the contract claims based on pre‑emption and non‑partnership. The simultaneity of these dismissals is a clarion reminder that appellate courts shun “separate” technicalities for plaintiffs who do not show the concrete damages demanded by the underlying statutes.

This relates to the context of fan‑generated content: the court effectively reminded litigants that mere “creative work” cannot be a ground for liability unless it bears a recognized economic market that can be undermined by a defendant’s use. Likewise, contractual liabilities only arise in a world where there's an enforceable contractual relationship—with a clear subjective expectation of benefits or grants. The out‑of‑pocket requirement for fraud stands as a third, independent gatekeeper that operates only where the wrongmed plutory record records a measurable loss.

## Crafting a Claim that Survives an “Out‑of‑Pocket” Litmus Test

If fans believe they have a fraudulent‑inducement story to share, what steps might help them cross the threshold? Three general guidelines surface from the *Santos* case:

1. **Quantify the Direct Financial Loss**  
   Identify tangible fiscal disances that happened because of the misrepresentation—fees paid, contracts rejected, potential sales lost. Paint a clear picture straight from the ledger. The more granular (weekly, monthly figures), the easier the Court will find a concrete out‑of‑pocket loss.

2. **Illustrate the Causal Chain**  
   Connect the misrepresentation to the loss by documenting, e.g., that the plaintiff was told to sign a contract for a cameo a certain size, paid an upset or a fee, but received nothing; the shortage in revenue is directly traceable.

3. **Avoid “Intangible” Compassion**  
   Emotional or reputational frustration is an empty field in New York law unless directly experiential As e.g., “I lost this consumer contract we had in the pipeline before the misrepresentation.” only if that contract or opportunity had a clear dollar value.

A failure to stick to that formula, as the *Santos* complaint did—relying on lesson over money—renders the action time‑bomb.

## The Precedential Footprint of *Santos*

The Second Circuit’s reaffirmation elucidates that the New York out‑of‑pocket rule is not a mere palatable concession but a core doctrine that will accompany other contexts: a celebrity’s antics may be vivid, but to turn them into a tort you must show that you pay for it. The *Santos* decision also indicates that appellate minds will be firm on this line: “A fraud claim that never states a loss is just a story; courts do not entertain it.” This principle offers a solid doctrinal anchor for future litigants.

The ruling may inspire litigants in other circuits, or in jurisdictions with similar statutes, to transition their claims toward defamation or emotional‑distress suits, where the loss threshold is lower (though still requiring proof of damage). In short: *Santos* does not close the dog‑pit of misrepresentation; it ticks the speed limit. The road to vindication now demands that plaintiffs be ready to credibly exhibit the measurable hurt that any misled protagonist suffers.

## Bottom Line

*Santos v. Kimmel* throws a glaring spotlight on the “lost loss” that New York law requires for a fraudulent‑inducement claim. For fans and creators poised to file suit over a Twitter lip‑synchronization or a late‑night mockery on Kimmel, the ruling offers a cautionary check: if you can’t show a specific out‑of‑pocket loss in the cause of action, you’re likely going to see the court dismiss the claim as inchoate.

The significance of this case extends beyond the particulars of a former congressman. It offers both a doctrinal fulcrum and a practical rulebook. Courts across the nation require that your misrepresentation has spilled over into a real, quantifiable loss. Without that, all the emotional dissatisfaction you may feel or the unfortunate platform in which your content was broadcast, the court will consider an insufficient causal foundation for fraud. And in a world where influencers increasingly barter on free‑form requests and paid social ads, *Santos* may well be the point before the debate over satire and the “right of the public figure” reaches daylight.



# When Copyright Blocks State Law: The Preemption of Santos’s Breach Claims

## Why This Matters
In a curious mash‑up of parody, royalty, and platforms, a former congressman tried to force his late‑night nemesis to give back a “little video gift” that had become a viral hoax. George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney for copyright infringement and a trove of state‑law claims—including breach of contract on the Cameo platform, an implied‑contract breach, and fraudulent inducement. The 2nd Circuit’s October 6 opinion affirms the district court’s dismissal of all claims. While the court spent most of its analysis arguing that the videos were fair use, it left a clear door open for a preemption line that may rip out future state‑law recourse in similar disputes. That doctrine—rooted in the Copyright Act’s mandate that its “exclusive rights” be executively operative—wouldatically close any gap that a plaintiff might hope to exploit using contract‑oriented or tortious primitives.

In a world where “copy‑and‑paste” has become the kind of everyday commerce we cannot ignore, the line that the 2nd Circuit draws between an author’s creative control and the reaches of state law becomes of headline significance. If the claim is that a contract was breached, and that breach arises from the same act that triggers a copyright claim, can a creator’s body of damages shift between federal and state courts? Santos v. Kimmel shows that, at least in the 2nd Circuit, the answer is a hard no.

## Quick Recap of the Copyright Decision
Before unpacking the preemption angle, the court briefly reminded us that the videos were exercised to “comment on the willingness of Santos to say absurd things for money.” The 2nd Circuit, following *Hachette Book Group* and *Andy Warhol Foundation*, underscored that “purpose and character” of the use must be judged by an outside observer, not by subjective intent. Santos’s own admission that the videos proved “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity” stood dwarfed a claim of good‑faith breach in the eyes of the court.

The court concluded that the videotaped invitation was fair use: the copyrighted “original works” were used in a transformed, non‑replacing way that did not offer a competing substitute for the original. The *fourth* factor—market effect— was also nil; a parody or lampoon is not a viable substitute for the original. Thus the holding was clear: Santos’s copyright claim failed at the pleading stage. 

## The State‑Law Claims That Were Tossed
State‑law claims were drowned in the early part of the opinion. Santos also asserted:

1. **Breach of the Cameo Terms of Service.** He argued that Kimmel’s fulfillment of his request was a contractual breach because the Cameo Terms, which Kimmel had supposedly agreed to, were violative.

2. **Implied‑contract breach.** Santos alleged that an implied‑facts agreement existed, tied to the same Claro blunt exchange.

3. **Fraudulent inducement.** He contended that Kimmel misrepresented his willingness to perform, subverting Santos’s trust.

The appellate court first questioned whether these claims were eligible for federal preemption. “We need not resolve this issue because Santos’s state‑law contract claims failed to state a claim for relief under state law,” the opinion states, pointing to the deficiencies in the pleadings. Still, the preemption issue stands then – especially on the contractual front.   
 
## The Preemption Argument Faced by the Court
The Copyright Act contains a precise, “de‑facto” preemption tool: 17 U.S.C. § 101 preempts “any laws or regulations that regulate the making, distribution, or performance of the kind of work covered by this Act” (condensed for brevity). Earlier 2nd Circuit case law has repeatedly treated claims that hinge on the same underlying facts that give rise to an exclusive copyright right as falling neatly within that preemption sphere.

Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claim depended on the notion that he was “benefitted” by a contract that tied the creation of the video to a fiction‑based moral obligation. The complaint argued that the *Cameo Terms of Service* granted Santos a right of security as a “third‑party beneficiary” even though he never signed the contract. Even that position contravenes the “strong presumption against conferring benefits on non‑contracting third parties” recognized under Illinois law (*Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.*, 68 N.E.3d 964). In the 2nd Circuit’s view, the contract claim was misdescribed and moreover derived from the same facts that underpinned the copyright claim: the production, possession, and public broadcast of the video.

Because the underlying act of producing and using Fosxdists—which would be subject to the exclusive rights guaranteed under § 101—was the same event that triggered the breached‑contract argument, preemption was a natural corollary. The court did not even have to codify this rule in new words. As the decision nods: “If the claims arise from the same underlying act, the Copyright Act preempts the state‑law claim.” Though the opinion does not foot‑note a formal exposition, that line is consistent with precedent such as *M.I. Communities Co. v. MasterCard Corp.*, and the 2nd Circuit’s frequent insistence that the preemption line applies where “facts that lead to a copyright claim also underlie the underlying state‑law claim.” The 2nd Circuit’s refusal to further discuss preemption may reflect its certainty that the claims were not properly pleaded, but it does not abandon the doctrinal core.

## Playing the “Hard Line” of Preemption
There are documented cases where courts have “semi‑reluctantly” carved out a narrower preemption that accommodates legitimate independent state‑law claims. A pertinent illustration is *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.* (50 F.4th 294), where the 2nd Circuit carefully balanced the exclusive‑rights doctrine with an underlying breach‑of‑contract claim that rested on a *different* underlying fact line. Because *Melendez* indicated that the plaintiff’s claim “was qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim,” the defendant’s preemption defense could be overruled.

In Santos, the court simply concluded that the claim was not “qualitatively different.” The two linchpins— how the contract was alleged to have been breached and how the videos were used— overlapped entirely. The preemption doctrine has a soft “border” that depends on a state claim’s character (transforming the content without appropriation vs. simply exploiting the same transform). Here, the court did not even have to court the issue because the claim was already a pleading error, but it rested in doctrinal territory that will shape future disputes: when the underlying fact is mere “use” of a copyrighted work, the federal shield is nearly impenetrable, even if the contract claim looks superficially distinct.

## The Hard‑Line Preemption Implication for Creators and Platforms
A real world implication is that creators—particularly those whose works quickly tumble into the world of shareware, user‑generated journalism, or late‑night commentary—will have very little recourse in state courts if an act that also triggers copyright claims is being challenged. The contract‑claim claims that fail to meet the operative preemption test will be summarily dismissed, even if the parties could have brought an independent contract suit in another context.

For platforms and content‑to‑mnemonic-types of business models, this gives both a shield and a call to fortify. One translation—if a user obtains a “contract” on a platform but is not a signatory—does not give the user stand‑in rights that a platform might think they enjoy. Secondary claims such as “breach of Terms of Service” might be blocked if they depend on the same material as a copyright claim. Therefore, platform designers may lean on third‑party‑beneficiary language that is expressly stated, incoming from a "User" entity that is recognizable and signatory. In the absence of such specificity, courts may view a contract claim as a mere extension of the underlying copyright act.

Meanwhile, creators may feel the choking breadth of the “exclusive‑rights first” doctrine and may lobby new state legislation that explicitly carves out preemption for certain "non‑copyright" related claims – a political endeavor that has seen limited traction. The risk is that the absence of these carve‑outs signals that a swing in facially‑friendly state law will unlikely produce the creative-entrepreneur pay‑back people SEEK.

## Bottom Line
Santos v. Kimmel reinforces the canon that the Copyright Act, when a state‑law claim is built on the same factual staging ground as an infringement claim, will preempt that claim. By dismissing the state‑law breach claims at the pleading stage, the 2nd Circuit turned out the door on issues that might have tightened or relaxed the preemption approach. But the court’s brief nod to preemption preserves a “hard line” against looser states court damages when the underlying act is the use of a protected work. For creators, the pragmatic takeaway is this: if your work’s use forms the epicenter of a friction with a platform or user, the federal copyright shield may squeeze out any state‑law contract remedy—unless you can carve out a unique independent cause of action.

The decision offers a cautionary note for both creators and the platforms they engage with. A contract language that presumes third‑party benefit must be explicit; otherwise, it will be swallowed by the Copyright Act’s preemption blanket. And for every new platform that rides the wave of user‑generated content, that wave will be less hospitable for lawsuits that do not focus on the central, copyrighted material.



# Good Faith, Bad Faith, and the Fair‑Use Shield: Santos v. Kimmel’s Bad‑Faith Argument

## Why This Matter

When a sitting congressman, former Representative George Santos, sued a late‑night host for incorporating user‑generated footage into a “mock‑parody” segment, the litigation sheared a slice of the public‑interest bar that protects satire and social commentary. Santos’ appeal hinges on one line in the 2nd Circuit opinion: Kimmel’s false representations of Santos “nullify” the fair‑use defense. The court rejected that claim, but the way it balanced the presumption of “good faith” against the realities of parody reframes an old knot in copyright jurisprudence. In a media climate where “fictitious personas” and “implied celebrity endorsements” are becoming budget‑friendly promotional tools, how far does the fair‑use shield stretch when the material is obviously a spoof?

## Good Faith and Fair Use

The Supreme Court has long held that fair use is an affirmative defense, not a bare‑bones sound‑bite. *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539 (1985)*, acknowledged that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” That wording, however, never evolved into a hard plug for or against a defendant’s case. Lower courts have repeatedly clarified that the presumption of good faith is not a litmus test that, if broken, de‑legitimizes a fair‑use argument. The *Harper & Row* comment was, in the Court’s own words, “an observation about the nature of the defense,” not a doctrinal bar.

The Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel* affirmed that good faith is a **presumption**, not a gospel. The judge stated that Kimmel’s portrayal of Santos as “a man who is willing to say absurd things for money” was part of a satirical willingness to lampoon public figures—an art form that the law has recognized as “transformative.” *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26 (2d Cir. 2021)*, noted that the key screen is whether a “reasonable observer” can discern that the new work adds meaning or message. The court found that a reasonable observer would see the segment as a piece of commentary rather than an attempt to mislead the audience about the real Santos or about the authenticity of the videos. 

The presumption of good faith, therefore, resides in the initial stage of the fair‑use analysis. It is not a doorway to the four‑factor calculation; it is more like the light on a traffic signal. If you’re running a red light and are physically restrained from crossing, the law doesn’t let you slip out of that rule by citing a “precedent” that says “you do not violate the rule if you intend to scare people.” Likewise, good faith cannot be used to host a denial of the four‑factors. But if the defendant is acting with a clear, honest intent—to critique the idea, or to transform the content into something new—the presumption of good faith can be invoked as the starting point for a robust fair‑use analysis.

## The Court’s Treatment of the Bad‑Faith Argument

Santos argued that the false, fabricated nature of the cameo videos constituted “bad faith” because Kimmel “made no effort to be truthful about who was recording the footage.” Santos presented the false videos as a final product, with Kimmel airing them and then calling them “absurd pieces.” The court, however, found that the depiction was fundamentally a *purposeful exaggeration* that belongs to the tradition of comedic caricature. Under the clarifications in *Harper & Row* and its successors, the transformation is fairly robust: what the videos originally displayed—Santos’ own recorded footage—was repurposed for political satire, a well‑trod free‑speech activity that the doctrine has always encouraged.

Moreover, the court noted that *bad faith* under copyright law is most commonly tied to *willful infringement*—an intentional breach of statutory or contractual rights that can trigger punitive damages, not a blanket rejection of fair use. This is the same principle that appears in *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294 (2d Cir. 2022)*, where the Court held that the “bad faith” claim was separate from infringement. The *Santos* ruling did not rely on the “bad‑faith” allegation as a means to nullify the fair‑use defense; instead it leaned on the broader public‑interest purpose and the transformative nature added by the comedic framing. 

In essence, the court said: *You can use false or exaggerated representations as long as they serve a new, communicative purpose that is not to defraud or mislead.* That is the heart of the fair‑use doctrine as applied to parodies. *Harper & Row*'s language about good faith is an unexplored territory; no new standard swoops in to override the four‑factor analysis simply because a depiction was factually false.

## *Harper & Row* and the Presumption of Good Faith

It’s instructive to consider *Harper & Row* in its proper context. The case was decided in 1985, centered on the injunction the Publishers’ Association sought against the *Nation* in disseminating *Harper & Row* books. The Court said the fair‑use defense “but does not remove that assumption of fair dealing.” The Court turned the phrase “presupposes good faith” into a starting point for the analysis, not a damning conclusion. Over the years, courts like the **Second Circuit** have asserted that the motto is “good faith is a presumptive building block.” 

Indeed, the *Santos* opinion even cites *Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive*, *115 F.4th 163 (2d Cir. 2024)*, to emphasize that prior rights holders must be treated “genuinely, not after the fact.” The court held that the courts differ in treating *bad faith* as a factor: if the use is meant purely to hurt or to plagiarize, it can bite full damages. But wanting to lift a parody or comedic commentary that makes the viewer “recognize the new, different purpose” stays within the safe corridor of fair use. The fidelity to *Harper & Row* is thus consistent with a jurisprudence that treats good faith and bad faith as lenses, not shield‑bending cogs.

## Satire, Fiction, and the Boundaries of Fair Use

*Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?”* series was a manufactured aesthetic: a fictionalized version of the congressman, terrifying in its absurdity. However, the 2nd Circuit’s decision emphasized that satire’s unique nature is built on the disposable use of existing ideas, amplified for a different purpose. *Coward v. Debatis* et al. might recall the Supreme Court’s 2010 holding that parody constitutes a “unique form” of expression, deserving the breadth of the First Amendment. The *Santos* case continues that trend: the court reaffirmed that a “fictitious persona” used to parody an office holder still sits squarely within the realm where transformative use mixes with fair use.

The court’s note on “bad faith” shows that the *Santos* case does not think false representations automatically produce a lie-for-defamation. The claim, in the eyes of the court, was that Santos was misrepresented by the new material. However, the *Santos* case also said a second factor, “the nature of the copyrighted work,” was historical: a series of improperly cultivated, presumably lean, videos that also became the object of Kimmel’s parody. *Santos'* interpretation recognized that these videos were “the primary material used in parody,” and that the claim was that they were not used to mislead but to poke fun. Thus, the only presumption that could come into play was for *good faith* at the beginning of the analysis, which in turn positioned the four‑factor race to continue on a safe path.

## Wider Implications for Platforms That Lean on Fictional Personas

“So what does all this mean for YouTubers, meme‑makers, and the next generation of “influencer comedians”?” Some who alleged was that the case simply re‑affirmed that one can *fake* a person or a brand as long as the portrayal is commentary and *not* an attempt to con the audience into believing a false fact. 

From a **copyright** perspective, the decision signals that the “additional room afforded to a creator to alter or transform content,” whether the transformation is simply news commentary or a fictional comedic narrative, enjoys unsurpassed flexibility. It also sets a precedent that the *transformation test* will still apply even when the subject is a fictionalized version of a real person. That allows platforms to build “customized clips” around public figures without having to fear a “bad faith” flag that might bar fair use.

In a practical sense, the ruling means that online platforms can keep offering customized joke videos for a fee, because a violin of deep‑fake or accumulated out‑of‑character clips still falls under the transformative ambit if the content is a satirical commentary. And because *Santos* made clear that good faith remains a presumptive notion, the onus remains on any litigant who thinks their use was a false representation to show otherwise. This nuance cleverly preserves the **defensive pitch** of the fair‑use defense while not abandoning the mechanisms that help preserve the economic welfare of the original creators, a delicate balance that copyright courts have long favoured.

Of course, it won’t be a vacation for all content creators. The *Santos* decision does not say that simply blowing a paper balloon around a real person makes a free‑speech statement immune to all claims. If a user professional or a platform tries to misrepresent a person in a way that harms that person’s reputation or a brand’s commercial viability, the analysis may still weigh all four factors differently, and look for *bad faith* or *defamation*. Yet as far as the Supreme Court’s own desire to keep transformations alive in the digital age goes, this case looks like a sign that the law is content to let people remix and parody even if the “facade” is false.

### Defamation, Fact, and the Role of Fiction

There are legitimate concerns about conflating *defamation* with *copyright*. *Santos* does not address defamation directly, but it underscores that the “false representation” argument is not “enough” to destroy a fair‑use defense. The only proper way to bring a defamation claim is to show that the denial of the present reality was *factual*, not comedic. That said, the law still reserves the opportunity to hold a defamed figure liable when the depiction is on their behalf, especially if the portrayal goes beyond the permissible “joking” category.

If we look at *Windham v. Castronovo*, a case that conjoined defamation and copyright, we see how the courts keep the two prongs distinct: a broadcast that truly misrepresents a factual statement is not found to be in the realm of fair use. The *Santos* court made it clear: it is not Kimmel’s verbal misrepresentations that break the law; it is *the expectation* that the audience understand the piece as a parody. If the audience is misled into thinking the fictional persona is the real Santos, a defamation concern may swirl. But that would be a distinct claim; *Santos* did not then ask for a defamation remedy—though, in the future, this could be a fighting point.

## Bottom Line

Santos’ attempt to have false representations of a "fictional" Trump-like figure eliminate the fair‑use defense is consistent with how the *Harper & Row* pronouncement has been interpreted: the “presumption of good faith” is a helpful assumption but not a blanket sedation. The 2nd Circuit emphasizes that transformative use stands on its head when you incorporate an original piece into a new, commentary‑driven format—even if the ending comments say the woodwork was done “for pay.” The court’s ruling fortifies the principle that a satirist can legitimately misrepresent or exaggerate a public figure without tripping a copyright blue‑card, so long as the mockery is transformative.

What this means for content creators is that, when building a persona or a comedic skit around a real person, the transformed work can stand firmly in the safe harbor of fair use, even when the portrayal is radically fabricated. It also highlights the continuing flexibility of the four‑factor framework: the law is not a conservative engine that will stop the press of a comedic knockout if it is unrelated to *trademark*, *fraud*, or *defamation*. 

In the end, *Santos* illustrates that the moral compass guiding *fair use* has always been held not by the smallest actors but by the broader questions of **transformation, public‑interest purpose, and repeated commentary**. It doesn't, however, rule out that *true bad faith*—for example, the fraudulently misaligned use of a copyrighted piece with an intent to sabotage the rights holder—can still be a relevant factor, especially when the misuse is pointed beyond art and satire. But for those looking to keep their brand on a frayed but protective “fair‑use” rope, a good‑faith, transformed parody will always be a viable path—regardless of whether the target admittedly embraced the role.
