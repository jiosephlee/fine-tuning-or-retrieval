Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Segregation Scruples: The ‘No Meaningful Segregable Portion’ Rule in FOIA  

## Why the Case Matters  

In the federal courts, FOIA disputes rarely hit the headlines.  What happens in *Foad Farahi v. FBI* (9‑C‑5243, 2025) is “normal” for the Agency and Litigants, but it is exceptional for the broader FOIA community because the court affirms that an entire 10,750‑page, 80‑CD FBI file will remain hidden from a private citizen under Exemption 7(A).  The ruling clarifies an elusive element of FOIA litigation: when does “reasonable segregation” fail because no useful, non‑exempt subset can be isolated?  For FOIA seekers, the answer has bite: the “no meaningful segregable portion” doctrine is not just a footnote; it can swallow an entire case.  For agencies, the ruling consolates the “deference” that the law affords them when they invoke national‑security exemptions, but it also reminds them that a mere categorical withdrawal can be insufficient.

The case also illustrates the fine line between “reasonable steps” to segregate information and the court’s discretion to accept “ex parte” affidavits when the subject matter is too sensitive to make public.  Meanwhile, it gives FOIA researchers a set of cautionary hard facts: large documents that intermix exempt and non‑exempt content are a unique challenge, and the court’s current precedent will require creative FOIA tactics if litigants want a bite of a sprawling dossier.

## Exemption 7(A) and the Deference Framework  

The FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) eliminates “records compiled for law enforcement purposes” when their release could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”  The Court’s logic is shaped by a body of law that has granted agencies a deference that rises from their technical expertise and the national‑security implications of the data.  In *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (CREW), the court explained that when an agency such as the FBI invokes Exemption 7(A), the agency’s conclusion deserves a “fair measure of deference” (as echoed in *Clemente v. FBI* and *Campbell v. DOJ*).  That deference is strongest for national‑security investigations, where the court has repeatedly urged that the executive branch not be tipped off by FOIA disclosure.  

The burden on the agency is to show, on the balance of probabilities, that the information was compiled for a law‑enforcement purpose and that its disclosure would interfere with *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings.  The district court in *Farahi* affirmed that the FBI’s updated affidavits—both public (the Hardy declaration) and ex parte (the Bender declaration)—clearly met the first two prongs.  Importantly, the court agreed that the continued existence of an ongoing investigation was integral to the exemption.  Those earlier conclusions set the stage for the second prong: the “reasonable steps” requirement for segregation.

## The “No Meaningful Segregable Portion” Doctrine  

FOIA requires a *reasonable* effort to segregate and release non‑exempt information.  The courts operationalized this through the “no meaningful segregable portion” doctrine, beginning in *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force* (Mead).  The Mead court held that when exempt and non‑exempt data are “inextricably intertwined” with each other—especially when exempt information appears inside the same word or line as non‑exempt material—it can be impossible to prune the file without destroying both the exempt and the useful parts.  The rule has been applied most famously in *Juarez v. DOJ* to justify withholding a record that could not be reasonably split.  The *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs* decision reiterated that the court may rely on agency affidavits that provide “reasonable specificity” on why segregation is impossible.

In *Farahi*, the court relied on Mead, Juarez, and Perioperative to support its conclusion that the 10,750 pages were a single, inseparable unit.  The FBI’s original claim was that the file “includes a significant number of recordings in which FBI agents documented Farahi’s connections to high‑level terrorists.”  That content is intrinsically linked to investigative narratives that the FBI professed to be still active.  Because the affidavit revealed that the susceptible elements—such as the detailed analytic narratives and the raw recordings—were interwoven with the exempt portions, the judge held that the file had no “meaningfully” segregable non‑exempt portions.  That conclusion satisfies the court’s “reasonable steps” standard because the agency could not provide any clear, workable method of carving out non‑exempt material without compromising the exhaustion of evidence useful for any future liability.

## How the Court Applied “Reasonable Steps” to the Farahi File  

The “reasonable steps” requirement is a routine standard, but it becomes knotty when the records are dense, convoluted, and heavily interhashed with sensitive content.  In *Farahi*, the court observed that the FBI had previously responded by providing category-based internal documents and affidavits also covered by Exemption 7(A).  Importantly, the judge noted that the agency’s *Bender* declaration was not a new sketch of the file’s contents; instead, it was a brief confirmation that no change had occurred since the *Hardy* affidavits—an admission that the investigations themselves remained ongoing to March 2023.  The court’s presumption that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden earlier meant that Bender’s lean testimony was sufficient to reaffirm that the exemption continued.

However, the court acknowledged that Farahi’s argument about the “vague” or “conclusory” nature of the Bender declaration should be weighed with the earlier, more detailed, written affidavits.  The judge therefore decided that the Bender declaration could stand as a clarifying update rather than a faulty primary assertion.  In so doing, the court upheld the agency’s rationality in explaining that the exempt content could not be presently segregated.  The ultimate message is that a single, clear declaration—whether ex parte or public—together with evidence that the examination is active, satisfies the reasonable‑steps requirement if the file’s structure makes segregation impossible.

## What FOIA Seekers Can Take from This Decision  

1. **Ask for Structured, Narrow Requests**  
   If you can anticipate that a record will be bulk‑withdrawn, split your request into *distinctly* categorized requests.  Use the *CREW* principle that categories must be sufficiently distinct that the court can evaluate the exemption for each one.  That way, if a category is wholly exempt, you still get the other categories.  When the record is a mass of mixed material, this tactic reduces the chances you will get “no‑meaningful‑segregable‑portion” at once.

2. **Pursue “Semi‑Open” or “List‑Other” Requests Early**  
   In contentious cases, FOIA practitioners often use a “list‑other” or “open‑content” request before the main one to identify potentially non‑exempt “blue‑cell” items (e.g., publicly available footnotes, metadata).  This strategy has a proven record in cases such as *Perioperative Services* where the plaintiff managed to isolate small fragments that survived the segregation test.

3. **Consider PAGs and Fascist‐like De‑Classifying Steps**  
   If the Federal Agencies provide ‘segregation logs’ or ‘meta‑analytic reports’ that break out exempt from non‑exempt content, these can be used in court to argue that a “meaningful segregable portion” exists.  The judge will examine whether the segmentation was indeed reasonable and not just a legal artifice.

4. **Plan for Ex Parte or Sealed Submissions**  
   The *Farahi* case underscores that ex parte affidavits are powerful tools for the government when dealing with the unknown aspects of a file.  FOIA plaintiffs cannot rely on full transparency in these contexts; instead, they may need to argue that the ex parte information is materially necessary and that it is being held under an existing precedent that limited its exposure.  Recognizing that, FOIA practitioners may try to link their requests to previously disclosed or published summaries to reduce reliance on sealed data.

5. **Ask the Court to Encourage Agency Disclosure of “Professional Segregation Records”**  
   Agencies can provide, in addition to the file itself, a “segregation plan” and accessibility logs showing why removal is not feasible.  Courts have varied in willingness to accept such plans; however, the *Farahi* opinion suggests that a brief but thorough explanation can be enough if the underlying cache truly intermixes exempt and non‑exempt content.

## Doctrinal Implications and Potential Congressional Reforms  

The *Farahi* ruling reaffirms a doctrine that regulates a very narrow but consequential sphere of FOIA practice: the “no meaningful segregable portion” analysis.  At the same time, it hints at an opportunity for Congress:

- **Clarify the “reasonable steps” Standard**  
  The FOIA remains half‑shrouded when it comes to the agency’s burden of demonstrating that segregation is impossible.  A statutory standard clarifying the acceptable linguistic and technical means of segregation might streamline courts’ decisions and reduce the reliance on highly technical analysis that keeps FOIA litigants in the shadows.

- **Enhance the “Category” Requirement**  
  Congress could require that agencies provide a *catalogue* of each category in which every document can be placed, with accompanying justification.  This would reduce the need for ex parte affidavits and would improve FOIA transparency for ordinary citizens.

- **Publicly Announced “Standard Templates” for Segregation**  
  Another promising legislative angle is to make standard segregation templates public to guide agencies and litigants, lowering the cost of compliance on both sides.

For now, the *Farahi* court places a low wall in the path of FOIA seekers whose files are heavily intertwined with exempt material.  It also signals that the “reasonable steps” doctrine no longer is a flexible policy tool but a geographic constraint on the kind of FOIA litigation that agencies can effectively defend.

## Conclusion  

The *Farahi v. FBI* decision may appear to be a routine adjudication of national‑security secrecy, but it sharpened the contours of FOIA’s most furtive exemption.  It underscored that, when exempt and non‑exempt content are inextricably linked, the court will let the agency keep the whole thing, because splitting it would destroy at least one side of the story.  This offers FOIA practitioners a cautionary tale: large, sticky files are almost guaranteed to be withheld, unless the agency provides concrete, objectively “meaningful” segregation.  And it offers national‑security officials a confirmation that their authority to withhold under Exemption 7(A) will not be lightly brushed aside by a court looking for a small slice of sensitive data.  

Hence, those who adapt to the *Farahi* reality—through meticulous, narrowly tailored requests and ingenuity in dust‑picking the sparse non‑exempt nuggets—are the ones who will survive the FOIA gauntlet.  Meanwhile, the balance of deference and scrutiny remains tilt‑gear for future FOIA takers and the agencies they confront.
