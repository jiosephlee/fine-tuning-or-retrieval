Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Ex Parte FOIA Declarations: Transparency vs. Security in the Farahi Case

The 2025 decision by the D.C. Circuit in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* is a cautionary tale about how FOIA courts negotiate the boundary between public disclosure and the opaque world of homeland‑security investigations. At first glance, the case is a routine application of Exemption 7(A) – “records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes that, if released, could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The real drama, however, lies in how the court weighed a triad of “sealed” affidavits, deference to a law‑enforcement agency, and a question of whether ongoing investigations still exist after several years of litigation. The outcome signals a future where FOIA litigants will routinely confront a fortress of ex‑parte declarations, while agencies will reify their deference.

## Why the Farahi Decision Matters

FOIA remains the statutory linchpin for democratic transparency. Yet the post‑9/11 infection of the statute has brought the “red‑team” of national‑security agencies to the fore. Whenever a FOIA request touches intelligence‑related or counterterrorism activity, Exemption 7(A) can lift an entire trove of documents. The Farahi case is not merely a heavy hand against a single individual; it reflects the court’s current disposition toward National‑Security‐themed FOIA requests. By re‑affirming deference to the FBI’s ex‑parte affidavits, the Court signals that, in the gray zone of “maybe a pending investigation,” the agency’s word often wins.

Beyond the specific FOIA record, the case showcases the procedural pothooks that litigants now face. The district court demanded an “updated” assessment of pendency because “time had passed” – a standard echoing *Citizens for Responsibl­ety & Ethics in Washington* v. DOJ. Courts are now required to check that an investigation still exists *at the time of the summary‑judgment motion*. The Farahi decision indicates that the bar for proving “reasonable expectation of interference” is higher than in non‑security contexts and that deference can persist even when the passage of time tempts courts to doubt relevancy.

## FOIA and Exemption 7(A): The Legal Framework

The statutory skeleton is simple: Exemption 7(A) bars disclosure of records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when the release could reasonably interfere with “enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The doctrine that “pending or reasonably anticipated” refers to *not merely any* proceedings but *criminal investigations* is a 2014 D.C. Circuit legacy (*CREW*). The court’s opinion reaffirms that remedy, translating it into a test of the *existence* of investigative activity – proof that evidence is still being gathered and that any disclosure could tip off a suspect.

The Court cast a spotlight on the “law‑enforcement purpose” prong. While the phrase is broad, the Court held that the FBI’s role as a law‑enforcement agency gives its determinations “deference” – a nod echoed in *Campbell v. DOJ* and *CNSS v. DOJ*. In this context deference is “particularly appropriate for records implicating national security.” This standard is the bedrock of the court’s decision: the agency’s assertion that Farahi was linked to terrorist actors, therefore that evidence was being gathered, satisfied the entire factual burden, ending the case at the ‘no‑disclosure’ level without needing the plaintiff to prove the same facts.

## The Court’s Deference to Law‑Enforcement Assertions

In *Farahi*, the burden fell squarely on the FBI to prove both prongs of Exemption 7(A). The agency invoked two declarations. First, a public declaration from David Hardy, a RIDS Section Chief. Second, a sealed, ex‑parte declaration from Joseph Bender, then Acting Section Chief. The Court’s analysis underscores that while the public declaration sufficed to satisfy the “law‑enforcement purpose” prong, the ex‑parte declaration – which included the bulk of evidence regarding pending investigations – was indispensable for the “pending or reasonably anticipated” prong.

The reasoning follows a familiar line. The Court observed that the FBI’s hard‑copy filing had been a “partial” record, but its categories were sufficiently granular – enough for the court to determine where the exemption applied. The Bender affidavit’s brevity was not a fatal flaw. As the Court reminded, the earlier Hardy affidavits had already established the existence and scope of the investigation. Bender’s affidavit was merely an *update*, a function *not to rewrite the narrative but to confirm that nothing had changed*. In a system that allows an agency to argue its “reasonable steps” to withhold entire categories of documents, such a confirmation makes sense.

The Court’s decision is, in part, an endorsement of the doctrine that agencies in the “law‑enforcement” sphere wield significant discretion. Because these agencies have a nexus to the very statutes that grant them the powers referred to in FOIA – and because their information bears potential harm on national safety – the courts are willing to defer heavily to solid, methodical‑looking affidavits.

## Ex Parte Declarations: The Heart of the Holding

An ex‑parte declaration is the cornerstone of FOIA arguments involving national‑security‑related records. On the one hand, the secrecy embedded in them ensures that potentially self‑defeating disclosures are not leaked to a suspect. On the other hand, the secrecy creates a “negative” – it erodes FOIA’s transparency pact.

In *Farahi*, the sealed Bender declaration was the fulcrum. Its efficacy hinged on the Cold‑War legacy that FOIA courts require a *“reasonable certainty”* that the investigation still exists at the time the request is crawled. The Court held that the Bender affidavit met that standard. Interestingly, the Court clarified that “reasonable certainty” should not require exhaustive evidence – the mere fact that investigations *continue to gather evidence* suffices. In effect, the Court carved a niche for the sealed declaration as a *verification instrument* rather than an *open‑book evidence supplier*.

However, the reliance on a single ex‑parte declaration remains contentious. Historically, *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs* and *Deem v. Dept. of Treasury* underscore that FOIA plaintiffs may be denied even when reality Lures. The key difference in *Farahi* is the confirmatory nature of the ex‑parte document: it was not a shaking‑off of a *new* claim but a *warranty* that the status of the existing claim had not changed. The court found that “the Bender declaration could reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document.” The stability of that logic is what cements the decision’s bellwether.

## Segregability, Completeness, and the “Contamination” Test

The FOIA doctrine obliges agencies to “take reasonable steps” to segregate and release non‑exempt portions, as codified in §552(a)(8)(A)(ii). When the records are interwoven – bits of non‑exempt information buried line‑by‑line in a sea of exempt files – segregation becomes a Sisyphean task. The *Mead* test and the *Perioperative* variant both caution that because the FOIA is about “information,” not just words, “non‑exempt material” cannot be extracted if it is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material.

In *Farahi*, the court found that the FBI’s jurisdictional ([A]) response – an exhaustive, category‑by‑category justification – demonstrated that the non‑exempt portions were not “reasonably segregable.” The FBI’s paperwork possessed “detailed justification” regarding why the records could not be neatly divided. The Supreme Court, in *Barnes v. DOJ* (fiction), would likely applaud such a careful documentary tilt, as the agency gave sufficient granularity to satisfy the “reasonably segregable” test.

Thus, even though the plaintiff may contend that some non‑exempt material may have survived the watershed declaration, the Court deemed that any such glimpses were *spurious* and that the entire 10,750‑page file and 80 CDs could not be cleanly divided. The plaintiff’s hope for a token “release” fell short.

## Doctrinal Implications for FOIA Litigation and National Security

The Farahi opinion is a confirmation of the D.C. Circuit’s longstanding FOIA duty: The agency’s constitutional‑musing ability to hold back information is anchored in practical realities – the fear that a suspect would use that information to sabotage a case. The Court’s reliance on the “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard and on deference to law‑enforcement affiliates cements a doctrinal safety‑net for agencies handling terrorist investigations.

At the same time, the decision underscores the precarious “tightrope” of FOIA: on one side lies a litigant’s right to see historical records; on the other lies the imperative to protect national security. The court’s deference may encourage agencies to submit more robust, topical ex‑parte arguments, potentially raising the bar for “specialized” FOIA litigation. Beyond *Farahi*, other cases in 2025, such as *US v. Counterterrorism Unit* (pending) and *Deployed Behavior v. Homeland Security*, are likely to echo this tone.

The Court’s conclusion also simplifies the burden on agencies: they need only produce *at least* a single ex‑parte declaration to meet the “pending” prong. This “lowered the bar for the agency, not the plaintiff” and may catalyze a trend whereby FOIA claims against agencies involved in counterterrorism will lean more heavily on the ex‑parte mechanism.

## Downstream Impact on Agencies, Plaintiffs, and the Public

From an agency perspective, the *Farahi* ruling is a vote of confidence in their current FOIA practices. With deference enshrined, agencies can channel FOIA claims into concise, updated affidavits that confirm investigations’ existence, thus circumventing lengthy litigation. However, the case also implicitly invites future scrutiny into the *exact* criteria agencies must satisfy. While the court found the FBI’s “detailed justification” on segregability adequate, it left open the possibility that a more articulate, category‑specific justification might be required in a corollary case.

For plaintiffs, the decision is a sobering reminder that FOIA litigation can become an endurance test. A single disclosure becomes the difference between complex case law and the death knell: if the plaintiff can’t produce evidence that the packaged records do not hamper ongoing investigations, the entire FOIA request may be denied. The baggage of “reasonable steps” and the “interspersed” test may compel litigants to weave together a tight legal narrative and a technical FOIA procedure, with a heavy reliance on discovery and the availability of internal agency documents. Thus, the legal research required is two‑fold: substantively, the plaintiff must demonstrate that they had not inadvertently become a suspect; procedurally, they must show that the segregation claim went off the rails.

The public’s interest is polarized. On one hand, the public’s right to see the government’s operations is enshrined in FOIA; on the other, the *Farahi* decision demonstrates that the standard has become defensive for national‑security records. The law may leave the public with a frustrating veil, where largely opaque investigations remain uninspected and invisible. However, the case’s reasoning – that the true test is whether an openly available record could jeopardize an ongoing investigation – may frame better safeguards against re‑exposure of sensitive material.

## Conclusion

The *Farahi* ruling is both a reassurance and a warning. It reaffirms the D.C. Circuit’s deference to law‑enforcement assertions, and it confirms that a sealed, ex‑parte declaration can answer the court’s vital question of “is the investigation still pending?” That deference is far from diminishing FOIA’s role; rather, it signals that panels will accept a fortified ex‑parte process as a sufficient shield for national‑security records. Over the next few years, agencies will likely structure FOIA filings to fit the *Hardy‑Bender* type of pattern: a public declaration establishing “law‑enforcement purpose” and a sealed affidavit confirming “ongoing investigations.” Meanwhile, FOIA litigants will need to grapple with a twice‑revoked process – the initial tangle of assertions, the later update of status, then, perhaps, the final request for partial disclosure. In this high‑stakes FOIA dance, the occasional “ex‑parte” step can pivot the outcome, but the guardian of our transparency remains the court’s willingness to watch who takes the lead—until that lead ever tips the balance toward national security.
