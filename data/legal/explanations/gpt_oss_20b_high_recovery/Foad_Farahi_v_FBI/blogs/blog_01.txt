Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Time's Trouble: When Does Exemption 7(A) Lose Its Clamp?

## Introduction  
On September 30 , 2025 the D.C. Circuit reaffirmed a doctrine that has long plagued FOIA litigants who seek records tied to counter‑terrorism investigations: unless an investigation is truly *pending* or *reasonably anticipated*, Exemption 7(A) cannot be invoked. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, 839 F. Supp. 3d 1 (D.D.C. 2023) the court held that the FBI’s declarations—made over a five‑year span and updated in 2023—were sufficient to establish that the subject’s file was still bound in an ongoing law‑enforcement inquiry. The decision thereby distances the statutory “pendency” standard from a mere fact of persecution or evidence gathering that eventually came to a halt, and instead ties it to the *agency’s own assessment* of the investigation’s status.

For FOIA practitioners, the ruling is a stark reminder that the clock on a request does not, in itself, weaken a government’s exemption. It also illustrates how courts will defer to agencies, especially those with counter‑terrorism mandates, when the only way to decide a case is to hear sealed affidavits that lubricate their ability to keep dossiers hidden. Let's unpack how the court reached its decision, why the “pending or reasonably anticipated” test matters, and what this means for future FOIA litigants whittling down the boundaries of secrecy.

## The Pendency Doctrine and Its Historical Roots  
Exemption 7(A) strings a government agency’s right to keep law‑enforcement records from an unrelated, public‑interest right to disclosure. In *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. Department of Justice* (CREW), 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014), the court defined “enforcement proceedings” as proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (Rev. 2014). The held that an agency could only invoke the exemption if evidence under review still had the potential to be used against a suspect later—an evaluation that can shift over time.

*Dr. Juarez v. Department of Justice*, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008), cemented the idea that stalled investigations, even if the agency remains gathering evidence, can still satisfy the pendency requirement so long as the investigation is represented as impending. The decision did not elaborate on how long after a request the “reasonably anticipated” bar could be presumed true, leaving a vacuum. The Farahi case is the first Dominican that maps the gap onto the typical scenario where a FOIA request is filed, responses take years, and the agency offers to renew its claim of existing investigations.

## Farahi, the FBI, and the Court’s Application  

Farahi was an Iranian national who, since 2002, has repeatedly sought admission to the U.S. while subject to repeated removal, asylum, and other immigration proceedings in which DHS cited FBI evidence of ties to terrorist networks. The FBI, upon receiving a FOIA request, located a vast file of 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Farahi sued after the agency missed the statutory deadline. Over the next handful of years the FBI reviewed its holdings and moved for summary judgment twice—once in 2019 and again in 2023—submitting updated affidavits that the underlying investigations were still active.

In 2020 the district court granted the FBI’s first motion on the face of it, after *“seemingly”* showing that the investigations were pending or reasonably anticipated and that the FBI could not categorize its materials in a segregable fashion. In 2023, the court denied the FBI’s second summary‑judgment motion because the FBI had not supplied updated evidence on whether the investigations still existed. The Supreme Court of the United States had never held that time alone imposes a new pendency standard; the Farahi decision respects this. By validating the FBI’s updated 2023 affidavit that the subject’s file “remained ongoing,” the panel left the pendency question in the hands of the agency—yet they tied it to the assistant agent’s statement that “investigations remain ongoing” and the confirmation by a higher‑ranking bureaucrat that they were still “ongoing as of March 2023.”

The court supported the FBI’s claims by stating that its declarations met the “reasonable steps” required under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii): the FBI’s statements explained that the allegations were linked to active collaborations with foreign terrorist organizations, which *“could reasonably be expected to interfere with the investigations.”* The court frankly mulled that this assessment satisfied the pendency bar per CREW and Juarez, noting that a “concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding” continues to exist when an agency argues that evidence is still being collected for a future prosecution. In short, if the FBI believes it is still compiling evidence—even if no charges appear on the docket—the exemption remains applicable.

The decision also addressed the longstanding FOIA boarding question of *segregability*. It found that the FBI and the court had satisfied the burden in that *“no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.”* In enough detail—such as the mixture of classified and unclassified language, or the widespread “interweaving” of records—such a determination is hard to override unless the government can prove a workable set of categories that clearly separate exempt from non‑exempt portions.

## Critiques, Alternative Views, and the Weight of Ex Parte Affidavits  
Despite the court’s carefully circumscribed deference to the FBI, the reliance on ex‑parte affidavits draws a familiar criticism. FOIA literature has long warned of the risk that governments can promise secrecy to themselves by asserting “informal” or “secret” continuity of investigations. The *Farahi* decision, however, sidestepped the worry by clarifying that the FBI did not generate the pendency claim from scratch; it simply updated earlier public and hidden declarations. That narrative may be persuasive given that earlier filings already acknowledged the *ongoing* principal, but if Farahi had truly alleged that investigations had stopped, the court would have likely found a gap. The more pressing question is whether a FOIA defendant can continue to protect wide swaths of evidence more than a decade after a request ran due to the *pending or reasonably anticipated* bar. 

In the near‑term, this decision underscores that *time alone does not terminate an exemption.* This places the onus on FOIA seekers to move more aggressively: They must request no‑time‑limit statements from the agency, or produce evidence that the investigation actually ceased—perhaps a notice from a superior or a de‑classified briefing—before the requested records can be released. At the same time, the case signals that the agency’s own assessment, pursuant to the statutory envelope, can carry weight even when the public is left in the dark about the investigation’s present status.

## Broader Implications and Onward Paths  

The ruling confirms that JEHO’s policy of *blanket confidentiality* for counter‑terrorism files remains robust. For FOIA litigants the stakes are twofold. First, the decision re‑affirms that the pendency test is a moving target; FOIA practitioner must treat an agency’s *latest* affidavit as the starting point, not the original June 2020 filing. Second, the case illustrates the persistent demand for FOIA attorneys to contest the credibility of ex‑parte affidavits in the absence of tangible evidence about an investigation’s status. The Court’s brief reliance on the Bender declaration highlights how an updated affidavit can suffice, even when it is terse, if the contexts—i.e., the earlier Hardy affidavits—afford it.

From a doctrinal perspective, *Farahi* solidifies that Exemption 7(A) is *not a post‑hoc justification* for witholding records indefinitely. It is tethered, albeit loosely, to the agency’s *present belief* that evidence is still being gathered. In times when counter‑terrorism investigations can outlast a decade, the law’s “reasonable anticipation” bar could become a universal de facto excuse for blanket secrecy. The question remains: Will the standard evolve to set a *temporal ceiling* on the pendency claim? As courts continue to turn to the *reasonable steps* language and the *segregation* requirement, we may eventually see a friction point—as FOIA scholars hope—to force agencies to offer more granular disclosures.

Until then, *Foad Farahi* will sit in the annals of FOIA cases as an example of the state’s power to bind the public’s right to information with a procedural label—“ongoing investigations” that, legal or not, remain sticky. FOIA seekers should watch how the next wave of grand challenges to 7(A) unfolds, and they should be prepared to ask agencies for timelines, internal memos, or the testimony of individuals who are not the subject of the file itself. Only then will the cap on these “pending” or “reasonably anticipated” investigations be tested on its merits rather than the method in which the government chooses to assert them.
