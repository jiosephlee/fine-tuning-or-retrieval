Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What evidence must an agency provide to prove a record was compiled for law enforcement purposes under FOIA Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
In the Foad Farahi v. FBI case, the court said the FBI had to show that Farahi's file was compiled in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject's association with individuals directly involved in such violations. I am unsure what kind of evidence is required to satisfy the compiled for law enforcement purposes part of Exemption 7(A). Does the agency need to show a direct nexus to a specific enforcement action, or is a general statement of investigative intent enough? What standards do courts use to evaluate this claim?
Answer:
**Short answer**

An agency must show that the record was put together *in the course of a law‑enforcement investigation*.  The court in *Farahi v. FBI* found that a statement by a senior FBI official that the file was compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism…to include pending law‑enforcement proceedings” was sufficient.  The agency does **not** have to identify a particular pending case; it just has to establish a *reasonable, specific* nexus between the investigation and a law‑enforcement duty.  Courts review such affidavits de novo, giving the agency some deference because it is a law‑enforcement body, but still require that the nexus be real, not merely a vague claim.

---

### 1.  What is the “compiled‑for” element?

FOIA § 552(b)(7)(A) protects records that were “*compiled for law‑enforcement purposes*.”  The 2025 D.C. Circuit distilled the standard in *Farahi*:

> “Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its determination on this point is entitled to a fair measure of deference… The government must establish **a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties,’** as well as a **connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’**”  
> *Farahi*, J.A. 42 (citing *Campbell* and *Pratt v. Webster*).

In other words, the record must be linked to an investigation that is **intended to serve a law‑enforcement purpose**—e.g., gathering evidence for a possible arrest, prosecution, or other enforcement action.

---

### 2.  General investigative intent – is it enough?

The *Farahi* decision shows that a **general statement of investigative intent can satisfy the court** when it is supported by reasonably specific information.  The FBI’s public declaration—“*…compiled in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism…to include pending law‑enforcement proceedings*”—was deemed sufficient to establish the required nexus (J.A. 42).  The accompanying ex‑parte declaration supplied “consequential detail” about the nature and scope of the investigation (J.A. 49).  The court explicitly noted that the FBI does not have to point to a *particular* case number:

> “A court may rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld … cannot be further segregated.”  
> *Farahi*, J.A. 139 (quoting *Juarez*).

Thus, a single, well‑articulated statement—made by a competent official and backed by internal evidence that the investigation is ongoing—can establish the “compiled‑for” element.

---

### 3.  What about a *direct* tie to a specific proceeding?

No specific case or indictment must be named.  What is required is that the record be linked to an investigation **that is pending or reasonably anticipated**.  The court held that “pending” includes any ongoing criminal investigation, not necessarily one in a federal courtroom:

> “Ongoing criminal investigations count as ‘proceedings’ for these purposes… the government may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their ‘existence.’”  
> *Farahi*, J.A. 49–50 (quoting *CREW* and *Juarez*).

In *Farahi*, the FBI’s declarations that the investigation “remains ongoing” satisfied this requirement (J.A. 131). The court therefore did not require a specific indictment or case number; it accepted the evidence that the investigative activity itself was underway.

---

### 4.  How do courts evaluate the claim?

1. **De novo review** (the burden remains with the agency).  
2. **Deference to law‑enforcement expertise** – the court will accept the agency’s own affidavits unless they are conclusory or unrelated to the investigation.  
3. **Nexus test** – the agency must show a *reasonable, specific* connection between the record and a law‑enforcement investigation.  
4. **“Pending or reasonably anticipated”** – for the interference component, the court looks for evidence that the investigation is still in progress or will probably turn into an enforcement proceeding.  
5. **Segregability** – once the “compiled‑for” and interference elements are satisfied, the agency must show why the exempt portion cannot be separated from non‑exempt material (the FBI complied with that in *Farahi*).

The *Farahi* decision emphasised that a *vague* statement is not enough; the agency’s evidence must be **reasonably specific** and, when combined with other documentation, show that the record is genuinely part of an active law‑enforcement effort.

---

### Bottom line for your FOIA request

- **Show that the record was created “in furtherance of a law‑enforcement investigation.”**  
- **Provide a reasonably specific description of that investigation** (e.g., “investigation of suspected ties to a designated terrorist organization” or “interrogations relating to a national‑security investigation”).  
- **Affirm that the investigation is ongoing or will likely lead to a future enforcement action**.  
- **If possible, give an internal confirmation (affidavit, memo, etc.)** that the investigation is still active; if the agency’s officials say the case “remains ongoing,” that is generally acceptable.

If you can produce that kind of evidence—or convince the agency to submit it—then the “compiled‑for” portion of Exemption 7(A) should be satisfied.  The *Farahi* opinion makes it clear that the court is willing to accept a general, well‑substantiated statement of investigative intent rather than demanding a concrete, named prosecution.

### How is pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings determined for FOIA Exemption 7(A) when an investigation has been open for years?
Question:
The court highlighted that the FBI’s Bender declaration confirmed that the investigations remain ongoing as of March 2023, but Farahi argued that the passage of time undermines the claim of pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. How do courts apply the standard over such a long period? Is a mere statement of ongoing sufficient, or must the agency demonstrate a realistic expectation that a proceeding will be taken within a specified time? What case law informs this analysis?
Answer:
**Pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings under FOIA 7(A)**  
The test is set out in the statute and in the D.C. Circuit’s own precedent:

* “records *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* … *can reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings*… that are **pending or reasonably anticipated**.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A) (hereafter “Exemption 7(A)”).  
* The District Court in **Crews v. DOJ** (746 F.3d 1082, 1096‑99) held that “pending or reasonably anticipated” reads as “the investigation is **in progress** or that the agency has a **realistic expectation** that a proceeding will eventually be taken.”  
* The Circuit has repeatedly reiterated that an *ongoing criminal investigation* is a “proceeding” for the purposes of Exemption 7(A) and that the agency may satisfy the pendency test simply by “attesting to its existence.” *Juarez v. DOJ* (518 F.3d 54, 59‑61).

**How the standard is applied over a long‑running case**

1. **Timing of the evidence** – The court evaluates the agency’s evidence *as of the date the evidence was filed*.  The Bender declaration was dated “as of March 2023,” so the agency’s claim that the investigations “remain ongoing” was current at the point of review.  The court expressly noted that this “updated declaration” merely reaffirmed a status already established by the earlier Hardy declarations and therefore did not have to be independent proof on its own.  See *Foad Farahi*, 949 F.4th at 138–39.

2. **Sufficiency of a statement of “ongoing”** – A plain statement that the investigations are still active can satisfy the *pending* portion if the court has reason to believe that the agency continues to gather evidence that could be used in a future case.  In *Crews* the Circuit held that “the existence of an ongoing investigation … is enough” for a pending claim.  The Farahi court rejected the argument that the mere wording was “conclusory,” reasoning that it was read *in the context* of the earlier, more detailed declarations.  The agency need not predict a specific hearing date; it only has to demonstrate that a “concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding” still exists.  The majority concluded that the FBI’s 2023 confirmation that its investigations “remained ongoing” and that its ‘concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding’ continued to exist satisfied that requirement. § IV B, 949 F.4th at 145‑47.

3. **“Reasonably anticipated”** – The standard is not a “reasonable expectation to prosecute within *x* months.”  The agency may point to a continuing investigative effort that could, at a later date, give rise to a criminal case.  The Circuit has treated a *long‑term investigative series*—with no immediate hearing yet—as “reasonably anticipated” provided that the agency can still predict that it intends to use the data in a future prosecution.  The Farahi decision reinforces that principle by accepting the hard‑dated Bender declaration as proof that the investigative effort had not been discontinued.

**Bottom line**

- A **single, unqualified “ongoing” statement can be enough** only if it is part of a broader factual matrix that shows the agency is still collecting and analyzing evidence that could be used in a future case.  
- The agency **does not have to set a timetable**; it must simply present a realistic expectation that a proceeding will be taken *at some point* in the foreseeable future.  
- The precedent that supports this view is chiefly *Crews* (pending or reasonably anticipated), *Juarez* (ongoing investigations count as proceedings), and *Mapother* (the agency bears the burden of showing a real possibility of a proceeding). 

In *Foad Farahi*, the court applied those cases to the Bender declaration: because Bender’s statement was an update confirming that nothing had changed, the earlier, more detailed declarations already satisfied the agency’s burden, and the “ongoing” assertion was sufficient.  Thus, the passage of time alone does not undermine the pendency or anticipation requirement; what matters is that the agency still has a current, plausible expectation that its investigations will eventually lead to enforcement.

### What level of judicial deference is applied to the FBI’s Exemption 7(A) determinations in national-security FOIA cases?
Question:
The opinion states that the government's determination is entitled to deference, especially in national-security contexts. But how is that deference quantified? Are there any limits or optional standards, such as deference only if the determination is reasonable or non-arbitrary? How do cases like Campbell v. DOJ, CNSS v. DOJ, and others guide the court's deference? When can a court override such a determination?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
In national‑security FOIA cases the courts give a *preponderance*‑based, “reasonable‑basis” deference to an agency’s Exemption 7(A) finding—especially when the agency is a law‑enforcement or national‑security body such as the FBI.  The agency must satisfy the statutory burden that (1) the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and (2) their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.”  If the agency meets that burden, the court will give the agency’s determination “preponderant weight”; it can still overturn the determination only when the agency’s evidence is insufficient, arbitrary, or plainly erroneous.  

---

### 1.  What the opinion says about deference

> *“Because the FBI specializes in law‑enforcement, **its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference**.”*  
> *“Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security.”* – Farahi, J.A. 49–50

The court couching deference in “entitled to deference” is based on the agency’s specialized expertise.  The deference is **not absolute**; the agency still bears the **statutory burden** under § 552(a)(4)(B).  The court therefore reviews the agency’s findings de‑novo (see *Reps. Comm. v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361, D.C. Cir. 2021), looking strictly to whether the agency provided a **reasonable, factual basis** for the exemption.

---

### 2.  The standard that quantifies the deference

| Element | What the court requires | How deference is applied |
|---------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Compiled for law‑enforcement** | Agency must connect the material to a *law‑enforcement function* (e.g., the FBI’s investigations of terrorism). | The agency’s factual record (Hardy, Bender, etc.) is treated as “good evidence.”  If satisfied, the court gives it pre‑ponderant weight. |
| **Interference with pending / reasonably anticipated proceedings** | Agency must show the record *could reasonably* interfere (e.g., by tipping off targets). | The court applies the *reasonable‑expectation* test (cf. *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 59; *CNSS v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 927).  If the agency’s evidence meets the statutory threshold, the court respects the exemption. |
| **Reasonable steps to segregate non‑exempt material** | Agency must explain why exempt and non‑exempt portions are “inextricably intertwined.” | The court evaluates the justification and, if satisfactory, will not release any portion. |

The standard resembles a **“reasonable‑basis” check**: if the agency’s evidence shows that the record *does* meet the statutory elements, the court treats it as *credible* unless the record is shown to be grossly incorrect.

---

### 3.  Guidance from earlier cases

| Case | What it teaches about deference |
|------|---------------------------------|
| **Campbell v. DOJ**, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998) | The court explicitly said the FBI “is entitled to deference” when invoking Exemption 7(A), especially where national security is involved. |
| **CNSS v. DOJ**, 331 F.3d 918 (D.C. Cir. 2003) | Reinforced that the agency’s determination “is viewed with great deference given its national‑security stakes.”  It clarified that an agency’s *ex parte* affidavit can be accepted if it is “reasonably specific.” |
| **Juarez v. DOJ**, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008) | The court held the agency may withhold the entire record when the exempt portion is “inextricably intertwined” with the non‑exempt.  This establishes that deference also extends to *segregation* decisions, not just to the mere invocation of the exemption. |
| **CREW v. DOJ**, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014) | The court emphasized that “the vague nature” of a statement that investigations remain ongoing is insufficient; the evidence must be specific.  Farahi relied on this to scrutinize Bender’s brief declaration. |
| **Reps. Comm. v. FBI**, 3 F.4th 350 (D.C. Cir. 2021) | Affirms the *de novo* standard for summary‑judgment review of FOIA determinations, even when the agency claims deference. |

These cases, referenced in *Farahi*, frame the deference: it is **strong when the agency’s evidence satisfies the statutory pillars**, but **limited by the need for factual specificity**.

---

### 4.  Limits on the deference

* **The agency’s evidence must be *reasonable* and *non‑arbitrary***.  
  *“The agency must show ... the records could reasonably be expected to interfere …”* (Farahi, J.A. 49).  Mere assertions without evidence do not satisfy the burden.

* **The agency must articulate the *specific* reason it believes the interference is reasonably expected**.  
  Campbell and CNSS require “reasonably specific” testimony; CREW warns against “vague assertions.”

* **The agency must demonstrate that the paperwork is *intertwined* or that *segregability* fails**.  
  If the agency fails to show that the exempt material can be separated (or can’t find a concise segregation method), the court will accept the blanket withholding; this is a *limited* exercise of deference.

* **The deference applies only to the *factual determination* of the exemption, not to the *procedural* aspects** (e.g., the timing of responses).  The court can still correct procedural errors or order remedial action (as it did in *Farahi* when it required updated declarations).

---

### 5.  When a court can override the agency’s determination

| Condition | Hook from the opinion/cases | How the court acts |
|-----------|----------------------------|--------------------|
| **Insufficient evidence** | If the agency’s affidavits are “mere speculation” or lack detail (cf. *CREW*). | The court finds the statutory burden unmet and denies the exemption. |
| **Arbitrary or illogical assertions** | The court said the Bender declaration was “conclusory” but acceptable only if it confirmed prior evidence.  If the agency’s assertion were wholly unsupported, the court would reject it. | The court rejects the exemption and orders disclosure. |
| **Segregation fails** | *Juarez* requires proof that exempt and non‑exempt portions cannot be separated. | The court may either withhold all or, if the agency shows reasonably segregable sections, order a partial release. |
| **Exemption misapplies** | Exemption 7(A) only protects records *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* that can interfere with “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings (Farahi, J.A. 49). | If the records were merely investigative notes or not connected to a prospective case, the exemption collapses. |
| **Policy discretion expired** | When the “duration” of a pending proceeding lapses or future proceedings are no longer anticipated (not shown in *Farahi* but a logical extension). | The court can waive the exemption after the <10‑year statutory window or loss of an anticipated proceeding. |

Thus, the court exercises its review power to “uphold the statutory balance”—protecting sensitive information *only* when the agency satisfies the statutory requisites.  If the agency’s burden is not met, the court has the final say, even over the agency’s asserted deference.

---

### Bottom line

- **Deference in FOIA Exemption 7(A) is a *reasonable‑based* belief that the agency has met its statutory burden,** not a "ruling over the agency."  
- **The court applies “de novo” review** but gives **preponderant weight** to the agency’s factual record when it is *specific, reasonable, and non‑arbitrary.*  
- **The deference is limited** in that the agency must **prove** that the records are both *compiled for enforcement* and that their release would *reasonably interfere* with *pending or reasonably anticipated* proceedings.  
- **A court can override** any determination that fails to satisfy those statutory elements, is arbitrary, or is based on insufficient evidence—cases like *CREW* and *Juarez* demonstrate the boundaries.  

When you see a court say the FBI’s Exemption 7(A) determination “is entitled to deference,” think: *The court trusts the FBI’s judgment so long as it is backed by a clear, reasonable factual basis.* If the FBI’s record is flimsy, the court will not accept it.

### What qualifies as a properly distinct category of documents for FOIA Exemption 7(A), and how do courts prevent overbroad categorization?
Question:
The court noted that categories must be sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the claimed exemption applies to each category. In the Farahi case, the FBI used several categories but lacked detail. How do courts decide whether a category is overbroad? What criteria must the agency provide to justify the use of a category rather than itemizing documents? Is a brief description enough or does it require more specific information?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under § 552(b)(7)(A), exemption 7(A) can shield *records that were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes *and whose disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”  The Civil‑Rights‑Watch (CREW) decision set the procedural bar that an agency’s categorization of withheld material must be **“sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the claimed exemption applies to each category”** (746 F.3d 1088; 746 F.3d 1096).  The court in *Farahi* applied that standard and found the FBI’s categories acceptable, because the FBI furnished a *detailed justification* rather than a flat, one‑word label.

| Question | How courts assess it | What the agency must supply | Typical Level of Detail |
|----------|---------------------|-----------------------------|------------------------|
| **Is a category overbroad?** | 1. Does the description lump together exempt *and* non‑exempt items in a way that the court cannot isolate the exempt portion? <br>2. Are the boundaries of the category clear enough to apply the statutory test to each subset? | 1. A minimum of a written definition that **identifies the content or scope** of the category.<br>2. Evidence that the category **does not mix materially** exempt and non‑exempt items (e.g., a single “record of ongoing investigations” is fine only if the court can see that all items in that set are exempt). | When the description is **specific enough** to let a judge separate the exempt part from the non‑exempt – a short paragraph or a set of definitions, not an ellipsis. |
| **Why use a category instead of itemizing?** | *Fed. Reg.* allows categorization when itemization is impracticable; the burden is on the agency to show that a category is a *reasonable* way to segregate.  (Farahi: “...the FBI’s 'detailed justification' for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable … satisfied the agency’s burden.”) | 1. An explanation that **segregation by individual line or sheet would be “inexorable” or uneconomical**.<br>2. A coarse‑grained but **legally meaningful** distinction (e.g., “Date‑range test‑imon” vs. “Contact‑log”). | The agency can simply state “the file is divided into *four* distinct segments: 1) Investigative notes, 2) Interview transcripts, 3) Surveillance logs, 4) Correspondence.”  As long as each segment corresponds to a separate set of records *that can be judged independently*, the court will hear it. |
| **Is a brief description enough?** | No.  The doctrine requires that a court be able to “determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to **each category** (CREW, 746 F.3d 1088; Gallant, 26 F.3d 168).  A one‑word label such as *“all documents”* or *“relevant records”* fails, because the court cannot decide which parts of that bucket are exempt. | The agency must give at least a short paragraph, a list of key identifiers, or a map of the file that shows the *nature* of the documents within the category. | In *Farahi*, the District Court said the FBI’s categorization was “sufficiently distinct” “…after the agency supplied a detailed justification for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable” (J.A. 139).  The FBI’s description had to explain how the categories aligned with *ongoing investigations*, *specific individuals*, and *time periods* – not just “documents that might be exempt.” |

**Bottom line**

1. **Overbroad category** = a bucket that hides a mix of exempt and non‑exempt material in a way that a court can’t isolate them.  
2. To *legitimately* rely on a category, the agency must give the court a *reasonably detailed description* that shows the category’s boundaries and the statutory reasons the contained items are exempt.  
3. A *single, vague sentence* is not enough; the agency must provide a description that allows the court to apply the exemption test to that subset, or the court will order an itemized review. 

In *Farahi*, the FBI succeeded in showing that each category reflected a *legally distinct* investigative thread and that the categories were “reasonable” under the “reasonable steps” requirement.  The court therefore denied the plaintiff’s argument that the categories were overbroad.

### What are the consequences of an FBI’s failure to meet the statutory FOIA response deadline?
Question:
The office missed the statutory deadline and the plaintiff sued. In my understanding, FOIA requires agencies to respond within 3 months (extendable to 6 if needed). What remedies are available to plaintiffs when this deadline is missed? Does the failure automatically constitute a violation, or must the plaintiff file a complaint? How does the court treat such delays regarding summary judgment motions?
Answer:
**Short answer**

- A missed 3‑month (or 6‑month, if a valid extension was granted) FOIA deadline is a procedural violation, but the statute does not automatically award a penalty.  
- The requester must file a civil action (or file a FOIA complaint) to compel the agency to comply and may sue for statutory damages under §552(d)(4).  
- When the agency in a FOIA case seeks summary judgment, the court will scrutinize the evidence it presents under the “reasonable steps” and “segregability” requirements and will take the time that has elapsed into account.  In *Farahi* the district court denied summary judgment because the FBI had not yet shown that its records were still protected against disclosure; it required the FBI to update its claims and therefore could not grant summary judgment without prejudice.  Once the FBI filed the updated declarations and the court was satisfied that the records remained exempt, it granted summary judgment.

---

### 1.  FOIA deadline and the “right to a complaint”

FOIA §552(a)(3)(b) requires an agency to provide a written determination, usually by releasing the requested records, within **three months** of receipt of the request, or within six months if the agency properly requests an extension and it is justified.

If the agency does not comply within that time, the request is *considered denied* and the requester **may** file **a civil action** to compel disclosure (§552(d)(2)) and, if the refusal was willful, may *recover monetary damages* under §552(d)(4).  
The statute does **not** impose an automatic penalty for a missed deadline; the remedy becomes available only when the requester files a complaint.

> “The Office of the Requester … may file a suit… to compel… the agency to comply.” (FOIA §552(d)(2))  
> “The right to recover damages… for any willful denial or refusal… is provided in §552(d)(4).” (FOIA)

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI* the agency missed the deadline and the plaintiff filed an action under the FOIA statute.  The court accepted that the failure to meet the deadline was grounds to treat the request as denied, but the remedy still required the plaintiff’s filing of a claim.

---

### 2.  Consequences of a missed deadline

1. **Statutory remedy** – The requester may sue for a *summary judgment* in the district court.  
2. **Damages** – If the agency’s non‑response is found willful, §552(d)(4) allows the court to award statutory damages.  
3. **Injunctive relief** – The court can issue a binding order directing the agency to provide the information (or to segregate and release non‑exempt portions under §552(a)(8)(A)(ii)).  
4. **Administrative review** – If the agency seeks an appeal, the court will consider the missed deadline as evidence of indifference or willfulness.

The *Farahi* opinion emphasizes that the agency itself bears the burden of *exhibiting* that its omission was neither justified nor “reasonable steps” were taken, and that failure to meet the deadline does not obviate the agency’s responsibility to prove all exemption claims.

---

### 3.  How the court treats missed deadlines in a summary‑judgment motion

In *Farahi* the FBI moved for summary judgment in 2019, six years after the record‑keeping request.  The district court’s February 2022 order (J.A. 121‑22) read:

> “Given the significant passage of time, the court ordered the FBI to update its assessment….”  

The court therefore *denied* summary judgment “without prejudice” because the agency had not yet demonstrated that the records remained exempt **in view of the elapsed time**.  The agency was required to submit new evidence that the implicated investigations were still pending or reasonably anticipated.

> “The court scheduled a supplemental motion… requiring the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated.” (J.A. 122)

In a late‑stage update, the FBI furnished the required declarations (Hardy, Bender), and the court found its evidence sufficed to satisfy the burden of Exemption 7(A) and the “reasonable steps” requirement.  The court then granted summary judgment (J.A. 138‑39).

Thus, a missed deadline **does not automatically bar summary judgment**, but it forces the agency to supply contemporaneous evidence.  If it cannot, the court will deny summary judgment and may wait for updated evidence or, in some instances, impose sanctions or damages.

---

### 4.  Key doctrinal points from the opinion

* **Deference to the agency** – When an agency (here, the FBI) asserts that a record is exempt under 7(A), the court defers to its “fair measure of deference” (see *CCR v. DOJ* and *Campbell v. DOJ* cited in the opinion).  
* **Burden of proof** – The agency must show (1) that the record was compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and (2) that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with a “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceeding (U.S.C. §552(b)(7)(A)(B)).  
* **Segregability** – If records cannot be segregated without revealing exempt portions, the agency may refuse to release them entirely (*Juarez v. DOJ*).  
* **Staleness** – The court may consider the time elapsed when evaluating “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings, as the *Farahi* case demonstrates.

---

### Bottom line

A missed FOIA deadline is a *statutory procedural violation* that gives the requester a clear path to a civil action, possible statutory damages, and injunctions, but it does not automatically punish the agency.  In a summary‑judgment context the court will assess whether the agency still satisfies its burdens, taking the missed deadline and any resulting staleness of evidence into account.  The *Farahi* opinion illustrates that the court can deny summary judgment if the agency fails to update its evidence about ongoing investigations, but once the agency supplies updated evidence the court may grant summary judgment under FOIA’s procedural framework.

### Why does a court review a FOIA summary‑judgment grant de novo, and what does that mean for the parties?
Question:
The court reviews a FOIA summary-judgment grant de novo. How does this differ from ordinary appellate review? What evidence can the moving party present, and what burden does the respondent carry? Why is the standard higher or lower for FOIA judgments, and what specific FOIA provisions influence this approach?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In FOIA cases the appellate court *ever* applies a **de novo** review of a summary‑judgment grant.  The court re‑examines every allegation that the records are exempt, re‑looks at the agency’s evidence, and adjudicates the statutory question from scratch.  That is different from the *usual* appellate practice of treating a summary‑judgment order as a procedural decision that the court relies on unless it shows the trial court abused its discretion.  A FOIA summary‑judgment order is a statutory determination, so the court must decide the law itself.

Because the government bears the burden of proving the exemption (§ 552(a)(4)(B)), the moving party must present the *all‑reasonable* evidence that the claims are true—the affidavits, internal memoranda, and, when necessary, ex‑parte statements that the documents were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings” (§ 552(b)(7)(A)–(B).  The respondent must rebut that burden with evidence that the records are not exempt, that the “enforcement proceedings” are no longer pending, or that the material is reasonably segregable (§ 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); § 552(b)).  The de novo standard combined with the statutory deference given to law‑enforcement agencies makes the court’s review both thorough and, at the same time, very agency‑friendly.

---

### 1. Why the court reviews a FOIA summary‑judgment grant *de novo*

> *“In FOIA cases, as in all others, we review a grant of summary judgment **de novo***.  
>  *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361* (D.C. Cir. 2021).

The phrase “de novo” means that the appellate court does **not** abscond with the trial‑court findings.  The court must evaluate all evidence and decide the statutory question of whether the records are exempt.  That is the standard whenever a statute itself must be interpreted (e.g., a statutory exemption such as § 552(b)(7)(A) is a question of law).

In “ordinary” civil appellate review, a summary‑judgment order is treated as a *procedural* matter; the appellate court usually gives the trial court a *presumption of correctness* and only intervenes on a show‑cause or abuse‑of‑discretion basis.  The FOIA, however, is a statutory right that the court is sworn to enforce; thus the appellate court cannot simply defer to the trial court’s determination of an agency’s legal claim.

---

### 2. What the moving party (the agency) must present

1. **Affidavits / declarations** from agency officials that  
   * the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes; and  
   * that their disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings” (cf. § 552(b)(7)(A)–(B)).

   In *Foad Farahi*, the FBI submitted public and ex‑parte declarations from the RIDS chief and Acting Section Chief that the investigation “remained ongoing.”  Those declarations were the *sole* evidence the court used.

2. **Supporting internal documentation** that shows the conduct of the investigation—dates, names of investigators, documents that tie the investigations to an “enforcement proceeding,” etc.  The agency must show that the records are *sufficiently distinct* so that the court can apply the correct exemption to each category (see *CREW*, 746 F.3d 1088).

3. **Proof of non‑segregability** if the agency withholds material in its entirety (cf. *Juarez*, 518 F.3d 54; *Mead*, 566 F.2d 242).  The FBI must detail how the exempt and non‑exempt portions are interwoven.

The higher the evidence the agency presents, the more likely it is to survive a de novo review.  Relative to ordinary appellate notice‑and‑comment proceedings, the onus is heavier—because the agency must prove the **existence** of a valid exemption, not merely argue that the court should accept it.

---

### 3. The respondent’s burden

The respondent (here Farahi) must:

1. **Disprove or raise doubt** about the agency’s claims that the documents are exempt under § 552(b)(7)(A).

2. **Show that “enforcement proceedings” are not pending or reasonably anticipated** (e.g., the investigations are closed, no new charges are being pursued).  The respondent may provide evidence from the courts, the agency itself, or independent research.

3. **Demonstrate that a reasonably segregable portion exists** (see § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and the accompanying statutory language that “any reasonably segregable portion…must be provided”).

Farahi’s defense could rely on evidence such as closing dates of the investigations or explicit statements that the FBI no longer considers the file relevant, but the failure to object proved that the court would ignore his contention.

Because the court reviewed the premise *de novo*, it was able to consider evidence the district court either omitted or could not see, and it temporarily revived the agency’s burden after the district court’s denial [“the court again reviewed the evidence”].  This de novo review forces the respondent to actively counter the agency’s evidence.

---

### 4. The standard – higher or lower and why

The **standard is de novo**: the court treats the issue as a fresh question of law and fact, regardless of any prior ruling.  That effectively **raises the standard** for the agency, because the agency cannot simply take credit for the trial‑court’s findings; it must win afresh.

At the same time, the statute and case law grant the agency a **fair measure of deference** where it is an expert (e.g., *Campbell*, 164 F.3d 20; *CNSS*, 331 F.3d 918).  The court may defer to the agency’s factual assertions about “ongoing investigations” (as it did in *Farahi*), but it must test the *legal* application of the exemption.  Thus the review is neither a “rub‑ber‑stamp” nor an *abuse‑of‑discretion* review: it is a middle‑ground, rigorous test that leans on the agency’s expertise but still obliges it to prove each element.

In effect:  
* **De novo** – the court has the authority to re‑decide, not just to dismiss.  
* **Burden on Agency** – it must provide evidence that the exemption truly applies.  
* **Burden on Respondent** – it must rebut that evidence or offer a new exempt category.  

When the court applies de novo, it is able to uphold the *statutory* mandate to protect citizens’ FOIA rights while still respecting the administration’s specialized knowledge.

---

### 5. FOIA provisions that shape the approach

| Provision | How it shapes the rule | How the court applied it in *Farahi* |
|-----------|-----------------------|------------------------------------|
| **§ 552(a)(4)(B)** | States the *government bears the burden* to prove an exemption. | The court made the FBI present affidavits, categorizing 8,750 pages and 80 CDs as exempt. |
| **§ 552(b)(7)(A)** | Defines Exemption 7(A) – records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” whose disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere” with enforcement proceedings. | The FBI had to show both “compiled for law‑enforcement” and interference; the court confirmed the agency satisfied both elements. |
| **§ 552(b)(7)(B)** | Requires the proceedings be *pending or reasonably anticipated*. | The court scrutinized the agency’s declarations that the investigation remained ongoing; it accepted the evidence that the investigations were still pending. |
| **§ 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) & § 552(b)** | Guarantees “reasonable steps” to segregate non‑exempt material; any *reasonably segregable portion* must be released. | The FBI’s supplemental declaration had to explain why the data were not reasonably segregable. The court found no meaningful segregation was possible. |
| **Statutory *deference* to agency expertise** | Courts may defer to law‑enforcement agencies on *factual* determinations, especially under national‑security concerns. | The court gave the FBI’s statements “a fair measure of deference,” but still applied de novo to the question of the exemption’s validity. |

The interplay of these statutory mandates and the court’s de novo review ensures a *balanced* outcome: the agency must convincingly show the exemption applies, but the court is not bound by the *trial‑court’s* procedural judgment.

---

### 6. Bottom line for the parties

* **Agency** – must present enough *credible* evidence (affidavits, internal memos, ex‑parte statements) to prove the records fall squarely under an exemption and that the agency genuinely believes they would interfere with ongoing proceedings.  The more thorough the evidence and the clearer the documentation of current investigations, the more likely the FOIA summary‑judgment will stand after de novo review. 

* **Respondent** – cannot simply file a motion for reconsideration; it must actively refute the agency’s evidence. This may mean requesting additional internal documents, arguing the “enforcement proceedings” are no longer pending or that the records are discrete enough to segregate. They might also rely on *agency’s own* disqualifying statements that the investigation has closed or that the requirements for interference have passed the statutory temporal window.

* **Standard** – A de novo review is more demanding on the agency but still permits deference to its expertise. The statutory framework makes this a “tight‑rope” situation: the court gives the agency a full slate to argue, but it will take the agency’s word *only if* the evidence satisfies the statutory elements.

So, in FOIA cases the appellate court does not “rub‑rub” the district court’s summary‑judgment order.  Instead, it steps in as a *statutory adjudicator*, re‑examining every claim that the records are exempt.  That de novo practice protects FOIA’s public‑interest mandate while still allowing agencies to protect truly sensitive law‑enforcement material.

### When are ex parte FOIA affidavits allowed, and how do courts evaluate them against public affidavits?
Question:
What rules govern the allowance of ex parte affidavits in FOIA cases? How do courts assess their reliability, especially when no counterpart is available? In the Farahi case, ex parte affidavits were pivotal; what precedent supports their use and what limitations exist?
Answer:
**When can a court rely on an ex‑parte FOIA affidavit, and how does that weigh against a public declaration?**

The FOIA, unlike the *Privacy Act* or *Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) in other contexts*, does not forbid ex‑parte affidavits. Rather, it prescribes that the agency “must take *reasonable steps* to release non‑exempt information” (5 C.F.R. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)) and that it may present evidence privately to a court when the very facts it seeks to test would be detrimental to an ongoing investigation or national‑security matter.  The courts treat such affidavits as evidence in the *de‑bona* application of an exemption.

| Question | Rule / doctrine | Practical application in *Farahi* |
|----------|-----------------|-----------------------------------|
| **When are ex‑parte affidavits allowed?** | FOIA’s “reasonable steps” rule allows the agency to submit affidavits to the court *instead of* a public disclosure.  In confirming an exemption, the agency may use a sealed statement if the facts it seeks to prove would “expose the subject of the investigation” and the petitioner has no alternative evidence. | The FBI sent Hardy’s declaration (both public and ex‑parte) and later Bender’s to the court to show that the file was law‑enforcement material and that release could interfere with pending or reasonably‑anticipated proceedings.  The court concluded the filings were permissible because they met the *reasonable‑specificity* requirement. |
| **How are such affidavits evaluated against public declarations?** | The court gives *deference* to the agency’s *own* factual determination under Exemption 7(A) when it is “reasonable, specific, and not merely conclusory” (see *CREW* v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096; *Juarez* v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61).  The affidavit must show a *nexus* between the investigation and a law‑enforcement duty and a *connection* between the individual or incident and a risk.  If the affidavit is too brief, the court may require a counter‑statement or additional evidence, but a single “update” that confirms an ongoing investigation may be enough when earlier affidavits already explained the basis for the exemption. | In *Farahi*, the district court treated Bender’s concise statement as an *update* that “confirmed that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing” (J.A. 138‑39, 139‑40).  The court looked back to Hardy’s earlier affidavits for the full factual layout. |
| **Reliability of an ex‑parte affidavit when no counterpart exists** | The FOIA does not require a “bill of rights” conversation between the parties.  When the facts the affidavits assert *would compromise the investigation* if made public, the court may accept the ex‑parte statement on a *deference* basis, provided the agency has sufficiently explained the relationship between the information and the exemption (e.g., *Juarez*, 518 F.3d 59).  The court may still reject the affidavit if it is “conclusory” or “lacks detail” (cf. *CREW*, 746 F.3d 1098). | The *Farahi* court rejected the notion that Bender’s statement alone was “conclusory” (J.A. 131).  It found that the affidavits had been “reasonably specific” and that the FBI had already satisfied the *precedent* requirement that the information was “compiling for law‑enforcement purposes” and “could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably‑anticipated enforcement proceedings” (*CREW*, 746 F.3d 1096). |
| **Precedents that support this practice** | - **CREW** v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082 (2014) – “the court reviewing a grant of summary judgment in an FOIA case must consider the agency’s affidavits with deference.” <br>- **Juarez** v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54 (2008) – “the government may rely on affidavits that show why exempt material cannot be segregated.” <br>- **CNSS** v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918 (2003) – “exemption 7(A) claims in counterterrorism investigations rely on the agency’s statements regarding ongoing investigations.” <br>- **Campbell** v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20 (1998) – “deference to a law‑enforcement agency under FOIA.” | These cases collectively affirm that an agency’s ex‑parte affidavits are admissible evidence when they address the *burden* of a FOIA exemption, especially 7(A), and the court should treat them with deference unless they are “conclusory or overly broad.” |
| **Limitations** | 1. **Must be “reasonable”** – the affidavit must specify the factual basis for claiming an exemption; mere assertions are insufficient. <br>2. **No reliance on the very information sought** – an affidavit cannot be used to *disclose* the exempt material it is meant to keep secret. <br>3. **Subject‑matter threshold** – the agency must demonstrate that the sought records are *law‑enforcement* material, has “enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and that the disclosure would *interfere* with those proceedings. <br>4. **No “one‑size‑fits‑all” rule** – if the affidavit lacks the necessary specificity, the court may require court‑ordered discovery or a counterpart affidavit. | In *Farahi*, the court found Bender’s affidavit met all those criteria.  It rejected any claim that the affidavit was *too brief* because it served as an *update* to earlier, more detailed affidavits.  The court also echoed the *segregation* requirement under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and concluded the FBI’s detailed justification that no portion could be reasonably segregated was sufficient. |

**Bottom line**  
Ex‑parte FOIA affidavits are permitted when the facts the agency wishes to assert would jeopardize an investigation or national‑security issue if released.  Courts defer to the agency’s own affidavits under Exemption 7(A) as long as the affidavits are *reasonable, specific, and address the statutory elements* of the exemption.  The *Farahi* opinion exemplifies a court’s application of this standard: it accepted the FBI’s ex‑parte Hardy and Bender affidavits (after the district court had already found the agency’s earlier affidavits “reasonably” explanatory), gave deference to the agency’s determination that the file was law‑enforcement material whose release could interfere with pending or reasonably‑anticipated proceedings, and enforced the *reasonable‑steps* requirement of § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii).  The decision illustrates the doctrine’s limits—affidavits must be *explicit*, not merely sweeping statements, and they cannot replace a court‑ordered disclosure of non‑exempt content.

### What does FOIA require of agencies to reasonably segregate and release nonexempt information when exempt and nonexempt text are interwoven?
Question:
The faraha case involved ten thousand pages with potentially interspersed nonexempt words. The court found no reasonable segregation was possible. How do courts decide whether segregation is feasible? Are there specific tests or thresholds? What does past precedent (Mead Data Cent. v. US Dep't of Air Force, Perioperative Servs. v. US Dep't of Veterans Affs.) say about line-by-line redaction versus block segregation? When can a court demand a more detailed partial release?
Answer:
**Short answer**

FOIA’s “reasonable‑segregation” requirement is a *functional* test: an agency may release only that portion of a record that can be extracted without having to strip out or alter the rest of the document, and the removal of the exempt portion must not be so burdensome as to defeat the purpose of the law.  
*Mead Data Center* (1977) and *Perioperative Servs.* (2023) established that when exempt and non‑exempt material are “inextricably intertwined” (e.g., one exempt word occurs on almost every line of a 10 000‑page file), the record is **not reasonably segregable** and the entire file can be withheld.  
The *Farahi* decision embraces that same standard.  If a requestor or the court finds the agency’s explanation of segregation infeasible or insufficient, the court can order the agency to provide a **more detailed segregation plan** (page/line numbers, sample text, etc.) or to show, in a supplemental affidavit, exactly how the non‑exempt material would be isolated.

---

### 1. Statutory framework

5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) says an agency “*must* take reasonable steps to segregate and release non‑exempt information from any record it withheld.”  
The *FOIA* requirement is **no‑exemption‑meets‑value‑test**; the agency bears the burden of showing *both* that a record is exempt *and* that the exempt portion is not “reasonably segregable.”

---

### 2. The “reasonable segregation” test

The meets‑problem is a *functional* one:  
* Is the exempt text so embedded in the record that separating it would be time‑consuming, costly, or would defeat the record’s integrity?*  

The courts have been guided by two key cases:

| Case | Holding | Application to *Farahi* |
|------|---------|-------------------------|
| **Mead Data Center v. Dept. of Air Force** (1977) | When exempt and non‑exempt portions are interwoven “to the extent that *no meaningful portion* can be removed without destroying the meaningfulness of the rest of the record,” the entire record may be withheld. | The FBI in *Farahi* gave a “detailed justification” (see J.A. 139–40) that the exempt portion was “inextricably intertwined.” The court held “no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.” |
| **Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. Dept. of Veterans Affairs** (2023) | The FOIA’s focus is on *information*, not merely words.  Random non‑exempt words interspersed across a document do not satisfy the “reasonable segregation” requirement. | The *Farahi* court repeated Mead’s language: “*the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down*” (J.A. 139). |

Both cases emphasize that **block or line‑by‑line removal is only permissible if the exempt material can be isolated without excessive burden or loss of meaning**.

---

### 3. Line‑by‑line vs. block segregation

*Line‑by‑line* redaction is allowed only when a *small* amount of exempt text is confined to a single page or a few pages that can be cropped out without affecting the rest of the record.  
*Block* segregation (e.g., a block of pages jointly exempt) is permissible when the exempt portion is a contiguous segment – but again the agency must show that the rest of the record can be released *cleanly*.

In *Mead*, the court rejected line‑by‑line removal in a record where “*the exempt material occurs on every line*,” thus treating the entire file as non‑segregatable.  
*Perioperative* reiterated this stance: the intermixing of exempt text on a line‑by‑line basis “does not permit a *meaningful* segregation.”  The *Farahi* court followed suit: the FBI’s request contained “10,750 pages and 80 CDs” with presumed interspersed non‑exempt words, and the court held that *no meaningful information* could be extracted without undermining the record’s integrity.

---

### 4. When a court can demand a more detailed partial release

The statute requires agency *”reasonable steps.”*  
If those steps are inadequate — e.g., the agency gives only a vague “the record is not segregable” statement or fails to specify which pages/lines are exempt — the court may:

1. **Order a more detailed segregation plan** (page and line numbers, the specific text that must be removed, evidence of the feasibility of that extraction).  
2. **Require a supplemental affidavit** in which the agency explains *why* segregation is unfeasible, using examples or a sample of the document.  

In *Farahi*, the court found the FBI’s supplemental *ex‑parte* declaration (Bender), presented in J.A. 139, was sufficiently specific: it detailed “why the protected records were not reasonably segregable.”  Because the agency met that burden, the court did *not* order a further release.  The rule is thus: **if the agency cannot provide that level of justification, the court may compel it to furnish a more granular plan.**

---

### 5. Summary

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| **How do courts decide segregation feasibility?** | They examine whether exempt text is *inextricably intertwined* with non‑exempt text to the point that isolation would be too burdensome or would destroy the informational value of the record. |
| **Is there a specific test or threshold?** | The “reasonable‑segregation” test drawn from *Mead* and reiterated in *Perioperative*.  No hard numerical threshold; the analysis is functional and tailored to the particular record. |
| **Line‑by‑line redaction vs. block segregation?** | Line‑by‑line is permissible only when the exempt portion is limited to a small, easily cut‑out segment.  If exempt words appear in almost every line, the entire record is held non‑segregatable. Block segregation is allowed when the exempt portion is a contiguous block and the rest can be given intact. |
| **When can a court demand a more detailed partial release?** | When the agency’s justification for non‑segregability is too vague or insufficient; the court can order a supplemental affidavit or a specific segregation plan. In *Farahi*, the agency’s Bender declaration was deemed sufficiently detailed, so the court refrained. |

The *Farahi* court held that the FBI’s explanation satisfied the statute’s “reasonable steps” requirement and that the 10 k‑page file was *not* reasonably segregable.  The decision is in line with *Mead*, *Perioperative*, and the underlying statutory purpose of FOIA.  When a requestor believes the agency’s segregation justification was inadequate, the court retains the authority to demand a clearer, more specific plan before granting a partial release.*
