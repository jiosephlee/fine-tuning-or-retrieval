Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Time's Trouble: When Does Exemption 7(A) Lose Its Clamp?

## Introduction  
On September 30 , 2025 the D.C. Circuit reaffirmed a doctrine that has long plagued FOIA litigants who seek records tied to counter‑terrorism investigations: unless an investigation is truly *pending* or *reasonably anticipated*, Exemption 7(A) cannot be invoked. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, 839 F. Supp. 3d 1 (D.D.C. 2023) the court held that the FBI’s declarations—made over a five‑year span and updated in 2023—were sufficient to establish that the subject’s file was still bound in an ongoing law‑enforcement inquiry. The decision thereby distances the statutory “pendency” standard from a mere fact of persecution or evidence gathering that eventually came to a halt, and instead ties it to the *agency’s own assessment* of the investigation’s status.

For FOIA practitioners, the ruling is a stark reminder that the clock on a request does not, in itself, weaken a government’s exemption. It also illustrates how courts will defer to agencies, especially those with counter‑terrorism mandates, when the only way to decide a case is to hear sealed affidavits that lubricate their ability to keep dossiers hidden. Let's unpack how the court reached its decision, why the “pending or reasonably anticipated” test matters, and what this means for future FOIA litigants whittling down the boundaries of secrecy.

## The Pendency Doctrine and Its Historical Roots  
Exemption 7(A) strings a government agency’s right to keep law‑enforcement records from an unrelated, public‑interest right to disclosure. In *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. Department of Justice* (CREW), 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014), the court defined “enforcement proceedings” as proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (Rev. 2014). The held that an agency could only invoke the exemption if evidence under review still had the potential to be used against a suspect later—an evaluation that can shift over time.

*Dr. Juarez v. Department of Justice*, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008), cemented the idea that stalled investigations, even if the agency remains gathering evidence, can still satisfy the pendency requirement so long as the investigation is represented as impending. The decision did not elaborate on how long after a request the “reasonably anticipated” bar could be presumed true, leaving a vacuum. The Farahi case is the first Dominican that maps the gap onto the typical scenario where a FOIA request is filed, responses take years, and the agency offers to renew its claim of existing investigations.

## Farahi, the FBI, and the Court’s Application  

Farahi was an Iranian national who, since 2002, has repeatedly sought admission to the U.S. while subject to repeated removal, asylum, and other immigration proceedings in which DHS cited FBI evidence of ties to terrorist networks. The FBI, upon receiving a FOIA request, located a vast file of 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Farahi sued after the agency missed the statutory deadline. Over the next handful of years the FBI reviewed its holdings and moved for summary judgment twice—once in 2019 and again in 2023—submitting updated affidavits that the underlying investigations were still active.

In 2020 the district court granted the FBI’s first motion on the face of it, after *“seemingly”* showing that the investigations were pending or reasonably anticipated and that the FBI could not categorize its materials in a segregable fashion. In 2023, the court denied the FBI’s second summary‑judgment motion because the FBI had not supplied updated evidence on whether the investigations still existed. The Supreme Court of the United States had never held that time alone imposes a new pendency standard; the Farahi decision respects this. By validating the FBI’s updated 2023 affidavit that the subject’s file “remained ongoing,” the panel left the pendency question in the hands of the agency—yet they tied it to the assistant agent’s statement that “investigations remain ongoing” and the confirmation by a higher‑ranking bureaucrat that they were still “ongoing as of March 2023.”

The court supported the FBI’s claims by stating that its declarations met the “reasonable steps” required under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii): the FBI’s statements explained that the allegations were linked to active collaborations with foreign terrorist organizations, which *“could reasonably be expected to interfere with the investigations.”* The court frankly mulled that this assessment satisfied the pendency bar per CREW and Juarez, noting that a “concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding” continues to exist when an agency argues that evidence is still being collected for a future prosecution. In short, if the FBI believes it is still compiling evidence—even if no charges appear on the docket—the exemption remains applicable.

The decision also addressed the longstanding FOIA boarding question of *segregability*. It found that the FBI and the court had satisfied the burden in that *“no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.”* In enough detail—such as the mixture of classified and unclassified language, or the widespread “interweaving” of records—such a determination is hard to override unless the government can prove a workable set of categories that clearly separate exempt from non‑exempt portions.

## Critiques, Alternative Views, and the Weight of Ex Parte Affidavits  
Despite the court’s carefully circumscribed deference to the FBI, the reliance on ex‑parte affidavits draws a familiar criticism. FOIA literature has long warned of the risk that governments can promise secrecy to themselves by asserting “informal” or “secret” continuity of investigations. The *Farahi* decision, however, sidestepped the worry by clarifying that the FBI did not generate the pendency claim from scratch; it simply updated earlier public and hidden declarations. That narrative may be persuasive given that earlier filings already acknowledged the *ongoing* principal, but if Farahi had truly alleged that investigations had stopped, the court would have likely found a gap. The more pressing question is whether a FOIA defendant can continue to protect wide swaths of evidence more than a decade after a request ran due to the *pending or reasonably anticipated* bar. 

In the near‑term, this decision underscores that *time alone does not terminate an exemption.* This places the onus on FOIA seekers to move more aggressively: They must request no‑time‑limit statements from the agency, or produce evidence that the investigation actually ceased—perhaps a notice from a superior or a de‑classified briefing—before the requested records can be released. At the same time, the case signals that the agency’s own assessment, pursuant to the statutory envelope, can carry weight even when the public is left in the dark about the investigation’s present status.

## Broader Implications and Onward Paths  

The ruling confirms that JEHO’s policy of *blanket confidentiality* for counter‑terrorism files remains robust. For FOIA litigants the stakes are twofold. First, the decision re‑affirms that the pendency test is a moving target; FOIA practitioner must treat an agency’s *latest* affidavit as the starting point, not the original June 2020 filing. Second, the case illustrates the persistent demand for FOIA attorneys to contest the credibility of ex‑parte affidavits in the absence of tangible evidence about an investigation’s status. The Court’s brief reliance on the Bender declaration highlights how an updated affidavit can suffice, even when it is terse, if the contexts—i.e., the earlier Hardy affidavits—afford it.

From a doctrinal perspective, *Farahi* solidifies that Exemption 7(A) is *not a post‑hoc justification* for witholding records indefinitely. It is tethered, albeit loosely, to the agency’s *present belief* that evidence is still being gathered. In times when counter‑terrorism investigations can outlast a decade, the law’s “reasonable anticipation” bar could become a universal de facto excuse for blanket secrecy. The question remains: Will the standard evolve to set a *temporal ceiling* on the pendency claim? As courts continue to turn to the *reasonable steps* language and the *segregation* requirement, we may eventually see a friction point—as FOIA scholars hope—to force agencies to offer more granular disclosures.

Until then, *Foad Farahi* will sit in the annals of FOIA cases as an example of the state’s power to bind the public’s right to information with a procedural label—“ongoing investigations” that, legal or not, remain sticky. FOIA seekers should watch how the next wave of grand challenges to 7(A) unfolds, and they should be prepared to ask agencies for timelines, internal memos, or the testimony of individuals who are not the subject of the file itself. Only then will the cap on these “pending” or “reasonably anticipated” investigations be tested on its merits rather than the method in which the government chooses to assert them.



# Ex Parte FOIA Declarations: Transparency vs. Security in the Farahi Case

The 2025 decision by the D.C. Circuit in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* is a cautionary tale about how FOIA courts negotiate the boundary between public disclosure and the opaque world of homeland‑security investigations. At first glance, the case is a routine application of Exemption 7(A) – “records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes that, if released, could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The real drama, however, lies in how the court weighed a triad of “sealed” affidavits, deference to a law‑enforcement agency, and a question of whether ongoing investigations still exist after several years of litigation. The outcome signals a future where FOIA litigants will routinely confront a fortress of ex‑parte declarations, while agencies will reify their deference.

## Why the Farahi Decision Matters

FOIA remains the statutory linchpin for democratic transparency. Yet the post‑9/11 infection of the statute has brought the “red‑team” of national‑security agencies to the fore. Whenever a FOIA request touches intelligence‑related or counterterrorism activity, Exemption 7(A) can lift an entire trove of documents. The Farahi case is not merely a heavy hand against a single individual; it reflects the court’s current disposition toward National‑Security‐themed FOIA requests. By re‑affirming deference to the FBI’s ex‑parte affidavits, the Court signals that, in the gray zone of “maybe a pending investigation,” the agency’s word often wins.

Beyond the specific FOIA record, the case showcases the procedural pothooks that litigants now face. The district court demanded an “updated” assessment of pendency because “time had passed” – a standard echoing *Citizens for Responsibl­ety & Ethics in Washington* v. DOJ. Courts are now required to check that an investigation still exists *at the time of the summary‑judgment motion*. The Farahi decision indicates that the bar for proving “reasonable expectation of interference” is higher than in non‑security contexts and that deference can persist even when the passage of time tempts courts to doubt relevancy.

## FOIA and Exemption 7(A): The Legal Framework

The statutory skeleton is simple: Exemption 7(A) bars disclosure of records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when the release could reasonably interfere with “enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The doctrine that “pending or reasonably anticipated” refers to *not merely any* proceedings but *criminal investigations* is a 2014 D.C. Circuit legacy (*CREW*). The court’s opinion reaffirms that remedy, translating it into a test of the *existence* of investigative activity – proof that evidence is still being gathered and that any disclosure could tip off a suspect.

The Court cast a spotlight on the “law‑enforcement purpose” prong. While the phrase is broad, the Court held that the FBI’s role as a law‑enforcement agency gives its determinations “deference” – a nod echoed in *Campbell v. DOJ* and *CNSS v. DOJ*. In this context deference is “particularly appropriate for records implicating national security.” This standard is the bedrock of the court’s decision: the agency’s assertion that Farahi was linked to terrorist actors, therefore that evidence was being gathered, satisfied the entire factual burden, ending the case at the ‘no‑disclosure’ level without needing the plaintiff to prove the same facts.

## The Court’s Deference to Law‑Enforcement Assertions

In *Farahi*, the burden fell squarely on the FBI to prove both prongs of Exemption 7(A). The agency invoked two declarations. First, a public declaration from David Hardy, a RIDS Section Chief. Second, a sealed, ex‑parte declaration from Joseph Bender, then Acting Section Chief. The Court’s analysis underscores that while the public declaration sufficed to satisfy the “law‑enforcement purpose” prong, the ex‑parte declaration – which included the bulk of evidence regarding pending investigations – was indispensable for the “pending or reasonably anticipated” prong.

The reasoning follows a familiar line. The Court observed that the FBI’s hard‑copy filing had been a “partial” record, but its categories were sufficiently granular – enough for the court to determine where the exemption applied. The Bender affidavit’s brevity was not a fatal flaw. As the Court reminded, the earlier Hardy affidavits had already established the existence and scope of the investigation. Bender’s affidavit was merely an *update*, a function *not to rewrite the narrative but to confirm that nothing had changed*. In a system that allows an agency to argue its “reasonable steps” to withhold entire categories of documents, such a confirmation makes sense.

The Court’s decision is, in part, an endorsement of the doctrine that agencies in the “law‑enforcement” sphere wield significant discretion. Because these agencies have a nexus to the very statutes that grant them the powers referred to in FOIA – and because their information bears potential harm on national safety – the courts are willing to defer heavily to solid, methodical‑looking affidavits.

## Ex Parte Declarations: The Heart of the Holding

An ex‑parte declaration is the cornerstone of FOIA arguments involving national‑security‑related records. On the one hand, the secrecy embedded in them ensures that potentially self‑defeating disclosures are not leaked to a suspect. On the other hand, the secrecy creates a “negative” – it erodes FOIA’s transparency pact.

In *Farahi*, the sealed Bender declaration was the fulcrum. Its efficacy hinged on the Cold‑War legacy that FOIA courts require a *“reasonable certainty”* that the investigation still exists at the time the request is crawled. The Court held that the Bender affidavit met that standard. Interestingly, the Court clarified that “reasonable certainty” should not require exhaustive evidence – the mere fact that investigations *continue to gather evidence* suffices. In effect, the Court carved a niche for the sealed declaration as a *verification instrument* rather than an *open‑book evidence supplier*.

However, the reliance on a single ex‑parte declaration remains contentious. Historically, *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs* and *Deem v. Dept. of Treasury* underscore that FOIA plaintiffs may be denied even when reality Lures. The key difference in *Farahi* is the confirmatory nature of the ex‑parte document: it was not a shaking‑off of a *new* claim but a *warranty* that the status of the existing claim had not changed. The court found that “the Bender declaration could reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document.” The stability of that logic is what cements the decision’s bellwether.

## Segregability, Completeness, and the “Contamination” Test

The FOIA doctrine obliges agencies to “take reasonable steps” to segregate and release non‑exempt portions, as codified in §552(a)(8)(A)(ii). When the records are interwoven – bits of non‑exempt information buried line‑by‑line in a sea of exempt files – segregation becomes a Sisyphean task. The *Mead* test and the *Perioperative* variant both caution that because the FOIA is about “information,” not just words, “non‑exempt material” cannot be extracted if it is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material.

In *Farahi*, the court found that the FBI’s jurisdictional ([A]) response – an exhaustive, category‑by‑category justification – demonstrated that the non‑exempt portions were not “reasonably segregable.” The FBI’s paperwork possessed “detailed justification” regarding why the records could not be neatly divided. The Supreme Court, in *Barnes v. DOJ* (fiction), would likely applaud such a careful documentary tilt, as the agency gave sufficient granularity to satisfy the “reasonably segregable” test.

Thus, even though the plaintiff may contend that some non‑exempt material may have survived the watershed declaration, the Court deemed that any such glimpses were *spurious* and that the entire 10,750‑page file and 80 CDs could not be cleanly divided. The plaintiff’s hope for a token “release” fell short.

## Doctrinal Implications for FOIA Litigation and National Security

The Farahi opinion is a confirmation of the D.C. Circuit’s longstanding FOIA duty: The agency’s constitutional‑musing ability to hold back information is anchored in practical realities – the fear that a suspect would use that information to sabotage a case. The Court’s reliance on the “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard and on deference to law‑enforcement affiliates cements a doctrinal safety‑net for agencies handling terrorist investigations.

At the same time, the decision underscores the precarious “tightrope” of FOIA: on one side lies a litigant’s right to see historical records; on the other lies the imperative to protect national security. The court’s deference may encourage agencies to submit more robust, topical ex‑parte arguments, potentially raising the bar for “specialized” FOIA litigation. Beyond *Farahi*, other cases in 2025, such as *US v. Counterterrorism Unit* (pending) and *Deployed Behavior v. Homeland Security*, are likely to echo this tone.

The Court’s conclusion also simplifies the burden on agencies: they need only produce *at least* a single ex‑parte declaration to meet the “pending” prong. This “lowered the bar for the agency, not the plaintiff” and may catalyze a trend whereby FOIA claims against agencies involved in counterterrorism will lean more heavily on the ex‑parte mechanism.

## Downstream Impact on Agencies, Plaintiffs, and the Public

From an agency perspective, the *Farahi* ruling is a vote of confidence in their current FOIA practices. With deference enshrined, agencies can channel FOIA claims into concise, updated affidavits that confirm investigations’ existence, thus circumventing lengthy litigation. However, the case also implicitly invites future scrutiny into the *exact* criteria agencies must satisfy. While the court found the FBI’s “detailed justification” on segregability adequate, it left open the possibility that a more articulate, category‑specific justification might be required in a corollary case.

For plaintiffs, the decision is a sobering reminder that FOIA litigation can become an endurance test. A single disclosure becomes the difference between complex case law and the death knell: if the plaintiff can’t produce evidence that the packaged records do not hamper ongoing investigations, the entire FOIA request may be denied. The baggage of “reasonable steps” and the “interspersed” test may compel litigants to weave together a tight legal narrative and a technical FOIA procedure, with a heavy reliance on discovery and the availability of internal agency documents. Thus, the legal research required is two‑fold: substantively, the plaintiff must demonstrate that they had not inadvertently become a suspect; procedurally, they must show that the segregation claim went off the rails.

The public’s interest is polarized. On one hand, the public’s right to see the government’s operations is enshrined in FOIA; on the other, the *Farahi* decision demonstrates that the standard has become defensive for national‑security records. The law may leave the public with a frustrating veil, where largely opaque investigations remain uninspected and invisible. However, the case’s reasoning – that the true test is whether an openly available record could jeopardize an ongoing investigation – may frame better safeguards against re‑exposure of sensitive material.

## Conclusion

The *Farahi* ruling is both a reassurance and a warning. It reaffirms the D.C. Circuit’s deference to law‑enforcement assertions, and it confirms that a sealed, ex‑parte declaration can answer the court’s vital question of “is the investigation still pending?” That deference is far from diminishing FOIA’s role; rather, it signals that panels will accept a fortified ex‑parte process as a sufficient shield for national‑security records. Over the next few years, agencies will likely structure FOIA filings to fit the *Hardy‑Bender* type of pattern: a public declaration establishing “law‑enforcement purpose” and a sealed affidavit confirming “ongoing investigations.” Meanwhile, FOIA litigants will need to grapple with a twice‑revoked process – the initial tangle of assertions, the later update of status, then, perhaps, the final request for partial disclosure. In this high‑stakes FOIA dance, the occasional “ex‑parte” step can pivot the outcome, but the guardian of our transparency remains the court’s willingness to watch who takes the lead—until that lead ever tips the balance toward national security.



# Segregation Scruples: The ‘No Meaningful Segregable Portion’ Rule in FOIA  

## Why the Case Matters  

In the federal courts, FOIA disputes rarely hit the headlines.  What happens in *Foad Farahi v. FBI* (9‑C‑5243, 2025) is “normal” for the Agency and Litigants, but it is exceptional for the broader FOIA community because the court affirms that an entire 10,750‑page, 80‑CD FBI file will remain hidden from a private citizen under Exemption 7(A).  The ruling clarifies an elusive element of FOIA litigation: when does “reasonable segregation” fail because no useful, non‑exempt subset can be isolated?  For FOIA seekers, the answer has bite: the “no meaningful segregable portion” doctrine is not just a footnote; it can swallow an entire case.  For agencies, the ruling consolates the “deference” that the law affords them when they invoke national‑security exemptions, but it also reminds them that a mere categorical withdrawal can be insufficient.

The case also illustrates the fine line between “reasonable steps” to segregate information and the court’s discretion to accept “ex parte” affidavits when the subject matter is too sensitive to make public.  Meanwhile, it gives FOIA researchers a set of cautionary hard facts: large documents that intermix exempt and non‑exempt content are a unique challenge, and the court’s current precedent will require creative FOIA tactics if litigants want a bite of a sprawling dossier.

## Exemption 7(A) and the Deference Framework  

The FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) eliminates “records compiled for law enforcement purposes” when their release could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”  The Court’s logic is shaped by a body of law that has granted agencies a deference that rises from their technical expertise and the national‑security implications of the data.  In *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (CREW), the court explained that when an agency such as the FBI invokes Exemption 7(A), the agency’s conclusion deserves a “fair measure of deference” (as echoed in *Clemente v. FBI* and *Campbell v. DOJ*).  That deference is strongest for national‑security investigations, where the court has repeatedly urged that the executive branch not be tipped off by FOIA disclosure.  

The burden on the agency is to show, on the balance of probabilities, that the information was compiled for a law‑enforcement purpose and that its disclosure would interfere with *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings.  The district court in *Farahi* affirmed that the FBI’s updated affidavits—both public (the Hardy declaration) and ex parte (the Bender declaration)—clearly met the first two prongs.  Importantly, the court agreed that the continued existence of an ongoing investigation was integral to the exemption.  Those earlier conclusions set the stage for the second prong: the “reasonable steps” requirement for segregation.

## The “No Meaningful Segregable Portion” Doctrine  

FOIA requires a *reasonable* effort to segregate and release non‑exempt information.  The courts operationalized this through the “no meaningful segregable portion” doctrine, beginning in *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force* (Mead).  The Mead court held that when exempt and non‑exempt data are “inextricably intertwined” with each other—especially when exempt information appears inside the same word or line as non‑exempt material—it can be impossible to prune the file without destroying both the exempt and the useful parts.  The rule has been applied most famously in *Juarez v. DOJ* to justify withholding a record that could not be reasonably split.  The *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs* decision reiterated that the court may rely on agency affidavits that provide “reasonable specificity” on why segregation is impossible.

In *Farahi*, the court relied on Mead, Juarez, and Perioperative to support its conclusion that the 10,750 pages were a single, inseparable unit.  The FBI’s original claim was that the file “includes a significant number of recordings in which FBI agents documented Farahi’s connections to high‑level terrorists.”  That content is intrinsically linked to investigative narratives that the FBI professed to be still active.  Because the affidavit revealed that the susceptible elements—such as the detailed analytic narratives and the raw recordings—were interwoven with the exempt portions, the judge held that the file had no “meaningfully” segregable non‑exempt portions.  That conclusion satisfies the court’s “reasonable steps” standard because the agency could not provide any clear, workable method of carving out non‑exempt material without compromising the exhaustion of evidence useful for any future liability.

## How the Court Applied “Reasonable Steps” to the Farahi File  

The “reasonable steps” requirement is a routine standard, but it becomes knotty when the records are dense, convoluted, and heavily interhashed with sensitive content.  In *Farahi*, the court observed that the FBI had previously responded by providing category-based internal documents and affidavits also covered by Exemption 7(A).  Importantly, the judge noted that the agency’s *Bender* declaration was not a new sketch of the file’s contents; instead, it was a brief confirmation that no change had occurred since the *Hardy* affidavits—an admission that the investigations themselves remained ongoing to March 2023.  The court’s presumption that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden earlier meant that Bender’s lean testimony was sufficient to reaffirm that the exemption continued.

However, the court acknowledged that Farahi’s argument about the “vague” or “conclusory” nature of the Bender declaration should be weighed with the earlier, more detailed, written affidavits.  The judge therefore decided that the Bender declaration could stand as a clarifying update rather than a faulty primary assertion.  In so doing, the court upheld the agency’s rationality in explaining that the exempt content could not be presently segregated.  The ultimate message is that a single, clear declaration—whether ex parte or public—together with evidence that the examination is active, satisfies the reasonable‑steps requirement if the file’s structure makes segregation impossible.

## What FOIA Seekers Can Take from This Decision  

1. **Ask for Structured, Narrow Requests**  
   If you can anticipate that a record will be bulk‑withdrawn, split your request into *distinctly* categorized requests.  Use the *CREW* principle that categories must be sufficiently distinct that the court can evaluate the exemption for each one.  That way, if a category is wholly exempt, you still get the other categories.  When the record is a mass of mixed material, this tactic reduces the chances you will get “no‑meaningful‑segregable‑portion” at once.

2. **Pursue “Semi‑Open” or “List‑Other” Requests Early**  
   In contentious cases, FOIA practitioners often use a “list‑other” or “open‑content” request before the main one to identify potentially non‑exempt “blue‑cell” items (e.g., publicly available footnotes, metadata).  This strategy has a proven record in cases such as *Perioperative Services* where the plaintiff managed to isolate small fragments that survived the segregation test.

3. **Consider PAGs and Fascist‐like De‑Classifying Steps**  
   If the Federal Agencies provide ‘segregation logs’ or ‘meta‑analytic reports’ that break out exempt from non‑exempt content, these can be used in court to argue that a “meaningful segregable portion” exists.  The judge will examine whether the segmentation was indeed reasonable and not just a legal artifice.

4. **Plan for Ex Parte or Sealed Submissions**  
   The *Farahi* case underscores that ex parte affidavits are powerful tools for the government when dealing with the unknown aspects of a file.  FOIA plaintiffs cannot rely on full transparency in these contexts; instead, they may need to argue that the ex parte information is materially necessary and that it is being held under an existing precedent that limited its exposure.  Recognizing that, FOIA practitioners may try to link their requests to previously disclosed or published summaries to reduce reliance on sealed data.

5. **Ask the Court to Encourage Agency Disclosure of “Professional Segregation Records”**  
   Agencies can provide, in addition to the file itself, a “segregation plan” and accessibility logs showing why removal is not feasible.  Courts have varied in willingness to accept such plans; however, the *Farahi* opinion suggests that a brief but thorough explanation can be enough if the underlying cache truly intermixes exempt and non‑exempt content.

## Doctrinal Implications and Potential Congressional Reforms  

The *Farahi* ruling reaffirms a doctrine that regulates a very narrow but consequential sphere of FOIA practice: the “no meaningful segregable portion” analysis.  At the same time, it hints at an opportunity for Congress:

- **Clarify the “reasonable steps” Standard**  
  The FOIA remains half‑shrouded when it comes to the agency’s burden of demonstrating that segregation is impossible.  A statutory standard clarifying the acceptable linguistic and technical means of segregation might streamline courts’ decisions and reduce the reliance on highly technical analysis that keeps FOIA litigants in the shadows.

- **Enhance the “Category” Requirement**  
  Congress could require that agencies provide a *catalogue* of each category in which every document can be placed, with accompanying justification.  This would reduce the need for ex parte affidavits and would improve FOIA transparency for ordinary citizens.

- **Publicly Announced “Standard Templates” for Segregation**  
  Another promising legislative angle is to make standard segregation templates public to guide agencies and litigants, lowering the cost of compliance on both sides.

For now, the *Farahi* court places a low wall in the path of FOIA seekers whose files are heavily intertwined with exempt material.  It also signals that the “reasonable steps” doctrine no longer is a flexible policy tool but a geographic constraint on the kind of FOIA litigation that agencies can effectively defend.

## Conclusion  

The *Farahi v. FBI* decision may appear to be a routine adjudication of national‑security secrecy, but it sharpened the contours of FOIA’s most furtive exemption.  It underscored that, when exempt and non‑exempt content are inextricably linked, the court will let the agency keep the whole thing, because splitting it would destroy at least one side of the story.  This offers FOIA practitioners a cautionary tale: large, sticky files are almost guaranteed to be withheld, unless the agency provides concrete, objectively “meaningful” segregation.  And it offers national‑security officials a confirmation that their authority to withhold under Exemption 7(A) will not be lightly brushed aside by a court looking for a small slice of sensitive data.  

Hence, those who adapt to the *Farahi* reality—through meticulous, narrowly tailored requests and ingenuity in dust‑picking the sparse non‑exempt nuggets—are the ones who will survive the FOIA gauntlet.  Meanwhile, the balance of deference and scrutiny remains tilt‑gear for future FOIA takers and the agencies they confront.



# FOIA, Immigration, and Counterterrorism: A Cross‑Jurisdictional Legal Minefield

## Why Foad Farahi’s case is more than a curiosity

When a former Iranian immigrant who has, since 2002, been embroiled in removal, asylum, and withholding‑of‑removal proceedings files a FOIA request for his FBI file, it feels less like a claim for transparency and more like a trans‑disciplinary audit of the adjudicative nexus between immigration law and national‑security investigations. The D.C. Circuit’s ruling in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* ( 2025 ) codifies a principle that has long been tacit in the “FOIA‑national‑security” corner: if a federal agency can show its files are compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure would indisputably interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated investigative proceedings, FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) provides an almost blanket shield. The case cements that deference is not simply a matter of “law‑enforcement agency” but also a matter of national‑security expertise. For those navigating the dual world of immigration adjudication and FOIA transparency, the decision offers a practical—and chilling—warning: the mere fact of an immigration proceeding does not shield a request from Exemption 7(A) if the sponsoring agency can point to an ongoing investigation that would be compromised by disclosure.

## The statutory skeleton of Exemption 7(A)

FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) is a two‑prong test under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The first prong requires that the records be “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes,” a condition readily met by the FBI. The second prong is sharper: the court must determine that the release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated” (Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119, at 119; see also *Crews v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096). The statutory language is purposely concise, but the precedent that the D.C. Circuit recognized with *Crews* defines pending or reasonably anticipated as “encompassing ongoing investigations that seek material for a future criminal case.” The “reasonably expected to interfere” clause is usually satisfied where the information could tip off suspects, jeopardize evidence, or otherwise erode the investigative advantage. As the law has affirmed numerous times—*Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61; *CNSS*, 331 F.3d 918, 929—the burden rests squarely on the agency, not on the FOIA claimant.

In *Farahi*, the court thus faced a classical situation: an agency with an unflagged national‑security investigation was asked to produce a vault of potentially relevant but highly sensitive files. The agency’s burden was clear, but the question was whether it could silence the request or provide a meaningful, segregated portion. The decision shows that the court will lean heavily toward deference when the agency is both a law‑enforcement body and a national‑security expert.

## How the court unfolded the deference calculus

The crux of the opinion (Katsas, Circuit Judge) lay in the agency’s persuasive affidavits. Two public affidavits from RIDS chief David Hardy and an ex‑parte declaration from Acting Chief Joseph Bender supplied the docket with “concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding” details. The court thanked these affidavits “for establishing that the withheld records were compiled in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” (J.A. 42). The court also noted that the legal details in the affidavits were echoed by the immigration proceeding record, where the DHS’s immigration affidavit labeled Farahi the “recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood” and “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed.”

The procedural history involved a timeshift: the initial summary‑judgment ruling, granted in October 2020, was joined by a Supreme Court–like requirement that the FBI update its asserting that investigations were still ongoing—the statute demands that the government keep a current timetable of ongoing investigations. The court refused to accept the “rough assertion” given the time lag and ordered an update. After that update, the new affidavits (including Bender’s ex‑parties line) were deemed satisfactory. The district court thus granted the summary‑judgment motion; the appellate turned the same conclusion in light of the *Crews* standard, reinforcing the deference principle.

Therefore, the D.C. Circuit’s standing restated that the law‑enforcement agency’s own judgments about the scope of its investigations are the sine qua non of the Exemption 7(A) analysis, provided the FOIA claimant cannot object that those judgments are “conclusory.” The court balanced the assessment that “assuming the investigations remain ongoing, release may tip off suspects” against the presumption that the FBI’s reckoning is authoritative. Policy‑wise, that maintains a screen against FOIA invasions into high‑stakes investigations but also reinstates the customary discretion that agencies like the FBI wield over their records.

## "Pending or reasonably anticipated” in the context of removal proceedings

Where *Farahi* raises a productive tension, it is the interplay between imminent removal proceedings and longer‑term counterterrorism investigations. The riddled question: does the fact that Farahi is the subject of an immigration proceeding provide sufficient grounds to *not* invoke Exemption 7(A) because the deportation is “pending and potentially imminent” and the FBI’s investigations are “distant or arguably ended”? The court overruled this line. *Crews* is clear that the pendency of the enforcement proceeding is irrelevant to the immigration proceeding: the enforcement proceeding remains whatever it is, regardless of the immigration suit. Because the government’s affidavit confirmed the investigations were still ongoing “as of March 2023” (J.A. 131), the article’s insistence that the time lag made the pendency questionable worn thin.

What the decision underscores is that “pending” is about the crime‑investigation itself, not the removal court. The nonconducive nature of the idea that the removal proceeding might be “reasonably anticipated” but not “pending” demonstrates the court’s caution not to let the immigration context override the statutory test. In practice, immigration litigants who seize FOIA requests that implicate national‑security investigations now have a clear reference: if the agency can reliably show its investigative processes continue, Exemption 7(A) may stand.

## Segregability: why “mixing words” can make a difference

A subsidiary but no less important issue the court addressed was the “reasonable steps” requirement for segregating exempt material (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)). The FBI was told to dissolve the withheld documents into narrower categories, but the court discovered that the agency’s category breakdown was “overbroad.” Farahi had declined to bring the argument to the docket, rendering it moot. Even if small fragments of non‑exempt material could be cherry‑picked, the court applied *Mead* (566 F.2d 242, 260) and concluded that line‑by‑line excision would not produce a “reasonably segregable” segment because “the exempt material is interspersed throughout the records.” That is consistent with precedent: the law does not wage a feuding duel over micro‑segregation of only a few words, and the exception is for substantial swaths of material. The key takeaway: a FOIA plaintiff cannot rely on “minimal segregation” as a shortcut to transparency in cases that involve “extensive, intertwined” national‑security data.

## The ex‑parte advantage

In *Farahi*, the agency exercised the FOIA privilege of submitting partial documents under seal. The court, following *Perioperative Services*, noted in its opinion that the ex‑parte declaration “provided the data irrelevant to the FOIA request” and was corroborated by the public affidavits. The court endorsed the procedure because revealing the very material that would be withheld would defeat the purpose of Exemption 7(A). The law acknowledges that an agency sometimes must rely on privileged information to decide whom to supply. The decision offers a new signpost for FOIA law: in contexts where the information withheld could directly affect national security or the integrity of an investigation, the court will be reluctant to force agencies to make ex‑parte declarations public when it would expose secrets. In short, the D.C. Circuit preserved FOIA’s fairness while respecting the “national‑security tests” that have been nurtured under its estoppel.

## Broader doctrinal reverberations

The *Farahi* case reverberates across three angles:

1. **Deference to law‑enforcement agencies** – the court reiterates the “special deference” principle that has guided FOIA’s interplay with national security. Even in the absence of a formal “security review” process, the agency’s reasoned declaration suffices.

2. **The “pendency” requirement** – the decision clarifies that the evaluator need not demonstrate immediate prosecutorial intent; merely showing that investigations are ongoing works. By tying the test to ongoing investigations, the court provides a tangible yardstick for future FOIA claims in areas overlapping with immigration or intelligence cases.

3. **Segregability standards** – the opinion reinforces that when exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with non‑exempt material, there is no duty to partially disclose the minutes. That sets a standard for regulators to carefully compile their categorization methods from the beginning; otherwise, much of the information could remain closed even if small semantic islands may be public.

These clarifications may temper the enthusiasm of civil‑rights groups who use FOIA to audit immigration agencies on judges' decisions; if a claim involves potential terrorism ties, the election of Exemption 7(A) becomes a pre‑approved default. Conversely, law‑enforcement agencies may feel emboldened to rely on Exemption 7(A) for a broader swath of immigration‑linked files—an effect that could extend beyond the borders of *Farahi*.

## A future where FOIA and immigration are interlocked

Finally, *Farahi* may be cueing the next wave of litigation: the rise of FOIA claims by asylum seekers and refugees who challenge immigration tribunals' reliance on secret agency files. While the D.C. Circuit provides a clear path for agencies to invoke Exemption 7(A), FOIA litigants might push the envelope by producing obstructions over “state secrets” rather than the “Narcotics” or "terrorism" absolutism of *Farahi*. The constitutional underpinnings—first‑amendment freedoms, due‐process rights—are still defensible, but the practical reality remains: FOIA is a tool for balanced transparency, and Exemption 7(A) is a sturdy shield for national‑security and investigative integrity. 

As immigration policy and counter‑terrorism concerns converge, *Fod/Farahi* will likely serve as a lodestone for future cases that again ask: how far can FOIA transparency go when a dead‑penny immigration hearing overlaps with a federal security investigation? The ruling signals that the courts will give the agency a significant margin of appreciation, especially when the stakes include prevention of terror or protection of covert operations. In effect, the Landac’s one‑paragraph declaration of the FBI’s investigative status can carry the weight of a national‑security shield. That, in and of itself, is a testimony of how diverse laws—FOIA, removal proceedings, and federal counterterrorism statutes—interlock to form a complex legal minefield.
