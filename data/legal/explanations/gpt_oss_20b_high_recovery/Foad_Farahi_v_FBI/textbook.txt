Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Case Introduction and Procedural Background

This chapter traces the factual and procedural journey that led to Foad Farahi’s appeal.  It begins with Farahi’s immigration background and the allegations that spurred the FBI’s investigations, moves through the FOIA request that precipitated the dispute, and follows the series of motions, court determinations, and updates that culminated in the appellate decision of September 30 2025.

## Farahi's immigration history and alleged terrorist links

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who arrived in the United States in 1993.  In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings for exceeding the limits of his visa.  During the course of those proceedings, Farahi repeatedly sought relief through asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure.  In opposition, DHS introduced documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who asserted that Farahi had “connections to high‑level terrorists.”  The FBI further alleged that Farahi was linked to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and other known Al Qaeda members, to the convicted terrorist Jose Padilla, and to charities that allegedly financed terrorism, such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation.  These alleged ties formed the basis for the FBI’s investigative efforts and the records that later became the subject of Farahi’s FOIA request.

## FOIA request details (10,750 pages, 80 CDs)

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking the FBI file that accrued during his immigration proceedings.  The FBI identified a volume of potentially responsive information amounting to about 10,750 pages and 80 compact disks.  The agency missed the statutory deadline for a response, prompting Farahi to sue on the ground that the FBI had failed to comply with FOIA.  Over the next several years, the FBI reviewed the documents, released some portions, and withheld the majority.  After completing its review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in October 2019, invoking Exemption 7(A) on the basis that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were then pending or reasonably anticipated.”

## Initial summary‑judgment motion and Hardy's affidavit

In its initial summary‑judgment motion, the FBI relied on a declaration by David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section.  The public Hardy declaration asserted that the withheld documents were part of investigations into national‑security violations and international terrorism, that they were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, and that their release could interfere with ongoing or impending enforcement actions.  Hardy also submitted an ex‑parte declaration containing additional detail.  The district court, in a November 2022 proceeding, found that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden to show that the records were law‑enforcement compilations and that their disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings.  However, the court noted the lapse of time and, citing issues of pendency and segregation, denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered the FBI to update its assessment.

## Court requires reassessment of pendency and segregation

Because the district court had identified a “significant passage of time,” it required the FBI to re‑evaluate whether the enforcement proceedings that the records implicated were still pending or reasonably anticipated.  The court also demanded more complete information addressing the ability to segregate non‑exempt portions of the records from the exempt portions.  The court’s order stayed the summary‑judgment determination and placed a deadline on the FBI for supplemental declaratory material that would meet the court’s evidentiary standards for pendency and segregation.

## FBI’s updated filings

Responding to the court’s order, the FBI filed new declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS.  Bender’s public declaration confirmed that the FBI’s files on Farahi were still under active investigation and that “the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.”  His ex‑parte declaration, submitted under seal, reproduced the substantive details from Hardy’s prior statements and addressed the court’s concerns about segregation, explaining why non‑exempt material was not reasonably separable from the exempt portions.  The district court reviewed the updated filings, credited Bender’s confirmation of ongoing investigations, and determined that the FBI had satisfied the pendency and segregation requirements.  Accordingly, it granted the renewed summary‑judgment motion, holding that the records were protected under FOIA Exemption 7(A).  Farahi appealed.

The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, writing in August 2025, affirmed the district court’s decision.  The appellate court held that the FBI had met its burden to show that the files were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.  The court thus upheld the application of Exemption 7(A) and the district court’s summary judgment, resolving the dispute in the government's favor.

# Statutory and Judicial Framework for FOIA Exemption 7(A)

The conflict in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* was whether the FBI’s refusal to disclose the vast bulk of Farahi’s file was properly grounded in FOIA’s Exemption 7(A).  The district court ultimately found that the FBI had met the statutory burden, and the appellate court affirmed that finding.  In order to grasp the court’s reasoning it is helpful to begin with the text of the exemption itself, then to examine the two critical elements the agency must prove: that the records were *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* and that their disclosure would *reasonably be expected to interfere* with *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings.  The judicial mandates that guide the interpretation of these elements—most notably *Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Use of Secrets* (CREW), *Mapother*, and *Counter‑National Security Study* (CNSS)—provide the context that the court applied.

## Statutory language of §552(b)(7)(A) & (B)

FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) is codified in 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).  The text reads in full: “The Government may withhold from disclosure records *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* if the disclosure would *unreasonably interfere with the conduct of a pending or reasonably anticipated... law‑enforcement investigation or proceeding*.”  The statute therefore contains two predicates that the agency must satisfy: (1) the files must be compiled for law‑enforcement purposes; and (2) disclosure would unreasonably interfere with enforcement actions that are either pending or reasonably anticipated.  Under FOIA, the statutory bar applies only where both predicates are met.  The D.C. Circuit’s analysis in *Farahi* therefore hinges on whether the FBI can demonstrate each of those elements in light of the statutory text.

## “Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” definition

The statutory phrase “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” does not describe a specific type of document but rather the context in which the information was gathered.  In *CREW*, the court clarified that this must be “a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties.”  The agency must demonstrate that the records were created *in furtherance of the agency’s law‑enforcement mission*—which in the FBI’s case included investigations of national‑security and international‑terrorism violations.  As the court explained in *Farahi*, the FBI’s declaration asserted that the withheld portions of Farahi’s file had been compiled *in furtherance of” investigations of violations of national‑security and terrorism law*.  The court accepted that assertion, noting that the agency’s documentation (the “Hardy” and “Bender” affidavits) established a connection between the records and a bona fide law‑enforcement investigation.  Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court gave the agency deference on this point, a deference consistent with *Campbell v. DOJ* and reiterated in *CNSS*.

## “Interference” requirement for enforcement proceedings

The second statutory predicate demands that disclosure would “reasonably be expected to interfere” with the investigatory or prosecutorial activity.  In *Mapother*, the court emphasized that interference need only be a *reasonable expectation of harm* to the agency’s efforts, not a demonstration that actual interference will occur.  The typical justifications the court recognizes are: i) the disclosure would tip off subjects or conspirators; ii) the disclosure would compromise the agency’s ability to gather further evidence; or iii) the release would undermine the integrity of evidence.  In *Farahi*, the FBI’s filings explained that the withheld materials could “tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation” and thereby jeopardize the FBI’s continued investigations.  The appellate court found that this explanation satisfied the interference requirement because the evidence showed ongoing investigations and the potential for tangible harm.

## Jurisprudence: CREW, Mapother, CNSS

The procedural and substantive guidance for Exemption 7(A) is largely derived from the three cases mentioned.  *CREW* established that the interference test requires the existence of *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings and that agencies must show that disclosure would *reasonably* interfere.  *Mapother* clarified the meaning of “reasonably expected to interfere,” placing the burden on the agency to demonstrate the likelihood of harm rather than to prove actual harm.  *CNSS* further reinforced that courts should afford a degree of deference when the exempt records intersect with national‑security concerns, particularly in cases involving counterterrorism investigations.  In *Farahi*, the appellate court followed each of these precedents, using *CREW* to assess pendency, *Mapother* to evaluate the interference expectation, and *CNSS* to justify deference to the FBI’s law‑enforcement assessment.

## “Pending or reasonably anticipated” threshold

The *CREW* decision introduced the concept of “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceedings as a higher threshold than merely “ongoing” investigations.  The court explained that a proceeding is pending if it is in progress or is bound to proceed given the current facts; it is reasonably anticipated if the evidence suggests that an administrative or criminal action is likely to be taken.  In *Farahi*, the district court, and later the appellate court, relied on the FBI’s affidavits to show that the investigations remained active—specifically that the FBI had been *in possession of new information* and had been *continually gathering evidence* that could lead to a future prosecution.  The court rejected the plaintiff’s argument that time had diminished the plausibility of pending proceedings, reasoning that the agency's own affirmations that the investigations “remain ongoing” matched the high *CREW* threshold.  Thus, the circumscribed application of the term “pending or reasonably anticipated” bolstered the procedural justification for withholding the records.

---

In sum, *Foad Farahi* underscores how FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) demands a two‑part proof: records must be compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and their disclosure must reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.  The court’s deference to the FBI for the first element, along with its application of *CREW*, *Mapother*, and *CNSS* to the interference and pendency analyses, explain why the appellate court affirmed that the records were properly exempted.

# Burden‑Shifting, Deference, and Ex‑parte Evidence in FOIA

FOIA’s twin goals are clarity of government action and protection of sensitive information.  The statute therefore requires that agencies first try to comply, but it also provides a set of constitutional‑and‑statutory safeguards that often compel withholding.  Understanding the procedural burdens that arise when an agency invokes an exemption, the courts’ deference to law‑enforcement fact‑finders, and the special role of ex‑parte affidavits is essential for scholars of FOIA law.

## Government's burden to prove an exemption under §552(a)(4)(B)

Under §552(a)(4)(B), the agency bears the burden of proving that a request is subject to a statutory exemption.  The preponderance of the evidence standard applies: the agency must show, on a balance of probabilities, both that the records are compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could **reasonably** be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.  The *Foad Farahi* court reinforced this dual‑prong test.  There, the FBI’s affidavits linked the records to investigations of national‑security violations and defended the claim that releasing the data could jeopardize ongoing probes.  In concurrence, the court affirmed that the agency had met its burden, noting that the facts and records the FBI produced satisfied federal requirements for applicability of Exemption 7(A).

## Deference principle for law‑enforcement agencies

Courts generally grant a high degree of deference to law‑enforcement agencies when they argue that an exemption applies, because agencies possess specialized knowledge and are better positioned to assess the potential impact of disclosure.  The *Campbell v. DOJ* decision articulated this “fact‑finding” deference, noting that agencies are entitled to a “fair measure of deference” for determinations within their expertise.  In *Farahi*, the appellate court recognized that the FBI is a “law‑enforcement agency” and that the FBI’s conclusions about the nature and scope of its investigations should carry that same deference.  The court explicitly acknowledged the “ordinary deference” owed to the agency’s determinations, while still inspecting the evidentiary basis that supported those conclusions.

## National‑security context and heightened deference

When FOIA disputes involve national‑security matters, the courts apply an elevated level of deference.  In *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*, the Supreme Court held that national‑security concerns “create a different set of presuppositions” and that the agency’s assessment of potential harm to such interests should be given more deference.  The *Farahi* court followed that guidance, noting the FBI’s investigation focused on terrorism and foreign‑terrorist organization ties.  Because the national‑security framework already demands restraint on information disclosure, the court concluded that the expository evidence presented by the FBI warranted a particularly skeptical, yet supportive, review that honored the heightened deference standard.

## Role of ex‑parte evidence

Ex‑parte affidavits are the procedural mechanism that allows an agency to present confidential information behind closed doors while still weighing the information against FOIA’s public‑interest purpose.  The statute authorizes agencies to file affidavits under seal when the information sought would otherwise reveal what is exempt.  In *Farahi*, the agency advanced *David Hardy’s* and *Joseph Bender’s* sworn statements, both internal officials with direct knowledge of the investigation.  The court accepted those affidavits as “public” and “ex‑parte” evidence, respectively, and treated them as conclusive evidence of ongoing investigations, provided they met the requirement of “reasonable specificity.”  This acceptance hinged on the affidavits’ qualifications (e.g., chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section) and the depth of detail they offered about the investigation’s status.

## Review standards for affidavits in FOIA cases

Appellate courts review affidavits de novo, but they apply an evidentiary standard that requires the submissions to show sufficient specificity and credibility.  In *Reps. Comm. v. FBI*, the court held that a summary‑judgment motion is reviewed de novo, but “the agency’s evidence should still be substantiated.”  The *Farahi* court likewise looked closely at the affidavits’ language, confirming that the FBI imposed the burden of demonstrating that the content was intertwined with exempt portions and that no meaningful segregable portion existed.  The court found that *Hardy’s* and *Bender’s* statements clearly explained how the records were “inextricably intertwined” with the ongoing counterterrorism effort, satisfying the statutory requirement to take “reasonable steps” to segregate.

In practice, the court will examine whether the affidavit:

1. Identifies the specific enforcement proceeding(s) it might affect;
2. Demonstrates that disclosure would materially interfere with that proceeding; and
3. Shows why the exempt portion cannot be severed without losing integrity.

When these criteria are met, as the *Farahi* court found, the affidavits are sufficient to uphold the claimed exemption.  The overall effect of the statutory and judicial frameworks is to give agencies the practical tools to protect sensitive information while affording courts the procedural flexibility to ensure that deference is exercised appropriately and not abused.

# Application to the FBI's Exemption 7(A) Claims

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit affirmed a district‑court ruling that the FBI’s withholding of Farahi’s file was justified by Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) Exemption 7(A). That exemption bars disclosure when the records are “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and the disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.” The court’s analysis hinged on five interrelated pillars: the FBI’s evidence of terrorist connections, Hardy’s declaration establishing the record’s law‑enforcement nature, Bender’s declaration confirming ongoing proceedings, the court’s treatment of the eight‑year gap between the initial request and the final adjudication, and Farahi’s timeliness argument. Below we examine each pillar and explain how the court reconciled the statutory requirements against the evidentiary record.

## Evidence of FBI investigations linking Farahi to terrorist organizations

The FBI’s primary justification for invoking Exemption 7(A) rests on its parallel claim that Farahi’s file contains evidence of associations with foreign terrorist organizations and individuals linked to international terrorism. The FBI documented this through internal investigative activities that, in the court’s view, constitute “law‑enforcement purposes.” The agency’s internal filings and subsequent filings in the pending civil case detail that its investigative mission encompassed: 1) potential recruitment of Farahi for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood; 2) his alleged cooperation with Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and other Al Qaeda affiliates; 3) his support of charities—e.g., the Holy Land Foundation or the Global Relief Foundation—used to finance terrorist activity. These claims, although originally articulated in the 2015 immigration proceedings before the Department of Homeland Security, were reiterated by the FBI in its 2019 summary‑judgment motion.

The court regarded this evidence as satisfying the first statutory test that “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” must be met. Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court accorded it a “fair measure of deference” (*Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119; *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32). The declarations the FBI supplied included not only statements of intent but also more specific details: the FBI believed it had sufficient evidence to be “guided by the scope of the investigation, the alleged act of recruitment, or the nature of the excitement for the extremist affiliation.” These declarations proved that the officer‑handwritten or typed documents were produced as part of a formal investigative effort directed at potential terrorism elements, satisfying the statutory hypothecal of “law‑enforcement purposes.”

## Hardy declaration establishing “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes”

David Hardy, the RIDS Section Chief, filed the first public and ex‑parte declarations. As the court described, the *public* Hardy declaration explained that the withheld records had been “compiled… in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” (J.A. 42). The *ex‑parte* Hardy declaration “referred to the various investigative actions that had been taken and that the FBI was continuing to undertake.” This expression of intent is “reasonably specific” enough to satisfy the court’s requirement that the agency “establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties” (*Campbell*, 164 F.3d 32). The FBI’s evidence in Hardy’s statements was further reinforced in the *Mapoth* and *CREW* cases previously decided, where the court required an agency’s factual determination that a record was “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” to be supported by more than a vanilla statement. In *Farahi*, however, the court accepted Hardy’s assertion because the declaration both identified the investigative target—terrorist affiliates—and the investigative function—collecting intelligence—through a brief yet substantive statement. In *Mapoth v. DOJ*, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 the court specified that “the agency must furnish the court with a rationale that links the held documents with the pertinent routine law‑enforcement activity.” The court found the FBI’s statement “sufficient” to meet that standard.

Because the FBI’s jurisdiction and expertise in investigations gave the agency a perspective not normally available to the court, *Farahi* followed the doctrine that “exemption 7(A) claims belonging to a law‑enforcement agency should be treated with deference, especially when national‑security concerns are at issue” (*Campbell*, 164 F.3d 32; *Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918). Accordingly, the court treated Hardy’s statements as a credible basis for the records’ law‑enforcement origin and dismissed the plaintiff’s counter‑claim that the FBI’s assertions were mere conjecture.

## Bender declaration confirming ongoing enforcement proceedings

The court’s consideration of the “pending or reasonably anticipated” element hinged on the documents that confirmed the continuity of the investigation. After a district‑court denial based on insufficient information, the FBI submitted a supplemental declaration by Joseph Bender, the Acting RIDS Section Chief. The *public* Bender declaration, short in length, confirmed that the FBI’s investigation of Farahi “remains ongoing.” More detailed ex‑parte material was also filed to satisfy the segregation requirements and to provide transparency on the lack of segregable portions.

Farahi challenged the declaration on the ground that it was “conclusory” and two‑sentence in length, lacking the “depth” necessary to satisfy the statutory threshold. The court, however, understood the Bender statement as an update to Hardy’s earlier declarations, rather than a standalone assertion that the FBI had reached a new conclusion. In *CREW*, the court had recognized that once a reasonable expectation of a pending proceeding had been established, it need only be reaffirmed on a timely basis. The Bender declaration was seen as that “update.” The court noted that it had already found the FBI “seemingly” met its burden when the DOJ’s records were first prepared to the determination (J.A. 122). Therefore, the Bender statement was a logical supplement rather than a mere bundle of facts.

Moreover, the court condemned the plaintiff’s criticism that “the passage of time” could invalidate the Bender declaration. The court explained that when investigations are ongoing, even over long periods, “the relevant statements” can remain valid as long as the factual basis has not been rebutted. In this sense, the Bender declaration served as a “reasonable justification” that the ongoing investigation had not ended and that a prospective proceeding was still likely. The court’s decision to accept this quotation directly aligned with the standard in *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61.

## Treatment of pendency after eight years

An eight‑year interval between the FBI’s denial and the final appellate ruling raised legitimate questions: could the courts still accept the FBI’s assertion that the records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings”? The statute does not impose a strict time limit on “pending or reasonably anticipated” and the appellate court must interpret it within the statutory text and case law. The precedent set by *CREW* indicated that a “pending” proceeding need not involve a contemporaneous charge but may in fact refer to an active investigative program that may culminate in a future prosecutorial case.

The court acknowledged that the markings of “ongoing” investigations might already be “vague” (*CREW* cautioned). However, the reason for that vagueness is not fatal as long as the agency’s doctrine remains credible. In *Farahi*, the FBI’s earlier and later affidavits, the statements within the immigration proceedings, and the docket’s indication that the case had been active all served to support the pendency claim. The FBI’s repeated assertion that it had “continues to gather evidence” was the script that the court accepted. Additionally, the FBI’s demonstrated “immediate risk” to law‑enforcement success: if the records were released, an opposing force could “gain situational awareness” and alter their approach, thereby undermining any future prosecution. This “reasonable expectation of interference” satisfied the statutory requirement.

As the case demonstrates, the passage of time in national‑security investigations is not unavoidably a bar. The court recognized that, in cases that operate on a long‑term intelligence‑gathering strategy, the investigative “fall‑off” can be obviated by the federal law that requires “exemption if interference might be expected.” Thus the eight‑year gap merely served as a signal that the court needed more specific evidence of a continuing, prospective proceeding, which the DOJ supplied in the Bender affidavit. The court accepted that the government had met its burdens.

## Counterarguments about timeliness

Farahi’s substantive argument that the eight‑year delay undermined the FBI’s claim is not trivial. He posited that the “vague” assertion of ongoing activity, together with the brevity of the Bender statement, failed to carry the weight required by the statute. In triaging this challenge, the court repeated the doctrine that “deference is adequate when a government agency has afforded a credible, forward‑looking claim that a proceeding remains pending.” The courts had routinely accepted “ex‑parte” affidavits as full evidence, particularly when the documents under review might reveal sensitive counterterrorism intelligence. As the district court notes, “nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration” (J.A. 139). Therefore, the plaintiff’s challenge that the court relied too heavily on Bender’s ex‑parte statement was misplaced.

The court also re‑affirmed the “reasonable steps” requirement for segregation. Consequently, even though a small portion of the documents might be non‑exempt, if the records are interspersed such that segregation would be impracticable, the agency can withhold the entire file. The court found the FBI satisfied this burden, as the “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental statement was sufficient. The plaintiffs’ comment that the court’s strengthening of the segregation evidence was “neither here nor there” was also dismissed because it did not detract from the search that the court had to confirm the existence of the exemption.

In sum, the appellate court affirmed the district court’s ruling and held that the FBI’s Exemption 7(A) claim was sound. The court’s deference to the FBI’s law‑enforcement determination, its weighting of the evidence of a continuing investigation, and its acceptance that extended periods of time do not automatically render the exemption inapplicable were central to the outcome. The decision reflects the broader principle that FOIA protections must be balanced against the government’s need to conduct and preserve investigations related to national security and counterterrorism.

# Segregability Standard and Release Obligations

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requires federal agencies to provide requested records unless an exemption applies. Even when an exemption is invoked, a non‑exempt portion of the record must be released if it can be separated in a *reasonable* manner. This chapter explains FOIA §552(a)(8)(A)(ii)’s segregation requirement, how courts determine whether a portion is “reasonably segregable,” and the way the district court in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* accepted the FBI’s justification that the exempt documents could not be segregated.

---

## FOIA §552(a)(8)(A)(ii) – Requirement to Segregate Non‑Exempt Portions

Section 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) obliges an agency to “take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information.” The statutory mandate is clear: a request cannot be denied merely because part of it falls under an exemption if some of the request is not exempt. The law guarantees the public access to the unredacted, non‑exempt information that is contained within or can be extracted from the responsive record, unless the agency can show that such segregation is impossible without damaging the exempt material or that the non‑exempt portion is so intertwined as to be practically inseparable. The Supreme Court and District of Columbia Circuit case law have repeatedly affirmed the agency’s duty to facilitate access, but recognize that the obligation has limits when the exemption is substantive and the non‑exempt data are embedded within the exempt.

In *Farahi*, the FBI responded to a FOIA request for his record with a mixture of disclosures and withholdings. After a decade of litigation and multiple summary‑judgment motions, the court turned to §552(a)(8)(A)(ii) to decide whether the FBI should apply a full‑record forfeiture or release the non‑exempt parts. The statutory language was read in light of the agency’s obligation to act in a “reasonable” manner.

---

## Definition of 'Reasonably Segregable' and Intertwined Documents

Courts define a portion as *reasonably segregable* when the non‑exempt information can be separated from the exempt material with “reasonably modest effort” and does not require the withholding of the exempt portion to protect a legitimate governmental interest. Conversely, a record is *inextricably intertwined* with exempt content when the exempt and non‑exempt portions are so blended that any attempt to extract the non‑exempt material would necessarily expose or require the disclosing of the exempt information.

The *Good good* standard is applied by examining the structure of the record. If the exempt data are in inner layers of a hard‑copy volume or are harvested from searchable electronic files where each word may belong to an exemption, the record may be deemed inseparable. If the exempt data consist of isolated pages or sightly marked sections that can be removed at the agency’s discretion, the non‑exempt portion may be released. The decision therefore hinges on the relationship between the exempt material and the rest of the record, not simply on the amount of content.

---

## Key Segregation Cases

| Case | Holding | Relevance to *Farahi* |
|------|---------|-----------------------|
| **Juarez v. DOJ**, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008) | The court held that a government may withhold the entirety of a record when the non‑exempt portions “are inextricably intertwined” with exempt portions. | *Farahi* relied on this precedent to justify its dismissal of the segregation claim because the FBI’s records were densely packed with law‑enforcement material and potential national‑security concerns. |
| **Mead Data Center, Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force**, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977) | The court explained that “reasonable steps” are required to segregrate non‑exempt information. The lack of a clear method for segregation can justify withholding the entire record. | The *Farahi* district court accepted the FBI’s characterization that no practicable segregation existed, echoing Mead’s emphasis on the necessity of a viable method. |
| **Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs**, 57 F.4th 1061 (D.C. Cir. 2023) | The court reiterated that intermixing of key evidence and exempt metadata can prevent segregation, especially when the exempt material includes investigative documentation. | *Farahi*’s record set comprised 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of integrated investigational data; the same reasoning underpinned the district court’s refusal to segregate the entire package. |

These cases collectively underscore a legal blueprint: an agency may rely on a full‑record stripping only if it demonstrates that segregation would not be feasible without harming a protected interest. The *Farahi* case follows this same reasoning, focusing on the FBI’s assertion that interdependence of the contours of the investigative record precludes segregation.

---

## FBI's Justification for Non‑Segregation

After the FBI missed the statutory response deadline, it sought summary judgment based on Exemption 7(A). The agency’s key argument for withholding the entire record rested on two findings:

1. **Law‑enforcement compilation and ongoing investigations** – The FBI’s declarations from Secretary‑level officials (Hardy and Bender) asserted that the 10,750‑page, 80‑CD corpus was compiled in “furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism.” They contended that these investigations were still “ongoing” and that any disclosure could jeopardize further enforcement actions.

2. **Inextricable intertwining** – In the supplemental declarations, the FBI described how the exempt content was “interwoven with non‑exempt information” so tightly that “reasonable segregation would require removing large segments of protected material, thereby compromising law‑enforcement interests.” The agency illustrated this with reference to the structure of the files – records listed sequentially, with internal pages that cannot be reliably isolated without destroying their contextual relevance.

The Bender declaration, submitted under seal and ex–parte, reinforced the earlier assertions by confirming that the investigations had not been terminated as of March 2023. The court treated this form as a *supplemental update* rather than a new basis for confidentiality. The supplemental explanation was also more detailed in its evaluation of the requested portion’s segregation, addressing the court’s earlier concern about the Bender letter’s brevity. In its supplemental order, the district court explicitly noted that the FBI’s explanation satisfied the statutory “reasonable steps” requirement, written in the judgment: “The FBI’s supplemental ex‑parte declaration afforded sufficient detail on why the exempt files could not be split from the non‑exempt portion.”

---

## Court's Acceptance of FBI’s Supplemental Explanation

The district court, guided by FOIA §552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and the custody framework established in *Juarez*, *Mead*, and *Perioperative*, reviewed the FBI’s second‑round disclosure. It acknowledged the agency’s burden to demonstrate that:

1. **No meaningful non‑exempt information exists in a segregatable format** – The court found the FBI’s depiction of a “triangular” structure of sensitive investigations and other references (e.g., associations with known terrorist organizations) sufficient to defeat a segregation request.

2. **The process of segregation would demonstrably harm the FBI’s investigative conduct** – By citing *Juarez* and *Mead*, the court recognized the government’s claim that extracting a single page would predispose the subjects of the investigation to evade law‑enforcement scrutiny.

Because the FBI had provided an ex‑parte, “detailed justification,” the court accepted that the agency had met its burden under §552(a)(8)(A)(ii). The supplemental order stated, in part, “The FBI has provided a detailed justification for why the protected material is not reasonably segregable,” and concluded that the present declaration satisfied the agency’s obligation to take reasonable steps to segregate and release non‑exempt information.

The appeal therefore upheld the district court’s judgment, affirming that the FBI legitimately withheld the entire record under Exemption 7(A) while satisfying the segregation requirement. The decision illustrates the strict compliance test: the agency must sandwich its statutory authority with factual detail, and the court must be persuaded that segregation is impracticable before the district court can afford to rely on the full‑record forfeiture.

---

**In summary**, FOIA §552(a)(8)(A)(ii) compels agencies to separate non‑exempt data whenever possible. A record is deemed “reasonably segregable” when the non‑exempt material can be extracted with a reasonable effort and without materially harming the exempt interest. Courts thus scrutinize the agency’s declarations for evidence of intertwining and procedural feasibility. In *Farahi*, the FBI satisfied these standards with a comprehensive, updated declaration, and the courts dutifully accepted that its assertion that the exempt portions could not be segregated was “reasonable,” rendering the suppression of the record lawful.

# Holding, Reasoning, Significance, and Future Directions

In the appellate decision, the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit affirmed the district court’s denial of summary judgment and its finding that the FBI’s withheld records were protected by Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The court explained that, given the FBI’s law‑enforcement specialty and the national‑security context of Farahi’s file, the agency’s arguments received a substantial degree of deference. The decision reaffirmed the strict burden on the government to demonstrate that withheld documents were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that their release could reasonably interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. Where the agency could not justify that portions of the records were reasonably segregable, the court held the records must be withheld in their entirety.

## Affidavit‑Based Deference and the Burden of Proof

The court followed the precedent that the government bears the burden of showing that an FOIA exemption applies and that a law‑enforcement agency’s determination warrants deference. Drawing on earlier cases that have applied a "deference" standard to the FBI—particularly in matters involving national security—the appellate court accepted the FBI’s declarations without requires the judge to independently verify the agency’s assertions of ongoing investigations. The Hardy and Bender affidavits recounted that investigations involving Farahi remained active as of March 2023; the court noted that the FBI’s own prior summary‑judgment motion had already convinced the district court of the existence of pending work. Even though the Bender summary was succinct, the court treated it as an update, not a new disclosure of evidence that could be segregated, noting that the pleadings had already established the pendency of enforcement proceedings. The court emphasized that the existence of an investigation need not be proven by exhaustive detail—mere attestation that an inquiry is “ongoing” is sufficient as long as the affidavit is specific enough to avoid being conclusory.

## Segregation and the “Inextricable” Standard

The court applied the segregation rule contained in § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii): an agency may withhold an entire record if non‑exempt parts are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt parts, and the agency must demonstrate that this is the case. The district court had reviewed the FBI’s ex‑parte submissions and found that the withheld documents were tightly woven with the investigative material in such a way that extracting meaningful, non‑exempt portions would be impossible. The court cited classic authorities that discourage the release of text that is paragraph‑by‑paragraph interleaved with sensitive content, noting that the “focus of the FOIA is information, not merely the words written” and that an attempt to cherry‑pick individual phrases can still risk jeopardizing investigations. Consequently, the court found no “meaningful” segregable material and upheld the FBI’s blanket withholding.

## Implications for FOIA Litigation in National‑Security Contexts

The decision solidifies the position that FOIA plaintiffs seeking records tied to national‑security investigations must meet a formidable burden. The court’s reliance on deference, the clear articulation of the interference standard, and the strict segregation requirement serve as a cautionary template for future cases. Plaintiffs who challenge FOIA denials under Exemption 7(A) will need to present affidavits that precisely describe how the withheld records are tied to ongoing investigations and demonstrate that the investigators’ activities could be materially prejudiced by disclosure. Moreover, the case illustrates that even the existence of an ongoing investigation can satisfy the “reasonably anticipated” element when backed by appropriately detailed affidavits.

## Transparency Versus Law‑Enforcement Effectiveness

At the heart of the court’s analysis lies the classic FOIA tension: the public interest in openness versus the state’s interest in effective policing. The appellate court reiterated that Exemption 7(A) is a narrow carve‑out precisely designed to protect law‑enforcement work that is more valuable when kept confidential. By upholding the FBI’s refusal to disclose large swaths of material, the court affirmed that the risk of compromising investigations outweighs the incremental transparency a FOIA request might provide. The decision also confirmed that the segregation standard is an instrument to prevent the inadvertent release of isolated, harmless passages that, if taken out of context, could still harm investigatory efforts.

## Potential Paths Forward for Litigants and Policy Makers

This ruling invites both litigants and policy makers to consider whether existing FOIA procedures for national‑security matters are sufficiently responsive to the public’s demand for accountability. Litigants may explore more extensive affidavits, possibly supported by corroborating evidence outside the sealed files, to strengthen claims that certain portions are segregable. Courts might develop clearer guidance on what constitutes a “reasonably specific” determination of segregation. On the policy side, Congress could mandate more transparent procedures, such as the creation of a protected disclosure review panel that examines whether a particular portion of a record can be safely released without jeopardizing investigations. Alternatively, FOIA reforms could clarify the “reasonably anticipated” benchmark, ensuring that courts do not rely solely on vague language to justify broad nondisclosure. Finally, the FOIA system might consider adopting a more robust data‑minimization framework, whereby agencies are encouraged to separate sensitive content at the earliest stages of documentation.

In sum, the appellate court’s affirmation sharpens the doctrinal lines that govern FOIA requests touching national‑security investigations, confirming the high bar for plaintiffs while underscoring the courts’ deference to law‑enforcement agencies. The decision serves as a procedural benchmark for future disputes and offers a clear starting point for any substantive or policy‑focused re‑examination of how FOIA promotes transparency beside the secrecy required by national‑security interests.
