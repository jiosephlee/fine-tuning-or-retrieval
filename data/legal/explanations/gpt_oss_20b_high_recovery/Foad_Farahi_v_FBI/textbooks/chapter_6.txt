Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 6: # Holding, Reasoning, Significance, and Future Directions

In the appellate decision, the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit affirmed the district court’s denial of summary judgment and its finding that the FBI’s withheld records were protected by Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The court explained that, given the FBI’s law‑enforcement specialty and the national‑security context of Farahi’s file, the agency’s arguments received a substantial degree of deference. The decision reaffirmed the strict burden on the government to demonstrate that withheld documents were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that their release could reasonably interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. Where the agency could not justify that portions of the records were reasonably segregable, the court held the records must be withheld in their entirety.

## Affidavit‑Based Deference and the Burden of Proof

The court followed the precedent that the government bears the burden of showing that an FOIA exemption applies and that a law‑enforcement agency’s determination warrants deference. Drawing on earlier cases that have applied a "deference" standard to the FBI—particularly in matters involving national security—the appellate court accepted the FBI’s declarations without requires the judge to independently verify the agency’s assertions of ongoing investigations. The Hardy and Bender affidavits recounted that investigations involving Farahi remained active as of March 2023; the court noted that the FBI’s own prior summary‑judgment motion had already convinced the district court of the existence of pending work. Even though the Bender summary was succinct, the court treated it as an update, not a new disclosure of evidence that could be segregated, noting that the pleadings had already established the pendency of enforcement proceedings. The court emphasized that the existence of an investigation need not be proven by exhaustive detail—mere attestation that an inquiry is “ongoing” is sufficient as long as the affidavit is specific enough to avoid being conclusory.

## Segregation and the “Inextricable” Standard

The court applied the segregation rule contained in § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii): an agency may withhold an entire record if non‑exempt parts are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt parts, and the agency must demonstrate that this is the case. The district court had reviewed the FBI’s ex‑parte submissions and found that the withheld documents were tightly woven with the investigative material in such a way that extracting meaningful, non‑exempt portions would be impossible. The court cited classic authorities that discourage the release of text that is paragraph‑by‑paragraph interleaved with sensitive content, noting that the “focus of the FOIA is information, not merely the words written” and that an attempt to cherry‑pick individual phrases can still risk jeopardizing investigations. Consequently, the court found no “meaningful” segregable material and upheld the FBI’s blanket withholding.

## Implications for FOIA Litigation in National‑Security Contexts

The decision solidifies the position that FOIA plaintiffs seeking records tied to national‑security investigations must meet a formidable burden. The court’s reliance on deference, the clear articulation of the interference standard, and the strict segregation requirement serve as a cautionary template for future cases. Plaintiffs who challenge FOIA denials under Exemption 7(A) will need to present affidavits that precisely describe how the withheld records are tied to ongoing investigations and demonstrate that the investigators’ activities could be materially prejudiced by disclosure. Moreover, the case illustrates that even the existence of an ongoing investigation can satisfy the “reasonably anticipated” element when backed by appropriately detailed affidavits.

## Transparency Versus Law‑Enforcement Effectiveness

At the heart of the court’s analysis lies the classic FOIA tension: the public interest in openness versus the state’s interest in effective policing. The appellate court reiterated that Exemption 7(A) is a narrow carve‑out precisely designed to protect law‑enforcement work that is more valuable when kept confidential. By upholding the FBI’s refusal to disclose large swaths of material, the court affirmed that the risk of compromising investigations outweighs the incremental transparency a FOIA request might provide. The decision also confirmed that the segregation standard is an instrument to prevent the inadvertent release of isolated, harmless passages that, if taken out of context, could still harm investigatory efforts.

## Potential Paths Forward for Litigants and Policy Makers

This ruling invites both litigants and policy makers to consider whether existing FOIA procedures for national‑security matters are sufficiently responsive to the public’s demand for accountability. Litigants may explore more extensive affidavits, possibly supported by corroborating evidence outside the sealed files, to strengthen claims that certain portions are segregable. Courts might develop clearer guidance on what constitutes a “reasonably specific” determination of segregation. On the policy side, Congress could mandate more transparent procedures, such as the creation of a protected disclosure review panel that examines whether a particular portion of a record can be safely released without jeopardizing investigations. Alternatively, FOIA reforms could clarify the “reasonably anticipated” benchmark, ensuring that courts do not rely solely on vague language to justify broad nondisclosure. Finally, the FOIA system might consider adopting a more robust data‑minimization framework, whereby agencies are encouraged to separate sensitive content at the earliest stages of documentation.

In sum, the appellate court’s affirmation sharpens the doctrinal lines that govern FOIA requests touching national‑security investigations, confirming the high bar for plaintiffs while underscoring the courts’ deference to law‑enforcement agencies. The decision serves as a procedural benchmark for future disputes and offers a clear starting point for any substantive or policy‑focused re‑examination of how FOIA promotes transparency beside the secrecy required by national‑security interests.
