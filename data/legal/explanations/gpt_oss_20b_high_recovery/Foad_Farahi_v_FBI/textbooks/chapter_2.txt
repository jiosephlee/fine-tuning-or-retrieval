Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Statutory and Judicial Framework for FOIA Exemption 7(A)

The conflict in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* was whether the FBI’s refusal to disclose the vast bulk of Farahi’s file was properly grounded in FOIA’s Exemption 7(A).  The district court ultimately found that the FBI had met the statutory burden, and the appellate court affirmed that finding.  In order to grasp the court’s reasoning it is helpful to begin with the text of the exemption itself, then to examine the two critical elements the agency must prove: that the records were *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* and that their disclosure would *reasonably be expected to interfere* with *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings.  The judicial mandates that guide the interpretation of these elements—most notably *Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Use of Secrets* (CREW), *Mapother*, and *Counter‑National Security Study* (CNSS)—provide the context that the court applied.

## Statutory language of §552(b)(7)(A) & (B)

FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) is codified in 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).  The text reads in full: “The Government may withhold from disclosure records *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* if the disclosure would *unreasonably interfere with the conduct of a pending or reasonably anticipated... law‑enforcement investigation or proceeding*.”  The statute therefore contains two predicates that the agency must satisfy: (1) the files must be compiled for law‑enforcement purposes; and (2) disclosure would unreasonably interfere with enforcement actions that are either pending or reasonably anticipated.  Under FOIA, the statutory bar applies only where both predicates are met.  The D.C. Circuit’s analysis in *Farahi* therefore hinges on whether the FBI can demonstrate each of those elements in light of the statutory text.

## “Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” definition

The statutory phrase “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” does not describe a specific type of document but rather the context in which the information was gathered.  In *CREW*, the court clarified that this must be “a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties.”  The agency must demonstrate that the records were created *in furtherance of the agency’s law‑enforcement mission*—which in the FBI’s case included investigations of national‑security and international‑terrorism violations.  As the court explained in *Farahi*, the FBI’s declaration asserted that the withheld portions of Farahi’s file had been compiled *in furtherance of” investigations of violations of national‑security and terrorism law*.  The court accepted that assertion, noting that the agency’s documentation (the “Hardy” and “Bender” affidavits) established a connection between the records and a bona fide law‑enforcement investigation.  Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court gave the agency deference on this point, a deference consistent with *Campbell v. DOJ* and reiterated in *CNSS*.

## “Interference” requirement for enforcement proceedings

The second statutory predicate demands that disclosure would “reasonably be expected to interfere” with the investigatory or prosecutorial activity.  In *Mapother*, the court emphasized that interference need only be a *reasonable expectation of harm* to the agency’s efforts, not a demonstration that actual interference will occur.  The typical justifications the court recognizes are: i) the disclosure would tip off subjects or conspirators; ii) the disclosure would compromise the agency’s ability to gather further evidence; or iii) the release would undermine the integrity of evidence.  In *Farahi*, the FBI’s filings explained that the withheld materials could “tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation” and thereby jeopardize the FBI’s continued investigations.  The appellate court found that this explanation satisfied the interference requirement because the evidence showed ongoing investigations and the potential for tangible harm.

## Jurisprudence: CREW, Mapother, CNSS

The procedural and substantive guidance for Exemption 7(A) is largely derived from the three cases mentioned.  *CREW* established that the interference test requires the existence of *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings and that agencies must show that disclosure would *reasonably* interfere.  *Mapother* clarified the meaning of “reasonably expected to interfere,” placing the burden on the agency to demonstrate the likelihood of harm rather than to prove actual harm.  *CNSS* further reinforced that courts should afford a degree of deference when the exempt records intersect with national‑security concerns, particularly in cases involving counterterrorism investigations.  In *Farahi*, the appellate court followed each of these precedents, using *CREW* to assess pendency, *Mapother* to evaluate the interference expectation, and *CNSS* to justify deference to the FBI’s law‑enforcement assessment.

## “Pending or reasonably anticipated” threshold

The *CREW* decision introduced the concept of “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceedings as a higher threshold than merely “ongoing” investigations.  The court explained that a proceeding is pending if it is in progress or is bound to proceed given the current facts; it is reasonably anticipated if the evidence suggests that an administrative or criminal action is likely to be taken.  In *Farahi*, the district court, and later the appellate court, relied on the FBI’s affidavits to show that the investigations remained active—specifically that the FBI had been *in possession of new information* and had been *continually gathering evidence* that could lead to a future prosecution.  The court rejected the plaintiff’s argument that time had diminished the plausibility of pending proceedings, reasoning that the agency's own affirmations that the investigations “remain ongoing” matched the high *CREW* threshold.  Thus, the circumscribed application of the term “pending or reasonably anticipated” bolstered the procedural justification for withholding the records.

---

In sum, *Foad Farahi* underscores how FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) demands a two‑part proof: records must be compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and their disclosure must reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.  The court’s deference to the FBI for the first element, along with its application of *CREW*, *Mapother*, and *CNSS* to the interference and pendency analyses, explain why the appellate court affirmed that the records were properly exempted.
