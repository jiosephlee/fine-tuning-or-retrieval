Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # Case Introduction and Procedural Background

This chapter traces the factual and procedural journey that led to Foad Farahi’s appeal.  It begins with Farahi’s immigration background and the allegations that spurred the FBI’s investigations, moves through the FOIA request that precipitated the dispute, and follows the series of motions, court determinations, and updates that culminated in the appellate decision of September 30 2025.

## Farahi's immigration history and alleged terrorist links

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who arrived in the United States in 1993.  In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings for exceeding the limits of his visa.  During the course of those proceedings, Farahi repeatedly sought relief through asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure.  In opposition, DHS introduced documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who asserted that Farahi had “connections to high‑level terrorists.”  The FBI further alleged that Farahi was linked to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and other known Al Qaeda members, to the convicted terrorist Jose Padilla, and to charities that allegedly financed terrorism, such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation.  These alleged ties formed the basis for the FBI’s investigative efforts and the records that later became the subject of Farahi’s FOIA request.

## FOIA request details (10,750 pages, 80 CDs)

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking the FBI file that accrued during his immigration proceedings.  The FBI identified a volume of potentially responsive information amounting to about 10,750 pages and 80 compact disks.  The agency missed the statutory deadline for a response, prompting Farahi to sue on the ground that the FBI had failed to comply with FOIA.  Over the next several years, the FBI reviewed the documents, released some portions, and withheld the majority.  After completing its review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in October 2019, invoking Exemption 7(A) on the basis that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were then pending or reasonably anticipated.”

## Initial summary‑judgment motion and Hardy's affidavit

In its initial summary‑judgment motion, the FBI relied on a declaration by David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section.  The public Hardy declaration asserted that the withheld documents were part of investigations into national‑security violations and international terrorism, that they were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, and that their release could interfere with ongoing or impending enforcement actions.  Hardy also submitted an ex‑parte declaration containing additional detail.  The district court, in a November 2022 proceeding, found that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden to show that the records were law‑enforcement compilations and that their disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings.  However, the court noted the lapse of time and, citing issues of pendency and segregation, denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered the FBI to update its assessment.

## Court requires reassessment of pendency and segregation

Because the district court had identified a “significant passage of time,” it required the FBI to re‑evaluate whether the enforcement proceedings that the records implicated were still pending or reasonably anticipated.  The court also demanded more complete information addressing the ability to segregate non‑exempt portions of the records from the exempt portions.  The court’s order stayed the summary‑judgment determination and placed a deadline on the FBI for supplemental declaratory material that would meet the court’s evidentiary standards for pendency and segregation.

## FBI’s updated filings

Responding to the court’s order, the FBI filed new declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS.  Bender’s public declaration confirmed that the FBI’s files on Farahi were still under active investigation and that “the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.”  His ex‑parte declaration, submitted under seal, reproduced the substantive details from Hardy’s prior statements and addressed the court’s concerns about segregation, explaining why non‑exempt material was not reasonably separable from the exempt portions.  The district court reviewed the updated filings, credited Bender’s confirmation of ongoing investigations, and determined that the FBI had satisfied the pendency and segregation requirements.  Accordingly, it granted the renewed summary‑judgment motion, holding that the records were protected under FOIA Exemption 7(A).  Farahi appealed.

The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, writing in August 2025, affirmed the district court’s decision.  The appellate court held that the FBI had met its burden to show that the files were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.  The court thus upheld the application of Exemption 7(A) and the district court’s summary judgment, resolving the dispute in the government's favor.
