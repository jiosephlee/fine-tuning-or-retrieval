Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 5: # Segregability Standard and Release Obligations

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requires federal agencies to provide requested records unless an exemption applies. Even when an exemption is invoked, a non‑exempt portion of the record must be released if it can be separated in a *reasonable* manner. This chapter explains FOIA §552(a)(8)(A)(ii)’s segregation requirement, how courts determine whether a portion is “reasonably segregable,” and the way the district court in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* accepted the FBI’s justification that the exempt documents could not be segregated.

---

## FOIA §552(a)(8)(A)(ii) – Requirement to Segregate Non‑Exempt Portions

Section 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) obliges an agency to “take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information.” The statutory mandate is clear: a request cannot be denied merely because part of it falls under an exemption if some of the request is not exempt. The law guarantees the public access to the unredacted, non‑exempt information that is contained within or can be extracted from the responsive record, unless the agency can show that such segregation is impossible without damaging the exempt material or that the non‑exempt portion is so intertwined as to be practically inseparable. The Supreme Court and District of Columbia Circuit case law have repeatedly affirmed the agency’s duty to facilitate access, but recognize that the obligation has limits when the exemption is substantive and the non‑exempt data are embedded within the exempt.

In *Farahi*, the FBI responded to a FOIA request for his record with a mixture of disclosures and withholdings. After a decade of litigation and multiple summary‑judgment motions, the court turned to §552(a)(8)(A)(ii) to decide whether the FBI should apply a full‑record forfeiture or release the non‑exempt parts. The statutory language was read in light of the agency’s obligation to act in a “reasonable” manner.

---

## Definition of 'Reasonably Segregable' and Intertwined Documents

Courts define a portion as *reasonably segregable* when the non‑exempt information can be separated from the exempt material with “reasonably modest effort” and does not require the withholding of the exempt portion to protect a legitimate governmental interest. Conversely, a record is *inextricably intertwined* with exempt content when the exempt and non‑exempt portions are so blended that any attempt to extract the non‑exempt material would necessarily expose or require the disclosing of the exempt information.

The *Good good* standard is applied by examining the structure of the record. If the exempt data are in inner layers of a hard‑copy volume or are harvested from searchable electronic files where each word may belong to an exemption, the record may be deemed inseparable. If the exempt data consist of isolated pages or sightly marked sections that can be removed at the agency’s discretion, the non‑exempt portion may be released. The decision therefore hinges on the relationship between the exempt material and the rest of the record, not simply on the amount of content.

## Key Segregation Cases

| Case | Holding | Relevance to *Farahi* |
|------|---------|-----------------------|
| **Juarez v. DOJ**, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008) | The court held that a government may withhold the entirety of a record when the non‑exempt portions “are inextricably intertwined” with exempt portions. | *Farahi* relied on this precedent to justify its dismissal of the segregation claim because the FBI’s records were densely packed with law‑enforcement material and potential national‑security concerns. |
| **Mead Data Center, Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force**, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977) | The court explained that “reasonable steps” are required to segregrate non‑exempt information. The lack of a clear method for segregation can justify withholding the entire record. | The *Farahi* district court accepted the FBI’s characterization that no practicable segregation existed, echoing Mead’s emphasis on the necessity of a viable method. |
| **Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs**, 57 F.4th 1061 (D.C. Cir. 2023) | The court reiterated that intermixing of key evidence and exempt metadata can prevent segregation, especially when the exempt material includes investigative documentation. | *Farahi*’s record set comprised 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of integrated investigational data; the same reasoning underpinned the district court’s refusal to segregate the entire package. |

These cases collectively underscore a legal blueprint: an agency may rely on a full‑record stripping only if it demonstrates that segregation would not be feasible without harming a protected interest. The *Farahi* case follows this same reasoning, focusing on the FBI’s assertion that interdependence of the contours of the investigative record precludes segregation.

---

## FBI's Justification for Non‑Segregation

After the FBI missed the statutory response deadline, it sought summary judgment based on Exemption 7(A). The agency’s key argument for withholding the entire record rested on two findings:

1. **Law‑enforcement compilation and ongoing investigations** – The FBI’s declarations from Secretary‑level officials (Hardy and Bender) asserted that the 10,750‑page, 80‑CD corpus was compiled in “furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism.” They contended that these investigations were still “ongoing” and that any disclosure could jeopardize further enforcement actions.

2. **Inextricable intertwining** – In the supplemental declarations, the FBI described how the exempt content was “interwoven with non‑exempt information” so tightly that “reasonable segregation would require removing large segments of protected material, thereby compromising law‑enforcement interests.” The agency illustrated this with reference to the structure of the files – records listed sequentially, with internal pages that cannot be reliably isolated without destroying their contextual relevance.

The Bender declaration, submitted under seal and ex–parte, reinforced the earlier assertions by confirming that the investigations had not been terminated as of March 2023. The court treated this form as a *supplemental update* rather than a new basis for confidentiality. The supplemental explanation was also more detailed in its evaluation of the requested portion’s segregation, addressing the court’s earlier concern about the Bender letter’s brevity. In its supplemental order, the district court explicitly noted that the FBI’s explanation satisfied the statutory “reasonable steps” requirement, written in the judgment: “The FBI’s supplemental ex‑parte declaration afforded sufficient detail on why the exempt files could not be split from the non‑exempt portion.”

---

## Court's Acceptance of FBI’s Supplemental Explanation

The district court, guided by FOIA §552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and the custody framework established in *Juarez*, *Mead*, and *Perioperative*, reviewed the FBI’s second‑round disclosure. It acknowledged the agency’s burden to demonstrate that:

1. **No meaningful non‑exempt information exists in a segregatable format** – The court found the FBI’s depiction of a “triangular” structure of sensitive investigations and other references (e.g., associations with known terrorist organizations) sufficient to defeat a segregation request.

2. **The process of segregation would demonstrably harm the FBI’s investigative conduct** – By citing *Juarez* and *Mead*, the court recognized the government’s claim that extracting a single page would predispose the subjects of the investigation to evade law‑enforcement scrutiny.

Because the FBI had provided an ex‑parte, “detailed justification,” the court accepted that the agency had met its burden under §552(a)(8)(A)(ii). The supplemental order stated, in part, “The FBI has provided a detailed justification for why the protected material is not reasonably segregable,” and concluded that the present declaration satisfied the agency’s obligation to take reasonable steps to segregate and release non‑exempt information.

The appeal therefore upheld the district court’s judgment, affirming that the FBI legitimately withheld the entire record under Exemption 7(A) while satisfying the segregation requirement. The decision illustrates the strict compliance test: the agency must sandwich its statutory authority with factual detail, and the court must be persuaded that segregation is impracticable before the district court can afford to rely on the full‑record forfeiture.

---

**In summary**, FOIA §552(a)(8)(A)(ii) compels agencies to separate non‑exempt data whenever possible. A record is deemed “reasonably segregable” when the non‑exempt material can be extracted with a reasonable effort and without materially harming the exempt interest. Courts thus scrutinize the agency’s declarations for evidence of intertwining and procedural feasibility. In *Farahi*, the FBI satisfied these standards with a comprehensive, updated declaration, and the courts dutifully accepted that its assertion that the exempt portions could not be segregated was “reasonable,” rendering the suppression of the record lawful.
