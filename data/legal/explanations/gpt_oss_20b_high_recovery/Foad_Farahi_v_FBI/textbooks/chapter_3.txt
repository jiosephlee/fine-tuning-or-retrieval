Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 3: # Burden‑Shifting, Deference, and Ex‑parte Evidence in FOIA

FOIA’s twin goals are clarity of government action and protection of sensitive information.  The statute therefore requires that agencies first try to comply, but it also provides a set of constitutional‑and‑statutory safeguards that often compel withholding.  Understanding the procedural burdens that arise when an agency invokes an exemption, the courts’ deference to law‑enforcement fact‑finders, and the special role of ex‑parte affidavits is essential for scholars of FOIA law.

## Government's burden to prove an exemption under §552(a)(4)(B)

Under §552(a)(4)(B), the agency bears the burden of proving that a request is subject to a statutory exemption.  The preponderance of the evidence standard applies: the agency must show, on a balance of probabilities, both that the records are compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could **reasonably** be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.  The *Foad Farahi* court reinforced this dual‑prong test.  There, the FBI’s affidavits linked the records to investigations of national‑security violations and defended the claim that releasing the data could jeopardize ongoing probes.  In concurrence, the court affirmed that the agency had met its burden, noting that the facts and records the FBI produced satisfied federal requirements for applicability of Exemption 7(A).

## Deference principle for law‑enforcement agencies

Courts generally grant a high degree of deference to law‑enforcement agencies when they argue that an exemption applies, because agencies possess specialized knowledge and are better positioned to assess the potential impact of disclosure.  The *Campbell v. DOJ* decision articulated this “fact‑finding” deference, noting that agencies are entitled to a “fair measure of deference” for determinations within their expertise.  In *Farahi*, the appellate court recognized that the FBI is a “law‑enforcement agency” and that the FBI’s conclusions about the nature and scope of its investigations should carry that same deference.  The court explicitly acknowledged the “ordinary deference” owed to the agency’s determinations, while still inspecting the evidentiary basis that supported those conclusions.

## National‑security context and heightened deference

When FOIA disputes involve national‑security matters, the courts apply an elevated level of deference.  In *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*, the Supreme Court held that national‑security concerns “create a different set of presuppositions” and that the agency’s assessment of potential harm to such interests should be given more deference.  The *Farahi* court followed that guidance, noting the FBI’s investigation focused on terrorism and foreign‑terrorist organization ties.  Because the national‑security framework already demands restraint on information disclosure, the court concluded that the expository evidence presented by the FBI warranted a particularly skeptical, yet supportive, review that honored the heightened deference standard.

## Role of ex‑parte evidence

Ex‑parte affidavits are the procedural mechanism that allows an agency to present confidential information behind closed doors while still weighing the information against FOIA’s public‑interest purpose.  The statute authorizes agencies to file affidavits under seal when the information sought would otherwise reveal what is exempt.  In *Farahi*, the agency advanced *David Hardy’s* and *Joseph Bender’s* sworn statements, both internal officials with direct knowledge of the investigation.  The court accepted those affidavits as “public” and “ex‑parte” evidence, respectively, and treated them as conclusive evidence of ongoing investigations, provided they met the requirement of “reasonable specificity.”  This acceptance hinged on the affidavits’ qualifications (e.g., chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section) and the depth of detail they offered about the investigation’s status.

## Review standards for affidavits in FOIA cases

Appellate courts review affidavits de novo, but they apply an evidentiary standard that requires the submissions to show sufficient specificity and credibility.  In *Reps. Comm. v. FBI*, the court held that a summary‑judgment motion is reviewed de novo, but “the agency’s evidence should still be substantiated.”  The *Farahi* court likewise looked closely at the affidavits’ language, confirming that the FBI imposed the burden of demonstrating that the content was intertwined with exempt portions and that no meaningful segregable portion existed.  The court found that *Hardy’s* and *Bender’s* statements clearly explained how the records were “inextricably intertwined” with the ongoing counterterrorism effort, satisfying the statutory requirement to take “reasonable steps” to segregate.

In practice, the court will examine whether the affidavit:

1. Identifies the specific enforcement proceeding(s) it might affect;
2. Demonstrates that disclosure would materially interfere with that proceeding; and
3. Shows why the exempt portion cannot be severed without losing integrity.

When these criteria are met, as the *Farahi* court found, the affidavits are sufficient to uphold the claimed exemption.  The overall effect of the statutory and judicial frameworks is to give agencies the practical tools to protect sensitive information while affording courts the procedural flexibility to ensure that deference is exercised appropriately and not abused.
