Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What is the harmless‑error standard for evidentiary rulings, and how was it applied here?
Question:
The opinion frequently references the doctrine that trials can be upheld if the error was harmless and cites cases like DeMarce and Keys. I’m not sure how the court determines whether an evidentiary error is harmless. What is the standard for assessing prejudice, the cumulative effect of errors, and how does it guide appellate reversal in this context?
Answer:
**Harmless‑error in federal criminal evidence**

1. **What the standard is, per the court**  
   *The court expressly cites DeMarce* → “We will not reverse a conviction … if the error was harmless.”  
   *And Keys* → “We will not overturn … unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.”

   In practice the court must decide whether the erroneous admission or exclusion:
   * (a) would have been admissible and substantial; **and**  
   * (b) would have given the jury a reasonable possibility of reaching a different verdict or sentence.  
   If the answer to both is *no*, the error is harmless and the judgment stands.

2. **How the standard is applied internally**  
   * **Admissibility & relevance** – The court first confirms whether the challenged evidence would have survived a proper test.  
     *Example:* The intrinsics of “previous possession” of the sawed‑off Marlin were upheld under **Battle**, so the evidence itself was admissible and crucial.  
   * **Effect on the verdict** – The court then asks: *would the evidence have mattered?*  
     * The record shows the government’s testimonial and physical evidence was “substantial.”  
     * Even though the defense objected to certain statements (e.g., D.F.’s remark about her sister’s death), the court held those remarks were within the scope of the broader statement, were admissible, and would not have shifted the jury’s mind.  
   * **Cumulative effect** – Under *Keys* the court looks at *all* trial errors together.  
     * The court noted that the errors “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict” (p. 998).  
     * Because the sum of the mistakes did not reach the threshold of “substantial prejudice,” reversal is unwarranted.

3. **Where the defendant’s burden lies**  
   * It is up to the defendant to prove that, *without* the erroneous evidence, the outcome would have likely been different.  
   * Cutbank did not meet that burden; the evidence was so strong that its exclusion (or the error in admission) would almost certainly still have led to conviction.

4. **Bottom line in this case**  
   * The evidentiary rulings were not erroneous (the evidence was admissible and admissible under the law).  
   * Even where the district court “allowed” evidence the court questioned, the appellate court found no real prejudice.  
   * Therefore, under the harmless‑error doctrine (DeMarce + Keys) the conviction and sentence are affirmed.
