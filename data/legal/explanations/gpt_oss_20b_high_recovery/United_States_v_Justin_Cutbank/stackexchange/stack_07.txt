Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) interact with sentencing memoranda in appellate review of guideline ranges?
Question:
The opinion notes that the PSR provided factual statements that the court adopted without objection. What is the role of the PSR in determining the advisory guidelines range, and how does appellate review treat errors that may arise from inaccuracies in the PSR versus errors in the district court’s own analysis?
Answer:
**In *Cutbank*, the PSR was the district court’s factual foundation for the sentencing‑memoranda calculations, and the appellate court reviewed the resulting range under the usual “correct‑calculation” test rather than an inquiry into the PSR itself.**  

---

### 1.  What the PSR does  

* **Facts for the memo.**  
  - The PSR “recommended a base offense level of 26… a three‑level increase… a two‑level increase… and a four‑level increase… for a total offense level of 35” (p. ??).  
  - It also assigned a Criminal History Category VI, yielding an advisory range of 292–365 months (p. ??).  

* **District‑court adoption.**  
  - The court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions” (p. ??).  
  - Because the PSR’s facts were on the record and accepted by the judge, the district court could rely on them in its sentencing‑memoranda and in the selection of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement.  

Thus the PSR’s role is to **provide the objective data that the sentencing memo uses to compute offense level and criminal‑history category**; the memo then lists the mandatory guideline range and the court’s consideration of § 3553(a) factors.

---

### 2.  Appellate review of the range  

The court followed the standard set in *Dunn* (723 F.3d 919) and *Rosales‑Mireles* (585 U.S. 129):

1. **Correct‑calculation test.**  
   * The appellate court must first establish that the district court *did not* make a “significant procedural error”—for example, “failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range” (p. ??).  
   * If the calculation is correct, the appellate court **presumes the sentence is reasonable** (Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793; Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809).  

2. **Where a PSR error could matter.**  
   * If a PSR fact is truly wrong (e.g., a prior conviction that did not exist), and that fact was *material* to the calculated offense level or category, the appellate court can treat the range as erroneous. The later step is a de‑novo review of the calculation—often resulting in a remand for a new calculation.  
   * In *Cutbank*, however, the appellate court found the court “did not err, much less clearly err, in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony” (p. ??). The PSR’s facts were therefore deemed accurate enough not to invalidate the range.  

3. **Distinguishing PSR errors from court‑analysis errors.**  
   * **PSR inaccuracies** are “questions of fact.”  The appellate court will examine whether the PSR incorrectly identified a prior conviction, a firearm alteration, or any other element that fed into the calculation.  If the misunderstanding would change the range, the court must correct it.  
   * **District‑court errors** are – in this context – errors in **applying** the guidelines or in **ignoring** contrary evidence.  The court in *Cutbank* was found to have “correctly calculated the applicable Guidelines range” (p. ??) and to have considered the § 3553(a) factors.  Any deviation from proper application would be a more serious procedural error that could trigger reversal.  

---

### 3.  Bottom line for appellate review

| Issue | PSR‑Based Fact | District‑Court Analysis |
|-------|---------------|------------------------|
| **Factual accuracy** | Review as a *question of fact*; if the PSR is wrong *and* that mistake changes the level or category, the appellate court may reverse or remand for a new calculation. | If the court misapplies law (e.g., wrong enhancement), a “significant procedural error” is found; appellate must correct or reverse. |
| **Guideline range** | The range is accepted if the PSR facts are accurate enough to produce it; the appellate court’s de‑novo test is applied to the calculation itself. | If the court’s calculation is wrong, the appellate court reverses or remands for a correct calculation. |
| **Substantive rationality** (post‑range) | Once the range is validated, the appellate court “presumes the sentence reasonable” and only looks at § 3553(a) factors if the record shows a potential departure. | Errors in the court’s independent analysis of § 3553(a) do not trigger reversal unless the sentence is substantively unreasonable. |

In *Cutbank*, the court’s **adoption of the PSR facts without objection** meant that the appellate panel treated those facts as part of the record.  Because the PSR’s facts were consistent with the district court’s application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the court correctly calculated the advisory range of 292–365 months, and the appellate court “presumed that the sentence was reasonable.”  No review of § 3553(a) factors was necessary because the range itself was unchallenged.

---

### TL;DR

The PSR supplies the data that feed the sentencing‑memoranda; the appellate court checks whether the court **correctly processed that data**.  An error in the PSR is a factual issue the appellate court can correct only if it changes the guideline range, whereas an error in the court’s own application of the guidelines is a procedural misstep that may invalidate the range or the sentence itself.
