Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the Confrontation Clause treat a witness’s prior statements to police when that witness also testifies?
Question:
The court says the Confrontation Clause is satisfied because D.F. “actually appear[s] in court and testify[s] in person.” How does that rule apply when the statements she made to police were made before she took the stand? Does the fact that she later testifies remove the hearsay exception? In what scenario would such prior statements still be considered inadmissible under the Confrontation Clause?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In *Cutbank* the court reaffirmed the 8th Circuit rule that a witness’s prior statements to police become admissible when that witness later **appears in court and testifies in person**.  
Thus D.F.’s statements made on the night of the incident are admissible even though they preceded her testimony, because her later appearance satisfies the Confrontation Clause and “overrides” the hearsay exception that applies to statements made to a police officer.  
The only time such statements would remain inadmissible is when the declarant **fails to appear and testify** in the trial (or a suitable “direct” witness is not present).  In that case the Confrontation Clause would not be satisfied and the statements would be barred as hearsay, unless they fall under another recognized hearsay exception.

---

### How the 8th Circuit rule applies in *Cutbank*

1. **Confrontation Clause requirement**  
   The opinion cites *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010):  
   > “The Confrontation Clause is satisfied *when the hearsay declarants … actually appear in court and testify in person*.”  
   The court therefore held that because D.F. later took the stand, the defendant could lawfully introduce her prior statements to the police.

2. **Effect on the hearsay exception**  
   The hearsay exception “statements to police officers” is a *categorical* exception, but – as the court reiterated – it is no longer fatal once the declarant appears in person.  
   The statements are still technically “hearsay” and would be disallowed if D.F. had been absent, but the presence of the witness satisfies the Confrontation Clause and makes those statements admissible as part of her own testimony.

3. **Why the case did not need a “limiting instruction”**  
   Because the declarant’s later appearance satisfies the Clause, the trial court considered the statements admissible without having to give a special hearsay limitation.  The court’s ruling was “not subject to abuse of discretion” under *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014).

### When prior statements would remain inadmissible

1. **Declarant absent or unable to testify**  
   If D.F. (or any declarant) had never taken the stand—whether because she was unavailable, incompetent, or refused to testify—the Confrontation Clause would **not** be satisfied.  In that scenario the earlier statements to police would be inadmissible as hearsay, even though they were made to law‑enforcement officers.

2. **No “direct” testimony of the statement**  
   The rule applies only when the witness is a *direct* testifying witness about the very statements in question.  If the witness never addresses or refers to the police statements in her testimony, the statements remain outside the scope of the testifying witness and would not be admissible.

3. **Statements that are not of the “testimonial” type**  
   While the opinion deals only with a testimonial statement to police, under the Crawford framework a statement that is **not** testimonial (e.g., to a private friend) is not governed by the Confrontation Clause.  In that case the statement would still be inadmissible as hearsay unless it fell under another exception.

---

**Bottom line:**  
In *Cutbank*, D.F.’s later appearance in court satisfied the Confrontation Clause and allowed the prosecution to rely on her prior statements to police.  Those statements would remain inadmissible only if she never appeared as a direct witness, or if the statements were not of the testimonial type and therefore were not subject to the Confrontation Clause at all.
