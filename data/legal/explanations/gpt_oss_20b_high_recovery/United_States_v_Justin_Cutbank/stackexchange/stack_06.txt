Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why must the appellate court confirm that the district court considered § 3553(a) factors when reviewing a sentence?
Question:
The appellate court discusses that the district court must have "actually considered" the 3553(a) factors, but it does not need to provide a mechanical recitation. What are the specific 3553(a) factors that a district court typically weighs, and how does the absence of an explicit recitation affect the appellate court’s ability to find a sentence unreasonable?
Answer:
**Why must the appellate court confirm that the district court considered § 3553(a) factors?**

The Supreme Court has made clear that § 3553(a) is a *statutory mandate* – a district court *must* deliberate on specific factors when setting a sentence.  The appellate court’s job is to be sure that that mandate was satisfied.  If the record fails to show that the trial judge actually looked at the factors, the sentence could be “unreasonable” under the statutory scheme, even if the sentence falls within the advisory range.

---

## 1.  The 3553(a) “factors” that a district court must weigh

> **§ 3553(a)** gives the court “particular regard” to **the following** factors (5 in all):  

| # | Factor | What it covers |
|---|--------|----------------|
| 1 | **Nature and circumstances of the offense** | The seriousness of the crime and the circumstances that affect it (e.g., use of a firearm, the presence of recent drug use). |
| 2 | **History and characteristics of the defendant** | Prior convictions, violent offenses, age, education, employment, health, etc. |
| 3 | **Community protection and deterrence** | The need to protect the community and to deter the defendant **and others** from similar conduct. |
| 4 | **Public protection** | The need to safeguard the public from future harm by the defendant. |
| 5 | **Measurable importance of correcting or reforming the defendant’s behavior** | Whether the sentence is intended to rehabilitate or correct the offender. |

(These factors are embedded in the federal sentencing guidelines, which emerged from the same statutory language.)

---

## 2.  How the appellate court uses the record

### 2.1  “Actual consideration” is required, but a *mechanical recitation* is not

The 8th Circuit says in *Justice* attitudes:

> “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.”  
> — *United States v. Maluoth*, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024)

In *Goodhouse*, the court affirmed that the “presumption that the district court exercised its discretion” persists once the record shows deliberation:  

> “A district court is presumed to have considered the § 3553(a) factors as long as it has reviewed the PSR, sentencing memorandum, and related documents.”  
> — *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023)

### 2.2  What constitutes “clear evidence” of consideration?

In the *Cutbank* opinion the appellate court looked for:

1. **Reference to the PSR** – “The court noted its review of the PSR and sentencing memoranda … and reviewed § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance.”  
2. **Explicit statement that the court “was aware” of the defendant’s argument** – this signals that the judge weighed the relevant factors.  
3. **Absence of any punitive pivot that suggests the judge ignored the factors** – the sentence stayed within the advisory range and the judge expressly rejected the defendant’s arguments.

If such statements were missing, the appellate court would typically assume the district court had failed to consider the factors, which could render the sentence “unreasonable” under § 3553(a).

---

## 3.  Consequences of an absent recitation

* **No explicit recitation ≠ irreligious omission.**  
  The appellate court can still affirm if the record shows the judge reviewed the PSR, the sentencing memorandum, or otherwise demonstrated consideration of the factors.

* **Without evidence of consideration the appellate court can reverse.**  
  The statutory mandate is *explicit*: a sentence is *unreasonable* if the court “fails to have particular regard” to *any* of the five factors.  If the record gives no indication that the judge did so, the sentence falls outside the statutory standard.  

* **Presence of deliberation precludes reversal for “lack of recitation.”**  
  In *Cutbank* the court found “the record [clearly] shows the district court considered the 3553(a) factors.”  Hence, even though the sentencing memorandum never listed each factor with a bullet‑point list, the appellate court declined to find an unreasonable sentence.

---

## 4.  Bottom line for the student

1. **Identify the 5 statutory factors** (nature of the offense, defendant’s history, deterrence, public protection, correction/reform).  
2. **Look for any indication that the district court reviewed the PSR or a sentencing memorandum** or otherwise discussed these factors.  
3. **If the record shows deliberation, an opponent of explicit recitation can still challenge the sentence** only if the judge’s treatment of the factors is demonstrably wrong or insufficient; otherwise the appellate court must defer to the trial judge's discretion.

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank* the appellate court affirmed the sentence because the record showed the district court had **actually considered** the § 3553(a) factors—no full mechanical recitation was required.  The absence of such a recitation, therefore, does not automatically make a sentence unreasonable; the court must first ascertain that the factors were indeed deliberated.
