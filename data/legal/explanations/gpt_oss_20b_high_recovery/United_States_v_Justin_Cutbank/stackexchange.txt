Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What does Rule 404(b) consider intrinsic evidence in a felon‑in‑possession case?
Question:
In the opinion the district court allowed D.F. to testify that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off Marlin before the incident, citing United States v. Battle. I’m confused about what makes this testimony “intrinsic” versus “extrinsic.” Does any prior possession of a firearm automatically satisfy Rule 404(b) in a felon‑in‑possession or is there a higher threshold? What distinguishing factors determine whether such prior‑possession evidence is admissible?
Answer:
**Rule 404(b) and the “intrinsic‑vs‑extrinsic” test**  
Rule 404(b) excludes evidence of a person’s character “for the purpose of proving that on a particular occasion the person acted in conformity with that character trait.”  But it expressly allows evidence that is *relevant to an element* of an offense—what the court calls **intrinsic** evidence.  The Supreme Court has held that a prior act that is needed to establish an element of the crime is intrinsically admissible, while evidence that merely shows a habit or propensity is extrinsic and normally excluded.

---

### What the 8th Cir. calls *intrinsic* in a felon‑in‑possession case  

In *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014) the court emphasized that evidence of a defendant’s prior *ownership or control of the specific weapon charged* is intrinsically relevant:

> “Prior possession of a firearm is *directly relevant* to proving later possession of that *same weapon* because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” ___ *Battle*

The *Cutbank* opinion follows this authority:

> “*We agree.* *Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.*” ___ *Cutbank*, § A(A) (quoting *Battle*)

In short:

| • | Condition for intrinsic evidence |
|---|------------------------------------|
| 1 | The evidence concerns the *same firearm* that is the subject of the felon‑in‑possession charge. |
| 2 | It is used to establish an element of the offense—here, that Cutbank *possessed* that gun. |
| 3 | The view is that proving continuous possession or ownership eliminates the need to speculate about the defendant’s motive or character. |

Because the evidence meets those criteria, the District Court allowed D.F.’s testimony that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off Marlin “handful of times” before the incident.

---

### “Any prior possession*‑does‑not* automatically satisfy 404(b)**  

- Prior possession of a *different* firearm, or only “he used guns before,” is *extrinsic*; it is a habit of gun use, not a fact needed to prove the current charge.  
- Extrinsic evidence must be weighed against the prejudice–probative‑value balancing test of 404(b).  The court in *Cutbank* noted this, but because the evidence was *intrinsic*, that balancing was unnecessary—intrinsic evidence is admissible unless prejudice outweighs probative value (which the court found it did not).

The distinguishing factor is **relevance to a specific element** (ownership/control of the exact weapon) versus mere demonstration of a general propensity to possess firearms.

---

### Key take‑aways for students

| Question | What the law says (from *Cutbank* and *Battle*) |
|----------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Does any prior possession automatically satisfy 404(b)? | **No.** Only *prior possession that is directly relevant to proving ownership/control of the specific weapon charged* is intrinsic. |
| What determines admissibility of a prior‑possession claim? | 1. Is the evidence about the *same weapon*? <br>2. Is it used to prove a fact needed for the offense (e.g., possession or continuous possession)? <br>3. If it is intrinsic, it is admissible; if it is extrinsic, it must survive the 404(b) prejudice–probative‑value test. |
| How did the Board decide it was intrinsic in the case? | The witness said she had seen Cutbank holding that very sawed‑off Marlin “handful of times” before the incident, tying directly to the *possession* element of the 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) charge. |

Thus, in a felon‑in‑possession case, *intrinsic* prior‑possession evidence is admissible when it represents the defendant’s prior ownership or control of the exact firearm charged, whereas general or unrelated prior ownership is considered *extrinsic* and normally excluded under Rule 404(b).

### How does the Confrontation Clause treat a witness’s prior statements to police when that witness also testifies?
Question:
The court says the Confrontation Clause is satisfied because D.F. “actually appear[s] in court and testify[s] in person.” How does that rule apply when the statements she made to police were made before she took the stand? Does the fact that she later testifies remove the hearsay exception? In what scenario would such prior statements still be considered inadmissible under the Confrontation Clause?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In *Cutbank* the court reaffirmed the 8th Circuit rule that a witness’s prior statements to police become admissible when that witness later **appears in court and testifies in person**.  
Thus D.F.’s statements made on the night of the incident are admissible even though they preceded her testimony, because her later appearance satisfies the Confrontation Clause and “overrides” the hearsay exception that applies to statements made to a police officer.  
The only time such statements would remain inadmissible is when the declarant **fails to appear and testify** in the trial (or a suitable “direct” witness is not present).  In that case the Confrontation Clause would not be satisfied and the statements would be barred as hearsay, unless they fall under another recognized hearsay exception.

---

### How the 8th Circuit rule applies in *Cutbank*

1. **Confrontation Clause requirement**  
   The opinion cites *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010):  
   > “The Confrontation Clause is satisfied *when the hearsay declarants … actually appear in court and testify in person*.”  
   The court therefore held that because D.F. later took the stand, the defendant could lawfully introduce her prior statements to the police.

2. **Effect on the hearsay exception**  
   The hearsay exception “statements to police officers” is a *categorical* exception, but – as the court reiterated – it is no longer fatal once the declarant appears in person.  
   The statements are still technically “hearsay” and would be disallowed if D.F. had been absent, but the presence of the witness satisfies the Confrontation Clause and makes those statements admissible as part of her own testimony.

3. **Why the case did not need a “limiting instruction”**  
   Because the declarant’s later appearance satisfies the Clause, the trial court considered the statements admissible without having to give a special hearsay limitation.  The court’s ruling was “not subject to abuse of discretion” under *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014).

### When prior statements would remain inadmissible

1. **Declarant absent or unable to testify**  
   If D.F. (or any declarant) had never taken the stand—whether because she was unavailable, incompetent, or refused to testify—the Confrontation Clause would **not** be satisfied.  In that scenario the earlier statements to police would be inadmissible as hearsay, even though they were made to law‑enforcement officers.

2. **No “direct” testimony of the statement**  
   The rule applies only when the witness is a *direct* testifying witness about the very statements in question.  If the witness never addresses or refers to the police statements in her testimony, the statements remain outside the scope of the testifying witness and would not be admissible.

3. **Statements that are not of the “testimonial” type**  
   While the opinion deals only with a testimonial statement to police, under the Crawford framework a statement that is **not** testimonial (e.g., to a private friend) is not governed by the Confrontation Clause.  In that case the statement would still be inadmissible as hearsay unless it fell under another exception.

---

**Bottom line:**  
In *Cutbank*, D.F.’s later appearance in court satisfied the Confrontation Clause and allowed the prosecution to rely on her prior statements to police.  Those statements would remain inadmissible only if she never appeared as a direct witness, or if the statements were not of the testimonial type and therefore were not subject to the Confrontation Clause at all.

### What is the harmless‑error standard for evidentiary rulings, and how was it applied here?
Question:
The opinion frequently references the doctrine that trials can be upheld if the error was harmless and cites cases like DeMarce and Keys. I’m not sure how the court determines whether an evidentiary error is harmless. What is the standard for assessing prejudice, the cumulative effect of errors, and how does it guide appellate reversal in this context?
Answer:
**Harmless‑error in federal criminal evidence**

1. **What the standard is, per the court**  
   *The court expressly cites DeMarce* → “We will not reverse a conviction … if the error was harmless.”  
   *And Keys* → “We will not overturn … unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.”

   In practice the court must decide whether the erroneous admission or exclusion:
   * (a) would have been admissible and substantial; **and**  
   * (b) would have given the jury a reasonable possibility of reaching a different verdict or sentence.  
   If the answer to both is *no*, the error is harmless and the judgment stands.

2. **How the standard is applied internally**  
   * **Admissibility & relevance** – The court first confirms whether the challenged evidence would have survived a proper test.  
     *Example:* The intrinsics of “previous possession” of the sawed‑off Marlin were upheld under **Battle**, so the evidence itself was admissible and crucial.  
   * **Effect on the verdict** – The court then asks: *would the evidence have mattered?*  
     * The record shows the government’s testimonial and physical evidence was “substantial.”  
     * Even though the defense objected to certain statements (e.g., D.F.’s remark about her sister’s death), the court held those remarks were within the scope of the broader statement, were admissible, and would not have shifted the jury’s mind.  
   * **Cumulative effect** – Under *Keys* the court looks at *all* trial errors together.  
     * The court noted that the errors “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict” (p. 998).  
     * Because the sum of the mistakes did not reach the threshold of “substantial prejudice,” reversal is unwarranted.

3. **Where the defendant’s burden lies**  
   * It is up to the defendant to prove that, *without* the erroneous evidence, the outcome would have likely been different.  
   * Cutbank did not meet that burden; the evidence was so strong that its exclusion (or the error in admission) would almost certainly still have led to conviction.

4. **Bottom line in this case**  
   * The evidentiary rulings were not erroneous (the evidence was admissible and admissible under the law).  
   * Even where the district court “allowed” evidence the court questioned, the appellate court found no real prejudice.  
   * Therefore, under the harmless‑error doctrine (DeMarce + Keys) the conviction and sentence are affirmed.

### How is the 4‑level USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) firearms enhancement calculated?
Question:
The district court applied a 4‑level increase because the firearm was used in connection with another felony. The opinion states this applies regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained. What exact facts must the district court find to justify this enhancement, and how does the presence of an altered or obliterated serial number factor into that calculation?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) the *four‑level* increase applies when the defendant **used or possessed a firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state or local felony offense** – the other felony need not be charged or convicted.  
The court must find from the record that the gun was in the defendant’s possession **while the defendant was committing a separate felony** (e.g., the kidnapping/assault of D.F. or the robbery of her phone). The mere presence of an altered or obliterated serial number is a **separate three‑level** increase; it does not affect the four‑level rule but is added to it.

---

### 1.  What facts must the district court establish for the 4‑level increase?

| Needed fact | Where it comes from in the case | How the opinion treats it |
|-------------|----------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **(a) The defendant possessed a firearm** | PSR: “the defendant possessed a sawed‑off Marlin .22” and “DNA on the gun matched Cutbank” | The district court cites this fact without error. |
| **(b) A separate felony was committed** | PSR: “He had … other felony convictions” and “… the gun was used in the kidnapping/assault of D.F. and the robbery of her phone” | The opinion notes that the “PSR’s factual statements … establish that the gun was ’used … in connection with another felony offense.’” |
| **(c) The firearm was used or possessed *in connection* with that felony** | The PSR puts the gun in the defendant’s hands during the assault on D.F. and the escape from the police; the same weapon was used to threaten the victim and to facilitate the theft of the cell phone. | The Court says the requirement is satisfied “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The factual basis in the PSR is deemed sufficient. |

The court therefore accepted the PSR’s findings that the sawed‑off Marlin was used *in connection with a different felony* (the kidnapping / assault of D.F. and the robbery of her phone). No actual “charge” or “conviction” for that other felony is a prerequisite; the factual connection alone is enough.

---

### 2.  How does an altered/obliterated serial number factor in?

* § 2K2.1(b)(4)(C) gives an **independent three‑level** increase when a firearm has an altered or obliterated serial number.  
* In *Cutbank*, the PSR also observed that the Marlin’s serial number was “altered/obliterated.” That fact alone triggers the 3‑level increase.  
* The serial‑number increase is **additive** to the 4‑level increase. The opinion explains the final offense level:

> “...a base offense level of 26 … plus a **three‑level increase** because the firearm had an altered/obliterated serial number, a two‑level increase because he recklessly created a risk of death … and a **four‑level increase** because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense … for a total offense level of 35.”

The serial‑number fact does **not** affect the existence of the 4‑level increase; it merely adds an additional set of levels that are summed with the base level and any other Guideline increases.

---

### Bottom line

The district court only has to show

1. **The defendant had the firearm**; and  
2. **The firearm was used/possessed in the course of *another* felony** (identified or inferred from the record, not necessarily charged in the current case).

Once that is established, the 4‑level increase applies automatically, regardless of any charges or convictions related to that "other" felony. The altered‑serial‑number fact is a separate, additive enhancement. In *Cutbank*, the PSR provided both sets of facts, so the court’s application of the 4‑level and the 3‑level increases is correct, and the resulting offense level of 35 is within the advisory range.

### Does an altered or obliterated serial number automatically trigger a 4‑level enhancement?
Question:
The opinion mentions a 2‑level increase for an altered serial number. Does that always apply, or is there more nuance? Under what circumstances would a court discount the serial‑number alteration when applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and how is that distinguished from the 4‑level enhancement?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No.  
A firearm whose serial number has been altered or obliterated only triggers the *feature‑based* enhancement – a 2‑/3‑level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6). That increase is evaluated and applied *separately* from the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) four‑level increase, which is triggered simply by the fact that the defendant *used or possessed* a firearm in connection with any other felony offense, regardless of whether the serial number was changed.

---

### How the opinion treats the two upgrades

| Guideline factor | How it’s described in the Cutbank opinion | How the court applied it |
|------------------|-------------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Altered / obliterated serial number** | “a three‑level increase because the firearm had an altered/obliterated serial number” (the PSR) | The district court accepted the PSR’s factual finding and added 3 levels to the base offense level. |
| **Possession / use of firearm in another felony** | “a four‑level increase because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense” (the PSR) | The court also accepted this finding and added 4 levels. |
| **Total level** | “for a total offense level of 35.” | The court’s sentencing range (292‑365 months) was based on that level. |

The appellate court expressly affirmed that “the district court did not err … in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.”  (Cutbank, 292‑360 m.)

---

### Why the serial‑number change is **not** the same as the 4‑level increase

* § 2K2.1(b)(6) lists several **feature‑based** increases, one of which is a **2‑ or 3‑level** increase for a "weapon or ammunition with an altered or obliterated serial number."  The guidelines treat that as a *separate* factor that adds to the basic offense level.

* § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is a wholly different provision: a **4‑level** increase is applied whenever “any firearm or ammunition was used or possessed in connection with another felony offense, **regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained.”**  The serial number is irrelevant to that provision.

Because the two provisions apply to different facts, a court can **discard the serial‑number enhancement** without affecting the 4‑level increase.  

- The court discards the serial‑number factor only if it finds no credible evidence that the serial number was altered or obliterated.  
- It can still apply the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4‑level increase if the firearm was in fact used or possessed in the commission of a separate felony, exactly as it was in Cutbank’s case.

---

### In Cutbank’s case

1. The PSR provided factual evidence (the gun was a sawed‑off Marlin .22 that bore an altered or missing serial number, evidence collected by forensic science).  The district court accepted that evidence and awarded the 3‑level increase for that feature.  
2. The PSR also provided evidence that the same gun was used during the violence against D.F. and in the escape from the police, thereby satisfying the requirement for the 4‑level enhancement.  
3. The appellate court affirmed because there was no indication that either of these factual foundations was flawed.

---

### Bottom line

*An altered or obliterated serial number does **not** automatically trigger a four‑level increase.*  
*The court treats the serial‑number factor as a distinct, lower‑level enhancement that can be omitted if the evidence is lacking, but the four‑level “felony‑firearm‑use” enhancement stands on its own and remains in effect as long as the weapon was used or possessed in the course of another felony offense.*

### Why must the appellate court confirm that the district court considered § 3553(a) factors when reviewing a sentence?
Question:
The appellate court discusses that the district court must have "actually considered" the 3553(a) factors, but it does not need to provide a mechanical recitation. What are the specific 3553(a) factors that a district court typically weighs, and how does the absence of an explicit recitation affect the appellate court’s ability to find a sentence unreasonable?
Answer:
**Why must the appellate court confirm that the district court considered § 3553(a) factors?**

The Supreme Court has made clear that § 3553(a) is a *statutory mandate* – a district court *must* deliberate on specific factors when setting a sentence.  The appellate court’s job is to be sure that that mandate was satisfied.  If the record fails to show that the trial judge actually looked at the factors, the sentence could be “unreasonable” under the statutory scheme, even if the sentence falls within the advisory range.

---

## 1.  The 3553(a) “factors” that a district court must weigh

> **§ 3553(a)** gives the court “particular regard” to **the following** factors (5 in all):  

| # | Factor | What it covers |
|---|--------|----------------|
| 1 | **Nature and circumstances of the offense** | The seriousness of the crime and the circumstances that affect it (e.g., use of a firearm, the presence of recent drug use). |
| 2 | **History and characteristics of the defendant** | Prior convictions, violent offenses, age, education, employment, health, etc. |
| 3 | **Community protection and deterrence** | The need to protect the community and to deter the defendant **and others** from similar conduct. |
| 4 | **Public protection** | The need to safeguard the public from future harm by the defendant. |
| 5 | **Measurable importance of correcting or reforming the defendant’s behavior** | Whether the sentence is intended to rehabilitate or correct the offender. |

(These factors are embedded in the federal sentencing guidelines, which emerged from the same statutory language.)

---

## 2.  How the appellate court uses the record

### 2.1  “Actual consideration” is required, but a *mechanical recitation* is not

The 8th Circuit says in *Justice* attitudes:

> “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.”  
> — *United States v. Maluoth*, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024)

In *Goodhouse*, the court affirmed that the “presumption that the district court exercised its discretion” persists once the record shows deliberation:  

> “A district court is presumed to have considered the § 3553(a) factors as long as it has reviewed the PSR, sentencing memorandum, and related documents.”  
> — *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023)

### 2.2  What constitutes “clear evidence” of consideration?

In the *Cutbank* opinion the appellate court looked for:

1. **Reference to the PSR** – “The court noted its review of the PSR and sentencing memoranda … and reviewed § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance.”  
2. **Explicit statement that the court “was aware” of the defendant’s argument** – this signals that the judge weighed the relevant factors.  
3. **Absence of any punitive pivot that suggests the judge ignored the factors** – the sentence stayed within the advisory range and the judge expressly rejected the defendant’s arguments.

If such statements were missing, the appellate court would typically assume the district court had failed to consider the factors, which could render the sentence “unreasonable” under § 3553(a).

---

## 3.  Consequences of an absent recitation

* **No explicit recitation ≠ irreligious omission.**  
  The appellate court can still affirm if the record shows the judge reviewed the PSR, the sentencing memorandum, or otherwise demonstrated consideration of the factors.

* **Without evidence of consideration the appellate court can reverse.**  
  The statutory mandate is *explicit*: a sentence is *unreasonable* if the court “fails to have particular regard” to *any* of the five factors.  If the record gives no indication that the judge did so, the sentence falls outside the statutory standard.  

* **Presence of deliberation precludes reversal for “lack of recitation.”**  
  In *Cutbank* the court found “the record [clearly] shows the district court considered the 3553(a) factors.”  Hence, even though the sentencing memorandum never listed each factor with a bullet‑point list, the appellate court declined to find an unreasonable sentence.

---

## 4.  Bottom line for the student

1. **Identify the 5 statutory factors** (nature of the offense, defendant’s history, deterrence, public protection, correction/reform).  
2. **Look for any indication that the district court reviewed the PSR or a sentencing memorandum** or otherwise discussed these factors.  
3. **If the record shows deliberation, an opponent of explicit recitation can still challenge the sentence** only if the judge’s treatment of the factors is demonstrably wrong or insufficient; otherwise the appellate court must defer to the trial judge's discretion.

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank* the appellate court affirmed the sentence because the record showed the district court had **actually considered** the § 3553(a) factors—no full mechanical recitation was required.  The absence of such a recitation, therefore, does not automatically make a sentence unreasonable; the court must first ascertain that the factors were indeed deliberated.

### How does the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) interact with sentencing memoranda in appellate review of guideline ranges?
Question:
The opinion notes that the PSR provided factual statements that the court adopted without objection. What is the role of the PSR in determining the advisory guidelines range, and how does appellate review treat errors that may arise from inaccuracies in the PSR versus errors in the district court’s own analysis?
Answer:
**In *Cutbank*, the PSR was the district court’s factual foundation for the sentencing‑memoranda calculations, and the appellate court reviewed the resulting range under the usual “correct‑calculation” test rather than an inquiry into the PSR itself.**  

---

### 1.  What the PSR does  

* **Facts for the memo.**  
  - The PSR “recommended a base offense level of 26… a three‑level increase… a two‑level increase… and a four‑level increase… for a total offense level of 35” (p. ??).  
  - It also assigned a Criminal History Category VI, yielding an advisory range of 292–365 months (p. ??).  

* **District‑court adoption.**  
  - The court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions” (p. ??).  
  - Because the PSR’s facts were on the record and accepted by the judge, the district court could rely on them in its sentencing‑memoranda and in the selection of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement.  

Thus the PSR’s role is to **provide the objective data that the sentencing memo uses to compute offense level and criminal‑history category**; the memo then lists the mandatory guideline range and the court’s consideration of § 3553(a) factors.

---

### 2.  Appellate review of the range  

The court followed the standard set in *Dunn* (723 F.3d 919) and *Rosales‑Mireles* (585 U.S. 129):

1. **Correct‑calculation test.**  
   * The appellate court must first establish that the district court *did not* make a “significant procedural error”—for example, “failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range” (p. ??).  
   * If the calculation is correct, the appellate court **presumes the sentence is reasonable** (Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793; Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809).  

2. **Where a PSR error could matter.**  
   * If a PSR fact is truly wrong (e.g., a prior conviction that did not exist), and that fact was *material* to the calculated offense level or category, the appellate court can treat the range as erroneous. The later step is a de‑novo review of the calculation—often resulting in a remand for a new calculation.  
   * In *Cutbank*, however, the appellate court found the court “did not err, much less clearly err, in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony” (p. ??). The PSR’s facts were therefore deemed accurate enough not to invalidate the range.  

3. **Distinguishing PSR errors from court‑analysis errors.**  
   * **PSR inaccuracies** are “questions of fact.”  The appellate court will examine whether the PSR incorrectly identified a prior conviction, a firearm alteration, or any other element that fed into the calculation.  If the misunderstanding would change the range, the court must correct it.  
   * **District‑court errors** are – in this context – errors in **applying** the guidelines or in **ignoring** contrary evidence.  The court in *Cutbank* was found to have “correctly calculated the applicable Guidelines range” (p. ??) and to have considered the § 3553(a) factors.  Any deviation from proper application would be a more serious procedural error that could trigger reversal.  

---

### 3.  Bottom line for appellate review

| Issue | PSR‑Based Fact | District‑Court Analysis |
|-------|---------------|------------------------|
| **Factual accuracy** | Review as a *question of fact*; if the PSR is wrong *and* that mistake changes the level or category, the appellate court may reverse or remand for a new calculation. | If the court misapplies law (e.g., wrong enhancement), a “significant procedural error” is found; appellate must correct or reverse. |
| **Guideline range** | The range is accepted if the PSR facts are accurate enough to produce it; the appellate court’s de‑novo test is applied to the calculation itself. | If the court’s calculation is wrong, the appellate court reverses or remands for a correct calculation. |
| **Substantive rationality** (post‑range) | Once the range is validated, the appellate court “presumes the sentence reasonable” and only looks at § 3553(a) factors if the record shows a potential departure. | Errors in the court’s independent analysis of § 3553(a) do not trigger reversal unless the sentence is substantively unreasonable. |

In *Cutbank*, the court’s **adoption of the PSR facts without objection** meant that the appellate panel treated those facts as part of the record.  Because the PSR’s facts were consistent with the district court’s application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the court correctly calculated the advisory range of 292–365 months, and the appellate court “presumed that the sentence was reasonable.”  No review of § 3553(a) factors was necessary because the range itself was unchallenged.

---

### TL;DR

The PSR supplies the data that feed the sentencing‑memoranda; the appellate court checks whether the court **correctly processed that data**.  An error in the PSR is a factual issue the appellate court can correct only if it changes the guideline range, whereas an error in the court’s own application of the guidelines is a procedural misstep that may invalidate the range or the sentence itself.
