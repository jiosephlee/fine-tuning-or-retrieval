Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# I. Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Incident description: November 19‑20, 2020 – firearms, methamphetamine use, and assault on girlfriend

On the night of November 19‑20, 2020, Justin Cutbank, who had an extensive criminal record and was under the influence of methamphetamine, awoke his girlfriend, D.F., and pointed a saw‑off Marlin .22‑caliber rifle at her face. After directing her into a closet and threatening her life, he held her there for what she testified was “hours,” struck her across the face with the gun, and later forced her to wake sleeping occupants of the residence, including her two teenage sons and housemate C.F. Cutbank’s violent conduct culminated when he fled with D.F.’s cell phone and the gun, prompting a police response that uncovered the weapon and a butcher‑style knife in the courtyard where Cutbank had barricaded himself for several hours.

## Charges: 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) (felon in possession) and 924(e) (ACCA‑enhanced penalty)

Cutbank was charged under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), which criminalizes possession of a firearm by a person convicted of a felony. The same indictment also charged him under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), which imposes enhanced penalties for a felon who possesses a firearm and has been convicted three or more times of violent or drug‑related felonies. The indictment contended that Cutbank’s 2020 conduct, wherein he used a saw‑off rifle while in custody of the police, satisfied the statutory definition of armed assault and qualified for the ACCA enhancement.

## Trial proceedings: admissible evidence, jury instructions, conviction

During trial, the government presented physical evidence (the Marlin .22 rifle, a knife, and D.F.’s cell phone) and testimonial evidence from D.F. and C.F. The defense challenged the admissibility of D.F.’s and C.F.’s statements about prior possession of the saw‑off rifle, arguing that such testimony constituted propensity evidence in violation of Rule 404(b). The district court agreed with the government that the testimony was intrinsic to the case: “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon” (United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014)). Accordingly, the court admitted the evidence without issuing a limiting instruction.

Other evidentiary rulings addressed the admissibility of statements Cutbank had made to police and the propriety of a jury instruction limiting the consideration of those statements until D.F. himself could testify; the court found that the testimonies were neither hearsay nor Confrontation Clause–violating (United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014)). D.F.’s statements regarding her familiarity with guns and her sister’s death by a gun were also admitted as responses to cross‑examination, which the court deemed non‑prejudicial under Rule 403 (Fed. R. Evid. 403). The jury ultimately found Cutbank guilty of the felon‑in‑possession offense and of having three or more prior violent felony convictions, thereby activating the ACCA enhancement.

## Sentencing: district court’s application of ACCA, PSR recommendations, USSG base offense level, range, and sentence of 292 months

Following conviction, the district court adopted the Presentence Investigation Report’s recommendation and a sentencing memorandum prepared by the prosecutor. The PSR assigned a base offense level of 26, recognizing the saw‑off nature of the firearm under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), as well as a two‑level increase for possession of a firearm with an altered or obliterated serial number and a four‑level increase for possession of the firearm in connection with the 2020 felony (a violation of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)). Because Cutbank had two prior violent felonies, the PSC added a three‑level increase, for a total offense level of 35. The Criminal History Category was identified as VI, placing Cutbank’s advisory guidelines range at 292 to 365 months. The district court imposed 292 months, the lowest figure in the range, explicitly noting that it had considered the § 3553(a) factors and found the sentence within the guidelines.

## Defendant’s appeal: claimed evidentiary violations, procedural errors, and incorrect application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

On appeal, Cutbank asserted that the district court committed multiple evidentiary and procedural errors that deprived him of a fair trial. These included a refusal to admit his motion to exclude D.F.’s and C.F.’s prior possession statements (Rule 404(b)), a refusal to grant his request for a Confrontation Clause‑related limiting instruction on police statements, and an unelaborated objection to the admission of D.F.’s testimony about her sister’s death by gun. Cutbank also argued that the court misapplied the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement that raises the offense level by four points for a firearm used in connection with another felony, contending that the enhancement was inappropriate because the related felony was not charged or proven.

## Appellate framework: standard of review for evidentiary rulings and sentencing determinations

The Eighth Circuit reviewed the trial court’s evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion, a standard that applies absent a claim of “clear error” or that the evidence is inherently prejudicial. The court emphasized that “a district court’s decision to accept or reject evidence that is intrinsically relevant to the offense is at least presumptively correct,” and that the appellate court must find such error “harmless” before reversing a conviction (United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009)). For sentencing, the Court applied the two‑step test set out in § 3553(a): first, the court ensures the district court accurately calculated the advisory guidelines range (United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023)); second, the Court reviews whether the district court reasonably considered the § 3553(a) factors and imposed a sentence within the range. Because the district court’s application of the ACCA enhancements and sentencing memorandum produced a sentence within the guidelines, the appellate court upheld the sentence as reasonably consistent with the § 3553(a) framework (United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (2025)).

# ii. Evidentiary and Procedural Issues: Rules, Challenges, and Court Rulings

The appellate panel’s opinion in United States v. *Justin Cutbank* systematically reviews the evidentiary and procedural contentions submitted by the defendant. Each issue—whether it concerns the admissibility of prior‑possession testimony, a limiting instruction on hearsay, the use of inconsistent statements for impeachment, a prejudice‑related out‑of‑scope remark, or the admissibility of police reports as extrinsic evidence—was resolved on the ground that the trial court’s rulings were within its broad discretion and did not sow probable prejudice. The court therefore affirmed on each ground. The following discussion explicates the relevant Rules of Evidence, constitutional standards, and the appellate court’s reasoning.

## A. Prior Possession Evidence under Rule 404(b): Intrinsic Evidence, *United States v. Battle* Precedent, and Admissibility Rationale

Rule 404(b) prohibits the admission of evidence of a person’s prior bad acts for the purpose of proving the character trait that the act demonstrates; however, prior acts that are **intrinsic** to the case may still be admitted. The defendant argued that the testimony of D.F. and C.F. that they had seen Cutbank possess the sawed‑off Marlin rifle prior to the apprehension was evidence of propensity and therefore inadmissible. This objection was denied on the basis that the statements were **intrinsic**. The appellate court reiterated the reasoning in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015). In *Battle*, the court held that evidence depicting prior possession of the same weapon is directly relevant to establishing how cut‑off junctures of ownership and control bring the prosecution’s possession charge into play. Because the plaintiff’s theory of the crime focuses on whether Cutbank controlled the firearm at the time in question, prior observations of him in possession of that firearm serve as **direct evidence** of the same controlling relationship, not as a character assessement of violent propensity. The district court, therefore, properly applied Rule 404(b) by distinguishing intrinsic from propensit, and the appellate court affirmed this decision.

## B. Limiting Instruction for Hearsay and the Confrontation Clause: Rule 806, Court’s Reliance on *United States v. Bennett*, and the “No Limiting Instruction at Trial” Standard

Cutbank sought a trial‑court instruction that would preclude the jury from using the victim’s earlier statements to police as substantive evidence of his conduct. The government argued that such statements merely establish the officers’ knowledge and context and that they complied with the confrontation clause because the declarants were present in court (Rule 806). The trial judge instead adopted the standard set by *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015): the court need not offer a limiting instruction for each item of evidence at the time it is introduced so long as an instruction is given at the close of trial. As the appellate court notes, “the court did not admit any hearsay during the trial, and D.F. herself testified.” The Confrontation Clause was satisfied because the declarants were in the courtroom and gave testimony in person, in keeping with *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). Since there was no abuse of discretion in wholly rejecting the necessity of the limitation, the appellate court upheld the ruling.

## C. Prior Inconsistent Statements: Admissibility for Impeachment, Cross‑Examination Allowance, and the Trial Court’s Refusal to Strike

Courts recognize that a witness’s prior inconsistent statements are admissible solely for the purpose of impeachment (*United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022)). Cutbank argued that the defendant’s prior inconsistent statements should have been excluded on grounds of unfair prejudice. The district court permitted the statements and allowed cross‑examination by the defense counsel. In the absence of a showing by the defendant that the statements introduced substantial confusion or prejudice, Rule 403 permits such evidence. The appellate court found that the first‑hand interrogation over the inconsistent statements was exempt from review under Rule 602; thus the district court’s ruling was correct, and the appellate court affirmed.

## D. Testimony Regarding the Victim’s Sister’s Death: Prejudice Analysis under Rule 403 and Why the Court Permitted the Statement

During cross‑examination concerning inconsistent prior statements, the victim, D.F., explained that “her sister was killed by a gun.” The defendant moved to strike the statement as prejudicial. The appellate court emphasized that Rule 403 requires a balancing of prejudice versus probative value. In similar circumstances, the court has repeatedly held that a statement that provides context to a witness’s background—in this case, a family history of gun violence—does not create sufficient prejudice to defeat its admission. The statement yielded relevant context regarding the witness’s history of personal restraint around firearms, thus a favorable balancing under Rule 403. Accordingly, the appellate court found no abuse of discretion in allowing the statement.

## E. Use of Police Reports as Extrinsic Evidence: Authentication Requirement under Rule 801(b)(3), *U.S. v. Cumbie* Guidance, and the Court’s Handling of the Defense’s Attempt to Read the Report

The defense attempted to introduce portions of a police report—authored by a law‑enforcement officer—into the record as extrinsic evidence to impeach witness C.F. The district court upheld the government’s objection that the report was not an admissible foundation since it was not directly authored by C.F. The appellate court cited *Cumbie* to underscore that extrinsic evidence must be authenticated. The judge’s denial of the defense’s request to read the presentation was in line with Rule 801(b)(3) because the report made no claims that required authentication as a witness’s verbal testimony. The appellate court therefore affirmed that the trial court did not err in refusing to admit the police report.

## F. Harmless‑Error Analysis: Application of *United States v. DeMarce*, *Keys*, and the Threshold of Substantial Prejudice

The defendant argued that any misadmissions at trial “harmlessly” diminished the jury’s deliberations. The appellate court applied the framework set by *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009), and *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019). Under that analysis, an evidentiary ruling is reversible only when it “swayed or may have substantially swayed the jury’s verdict.” The assessment of the trial record shows that reliable, corroborative evidence—D.F.’s own testimony, the recovered firearm with matching DNA, and multiple witnesses—doubled the strength of the prosecution’s case. A cumulative “harmless” effect argument fails at the threshold because the potentially prejudicial evidence was not marginal. The court thus held that the defendant could not clear the hurdle of substantial prejudice, and the convictions remain.

## G. Appellate Standards: Abuse of Discretion for Evidentiary Rulings and What Constitutes Reversible Error

Finally, the appellate court reminded that evidentiary rulings are reviewed by the appellate panel for “abuse of discretion” (or analogously, “reverse if resultant error” in the *case*). The panel applied the same de‑novo standard permissible under *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929‑30 (8th Cir. 2013), and *United States v. Rosales‑Mireles*, 585 U.S. 129, 143‑44 (2018). The standard requires the appellate court to presume the trial court’s reasoning is correct and to view any deviation as an abuse only when the evidence offered is manifestly patently wrong or the trial’s procedural fairness is compromised. In each of the defendant’s contentions, no such patently erroneous legal statement or clearly unconstitutional practice was on record. The panel noted that the appellate review was limited to the “presented questions,” and the absence of any “substantial error” precluded reversal. Therefore, all evidentiary and procedural rulings probed by the defendant were affirmed.

**Bottom line:** The appellate court found the district court’s evidentiary decisions were proper: prior possession evidence was intrinsic, no limiting instruction was required, prior inconsistent statements were admissible for impeachment, the witness’s family‑history remark was not overly prejudicial, police reports were unauthentic for impeachment, and any error was harmless. The case thus concludes decisively on evidentiary grounds.

# III. Sentencing Analysis: Armed Career Criminal Act, United States Sentencing Guidelines, and §3553(a) Factors  

The appellate court’s review focused on whether the district court applied the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG), and §3553(a) correctly and whether the imposed sentence was within the defendant’s advisory guidelines range. The court affirmed the conviction and sentence, finding no discretionary error.  

## Acquiring the ACCA Enhancement: §924(e) and the Requirement of Three or More Prior Violent Felonies  

Under §18 U.S.C. § 924(e), a defendant convicted of a felony, in addition to possessing a firearm, receives a mandatory sentencing enhancement if he has been convicted of three or more prior violent offenses for which he was imprisoned or sentenced to a term of incarceration of a year or more. The jury’s findings that Cutbank had been convicted of at least three violent felonies satisfied the statutory threshold, enabling the ACCA enhancement to apply. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e).  

## The U.S. Sentencing Guidelines Process: Offense Level Determination, Base Level Adjustments, Violent Felony Increases, Special Enhancements  

The PSR, which represents the district court’s primary factual source, set a base offense level of 26 because Cutbank used a sawed‑off .22‑caliber rifle described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a). Ordininarily, the USSG base level would then be adjusted upward for each applicable factor: two-level increase for the altered/obliterated serial number; two-level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing; and unfortunately a four‑level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony (the felony of being a felon in possession of a firearm). Simultaneously, the “violent felony” increase of one level is applied for each prior violent felony conviction, with a cumulative increase of three levels because of Cutbank’s three prior violent felonies. A three‑level increase would be added for each prior violent felony, resulting in a total of three additional levels. The PSR’s consolidated calculation produced an offense level of 35.  

## Detailed Breakdown of §2K2.1(b)(6)(B): Firearm or Ammunition in Connection with Another Felony – Statutory Text, Comment n.14(C), and Evidentiary Prerequisites  

Statutory text (USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)) enhances an offense by four levels if the defendant "used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained”." The court noted that the label “in connection with” requires that the firearm be used or possessed during the commission of another felony **or** in its planning or execution, with no requirement of a separate, specific charge. Comment n.14(C) clarifies that the application does not entail proof of separate criminal proceedings relating to the connecting felony. The PSR identified the possession of the sawed‑off rifle at D.F.’s residence and its subsequent use in the confrontation with the police as meeting the “connection” requirement, satisfying the evidentiary prerequisites established in United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919 (8th Cir. 2013) (affirming the adequate factual basis when the PSR sets out clear situational links).  

## PSR Recommendation Analysis: Base Offense Level 26, Violent Felony and Firearm Serial‑Number Increases, Recklessness Level, Four‑Level Possession Enhancement  

The PSR’s guidance stated:

1. **Base level 26** – sawed‑off firearm (26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)).  
2. **Serial‑number increase** – three levels for an altered or obliterated serial number.  
3. **Recklessness increase** – two levels for creating a reckless risk of serious bodily injury while fleeing.  
4. **Possession enhancement** – four levels under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for firearm use in connection with another felony.  
5. **Violent felony increases** – one level for each of the three prior violent felony convictions, totaling three levels.  

Adding these adjustments to the base level yields an overall offense level of 35.  

## Guideline Range Calculation: Level 35 vs Level 34, Categorization VI, 292–365 Month Range, and Comparison to Respondent’s Sentence  

The U.S. Sentencing Guidelines Table ETS 3—. Applying an offense level of 35 produced an advisory guideline range of 292 to 365 months, placing the defendant in Criminal History Category VI. The appellate court noted that had the possession enhancement been omitted, the offense level would have been 34, still within Category VI and yielding the same 292‑365 month range due to the step’s point entry. The district court’s sentence of 292 months—at the minimum of the range—was within the prescribed advisory range, satisfying the reasonableness standard of Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38 (2007).  

## Examination of §3553(a) Factors: Purpose “No Greater Than Necessary,” Review of the District Court’s Consideration of the Factors, and the Reasoning that the Sentence Falls Within the Recommended Range  

§3553(a) requires the judge to consider a broad set of factors and not impose a sentence “no greater than necessary” to serve the federal purposes. The district court explained that it had weighed the relevant factors, though not in a verbatim recital, and concluded that a sentence within the lower bound of the guidelines was appropriate. The appellate court cited Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786 (8th Cir. 2023), Masood, 133 F.4th 799 (8th Cir. 2025), and Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158 (8th Cir. 2024), noting that a mechanical instruction of each factor is unnecessary; the record must reveal that the court actually considered them. The court found that the record was adequate for this purpose and that, absent any indication of bias or prejudice, the sentence did not exceed the statutory “no greater than necessary” threshold.  

## Reasonableness and Abuse of Discretion: Appellate Review of Sentencing Discretion, Precedents (Goodhouse, Masood, Maluoth), and the Court’s Conclusion  

Appellate review of sentencing discretion under §3553(a) is deferential. The court affirmed that the district court had no significant procedural error, a finding underscored by the absence of evidence that the court misapplied §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) or abused its discretion in sentencing. The court concluded that the sentence, within the advisory range and based on a correct application of the guidelines and statutory enhancements, was reasonable. The appellate court held that the trial court’s findings and reasoning satisfied the standards set forth in United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786; United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799; and United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, and therefore affirmed the judgment.

# IV. Holding, Judicial Reasoning, and Practical Take‑aways

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Justin Cutbank* serves as a concise illustration of how appellate courts apply the harmless‑error doctrine and the statutory framework governing the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA).  The court affirmed both the judgment and the sentence, finding that the district court’s evidentiary rulings were correct and that the sentence was consistent with the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG).

## Affiliation of the conviction: no reversible evidentiary error identified, harmless‑error standard met

The court rejected every factual and procedural claim raised on appeal.  For the evidence of prior possession of the sawed‑off Marlin 22‑caliber rifle found in *A*, the court reiterated that such prior use is “intrinsic, relevant to proving possession of the same weapon, and therefore admissible under Rule 404(b)”; see *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015).  The appellate court emphasized that the trial court properly applied the *Battle* rule, which allows intrinsic evidence of prior possession to be admitted to prove a defendant’s ownership or control of a firearm during the conduct at issue.

In *B*, the court held that the district court was correct in refusing to give the jury a limiting instruction regarding police statements.  The statements were non‑hearsay once the declarant appeared in court, satisfying the Confrontation Clause (*United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010)).  The court noted that the district court followed *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), which holds that a limiting instruction is unnecessary if the appellate court determines the evidence was properly admitted.

For *C*, the court was satisfied that the district court’s decision to admit the testimony of defendant‑friend’s prior sightings of the weapon was safe because it was intrinsic to the possession charge, as in *Battle*.  In *D*, the refusal to strike statements about the victim’s sister being killed by a gun was allowed under the court’s discretion to allow unchallenged testimonial evidence; the appellate court referenced *Sprint/United Management Co. v. Mendelsohn*, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

The district court’s rulings regarding the admissibility and authenticity of extrinsic evidence in *E* were also found correct.  The court applied *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), which requires authentication of extrinsic evidence used for impeachment.  The appellate court reiterated that the absence of preservation of the officer’s police report was not a reversible error.

Because every evidentiary rule was applied correctly, any errors were judged harmless.  The court cited *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) and *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019), to show that the presence of substantial evidentiary material (both testimonial and physical) “prevailed over” any potential prejudice.  The conviction was thus affirmed.

## Affiliation of the sentence: correct application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), adherence to USSG, and reasonable sentence within the guideline range

The appellate court gave the sentencing decision a deference that is standard for district‑court determinations.  Under *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929‑30 (8th Cir. 2013), the court reviewed whether the district court made a “substantially erroneous” finding.  The court found no such error.

After obtaining a base offense level of 26 from the PSR, the judge added five statutory enhancements: a two‑level increase for the altered serial number, a two‑level increase for reckless risk posed while fleeing, a four‑level increase for “possession of a firearm in connection with another felony” per § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and a two‑level increase for the assault on a police officer.  The PSR also placed Cutbank in Criminal History Category VI, resulting in an advisory guideline range of 292 to 365 months.  The sentencing memorandum reflected a range entirely within those bounds, and the district court appropriately applied § 3553(a) factors (e.g., offender history, seriousness of the offense, risk to the community).  In *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023), the court affirmed a sentence within range unless there was abnormal deviation or lack of justification.  The court applied the same reasoning here, finding no indication that the judge unduly inflated the guideline range or that the § 3553(a) factors warranted a departure.

The appellate court emphasized that, under *United States v. Maluoth*, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024), a district court need not provide a mechanical recitation of every § 3553(a) factor—it is sufficient that the record shows consideration of these factors.  The statutory requirement of a four‑level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was recursively applied because the defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained” (see § 2K2.1, comment, n.14(C)).  The court affirmed that the judge used the PSR facts – a permissible method of factual determination in sentencing – and that the defendant’s prior violent convictions justified the increased offense level in light of ACCA thresholds.

Ultimately, the appellate court concluded that the deterrent focus of § 3553(a)(2) is satisfied by a sentence that is “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” to meet the purposes of federal sentencing.  The sentence returned to the court’s discretion, and the appellate court found it within the permissible range.

## Clarification on propensity evidence: Rule 404(b) admits prior possession when it is intrinsic to the charge

The court clarified that Rule 404(b)’s ban on “propensity” evidence does not prohibit evidence of prior possession when that evidence is intrinsic to the offense.  In *United States v. Battle*, the court reasoned that prior possession of a firearm is “directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon” because it establishes ownership or control.  This approach makes it clear that the “propensity” exception must be applied narrowly, and that only extrinsic evidence that reveals the defendant’s character is excluded.  Students should remember that evidence of prior possession is often admissible in firearm‑related cases for exactly this reason.

## Hearsay and Confrontation Clause guidance: limited instruction is unnecessary when the statements are admissible hearsay and the declarant appears

In *United States v. Charboneau*, the Supreme Court held that, under the Confrontation Clause, a declarant’s statements are admissible if the declarant appears to testify in person.  The appellate court applied this rule to the matter of police statements made by the defendant’s friends and to the defendant’s own statements under cross‑examination.  The district court followed the precedent in *United States v. Bennett*, ruling that a limiting instruction was unnecessary because the evidence was admissible and the declarant appeared.  The key takeaway is that, for hearing statements that are admissible draws from contextual evidence, the trial judge is not compelled to provide a litigative‑style limiting instruction.  The decision illustrates that the appellate level will rarely review the presence or absence of a limiting instruction unless the defendant successfully shows it was a reversible error.

## Firearm‑in‑connection‑with‑another‑felony enhancement: four‑level increase application and its impact on sentencing range

The court’s treatment of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) clarifies that the four‑level enhancement applies even when the defendant’s “felony crime” was not subject to a conviction at the time of sentencing.  The statutory language—“regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” – is unequivocal.  The court confirmed that the PSR’s factual statements on the defendant’s prior felony convictions, and the factual record of the crime (the possession of the weapon in a domestic‑abuse context while a felony was ongoing), compose a valid basis for the enhancement.  Students should note that the United States may apply the enhancement on an “in fact” basis for any factual connection between the firearm and an ongoing felony, and that the court may rely on PSR‑derived facts without the need for new evidence.

## Practical advice: ensuring evidence strength, correctly quoting witnesses, proper authentication of extrinsic evidence, careful application of sentencing guidelines

1. **Evidence Strength** – The trial court’s error check is more likely to be doomed to “harmless” if the overall evidence is strong and persuasive.  Therefore, ensure both the physical and testimonial evidence are robust when navigating evidence law.

2. **Correctly Quoting Witnesses** – The appellate court emphasized that the district court had the discretion to allow quotations without amendment, provided they were contextually accurate.  In *DeMarce*, robust controlling evidence mitigates the hardship if the district court errs in court language.

3. **Authentication of Extrinsic Evidence** – In *United States v. Cumbie*, the court held that extrinsic evidence used to impeach a witness must be authenticated, even if it appears only as a prior statement.  The defense must prepare an affidavit or physical evidence if planning to use prior statements to challenge a witness.

4. **Careful Application of Sentencing Guidelines** – When relying on PSR facts for sentencing enhancements, it is critical to tie the facts to the statutory standard and to articulate how the guideline level is increased.  The district court should also document any statutory or case‑law basis for deviations.

5. **Avoid Expanding Claims on Appeal** – As seen in *E*, appellate courts do not reward parties that add unfounded claims.  A narrow, precise presentation of reversible errors is more likely to survive.

## Long‑term impact: how this opinion reinforces trial‑court deference and sets a precedent for proper evidentiary and sentencing conduct in federal firearms cases

By affirming the district court’s evidentiary rulings and sentencing under the USSG, the Eighth Circuit reinforced the principle that trial‑court deference in sentencing matters remains paramount.  The decision also affirms that the ACCA’s four‑level firearm‑in‑connection‑with‑another‑felony enhancement can be applied even to firearms that are used in domestic‑violence and police‑protection contexts.  The underlying rule‑based approach to propensity evidence and Confrontation Clause considerations demonstrates that appellate courts will carefully scrutinize only genuine reversible errors.  For practitioners, the case highlights that ballistic and forensic evidence remains crucial to establishing possession, that procedural overruled objections may not be a bar to sentencing, and that district courts must articulate their considerations of § 3553(a) factors, even if a mechanical recitation is not required.

In sum, the appellate court’s holding exemplifies the standard of review: it draws a line between reversible evidentiary missteps and harmless errors, affirms a complex ACCA sentencing enhancement, and clarifies the boundaries of rule‑based evidence admissibility—all of which inform future practice in federal firearms prosecutions.
