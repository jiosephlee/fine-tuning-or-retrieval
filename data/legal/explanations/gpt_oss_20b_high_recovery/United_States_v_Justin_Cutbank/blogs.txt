Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Rule 404(b) and the Intrinsic Evidence of Prior Firearm Possession: The 8th Circuit’s Verdict in Cutbank

## Why It Matters

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the 8th Circuit addressed a question that reverberates through every federal courtroom where firearms are at issue: can a witness testify that a defendant had previously possessed a weapon, and, if so, under what auspices can that testimony survive a challenge under Rule 404(b)? The answer may seem obvious to seasoned practitioners—prior possession can be admissible when it is truly relevant to a specific element of a current charge—but the case reminds us that the line between “intrinsic” fact evidence and “propensity” evidence is a moving target. The court's affirmation of the district court’s rulings underscores the persistent tension that defenders confront when attempting to keep extraneous context from swaying juries about a defendant’s character, while prosecutors view prior possession as a vital piece of the puzzle.

The ruling also intersects with the Confrontation Clause and hearsay objections, illustrating how the same statements that a defendant’s witnesses may use for cross‑examination can be allowed as admissible evidence under the circumstances once the declarants testify in person. Together, these findings have practical ramifications for defense counsel strategizing evidence admission in firearms cases, and they highlight the potential for similar rule‑404(b) disputes to resurface in other circuits.

## Rule 404(b) and the Intrinsic/Propensity Distinction

Rule 404(b) permits prior conduct evidence only if it is “necessary to prove a fact in issue” or, when admissible, a “fact that is admissible on the merits.” That rule has been sharpened by the Supreme Court’s formulation in *United States v. Karp*, where the Court explained that intrinsic evidence—directly related to a particular issue (e.g., possession, control, or intent)—is permissible, whereas propensity evidence—evidence that simply attests to the defendant’s general character or tendency—faces a higher bar and is often struck unless it falls into an exception.

The distinction was crystallized in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014). In *Battle*, the court held that evidence of a defendant’s prior possession of a weapon was intrinsic to a firearms charge because it helps establish ownership or control of that same weapon, thereby satisfying the intrinsic/proneness test. The court therefore rejected the prosecution’s characterization of the evidence as “propensity” and permitted the testimony. That precedent—backed by the succinct rationale that “prior possession is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon” (see *Battle*, 774 F.3d at 511)—remains a cornerstone of firearms‑case evidence law.

## The 8th Circuit’s Application in Cutbank

In *Cutbank*, the jury's charge of felony possession hinged on whether the defendant actually possessed a firearm at an earlier time. The defense motion sought to strike out testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously observed the defendant with the sawed‑off Marlin .22, labeling it “propensity” evidence. The district court declined to do so, reasoning that the testimony was intrinsic: it established ownership and continuous control of the weapon—essential elements of the crime at issue.

The appellate court mirrored that line of reasoning. It underscored that the prior possession evidence was “directly relevant” to the current conviction as it “helped establish ownership or control” (*Battle*, 774 F.3d at 511). The court found no mischaracterization by the trial judge. By confirming the *Battle* standard, the 8th Circuit reinforced the idea that any evidence tied to the factual elements of a firearms offense—such as possession, control, or use—falls within the permissible scope of Rule 404(b), even if it references a prior, recent, or “handful” of instances. The court’s footnote was clear: a single, specific, prior possession is on the same theoretical footing as a continuous-term evidence basis.

One subtlety that the 8th Circuit clarified was that the testimony in question did not trigger the “misuse” exception for *exclusive hearsay* under Rule 8a, nor did it violate the Confrontation Clause (see *Charboneau*, 613 F.3d at 861).

## Confrontation Clause and Hearsay Rationale

The defense argued that the statements D.F. gave to police regarding the prior possession were hearsay and should be excluded. The appellate court held that the subpoena‑issued statements were not “outside‑the‑court” hearsay because D.F. was available for cross‑examination and ultimately testified in person. The court cited *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860‑61 (8th Cir. 2010), pointing out that “when declarants appear in court, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied, and no hearsay exception must be invoked.” Therefore, the statements could be introduced as part of the narrative of what the defendant had done.

In the same vein, the defense’s motion to instruct the jury that it could not consider D.F.’s statements to police as substantial evidence of Cutbank’s conduct until she herself testified was denied. The court relied on *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887‑99 (8th Cir. 2014), noting that a judge does not need a limiting instruction at the moment evidence is introduced, only that an instruction is provided at the close of trial or when necessary to avoid confusion. The court found the district judge’s choice to delay a Limiting Instruction was within discretion and did not prejudice the jury.

The uniqueness of the FBI witness application comes from the conviction of possession: the testimony was not extraneous but essential, and its admissibility was preserved by the court’s solid reading of the evidence rule and the Confrontation Clause. By upholding the appellate standard, the 8th Circuit reinforced a jurisprudential position that a witness testifying that the defendant had a prior possession of the same weapon is, by definition, a fact that bears on the defendant’s control of the firearm—an intrinsic fact.

## The Caveats and Practical Takeaways for Defense Counsel

The decision is a double‑edged sword for defense attorneys in federal firearms cases. On one hand, it confirms that the 8th Circuit will not strike testimony that shows a defendant’s prior possession of the very gun at issue, provided the testimony directly addresses possession or control. On the other hand, the rulings highlight that defense counsel must remain vigilant in preserving objections. While the court found the evidence not “propensity” to be harmful, a defense counsel could still strip a witness of the content if the evidence would only create an unfair prejudice (i.e., if the jury might lean on the “loyalty of a simple correlation” and lose the ability to separate the possession charge from a broader violent history). In practice, a defense attorney might wish to focus adversarially on the fact that prior possession, while admissible, is a historical fact that the defendant has an opportunity to refute during cross‑examination.

Given that the appellate court refused to limit the statements to the verbatim or purged contexts (like forensic statements or independent corroborative documents), the ruling suggests that the district court is permitted to allow a wide range of prior‑possession testimony—even if some of the groundwork includes somewhat florid narratives about the suspect’s “handful” of brush‑with‑firearms moments. For defense counsel, the upshot is that the safe harbor afforded by *Battle* is highly robust: the mere fact that a witness has seen the defendant with the weapon in earlier circumstances is almost automatically admissible as long as it is not framed as a “tendency to be violent” in an abstract sense.

The cutback cancellations in the Confrontation Clause rationale are also an eye‑opener. The court’s reasoning banks on the presence and willingness of the declarants to testify, which may be easier said than practiced. For defendants who waive the right to cross‑examine because witnesses are non‑testifying, a defense counsel might need to strategize how to preserve a Confrontation Clause motion or use a separate ruling.

## Doctrinal Ripple Effects

While the 8th Circuit’s decision is firmly rooted in the well‑trod *Battle* framework, the case is a potential endorsing signal for similar rulings in other circuits that have more recent, nuanced rules about intrinsically relevant prior‑possession evidence. Its emphasis on the *Confrontation Clause* and *hairsay* admissions may influence other judges who wrestle with borderline evidence. If the rule shapes practice nationwide, we might see a consolidation of the view that prior possession falls squarely under *intrinsic*, even when the prior instance is brief or disparate. 

Alternatively, the court’s language also opens a door for future litigation: does the rule apply when the prior assertion steps beyond “mere ownership” and into a more elaborate narrative about a suspect’s character? A defense attorney may not, for instance, argue that a defendant’s prior possession evidence is improper when the testimony insinuates he “often” kept his weapon in his possession or that “he tends to keep firearms around.” In those cases, the court might view the evidence as “propensity” even if the witnesses could claim a prior ownership fact. The 8th Circuit leaves it to the appellate courts to decide if and when such broader claims breach the line between intrinsic and propensity.

The sentencing portion of the opinion—though peripheral to the evidence debate—further signals a respect for the guideline‑based framework and the admissibility of excertion evidence under the 3553(a) factors. The court reaffirmed that a district court’s correct calculation of the guideline range is a critical step before the board explores the “reasonable” range.

## Conclusion

*Cutbank* stands as a test case for the rule that a witness may testify that a defendant has, at some earlier time, possessed a firearm that remains the same weapon under a current firearms charge. The 8th Circuit’s affirmation of the *Battle* standard, coupled with its robust read of the Confrontation Clause and the hearsay exception, signals that the rule is stable at least in this circuit for the foreseeable future. Its implications are practical: defense counsel must be tenacious in preserving objections, be ready to strike at “propensity” arguments that might masquerade as proving possession, and ensure that the evidence they seek to introduce remains anchored to the specific issues the jury must decide.

Beyond its immediate impact, this decision reinforces a broader judicial trend: the authoritative rule that prior‑possession evidence can be admissible as long as it is intrinsically tied to the core elements of the crime. That trend will likely encourage prosecutors to lean more heavily on such evidence, pushing defense attorneys to tighten their narrative defensives and sharpen their cross‑examination. In the end, *Cutbank* confirms that the judicial system is still willing to allow a triangulation of facts—present, past, and immediate—to sit in the jury’s deliberative space, provided those facts do not trespass into a territory of unearned character testimony.



# No Sentence Too High? The Cutbank Court Tackles ACMA’s Four‑Level Enhancement Under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

## Why This Case Matters

The question at the heart of United States v. Justin Cutbank is not whether a State or Federal court should punish a repeat violent offender, but whether the Office of Government Ethics’ sentencing tables should be construed to automatically add four months to a penalty for “possession of a firearm in connection with another felony.”  The Fourth Level of the Armed Career Criminal Act’s (ACMA) § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), while firmly embedded in the statutory language, has at times been warped by districts over the mechanics and the evidentiary flag required to trigger it.  In a clean legal decision, the Eighth Circuit affirms a district court’s precise use of that enhancement, the proper calculation of the advisory guidelines range, and the district court’s compliance with the “necessary but not greater than necessary” principle of § 3553(a).  For both prosecutors and defense counsel, the ruling syndicates the mechanics that should continue to guide ACMA sentencing.

## The Legal Issue in Plain Language

At its core, Cutbank’s appeal was straightforward: the district court allegedly added an off‑label, fourteen‑month boost to a sentence that was already, on the surface, within the statutory window.  Under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) the increase is entitled to a four‑level boost **if a firearm or ammunition is used or possessed in connection with another “federal, state, or local felony offense,” regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction obtained**.  The district court followed the PSR’s factual narrative that Justin Cutbank used a saw‑off .22 to threaten a female victim and that the same firearm was seized as he fled the scene of a formerly charged kidnapping.  In our reading of the district’s finding, the “connection” is unambiguously met: the firearm played a central role in the conduct that led to the earlier kidnapping conviction.

The appellate court, faced with a technical objection about a *four‑level* bump, evaluated it under the “straight‑forward” standard that the ACMA guidance is both statutory and statutory‑derived.  The court spent only a few lines on the substance, noting that the PSR’s factual statements were “primary sources” and no authentic “substantial error” was evident.  The court’s conclusion—that the district court’s application is proper, and that the sentence, once corrected for the Boost, remains within the advisory guidelines band—seems inevitable under the law as it stands.

## The Court’s Reasoning: Summary and Assessment

A quick glance at the opinion shows the appellate court proceeding along two tracks.  First, it rejects the evidentiary challenges made by the defense.  By invoking precedent such as United States v. Battle and United States v. Bennett, the court confirms that the weapon‑related testimony was intrinsic evidence and that no rule did oversight of the Confrontation Clause or Rule 403.  This part is essentially a “slide‑over” statement—not meant to upset the core of the matter.

The more substantive analysis concerns the sentencing enhancement.  The court’s opinion, braced by references to Dunn and the established statutory framework, reads, “the district court adopted, *without objection*, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions; thus, the court did not err, much less clearly err, in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.”  The court then weighs the “four‑level” increase in the context of a 35‑level offense.  The PSR’s calculation—26 base offense level, +4 for the “correlated felony”, +3 for the altered serial, +2 for reckless risk, and two further level placements for prior violent convictions—is established as fact.  The objectivity of the numeric totals is the only engine the court leaves to the mind of the judge, and the court confirms that the district judge’s final sentence of 292 months sits squarely within the 292–365‑month advisory range that the ACMA tables prescribe for an offense level of 35 and a criminal‑history category of VI.  

The appellate court draws a clear line—and then does not cross it: it reaffirms that a within‑range sentence is presumptively “reasonable” unless “significant procedural error” or “substantial prejudice” is evident, citing DeMarce and Keys in strong support.  The court’s final line, “We affirm,” is a single sentence that validates the district court’s entire discretion.

## Analytical Perspective: Is the Court Over or Under Sensitive?

A reading of the district court’s sentencing memorandum, available via the opinion’s appendices, shows that the judge considered a raft of § 3553(a) factors—the defendant’s criminal history, the seriousness of the conduct, the presence of mitigating circumstances, and the public safety implications of the sentence.  The appellate court agreed that “it is not required to give the judge a mechanical recitation of the factors weighed.”  That is consistent with the broader trend in federal sentencing that a mere “standing in the range” is all that must survive appellate review unless the sentence is “clearly excessive” or “significantly lower” than the range would allow.

One might argue that the district court could have applied a more granular ≥30‑month proration given Cutbank’s extensive prior convictions.  The Eighth Circuit’s satire on such “tweaking” is absent.  But remember that the ACMA's four‑level bump is mandated; it is not an open ship for the judge’s discretion.  By affording the maximum benefit of the four‑level enhancement and then situating the sentence at the *minimum* end of the range, the district court may have been practicing “sentence sparing” in a world of ballistic evidence and severe prior offenses.  Nevertheless, the appellate court chooses not to call out a potentially even lighter scheme or to compel a re‑calculation that could, hypothetically, lower the sentence to, say, 280 months.  The court respects the guidelines as the sentience of the sentencing “standard” and refuses to step into the judge’s decision of whether the schedule truly meets the purposes set forth in § 3553(a) (which the judge does in private, not written record).

The post‑judicial balance of apparently “harmless” errors and the presumption that guidelines capture the “reasonable range” is an echo of the modern case‑law precedent that places trial court sentencing decisions beyond the reach of appellate scrutiny unless they’re absurdly high or demonstrably error‑prone.  The Cutbank opinion follows that well‑trod policy.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The court’s plain application of the four‑level CPRA enhancement, coupled with the procedural posture that “within‑range” sentences are upheld, does two important things: first, it re‑affirms the unchanged statutory scope of the ACMA enhancement – as long as a firearm is *any* way used in the commission or conspiracy of another felony, the court may apply the boost.  Second, the repetition of the “within‑range” standard lowers the bar for appellate challenges in ACMA cases.  The dissenting parties may now have to prove a “substantial prejudice” that the sentence could have been lower—an even heavier burden than in the old 1986 *United States v. Cook* standard for “chilling” the sentence.

The decision may embolden prosecutors who still rely on the four‑level enhancement when we can convincingly tie a Roman numeral of the PSR to a weapon’s use in a domestic‑violence or kidnapping scenario.  Over the next few years, as the guidelines are updated—but the structure of § 2K2.1 remains firm—courts may lean even more heavily on the PSR to prescribe the levels rather than on the Table’s algorithm.  The Paday session on the “proper evidentiary foundation” for a *four‑level* enhancement will probably see no resurgence, because any PSR fact that can be tied to a felony will suffice.

There might be one doctrinal ripple that deserves mention: the court’s decision supports the view that the ACMA guidance is not merely advisory but *effectively* mandated.  The guidelines dictate the numeric offenses; the ACMA statutes augment them.  Meanwhile, the analyses of 3553(a) and the within‑range requirement also help keep the sentencing guidelines from becoming a live weapon of over‑punishment.  The Eighth Circuit’s stance helps to preserve a simple “if you get into that range, you’re in play.”

## The Bottom Line for Practitioners

For defense counsel, Cutbank signals that any attempt to strike the four‑level boost is bound to fail unless the district court failed to *realize* that the gun was used in the crime that led to any other felony—an unlikely event given the strict evidence rule.  The focus, then, should be on hygiene: ensuring the PSR facts are indeed there, the judge’s eventual real‑time weight of 3553(a) factors is mapped out in the final sentencing memorandum, and that no post‑sentencing appeal states a “substantial prejudice.”  For prosecutors, it signals that the PSR will continue to score highly not only in the crime serious‑ness domain but also as a *mechanically* predetermined enhancement that will help them tilt a sentence toward the higher end of the advisory range.  The message from Cutbank remains: in the ACMA world, the four‑level boost is a legal safety net that will launch correctly whenever the facts tell the truth.

In sum, United States v. Justin Cutbank does two things.  It affirms the current state of the ACMA enhancement, re‑establishing that the “possession of a firearm in connection with another felony” answer is as simple and as robust as the statutes intended.  It also lends further weight to the within‑range standard that will continue to numb appellate review of ACMA sentences, turning a 292‑month jail year into a mere fact rather than a legal controversy.  For the next generation of ACMA litigants, the perception is clear: if the facts are solid and the guideline range is respected, the Law and the Court will tend to leave the sentence where the judge sits.



# Harmless Error and Appellate Deference in Sentencing: The Cutbank Ruling as a Case Study

## Why This Case Matters

When the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit affirmed a *felon‑in‑possession* conviction and a 292‑month sentence in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, it did so against a backdrop of alleged evidentiary missteps and a questionable application of a 4‑level sentencing enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The court’s decision is a clarion point for litigators, judges, and scholars interested in the boundaries of appellate deference on sentencing matters, the severed “within‑range” review stream, and the hard‑line harmless‑error doctrine. Its procedural roadmap—reviewing the appropriate sentencing guidelines first, then looking at § 3553(a) factors—serves as the textbook model for the 8th Circuit, and fortifies the jurisprudence that a properly calculated guidelines range subsumes most sentencing challenges.

## The “Within‑Range” Doctrine Outlined

The “within‑range” doctrine is the procedural spine of appellate sentencing review in federal court. Rooted in *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38 (2007), it carries the understanding that if a district court imposes a sentence that falls inside the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines range, appellate courts will presume that the sentence is reasonable. The 8th Circuit, affirming *Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786 (2023), and *Masood*, 133 F.4th 799 (2025), repeats that a court’s rationality prevails unless it finds a substantive error that undermines the guidelines calculation.

In *Goodhouse*, the court emphasized that a sentence “within the range is presumptively reasonable” and “the appellate court should not second‑guess the discretionary decision” absent a significant procedural misstep. *Masood* reversed a district court that imposed a 290‑month term that lay outside the recommended Guidelines range but failed to recalculate the proper level. The 8th Circuit’s adoption of these precedents frames the Cutbank analysis: the appellate court will first confirm that the guidelines calculation was correct; only then will it consider the 3553(a) factors.

## Harmless‑Error in the Context of Sentencing Appeals

The harmless‑error doctrine—long a staple of criminal appellate review—remains the final arbiter of whether an evidentiary misstep should result in reversal. Its application in sentencing appeals is, however, restrained: courts refuse to overturn a sentence simply because a district judge erred in admitting evidence that was irrelevant or irrelevant to the capital question of the sentence, unless that error had a “substantial and demonstrable effect” on the outcome.

Cutbank’s appellate brief enumerated several procedural mistakes: failure to strike prior gun ownership testimony, failure to exclude or limit hearsay, and failure to remove extrinsic statements about a victim’s sister being killed by a gun. Under *DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989 ("the court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless"); and *Keys*, 918 F.3d 982 ("the court may not reverse a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice"). Applying these tests, the 8th Circuit ruled that the district court’s earlier evidentiary rulings were “harmless” because the overall evidence (particularly the physical DNA match and the witnessed possession of the sawed‑off rifle) was overwhelming and the sustained testimony bore the **weight** essential to the conviction.

Thus, even though the appellate panel let the jury nonetheless website reads, the court’s “harmless” determination underscores the deference that higher courts afford to district courts in murdering memorable evidence that is not central to the trial’s decisive evidence.

## Bifurcated Review: Calculate Guidelines First

A critical point in Cutbank is the court’s insistence that it must first verify that the district court’s guideline calculation was correct. This approach echoes *Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919 (8th Cir. 2013), and is expressly recited in the cited *Goodhouse* and *Masood* opinions. The court’s reasoning:

> “Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range.” (Masood)

In Cutbank, the district court's PSR set the offense level at 35 (base 26 plus four additional levels) with a Criminal History Category of VI, resulting in a 292‑365 month range. The appellate panel found no fault in that calculation, establishing a "preliminary presumption of reasonableness." Because the sentencing court had a solid foundation, the appellate court merely examined whether the sentence aligned with the Guidelines and the 3553 factors, ultimately concluding it did.

The bifurcated design serves as both a safeguard for the defendant and an efficient conduit to keep the appellate docket from becoming clogged with hyper‑technical arguments. It also has an unintended consequence: when the district court’s calculation is solid, global injunctions on evidence become moot. The appellate court does not look at 3553(a) factors if a procedural error—like an improper plea bargain—affects the guideline level, unless that error alters the baseline. This sharp “no‑tangle” rule forces defense attorneys to focus on the first leg of the task: the integrity of the guideline calculation.

## Evidentiary and Procedural Mishaps: A Post‑Decision Overview

Cutbank’s claims of wrongs at trial were, in sum, a string of ordinary evidentiary disputes. The district court's rulings—in refusing to exclude D.F.’s testimony about prior possession or to strike C.F.’s extraneous testimony about his sister—were grounded in the “intrinsic evidence” exception to 404(b) and the “no confrontation clause violation” logic of *Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860. Importantly, the brief on appeal made no attempt to demonstrate that these mistakes caused the jury to wrongfully convict or that they were “prejudicial” under *Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907. As Cutbank’s opponents did not posit a substantive reason to think that the evidence altered the decision and the appellate court applied its harmless‑error test successfully, the decision stands.

This outcome is exactly what the 8th Circuit expects when the defendant files an “error” that would be considered “harmless.” By sticking to the harmless‑error paradigm, the court both respects the trial court's authority and signals that appellate reversal requires more than a mere denial of procedural niceties or a handful of questionable equitable claims.

## Implications for Future Sentencing Appeals

The Cutbank decision, while grounded in the 8th Circuit’s customary framework, has broader implications:

1. **Sentencing Dispositions Remain “Tough” When Within Range**  
   A sentence that is within the guidelines cannot be altered by post‑sentencing “harmful” arguments as long as the district court’s guidelines calculation is correct. This transitional rule fends off appellate reliance on post‑sentencing manifestations of bad luck; once you’re in the partners’ circle, you are in.

2. **Evidentiary Misgivings Require Proving a Material Effect**  
   A defendant would need to tie a search‑and‑seizure or testimonial error directly to the acceptance of the *felon‑in‑possession* charge. Cutbank’s failure to meet that threshold is a textbook example of how a defense’s evidentiary complaint can fail to gain traction.

3. **The 4‑Level Enhancement Remains a Valid Scoring Tool**  
   The 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4‑level enhancement for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense was applied without dissent, underscoring that the district court can factually observe a change in the crime circumstances and add the corresponding level. Future cases will cite Cutbank to show that a “mere” fact is enough to trigger a 4‑level bump.

4. **Reaffirmation of Deference to the Sentencing Judge**  
   The 8th Circuit reaffirmed the principle that sentencing judges are meritoriously entrusted with “all factors set out in 3553(a)” if they have done a correct guideline calculation. The reading of *Goodhouse* and *Masood* underscores that appellate courts will not downplay a judge’s discretionary judgment unless there is a clear procedural hook.

5. **Minor Missteps Might Not Secure a Reversal**  
   Even when procedural miscalculations are flagged in a brief, the absence of a “substantial prejudice” finding is enough to preserve the status quo. The Cutbank panel’s rational tone (“no abuse of discretion; within-range sentence”) is a cautionary note for appellate teams pursuing overturn.

## Conclusion

Cutbank epitomizes the conservative balance that appellate courts draw in sentencing appeals: a partisan but clear separation between the procedural shield and the substantive sentencing factors. The 8th Circuit’s pastoral reading of the *within‑range* doctrine and its reliance on the harmless‑error threshold point to an appellate strategy that prizes procedural correctness over substantive reinterpretation of the 3553 factors.

For defense counsel navigating federal sentencing appeals, the Cutbank ruling offers a clear map: first, the district court’s guidelines calculation must be sealed; second, any alleged procedural error must be demonstrably prejudice; third, when a sentence sits comfortably inside the Guidelines, the appellate court will not disrupt unless an error is both obvious and material.

The case also reinforces the functional realities of the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines system: a properly applied guideline is a numinous shield against reversal. As the 8th Circuit’s verdict illustrates, the path to appellate success is narrower than one might imagine, and the real fencing lay in first ensuring the checklist—guidelines calculation, evidence admissibility, and denial of pleading disruptions—was ticked off.
