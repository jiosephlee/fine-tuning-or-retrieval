Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 2: # ii. Evidentiary and Procedural Issues: Rules, Challenges, and Court Rulings

The appellate panel’s opinion in United States v. *Justin Cutbank* systematically reviews the evidentiary and procedural contentions submitted by the defendant. Each issue—whether it concerns the admissibility of prior‑possession testimony, a limiting instruction on hearsay, the use of inconsistent statements for impeachment, a prejudice‑related out‑of‑scope remark, or the admissibility of police reports as extrinsic evidence—was resolved on the ground that the trial court’s rulings were within its broad discretion and did not sow probable prejudice. The court therefore affirmed on each ground. The following discussion explicates the relevant Rules of Evidence, constitutional standards, and the appellate court’s reasoning.

## A. Prior Possession Evidence under Rule 404(b): Intrinsic Evidence, *United States v. Battle* Precedent, and Admissibility Rationale

Rule 404(b) prohibits the admission of evidence of a person’s prior bad acts for the purpose of proving the character trait that the act demonstrates; however, prior acts that are **intrinsic** to the case may still be admitted. The defendant argued that the testimony of D.F. and C.F. that they had seen Cutbank possess the sawed‑off Marlin rifle prior to the apprehension was evidence of propensity and therefore inadmissible. This objection was denied on the basis that the statements were **intrinsic**. The appellate court reiterated the reasoning in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015). In *Battle*, the court held that evidence depicting prior possession of the same weapon is directly relevant to establishing how cut‑off junctures of ownership and control bring the prosecution’s possession charge into play. Because the plaintiff’s theory of the crime focuses on whether Cutbank controlled the firearm at the time in question, prior observations of him in possession of that firearm serve as **direct evidence** of the same controlling relationship, not as a character assessement of violent propensity. The district court, therefore, properly applied Rule 404(b) by distinguishing intrinsic from propensit, and the appellate court affirmed this decision.

## B. Limiting Instruction for Hearsay and the Confrontation Clause: Rule 806, Court’s Reliance on *United States v. Bennett*, and the “No Limiting Instruction at Trial” Standard

Cutbank sought a trial‑court instruction that would preclude the jury from using the victim’s earlier statements to police as substantive evidence of his conduct. The government argued that such statements merely establish the officers’ knowledge and context and that they complied with the confrontation clause because the declarants were present in court (Rule 806). The trial judge instead adopted the standard set by *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015): the court need not offer a limiting instruction for each item of evidence at the time it is introduced so long as an instruction is given at the close of trial. As the appellate court notes, “the court did not admit any hearsay during the trial, and D.F. herself testified.” The Confrontation Clause was satisfied because the declarants were in the courtroom and gave testimony in person, in keeping with *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). Since there was no abuse of discretion in wholly rejecting the necessity of the limitation, the appellate court upheld the ruling.

## C. Prior Inconsistent Statements: Admissibility for Impeachment, Cross‑Examination Allowance, and the Trial Court’s Refusal to Strike

Courts recognize that a witness’s prior inconsistent statements are admissible solely for the purpose of impeachment (*United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022)). Cutbank argued that the defendant’s prior inconsistent statements should have been excluded on grounds of unfair prejudice. The district court permitted the statements and allowed cross‑examination by the defense counsel. In the absence of a showing by the defendant that the statements introduced substantial confusion or prejudice, Rule 403 permits such evidence. The appellate court found that the first‑hand interrogation over the inconsistent statements was exempt from review under Rule 602; thus the district court’s ruling was correct, and the appellate court affirmed.

## D. Testimony Regarding the Victim’s Sister’s Death: Prejudice Analysis under Rule 403 and Why the Court Permitted the Statement

During cross‑examination concerning inconsistent prior statements, the victim, D.F., explained that “her sister was killed by a gun.” The defendant moved to strike the statement as prejudicial. The appellate court emphasized that Rule 403 requires a balancing of prejudice versus probative value. In similar circumstances, the court has repeatedly held that a statement that provides context to a witness’s background—in this case, a family history of gun violence—does not create sufficient prejudice to defeat its admission. The statement yielded relevant context regarding the witness’s history of personal restraint around firearms, thus a favorable balancing under Rule 403. Accordingly, the appellate court found no abuse of discretion in allowing the statement.

## E. Use of Police Reports as Extrinsic Evidence: Authentication Requirement under Rule 801(b)(3), *U.S. v. Cumbie* Guidance, and the Court’s Handling of the Defense’s Attempt to Read the Report

The defense attempted to introduce portions of a police report—authored by a law‑enforcement officer—into the record as extrinsic evidence to impeach witness C.F. The district court upheld the government’s objection that the report was not an admissible foundation since it was not directly authored by C.F. The appellate court cited *Cumbie* to underscore that extrinsic evidence must be authenticated. The judge’s denial of the defense’s request to read the presentation was in line with Rule 801(b)(3) because the report made no claims that required authentication as a witness’s verbal testimony. The appellate court therefore affirmed that the trial court did not err in refusing to admit the police report.

## F. Harmless‑Error Analysis: Application of *United States v. DeMarce*, *Keys*, and the Threshold of Substantial Prejudice

The defendant argued that any misadmissions at trial “harmlessly” diminished the jury’s deliberations. The appellate court applied the framework set by *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009), and *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019). Under that analysis, an evidentiary ruling is reversible only when it “swayed or may have substantially swayed the jury’s verdict.” The assessment of the trial record shows that reliable, corroborative evidence—D.F.’s own testimony, the recovered firearm with matching DNA, and multiple witnesses—doubled the strength of the prosecution’s case. A cumulative “harmless” effect argument fails at the threshold because the potentially prejudicial evidence was not marginal. The court thus held that the defendant could not clear the hurdle of substantial prejudice, and the convictions remain.

## G. Appellate Standards: Abuse of Discretion for Evidentiary Rulings and What Constitutes Reversible Error

Finally, the appellate court reminded that evidentiary rulings are reviewed by the appellate panel for “abuse of discretion” (or analogously, “reverse if resultant error” in the *case*). The panel applied the same de‑novo standard permissible under *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929‑30 (8th Cir. 2013), and *United States v. Rosales‑Mireles*, 585 U.S. 129, 143‑44 (2018). The standard requires the appellate court to presume the trial court’s reasoning is correct and to view any deviation as an abuse only when the evidence offered is manifestly patently wrong or the trial’s procedural fairness is compromised. In each of the defendant’s contentions, no such patently erroneous legal statement or clearly unconstitutional practice was on record. The panel noted that the appellate review was limited to the “presented questions,” and the absence of any “substantial error” precluded reversal. Therefore, all evidentiary and procedural rulings probed by the defendant were affirmed.

**Bottom line:** The appellate court found the district court’s evidentiary decisions were proper: prior possession evidence was intrinsic, no limiting instruction was required, prior inconsistent statements were admissible for impeachment, the witness’s family‑history remark was not overly prejudicial, police reports were unauthentic for impeachment, and any error was harmless. The case thus concludes decisively on evidentiary grounds.
