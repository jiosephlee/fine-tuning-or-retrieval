Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When Expert Testimony Backfires: The Court’s Revitalized ‘Substantial Evidence’ Standard  

## Why the Case Matters  

In a blistering dispute that began in Texas’s notorious patent‑forum courts, the First‑Tier Federal Circuit overturned a $166‑million damages judgment in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 9/24/25. The appellate panel didn’t merely nod that the district court had made a procedural error; it struck down the jury’s finding of infringement because the evidence in support of that finding was disjointed at its core: the expert testimony undercut the very claim element that the jury was asked to apply.  

For litigators, judges, and experts alike, the decision is a cautionary tale that the “substantial evidence” standard—long a staple of patent appellate review—continues to function as a hard chokehold around the ability of a jury to decide infringement. It reminds us that the judge’s “light most favorable to the verdict” is not a pseudo‑umbrella under which dubious expert‐footing can be smoothed away. In a world where decades‑old “tort of interpretation” approaches to experts are increasingly being rejected, the case is an early warning that the courts will not let a skittish, contradictory expert record slide unchecked into a conviction, even when the evidence on record is otherwise overwhelming.

## Mapping the Case Outline  

Both parties hurled a volley of damages claims. “Finesse” sued AT&T for infringing two patents tied to mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference: U.S. Pat. Nos. 7,346,134 (the “’134” patent) and 9,548,775 (the “’775” patent). A jury found all claimed infringement and awarded more than $166 million. The district court denied AT&T’s motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement and denied a new‑trial motion for damages, leaving the award standing.  

On appeal, the district court’s ruling was turned on its head for two principal reasons:

1. An expert testimony marshaled by Finesse bundled contradictory statements about whether the accused radios actually sampled “interference–generating signals” in a way that the claim required.
2. The expert provided no convincing account that the accused radios performed the set of “seven multiplies” the ‘775’ patent demanded.  

Because the jury’s verdict was not "supported by substantial evidence," the appellate court both ordered a JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award.

## The Substantial‑Evidence Lens

The Federal Circuit invoked the same standard that the Fifth Circuit would employ when reviewing an appellate denial of JMOL: a factual finding must be “supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” 25 F.4th at 969. “Substantial evidence” is not “clear and convincing evidence”; it is a lower bar in the arbitrational world but a higher bar on the appeals bench.

In practice, the Fifth Circuit’s de‑novo review of a district court’s denial of JMOL operates on two layers of jurisprudence. First, the judge must point to evidence that “strongly and overwhelmingly” reframes the parties’ burden to the point where reasonable jurors could not have found a contrary conclusion. 811 F.3d at 461. Second, the judge must confirm that a jury’s infringement verdict is “supported by substantial evidence.” 25 F.4th at 969. The Federal Circuit dutifully blended those layers. Its judgment states that the appellate panel first examined the district court’s report and notes that it “reviewed a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit… reversing ‘only if, when viewing evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion” [McKenna]. Then it applied the “substantial evidence” lens: “a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial,” as originally articulated in *Apple v. Wi‑LAN*.

The result: if an expert message is fractured—or worse, entirely contradictory—the jury’s burden loses its footing, and the appellate bench will lean away from reverse fidelity.

## Expert Testimony, Broken Mapping, and the Consequence

### On the ’134 Patent: “Sampling the Wrong Frequencies”

At the heart of the ‘134 patent’s claim construction is a *sampling* limitation that demands the accused radio first “over-samples, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference‑generating signals” *§1[a]*. The alleged “interference‑generating signals” must exist at the *time of sampling*. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, attempted to map the *red* signal lines in a Nokia design document into “signal of interest” and “interference‑generating signals”. He claimed the downlink‑TX reference was the signal of interest and the “modeled PIM path” was the interference–generating signal. However, the NC‑ADC—an analogue‑to‑digital converter—samples only before the PIM path is generated. When the court scrutinized Wells’s testimony, it found that he had not *actually* mapped the *x1* and *x2* signals on the red path to the essential claim elements. The expert himself oscillated between assertions, even after the cross‑examiner pointed out that the modeled PIM path is generated downstream of sampling.  The jury’s conviction that the radio met the claim was essentially based on a witness who could not tie the two required signals together.

As the court noted, “**There is no clear testimony indicating that the RF ADC samples a passband that includes the interference‑generating signals”.*[www]” Thus the district court’s decision to allow the jury to find infringement rested on a shaky foundation that the appellate court deemed unsupportable.

### On the ’775 Patent: “Performing Seven Multiplications”

The ‘775’ patent’s hallmark is a two‑step process that generates *inter‑modulation-product cancellation signals* by digitizing *three* inputs and computing *seven* third‑order multiplications. The expert’s evidence—largely a Nokia specification—showed only *three* distinct multiplication expressions: x1°|x1|², x1²x2′, and x1|x2|². The court found there was no evidence that the device performed the full set of *seven* multiplications the claim demanded. Even the apparent claim language that the *three* signals are “separately identifiable” was not met in the same way the expert argued. The court’s decision to grant JMOL hinged on the absence of evidence linking the accused device’s actual implementation to the *specific* arithmetic the ’775 patent requires.

### The Symptom: A “Confusing, Contiguous” Expert Record

The expert gave a long‑form testimony dripping with technical notation, but his statements wavered between definitions. The court cited that the expert “did not testify, even after the cross‑examiner’s challenge, that he mapped x1 and x2 onto the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals” and that his claims were “self‑contradictory.” The *Johns Hopkins v. Datascope* precedent—where the court reversed a district court after finding that an expert’s contradictory testimonies precluded a jury from finding that the accused device “lit” a vessel—was a clear analog. The Federal Circuit’s reversal shows the continuity of that principle.

## A Firm Stance on “Substantial Evidence”?

The court’s application of the standard was identical in name but arguably distinct in shade to prior Fifth Circuit rulings. While Sixth Circuit and other circuits have, on occasion, adopted a higher scrutiny for inventive‑step or obviousness, the Fifth Circuit has traditionally leaned toward a *more* flexible view of “substantial evidence.” Yet in *Finesse*, the appellate panel exercised a *tightening* approach: the evidence did not meet even a reasonable‑jury standard. That is perhaps the most useful reading of the case: the judge rewired the standard to follow the exact contours of the evidence, not the expectation that the jury should stumble over contrarian expert testimony.

## Implications for Patent Litigation

### Raising the Bar for Expert Submissions

This case confirms that appellate courts will not let a hodgepodge of expert testimony stand in place of a clearly articulated, claim‑by‑claim mapping. Patent attorneys now have no alternative but to ensure that expert reports are rigorous and that the expert is not simply “filling in the blanks” when the device’s operation is ambiguous. As a result, pre‑trial discovery will see a surge in the call for “expert depositions with live technical proofs” to substantiate claims like the ‘134’s sampling assertion or the ‘775’s multi‑multiplication requirement.

### Preventing “Damaging Inferences” from Overreaching Expert Claims

Because the damages were vacated, the decision signals that “$ millions is not a safeguard if the infringement conviction is called into question.” Once a JMOL vehicle is successful, any penalty that relies on that judgment must be scrapped. This may temper the calculus that many plaintiffs use when they file suits in Texas: the fear that a later vacuum of evidence will erase their award may force them to tightening evidence requirements earlier in the proceedings.

### A Test of Claims and the Role of Contextual Knowledge

The decisions also show that claim construction—especially interpretive requests for complex devices that mix hardware and software—needs to be rooted in a *complete understanding of the device’s architecture.” The expert’s reliance on a Nokia reference that has‑function is not necessarily indicative of the actual implementation in the accused device. Courts will likely read this as a caution that proving a low‑level circuit claim must depend on *validated* test data or a verified simulation, not a high‑level specification.

### Toward a More “Judicial Safeguarding” Template

This case illustrates a broader trend: appellate panels are moving toward a default skepticism of the jury’s verdict if the evidence, once distilled, does not carry the weight of the “reasonable‑jury” standard. It continues the Federal Circuit’s practice of tightening the standard in the face of discretion, thereby preserving the district court’s analytical mandate from losing ground to a big-ticket jury verdict that rests on shaky testimony.

## Conclusion

The Federal Circuit’s reversal in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility* is more than a single decision on two patents. It is a firm reiteration that appellate review, even on a high‑stakes infringement and damages award, remains bound by the *substantial‑evidence* rule. The court’s spotlight on contradictory expert testimony underscores the need for practitioners to invest in meticulous expert preparation, and to regard the risk of a misleading record as a legitimate threat to the very life of a large award. Just as in tort, the judge returns court to the principle that one must prove not only that a potential infringement exists but that the claimed device *really* does what the patent requires. The next time you draft a claim or hire an expert, warn yourself and your client: the jury’s verdict is under watchful eyes, and the appellate court will not pretend the evidence is clear just because you told it so.



# Add ‘And’ to the Claim: How the Three‑Signal Amendment Re‑shaped Claim Scope  

## Why This Decision Matters  

The Federal Circuit’s September 2025 ruling in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* is a landmark for patent‑litigation practitioners, not because of the extraordinary $166 million damages that were vacated, but for the court’s precise treatment of a seemingly modest claim‑amendment: the change from “two or three signals” to simply “three signals” in U.S. Pat. No. 9,548,775. The court’s analysis clarifies how an amendment that resorts to “clear and unambiguous” language can nevertheless fail to cement a surrender of scope, thereby protecting litigants when they confront prior‑art rejections. It also underscores the perils of relying on opaque expert testimony and technical documentation that does not directly support the infringement hurdle.

## The Amendment in Context  

At issue was a method claim that, in its final form, required a receiver to “digitally multiply three signals [S1, S2, S3] in seven multiplications” in order to generate cancellation signals for passive intermodulation products. In earlier prosecution versions the claim read “two or three signals” – a more flexible construction that could be satisfied with either two or three input signals. When the prior‑art examiner raised an obviousness rejection under *Filipovic* (which discloses a two‑input scheme), *Finesse* amended the claim to recite “three signals” as a direct counter. The question the appellate court faced was whether that amendment, while linguistically precise, unambiguously relinquished the “two‑signal” path and thereby restricts infringement under the prior‑art scenario presented in the trial.

The court emphasized that the amendment “does not rise to the level of ‘clear and unambiguous’ disavowal of claim scope over products, like the accused radios, with two unique input signals.” In its analysis the court relied on the standard that an amendment disavows scope only when the language is “clear, unambiguous, and relatively *definitive*” (citing *Cont’l Circuits* and *Wi‑LAN* precedents). The taking of the word “or” and the insistence on “three signals” were judged insufficient to render the claim historically overt. The court concluded that the amendment still requires “three separately identifiable signals” – a broad requirement that does not exclude the scenario in which two distinct signals are provided, one of which is simply a copy of the other.

## Prosecution‑History Estoppel and ‘Clear and Unambiguous’  

The ruling reinforces the enduring principle that prosecution history does not impose estoppel by default. A term’s amendment may curb the latitude of earlier scope, but only if the claim language is rendered so precisely that it noticeably narrows the meaning. The “clear and unambiguous” test serves as an interpretive safeguard: it protects the inventor’s intent and ensures that an amendment cannot be construed as a deliberate surrender of law unless the corpus *clearly* indicates such intent.

In the *Finesse* case, the court found the claim to be “relatively…definitive” only in the sense that it requires three *separately identifiable* signals, not in an exclusive sense that two signals alone would satisfy. *Finesse’s* attempt to drop the “or” was juxtaposed against the unchanged requirement that the three signals be “separately identifiable.” The judicial analysis underscores that the relationship between the two terms is a *requires‑but‑does‑not‑exclude* relationship: the claim captures all implementations that include at least three inputs, but it does not vanish the ability of an implementer to satisfy the claim with only two distinct signals if one is duplicated.

This distinction matters for litigants. When amending claims to evade a particular prior art, one must not rely on linguistic tightening alone. Instead, one must re‑structure the claim in a way that demonstrably alters the claim’s intent or the *essential* nature of the intended invention. Simply removing an “or” often yields a language that courts will interpret on its face and see as a broad, non‑narrowed conception.

## The Flawed Technical Evidence  

Beyond claim construction, the court found that *Finesse’s* infringement evidence was inadequate, both for the ’134 patent and for the ’775 patent. For the ’775, the crux was that Dr. Wells, the plaintiff’s expert, testified that the accused radios performed “seven claimed multiplications” but only documented “three distinct multiplications” in the Nokia radio‑spec sheet. The expert was unable to explain how the three statements mapped onto the seven claimed operations, nor did the board map the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia docs back to the seven claims. The court’s statement that *no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications* rests squarely on the absence of substantiating evidence that maps the detailed operations to the claim language. This underscores that if the amendment’s technical requirements are to remain significant, the plaintiff must bring forth clear, unambiguous, and direct evidence that the accused device’s logic satisfies every limitation.

## Implications for Patent Drafting and Litigation  

The decision carries several practical take‑aways:

1. **Draft with Precision, Not Ambiguity**. Amendments that are merely wording tweaks often fail to foreshadow an essential change in scope. This case demonstrates that omitting an “or” clause did not strip the claim of its two‑signal path because the essential requirement—*separately identifiable signals*—remains. Drafters should specify the exact nature of required “signals” (e.g., distinct input channels, unique frequencies) if the goal is to exclude fewer‑signal solutions.

2. **Prohibition of “Switch‑Back” Problems**. If a claim initially covered a broader scenario (two or three signals), a later amendment that eliminates the “or” but does not add a necessary limitation fails to create a “switch‑back” – wherein the claim is argued to cover a narrower scenario that the amendment’s language does not expressly preclude. In other words, the court will treat the claim as explicitly covering instances that meet its *currently* stated language and not automatically re‑assert the omitted alternatives.

3. **Estoppel Is Conditional**. The decision reaffirms that prior‑art estoppel requires an unmistakable admission that the claim’s meaning has changed. Courts will not retroactively imbue a claim with narrower presumptions unless its amended language indicates so unquestionably. Litigants should be mindful that they may be subject to prosecution‑history estoppel only with unmistakable proof of an intentional scope curtailment.

4. **Critically Evaluate Technical Evidence**. If the claim’s language is not unequivocally dispositive, then the evidentiary burden falls heavily on the claimant to demonstrate that the accused product truly satisfies every clause. The failure of *Finesse* to map the claimed multiplications onto an actual implementation dramatically weakens its position, regardless of the clarity of the claim language. Audits of technical documentation should seek tangible evidence of each logical step.

5. **Strategic Post‑Rejection Amendments**. When confronted with a prior‑art rejection that relies on a specific architectural solution, amending the claim to a more abstract description may not suffices; instead, adding clear, operative parameters that exclude the disallowed implementation can protect the claim’s breadth while maintaining the intended scope.

## Toward a Balanced Claiming Strategy  

Prosecution attorneys must now balance the dual desire to avoid an obviousness rejection with the imperative to preserve claim scope. The *Finesse* ruling reminds us that precise, unambiguous changes are necessary to engineer a narrowing of claim scope. It also furnishes a guiding principle: if a claim is intended to encompass any number of *separably identifiable* signals, simply removing “or” does not materially alter that scope.

Litigants should view the court’s decision not as a setback but as a clarifying lens. It cannot help them judge whether a claim’s ultimate reach will withstand infringement analysis; rather, it shows them the layer of scrutiny that will be applied to any claim‑amendment move. And it reiterates that the best route to preserving scope while amending claims is to employ an unambiguous restriction that specifically excludes the prior‑art implementation in question—by articulating, for example, “three distinct input signal paths” and specifying their functional role – rather than relying on a subtle linguistic tweak.

## Conclusion  

The *Finesse Wireless* decision is a masterclass in how the Federal Circuit interprets claim amendments and the hard limits of prosecution‑history estoppel. While the “three signals” amendment nominally appears stricter, the court did not see it as a decisive narrowing because the amended language still accepts implementations where two signals suffice. That can be read as an affirmation of the enduring principle that claim scope cannot be lost without explicit, unequivocal language. The decision, in leaving the $166 million judgment vacated only on the basis that the jury’s verdict was unsupported by substantial evidence, also reminds litigants that the burden of connecting a claim’s elements to a product’s actual operations remains paramount. Patentees will have to be meticulous in drafting claims and in amassing robust, direct evidence to support those claims. For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: when they wish to narrow scope to preclude prior art, the language change must be explicit and evidenced; a mere removal of an “or” will not do.



# Inside the Frequency Maze: Internal vs. External PIM and the Anatomy of Interference Claims

Punctured by a single mistake, an entire patent litigation can unravel.  In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* the Federal Circuit set out to untangle a knot built of high‑frequency troubleshooting, patent claim construction, and the thin line between a claim’s “signals of interest” and the “interference‑generating signals.”  The decision is more than a footnote in the history of PIM technology; it is a textbook illustration of how the precise language of a claim, the testimony of an expert, and the procedural posture of a lawsuit combine to determine whether a jury verdict should stand.

## Why Internal vs. External PIM Matters

Passive intermodulation (PIM) is not a trivial software glitch—it is a fundamental physics problem that can cause a smartphone or base‑station radio to drown in the very signals it is meant to hear.  When two sufficiently strong signals coexist on the same frequency band, their interaction in a passive component (copper wire, solder joint, or an antenna feed line) can produce new spurious tones called intermodulation products (IMPs).  If those IMPs fall within the useful band, they can degrade reception or even cause severe interference.  The *Adding‑intermodulation* problem is why makers of mobile handsets and base‑stations invest heavily in PIM‑cancellation techniques.

When a device’s own receive path can generate PIM—what the Federal Circuit calls “internal PIM”—the remedy differs from dealing with “external PIM,” generated outside the radio.  Internal PIM is a property of the device’s own hardware and firmware, whereas external PIM is caused by the environment in which the device operates.  The distinction mattered in *Finesse* because the defendants claimed that the patented PIM‑cancellation methods were performed either before or after the radio’s analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC) had sampled the incoming signal.  Whether the claimed devices had actually sampled a passband of signals that included both the “signals of interest” and the “interference‑generating signals” hinged on whether the PIM‑generating phenomena were internal (generated inside the device) or external (generated elsewhere).

## Claim Construction: The Heart of the Decision

The crux of the case rested on the Fifth Circuit's standard for judging a motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL).  The court reiterated that it reviews such motions de novo, asking whether, when the evidence is viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, it points so strongly and overwhelmingly toward one party that a reasonable jury could not have arrived at anything else.  In the *Finesse* district‑court trial, the jury found every claim of the two patents infringed and awarded $166 million in damages.  The Federal Circuit reversed that verdict by finding that the evidence presented failed to meet that stringent threshold of “substantial evidence” for two separate sets of claims.

### For the ’134 Patent

Claim 1 of the ’134 patent is centered around a method of separating the “signals of interest” from the “interference‑generating signals” before the RF ADC samples them, then generating an estimate of each in‑band IMP and canceling it.  The central limitation of “over‑sampling… to create a bit stream” required that the receiver’s ADC actually sample the full band—including both the desired signal and whatever has the potential to produce a PIM within that band.

Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, told the jury that the Nokia technical documents showed the accused radios’ RF ADC sampling an “interference‑generating” path that was modeled *after* the ADC had already finished sampling the signal.  When the cross‑examining counsel pointed out that a modeled path could not be “generated” before the ADC sampled, Wells pivoted, insisting that “the two signals on the red path” were the needed “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.”  The court was not convinced that his shifting testimony sufficed.  It found that he essentially asserted the modeled PIM path was an “interference‑generating signal” **while, in fact, the path was constructed only after sampling**—a clear contradiction that undermined the evidence standard.  The court therefore reversed the JMOL denial, vacating the damages that had been awarded for the ’134 claims.

### For the ’775 Patent

The ’775 patent’s hurdle was even sharper.  A claim limitation required the accused receiver to produce four complex multipliers—seven third‑order multiplications—based on *three* separately identifiable signals.  The defense argued that the accused radios used only two distinct transmitter signals (denoted x1 and x2) and a copy of one of them, which would not satisfy the "three separate" requirement.  The jury, persuaded otherwise, found infringement. 

Finesse relied on a Nokia technical document that listed only three distinct multiplications involving the two signals and their conjugates and magnitudes: x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2′, and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|.  The court held that these three multiplications were **not** the seven claimed ones.  Because the Nokia document did not disclose any mapping from the two physical signals into *seven* distinct third‑order multiplications, no reasonable jury could find the limit satisfied.  The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL, again vacating the damages award for the ’775 claims.

## Dr. Wells’s Testimony: A Case Study in Expert Ambiguity

At each step, the court confronted an expert whose testimony wavered between assertion and technical uncertainty.  In the ’134 case, Wells spoke of an “interference‑generating” path that, by definition, could not be captured by the ADC because it did not exist at the time of sampling.  His insistence on a PIM‑model path in a receiver that, in theory, was not part of the sampled mix illustrated the danger of translating engineering diagrams into legal arguments without a disciplined mapping to the claim elements.

In the ’775 case, Wells again floundered.  He strung together a story that sprinkled the required number of multiplications across a document that only gave three, misreading the mathematical expressions—especially complex‑conjugate operations—as equivalent to the claimed seventh multiplication.  The court concluded that the evidence was too thin to support the jury’s verdict.

Both episodes reinforce the principle that patent litigation is not a science experiment on its own terms; it is a legal narrative that must survive the judge’s scrutiny of credibility, consistency, and relevance to the claim language.

## Drafting Lessons: What Patent Attorneys Picked Up

1. **Define the “Signals of Interest” and “Interference‑Generating Signals” by Concrete Microarchitecture**  
   The claims here dragged the court into a debate over whether a modeled, post‑ADC path could be treated as an “interference‐generating signal.”  Precise claim language that ties “interference‑generating” to physically present path segments that are *present* at the time of sampling—and that uses operative verbs that do not ambiguously allow imbedded post‑processing—helps avoid courts interpreting the elements on unintended fodder.

2. **Be Careful With “Three Separate Signals”**  
   The court sent a clear message: a claim that requires “three separately identifiable signals” does not automatically imply the same physical signal cannot serve more than one role.  However, it does preclude a lone signal.  Literal claim drafting and enumeration of the scenarios where a single signal may be replicated or recombined must adhere to the spirit of “separate.”

3. **Avoid Ambiguous Cross‑Examining Techniques**  
   Assertions that a given path is “generated” after a critical action may be a red flag, especially if that action is a transducer (like an ADC).  Expert testimony should include verifiable timing diagrams and a clarity that won't be thrown out as contradictory.

4. **Amenditions as “Surrender”?**  
   The court concluded that the amendment to phrase the claim as “digitally multiplying three signals” did not razor‑edge the scope away from two signals.  The court explicitly noted the amendment’s lack of “clear and unambiguous” disavowal.  When you amend claims to overcome prior‑art rejections, a more thorough disclosure—perhaps an illustrative block diagram—could demonstrate to a court the exact range of admissible technical configurations.

5. **Document Assurance & Expert Isolation**  
   The appellate court noted that Finesse never presented to the jury a mapping from the Nokia ILR (illustrated logical representation) to the seven multiplications.  This highlights the importance of ensuring any expert mapping is available for the jury and not merely revealed to the panel during appellate brief preparation.

## Doctrinal and Practical Implications

*Finesse* adds to the Fifth Circuit’s body of work regarding substantial evidence and the judicial interpretation of “internal” vs. “external” PIM.  It reinforces that:

- **Claim construction depends not just on the words but on the *implementation*** of the claimed method. “Substantially similar” injection of signals must be physically plausible, not merely mathematically modeled within the software.  
- **Expert testimony that shifts from one technical concept to another without transparency is unreliable**.  Authors of technical patents can no longer rely on the doctrine of “best engineering practice” as a crutch; each claim element must be demonstrable.  
- **JMOL is a high bar**.  Unlike a negative, a motion for JMOL requires overwhelming evidence.  A jury verdict that can be undone by a single technical misinterpretation or gap in the evidence will flush at appellate review.  
- **Litigation over PIM may give patent attorneys a new battleground**.  Plaintiffs must provide unambiguous mapping between circuit diagrams and the claim elements that need to be proven; defendants, in turn, can use expert testimony to challenge such mapping.

On the technical front, the case signals that internal PIM mitigation can be patent‑protected, but proving infringement requires careful segregation of *observed* path elements and their required timing order.  The distinction between “interference‑generating signals” that exist pre‑ADC versus modeled after-sampling is not merely semantic; it can hinge the outcome of a starry‑sphere suit.

## Conclusion

The fine print of a patent claim is more than legal jargon; it is a map that translates the labyrinthine engineering of a radio into a jurisdictional boundary.  *Finesse* reminds us that any attempt to skirt this boundary—by making ambiguous promises of "separate signals" or implying post‑sampling modeled paths—generates a room for the court to snatch down.  The dollar‑staggering damages award lost to the reversal indicates the stakes are high: the quality of claim construction, the reliability of expert testimony, and the coherence of the technical story can decide between a win and a costly reversal.

For the practitioner, the lesson is simple: draft with explicitness, shepherd your expert’s testimony through the rigors of evidence, and remember that the court will not accept an engineering theory that cannot be consistently mapped to the claim language under scrutiny.  Whether you’re protecting an internal PIM cancellation algorithm or dissecting external interference, the intersection of law and physics remains a place where precision is indispensable.



# When Verdicts Snap: Why a $166 Million Damages Award Was Vacated After a JMOL Reversal  

## Why This Matters  

Patent litigation can be a numbers game. A single jury verdict can spin a case into the millions, and at times into the billions, if the jury deems the licensing or infringement to be highly profitable. The Finesse Wireless v. AT&T decision is a reminder that appellate courts are devoted to the same principle that underlies any judgment: no amount of money can sit on top of a finding that does not exist. In this case, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of a judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) that had preserved a jury verdict for infringement of two patents—an act that, by default, wiping out a $166 million lump‑sum damages award that had already burned through cameras and e‑mail inboxes in both the Wall Street and the Silicon Valley circuits.

That the court reversed a huge damages award not because of a procedural hiccup but because the infringement findings themselves failed the “substantial evidence” test makes the case a textbook illustration of the interplay between proof of infringement and the accountant’s spreadsheet of damages. It also signals that patent litigants who expect a Broadway‑worthy verdict must double‑check that the evidence actually survives a 12(b)(6), or else both their jury verdict and the profits that come with it may vanish in a single appellate blow.

## The Finesse Wireless v. AT&T: A Quick Recap  

Finesse Wireless owns U.S. patents 7,346,134 and 9,548,775, which claim ways of mitigating inter‑modulation product (IMP) interference in radio signal receivers—a highly specialized, if not essential, technology for modern smartphones and base‑stations. When AT&T and Nokia’s radios allegedly employed Finesse’s “passive‑intermodulation cancellation” (PIM‑C) feature, the company filed suit in the Eastern District of Texas. The jury found that all of the asserted claims were both valid and infringed and awarded Finesse an enormous $166 million lump‑sum for the remaining life of the patents.

But the jury verdict was not the end of the story. AT&T and Nokia moved after the verdict for: (i) JMOL of non‑infringement on each patent; (ii) JMOL of damages; and (iii) a new trial on damages. The district court denied all three motions, and the parties were left with a hefty damages award that looked as though it was a final nail in the yard of patent litigation.

## The Judge’s Stand‑still on Infringement  

The district court’s decision that rejected JMOL on infringement was anchored in what the Fifth Circuit calls the “substantial evidence” standard: a finding is supported if a reasonable jury could have reached it in light of the evidence. The court bragged that Dr. Jonathan Wells’s testimony was strong: he had mapped the RF analog‑to‑digital converter (ADC) in the Nokia radios as sampling both *signals of interest* (the downlink transmit reference) and *interference‑generating signals* (the modeled PIM path), thereby satisfying the key limitation of Patent ‘134. Because the district court believed that evidence, it tossed the JMOL motion.

The Federal Circuit, however, took the same evidence and, in a de‑novo review, concluded that the evidence was misleading at best and lawfully inadequate at worst. The court laid the blame on perverse expert testimony. Dr. Wells claimed that the *modeled PIM path* was a signal generated after the ADC had already done its sampling, meaning that the device never actually “sampled” interference‑generating signals as required. Worse, Wells bounced back and forth between appealing to a Nokia technical diagram and to the underlying signals x1 and x2, never clearly mapping either to “signals of interest” or “interference‑generating signals.” The court saw this as a classic shoot‑and‑pause case of self‑contradiction that’s not enough to let a jury surmise infringement.

Similarly, the damages‑fueled claims under Patent ‘775 required the device to multiply **three** signals in **seven** different third‑order multiplications. Yet the indictment of the radio’s behavior came from a Samsung‑style chart that lists only three distinct multiplication terms and does not demonstrate the seven prescribed multiplications. The court held that the evidence of “three signals” was not enough, nor was it enough to back a seven‑multiplication choreography. Therefore the facts were insufficient to support infringement, and the jury verdict should have been tossed on their feet.

In both patent claims, the appellate court was convinced that “reasonable jurors could not have reached any contrary conclusion.” In other words, the evidence did not support the infringement finding, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL, and the jury verdict was scrubbed.

## How Damages Get Caught in the Same Net  

When the infringement finding was overturned, the damages that were the direct fruit of that finding had no botanical root to stand on. The jury had awarded $166 million in a lump sum, predicated on proof of infringement. The Federal Circuit’s injunction—“the damages award is vacated”—wasn't a flashy new damages calculation; it was a sobering reminder that the existence of damages is contingent on the existence of infringement. A large award is no more secure if the very claim that underpinned it shows up to be mistaken.

From a procedural standpoint, the court’s analysis boiled down to the relationship between “denial of JMOL on damages” and “denial of a new trial on damages.” Both motions require the court to assess whether the evidence supports a damages calculation. If the evidence fails the underlying infringement test, a damages claim has nothing to calculate against. The appellate court rejected the district court’s denial of JMOL on damages by adding to its reasoning: “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of non‑infringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.” In effect, the damages were de‑facto gone because the underlying tax base—the infringement—was wiped away.

## JMOL on Damages Versus a New Trial on Damages  

A discernible procedural distinction exists between a motion for JMOL on damages and a motion for a new trial on damages. The former is an attempt by a party to obtain a judgment without a jury, claiming that the evidence simply does not support the amount of damages the jury chose. Courts look at the evidence afresh, but they usually hold that if a substantial body of evidence could support the amount of damages a jury awarded, the court must defer to the jury unless the deficit is so glaring that no problem‑free jury could find such an amount.

A motion for a new trial on damages, conversely, asks the court to order a new jury to decide the damages because the evidence is unsound or incomplete. It is a higher bar; the district court must find that the evidence is insufficient *in its entirety* and that no reasonable jury could correct the issue by a different finding. Only then can it order a new trial.

In this case, AT&T and Nokia argued that the appellate court should have granted a new trial on damages because the jury’s calculation was based on a false premise: that the patents were infringed. Yet because the court had already reversed the entire infringement finding, the jurors would not have even seen the case to weigh damages. A new trial on damages becomes redundant once infringement itself is lacking. The appellate court’s conclusion that it should vacate the award instead of giving a new trial is both procedurally tidy and logically inevitable.

## How This Shapes Doctrinal Expectations  

The decisions subtly reinforce the axiom that damages are tied to infringement. The jurisprudence already has this principle; the MPEP, for instance, says that the damages calculation is derived from the *actual or reasonably supportive evidence* of infringement. But the volatility in this case serves as a cautionary anecdote: the only way a massive lump‑sum can survive an appellate challenge is if it is anchored on verifiable infringement.

Moreover, the Federal Circuit’s emphasis on “substantial evidence” in the context of infringement is a reminder that expert testimony must be crystal‑clear and directional. Even a handful of conflicting statements can erode a jury’s confidence, and that confidence can become a lever for appellate courts to overturn entire verdicts. The decision also underscores that patent amendment history does not automatically limit infringement scope unless an amendment is “clear and unambiguous” in scope terms. In Finesse’s case, changing “two or three signals” to “three signals” was deemed insufficient to negate the possibility that the accused device only used two unique signals. That clarifies that claim amendment, absent a repudiatory tone, retains full scope.

Finally, the case phonies the interplay between infringement and damages. It underscores that a large damages award is not a separate sheet of paper that can survive a fully separate review. The mass of the $166 million figure is tied to the entire balance sheet of the infringement judgment. No amount of monetary appetence can outlive the structural proof that the patents were infringed, and the court’s vacatur is the logical completion of that logical chain.

## Practical Lessons: What Patent Litigants Can Do Better  

1. **Hold the Pre‑Trial Narrative Together**. In Finesse’s case, the expert testified to two mutual claims on the same signal path without distinguishing the “signals of interest” from the “interference‑generating signals.” A narrow departure from the claim language—like failing to demonstrate that the modeled PIM path actually existed in the sampled feed—was enough for the court to walk the evidence apart. The bottom line: if you want a jury to lean toward infringement, your experts must run a tight ship with their own technical speeches.

2. **Avoid Contradictory Mapping Between Internal and External Terms**. When a defense hinges on the idea that a signal is generated inside a device, but the device first samples that signal, you have a razor‑edge logical flaw. In this scenario, AT&T and Nokia discovered that the PIM model was generated after sampling, meaning the core claim’s limitation could not be satisfied. Designers and patent attorneys should ensure that the architect drawings, schematic definitions, and technical documentation all share the same naming conventions, and that contradictions are caught before the expert departs.

3. **Work with Claim Language That Is Defensible**. The court highlighted that “digitally multiplying three signals” was not a repudiation of “two signals.” That nuance may turn the tide in a record that is later examined. Plaintiffs should consider whether the claim limitations are sufficiently rigid that a potential infringer’s usage of fewer signals or fewer multiplications could be considered infringement. Likewise, defendants should be attentive to claim amendments that explicitly broaden or narrow scope.

4. **Build Direct Links Between Evidence and Damage Calculation**. Even if a jury awards damages, the appellate court can shear them away if the point of infringement is not compelling. Therefore, the damage evidence should be linked step‑by‑step to an infringement finding: e.g., showing how the device produced the same quantity of radio emissions as claimed, and that such emissions have turned into financial harm. It is not enough to rely on a patent’s intrinsic claim to carry the weight of the damages; the evidence we produce must trace the path from infringement to loss.

5. **Beware of Pre‑Trial “JMOL Loopholes.”** Some litigants might think that the path to a sizable damages award is merely a matter that a jury can decide. In Finesse, the denial of JMOL was meaningful because it paved the way for the jury to find infringement. The appellate court’s final weapon—the predetermined path of evidence—was, in ultimate measure, a “no‑go” area for the plaintiff. Planners should realize that the pre‑trial motions can be the first real line of defense for the defendant and that a robust, fact‑based defense can go through the same de‑novo analysis as the verdict itself.

## In a Nutshell  

The Finesse Wireless v. AT&T ruling is a stark reminder that blockbuster patent verdicts are, at base, a game of evidence: the jury’s find of infringement must be sustained, else any damages that hinge on that find will be rendered moot. The Federal Circuit’s vacatur of a $166 million award was a direct consequence of a failure to meet the “substantial evidence” threshold for infringement. It also demonstrates that the procedural distinction between JMOL on damages and a new trial on damages is essentially moot when the entire infringer finding collapses. For patent attorneys, the message is clear: keep the expert testimony tight, keep the claim language unambiguous, and be ready to show that the device’s operations meet the claim’s requirement not just by proximity but by type. And in the end, the largest lawsuit in a courtroom can end up in a smaller echo chamber—one where the court can simply remove the money because one of the facts it was based on was wrong.
