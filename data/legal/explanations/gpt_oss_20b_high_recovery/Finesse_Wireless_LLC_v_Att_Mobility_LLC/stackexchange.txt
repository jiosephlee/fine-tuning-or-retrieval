Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What standard does the Fifth Circuit use for reviewing a denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) in patent cases?
Question:
I see that the opinion says the Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL de novo, but I’m struggling to understand what that actually means for a patent infringement case. Does the appellate court get to re‑examine every element of the jury’s finding, or does it only look at the legal conclusion? How does this de‑novo standard differ from the standard used for reviewing factual findings or from the standards applied in other circuits?
Answer:
## What the Fifth Circuit’s “de novo” standard actually says

In Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility (2025) the court clarifies its approach to a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL):

> “**The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL de novo, reversing only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.**”  
>  *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016).*

In the same opinion the court also reminds us that it uses a **substantial‑evidence** standard when reviewing a jury’s factual findings—for a patent infringement claim that simply means *the evidence must be such that a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party*:

> “**We review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence.**”  
>  *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022).*

The de novo standard, therefore, is applied only to the *denial* of JMOL; the standard for the *finding* of infringement is distinct and lower.

---

## Does the appellate court re‑examine every element of the jury’s finding?

Yes, it can.  
A de novo review means the appellate court **does not give any deference to the district court’s determination** (whether that be a factual finding or a legal ruling). It looks at the evidence *as if it were fresh*—taking it “in the light most favorable to the verdict.” The appellate court may therefore re‑analyze any element that the record shows is in dispute: claim construction, the identity of the signals tested, the number of multiplications performed, etc. 

What it does not do is simply test a legal conclusion on a pre‑existing factual matrix. Instead, it asks: *Is the evidence so compelling and unmistakable that a reasonable jury could not have reached a different conclusion?* If the answer is “yes,” the denial of JMOL is reversed. If the evidence is only borderline, the denial is upheld.

---

## How does this differ from the “substantial evidence” standard for factual findings?

| Issue | Standard in the Fifth Circuit | How it works |
|-------|------------------------------|--------------|
| **Denial of JMOL** | **De novo** – “strongly and overwhelmingly” weighted evidence | The appellate court re‑examines the record, ignores the district court’s ruling, and overturns the denial if the evidence points emphatically in the other direction. |
| **Jury’s factual finding of infringement** | **Substantial evidence** | The appellate court simply asks whether a *reasonable jury* could have found in favor of the prevailing party. It does not require the same level of “overwhelming” evidence; “substantial” is a lower bar. |

Because de novo is stronger (it requires the evidence to be *strongly and overwhelmingly* in favor of one side), an appellate court is far less likely to reverse a denial of JMOL than it is to overturn a jury’s factual finding.

---

## How does the Fifth Circuit’s approach compare to other circuits?

The opinion references *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, which has been cited broadly. Some circuits have:

* **Abuse‑of‑discretion** standards for denial of JMOL (e.g., certain decisions by the Sixth Circuit).  
* A **preponderance** or **clear‑and‑convincing** test for infringement findings (e.g., Seventh Circuit).  
* A **de novo** standard but with a different wording, e.g., “substantial evidence” rather than “strongly and overwhelmingly.”

The Fifth Circuit’s distinguishing feature is the **explicit “strongly and overwhelmingly” language** coupled with review “de novo.” That phrasing makes the standard more stringent than a mere “substantial evidence” check and more open to reversal than a “preponderance” standard used elsewhere.  

---

### Bottom line for a student

When a district court denies a JMOL in a patent case, the Fifth Circuit will start from scratch. It will read the record with the light most favorable to the jury and decide whether the evidence **could have compelled a jury to find the contrary conclusion**. This is the de novo standard. When the court reviews the jury’s factual finding (e.g., that infringing claims were activated), it applies a **substantial‑evidence** test instead. Other circuits may treat JMOL denial as an abuse of discretion or use a different evidentiary threshold, so always check the circuit’s specific rule.

### In the ’775’ patent, why does the court treat ‘S1, S2 and S3’ as ‘separately identifiable’ but not ‘unique’, and how does that affect claim scope after amendment?
Question:
The opinion discusses that the district court construed the claim limitation that the three signals must be separately identifiable but not unique, allowing the accused radio to use two signals and a copy of one. I’m unclear on what the difference is between ‘separately identifiable’ and ‘unique’. Why doesn’t the court treat three distinct physical signals as a requirement? Also, the court says the amendment from ‘two or three signals’ to ‘three signals’ does not amount to surrendering claim scope – how is that conclusion reached?
Answer:
**Answer**

The district court’s construction of the ’775 claim limitation on the three signals can be read in two ways:

| Term | Legal meaning (governed by claim construction law) | Evidence the court found |
|------|---------------------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **separately identifiable** | Each of S1, S2, S3 must be a distinct “thing” that can be singled out in the claim. The term does *not* require that the underlying physical inputs be different from one another. | The wording of the claim (“given three signals S1, S2 and S3”) and the inventor’s testimony that the signals “represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals” (J.A. 109–14) support the view that the signals must be distinguishable in the claim, but their *source* need not be unique. |
| **unique** | The claim would be scoped so that S1, S2, S3 each have to be a *physically distinct* signal. | No intrinsic evidence – either in the claim wording or the specification – required that each signal be a separate physical source. The patent never used a term like “distinct” or “different” in a way that would give the court a basis to construe the limitation that way. Therefore the limitation is *not* “unique.” (J.A. 115, & J.A. 109–14) |

The judicial standard behind this split is the ordinary claim‑construction principle: the claim language is read in the light most favorable to the language itself (35 U.S.C. § 112(b)).  When a term could have more than one reasonable interpretation, the trier of fact decides which interpretation makes the claim consistent with the intrinsic evidence.  If no reasonable reader would impose the stronger “unique” interpretation, the court must not do so.

---

### Why the court allows a copy of one signal

Because the claim requires only that the three signals be *separately identifiable*, the accused radio is permitted to generate the same physical signal twice (e.g., send‑<sub>A</sub> and send‑<sub>B</sub> both equal to transmitted x<sub>1</sub>) and still satisfy the claim.  The court said “using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, was sufficient to meet its construction of three separately identifiable signals” (J.A. 38).  The intrinsic evidence (the seven multiplications that involve S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, etc.) is compatible with this reading; it does not require that all three signals be different physical waves.

---

### How the amendment “three signals” did not surrender claim scope

The amendment moved the claim limitation from “two or three signals” to “three signals” in order to distinguish it from the Filipovic reference that claims *two* signals.  The court explained that this amendment:

1. **Does not impose a *unique* requirement**, so the claim still covers any implementation that produces three *identifiable* signals, even if two of them are copies.  (J.A. 968‑69)
2. **Does not rise to the level of “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of claim scope** over products that use two unique input signals.  See *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019).  In other words, the amendment merely clarified the signal terminology; it did not abandon the possibility of two–signal implementations that still satisfy the “three separately identifiable” limitation.

Because the amendment did not explicitly exclude the two‑signal alternative, the court held it **did not sacrifice** the claim’s reach to products that could be built with only two distinct physical inputs.  The claim therefore remains broader than it would have been if the amendment had been construed as a “unique” requirement.

---

### Bottom line for the infringement decision

- **“Separately identifiable”** means S1, S2, S3 must each be a distinct element in the claim; **“unique”** would mean each must come from a distinct physical source—something the evidence does not support.  
- The court’s construction allows a device that uses two unique signals plus a copy of one of them, because that still renders three “separately identifiable” signals.  
- The amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals” was not a surrender of scope because it did not disavow two‑signal implementations; it simply added language that does not require all three sources to be different.

Hence, the claim continues to cover a broad range of receiver architectures, including the accused Nokia radios that implement the seven multiplications with only two distinct input signals plus a duplicate.

### When an expert’s testimony is contradictory, can a court still find evidence sufficient for a JMOL, and what are the principles that guide that decision?
Question:
Dr. Wells gave conflicting statements about whether the RF ADC sampled the modeled PIM path. The court calls his testimony self‑contradictory and therefore insufficient for a jury verdict. What rules govern when expert testimony that contains contradictions is admissible for a JMOL? Are there specific standards for consistency, clarity, or sufficiency that a court must apply?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under the Fifth Circuit’s standard for a judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), a court can grant JMOL even if an expert’s testimony is admissible but self‑contradictory.  The key is whether the *reliability* of that testimony allows a *substantial* body of evidence to support the verdict.  When a witness publicly contradicts himself—and no other evidence gives a jury a basis to reconcile the statements—the court may conclude there is *no substantial evidence* in the light most favorable to the verdict and therefore must grant JMOL.  The decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* (Fed. Cir. 2025) illustrates this rule in action.

---

## 1. The JMOL standard

### Rule 702 exception & “substantial evidence”

*Rule 702* governs the admissibility of expert testimony, but it contains an *exception* for unreliable witnesses.  A contradictory testimony is a classic symptom of unreliability.  The court may therefore refuse to give weight to the testimony, or, as in *Finesse*, conclude the evidence is *insufficient* to support a verdict.

The Fifth Circuit reviews denial of JMOL *de novo*, applying the principle in **Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple, Inc.** (Fed. Cir. 2016):

> *“The court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, and grant JMOL only if the evidence points so strongly in favor of one side that a reasonable jury could not reach a contrary conclusion.”*  
> **(Ibid. at 461).**

The “reasonable jury” test is the same one used for a *substantial‑evidence* determination in infringement cases:

> *“A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.”*  
> **(Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN, 25 F.4th 960 at 969).**

Thus, to deny JMOL, the court must show that, in the view most favorable to the verdict, the evidence (including expert testimony) makes the verdict *clear and overwhelming*.

### What “clear and overwhelming” means

The court is not required to *disprove* the other side; it only has to show that *no reasonable jury* could outside of that unilateral view reach a different conclusion. If the evidence contains a substantial element that a reasonable jury could plausibly weigh against the verdict—such as a self‑contradictory witness—the evidence is *not* “clearly and overwhelmingly” in either direction.

---

## 2. Contradictory testimony in *Finesse*

### Dr. Wells’ conflicting statements

* Dr. Wells first identified the two signals on the red path as the “DL (TX) reference” (signal of interest) and the “modeled‑PIM path” (interference–generating signal) and testified that the RF ADC samples **both** of those signals (J.A. 14914‑21, 14918‑19, 14925).  
* Cross‑examiner counter‑argued that the modeled‑PIM path is generated *after* the ADC samples, so it could not be an input to the ADC (J.A. 15010‑11).  
* Wells then “pivoted,” again naming x 1 and x 2 as the two signals on the red path but never clarified that x 1 = signals of interest and x 2 = interference‑generating signal (J.A. 15010‑19).  
* In every instance where he was asked to identify the mapping, he kept referring to the figures in the Nokia document—treating the labeled variables as the source signals—without providing a *clean mapping* between the diagram’s placeholders (x 1, x 2) and the patent’s claim elements (signals of interest, interference‑generating signals).  

These statements were “confusing, unclear, and without a clear or detailed explanation” (J.A. 15010‑19).  The court noted:

> *“it is not sufficient to support the jury verdict. . . . we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard”* (J.A. 19‑20, citing Johs H. Univ. v. Datascope, 543 F.3d 1342).

### How the court applied the standard

1. **Light most favorable to the verdict** – the court treated Wells’ initial testimony (that the RF ADC *does* sample both signals) as the most favorable evidence.  
2. **Was that evidence sufficiently reliable?** – The statements were internally contradictory.  As the court pointed out, a self‑contradictory testimony undermines reliability (see *Jacob’s principle* in *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope*).  
3. **Did the evidence point strongly enough?** – Because no other expert (e.g., a defendant‑made expert) offered a clear, consistent alternative, and the plaintiff’s own evidence rested on that shaky testimony, the court found **“no substantial evidence”** supporting infringement.  Therefore, the denial of JMOL was reversed.

---

## 3. General principles that courts must apply

| Principle | What it requires | How the *Finesse* court applied it |
|-----------|------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Reliable expert opinion** (Rule 702) | The opinion must be based on skill, knowledge, training, **or experience** and a *reasonable* method for applying that knowledge.  The method must be *reproducible* and *testable*. | Wells’ contradictory statements violated the *method* requirement (e.g., “two signals enter the RF‑ADC” **and** “the RF‑ADC does not sample the modeled‑PIM path”).  The court treated the testimony as *unreliable*. |
| **Substantial evidence** | A reasonable jury could find the plaintiff’s claim based on the evidence presented. | The jury’s sole support for infringement was Wells’ testimony.  Because that testimony was contradictory, the court concluded no reasonable jury could find infringement. |
| **Light most favorable to the verdict** | The court reviews *all* evidence *as if* it were unequivocally in favor of the verdict, then asks if it would still be clear and unambiguous. | The court imagined Wells’ testimony uncontradicted; still, the evidence was insufficient because other critical facts (e.g., whether the modeled‑PIM path could be sampled) were impossible. |

---

## 4. Bottom line for a law student

* **When an expert’s testimony contradicts itself, the court may consider it unreliable** and the evidence *insufficient* to support a verdict.  
* Under the Fifth Circuit (and, by extension, under APLM in many other circuits), a judge can grant JMOL if, after giving the jury the most favorable interpretation of the evidence, the evidence *does not effectively support* a clear outcome.  
* The court must show that **no reasonable jury, in the light most favorable to the verdict, could have reached a contradictory or different conclusion**.  

In *Finesse*, that was precisely what the court found when Dr. Wells’ contradictory statements left the jury with no rational basis to conclude that the accused radios met the “interference‑generating signals” limitation.  The judge therefore reversed the denial of JMOL and vacated the damages award.

### Why did the court find the accused radios did not perform the seven claimed multiplications in the ‘775’ patent, and what evidence is necessary to show a design meets such a multiplication requirement?
Question:
The opinion says the Nokia document lists only three distinct multiplications and does not show all seven claimed calculations. How does the court determine that the accused radio does not meet the seven‑multiplication requirement? Is it enough that the data sheet or internal document only shows fewer multiplications, or does the court need direct evidence that each of the seven is performed? What kind of expert testimony or engineering documentation would satisfy the court’s standard?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The court found that the accused radios *did not* perform the seven third‑order multiplications required by claim 1 of the ’775 patent because the only technical evidence presented—Nokia’s internal documentation—lists only three distinct multiplication expressions and provides no proof that the other four claimed operations are actually carried out.  A data sheet or internal document that merely shows fewer multiplications is insufficient; the jury must be able to see concrete, verifiable evidence that every one of the seven multiplications is performed.  To meet the court’s standard, an expert must map *each* of the seven claim‑limited multiplications to a documented operation (block‑diagrams, firmware code, simulation data, or test results) and show that the design truly executes them in its receiver path.

---

### 1.  Why the court rolled the infringement verdict

| Step | Evidence examined | Court’s finding |
|------|------------------|-----------------|
| **Claim construction** | The court construed the “three signals S1, S2 and S3” limitation as “separately identifiable” but not “unique.” |  The design may use two unique signals, but it must still carry out seven distinct third‑order multiplications. |
| **Nokia internal doc** | The file shows three distinct multiplications:<br>• x₁·|x₁|·|x₁|  <br>• x₁·x₁·x₂′  <br>• x₁·|x₂|·|x₂|  |  **J.A. 26486** – the document “does not show the accused radios performing the seven claimed multiplications.” |
| **Expert testimony (Wells)** | Wells testified that the accused radios *perform* the seven claimed multiplications, relying on the Nokia doc.  He did **not** explain how the three listed multiplications map onto the seven claimed operations, nor did he provide any evidence of the remaining four calculations. |  **J.A. 14966‑70** – the court concluded that “no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.” |
| **Trial‑court analysis** | The design can generate a copy of one input (x₂′), but that does not automatically provide the fourth and fifth claim‑limited multiplications. |  The court found the evidence “insufficient to support infringement under the ‘substantial evidence’ standard.” |

The court’s verdict was reversed on the basis that, even assuming the device used the two unique signals x₁ and x₂, there was **no direct evidence that all seven multiplications (S₁×S₁×S₂, S₁×S₂×S₂, S₁×S₂×S₃, S₁×S₁×S₃, S₂×S₂×S₃, S₁×S₃×S₃, S₂×S₃×S₃) were performed**.

---

### 2.  “Data sheet or internal doc shows fewer multiplications” is not enough

1. **Claim limitation requires performance** – Each multiplication operation in the claim is an essential limiting step.  Merely *potential* or *planned* multiplications in a design plan do not satisfy the claim.

2. **Evidence must be “direct”** – The court (citing Wi‑LAN) holds that to establish infringement the plaintiff must present evidence that *directly* shows the accused device performs the claimed steps.  A document that lists only three of the seven multiplications does not provide that direct evidence.

3. **Mapping is required** – A more complete document that lists ten multiplications (as the latter part of the Nokia doc did) would still not suffice if the plaintiff does not *map* ten to seven or explain which of the ten correspond.  The court stresses that **“the expert did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications.”**  (J.A. 14966‑70)

Thus, an internal doc that simply numbers fewer multiplications does not meet the court’s "substantial evidence" standard.

---

### 3.  What evidence would satisfy the court

| Type of evidence | What the court looks for | How it should be presented |
|------------------|--------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Block‑diagram of the MCU/Fabric logic** | Explicit nodes labeled with the seven multiplication operations, showing the flow of S1, S2, and S3 to each node. | Clear, labeled diagrams that match the claimed expressions. |
| **Firmware / HDL code excerpts** | Code that implements each of the seven multiplications, including comments that map code variables to S1, S2, S3. | Source or disassembled snippets with a “trace” that shows each multiplication is computed. |
| **Simulation or test‑bench results** | Numerical results that confirm the output of each multiplication operation within the receiver’s timing budget. | Plots or logs with timestamps, labels, and a clear link to the claim’s multiplications. |
| **Expert testimony mapping each operation** | An expert explains *without ambiguity* how the design’s operations satisfy the claim’s multiplication limiting step. | Testimony should, for each of the seven operations, identify the signals involved, describe the exact multiplication performed, and demonstrate that the operation indeed occurs in the device. |
| **Vendor documentation that explicitly claims the operations** | A vendor‑provided “implementation guide” stating “the PIM‑C block performs all seven third‑order multiplications.” | Copying the vendor’s own words, but the vendor must provide evidence the device actually implements the claims (e.g., a “Program File” that contains the multiplications). |

**Key point:** The proof must *demonstrate* that every one of the seven multiplications is **performed, not merely documented**.  If the data sheet contains only a subset, it must be supplemented by evidence that the missing multiplications are carried out—otherwise the court will rule the evidence “insufficient” because a reasonable jury could not find infringement.  (See court’s reference to “no reasonable jury could have found... the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.” J.A. 26486).

---

### 4.  Bottom line for a law student

- **Infringement predicates on the accused device actually performing the claim‑limiting steps.**  
- **A data sheet or design memo that lists fewer steps is not enough;** the prosecution must show *direct, verifiable execution* of each required multiplication.  
- **An expert’s testimony must explicitly map each claim step to a documented operation** in the device’s architecture and provide supporting technical evidence.  
- **Failure to do so will trigger a JMOL of infringement**, as the court applied in this case.

If you’re drafting a plaintiff’s case, make sure your expert can **walk the jury through the seven multiplications, using concrete engineering artifacts**; otherwise the evidence will be deemed “insufficient to support a finding of infringement.”

### What is the practical application of the ‘substantial evidence’ standard in infringement findings, and how does it differ from the preponderance standard?
Question:
The opinion states that the jury’s infringement verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. I’m used to the preponderance-of‑evidence standard in civil cases – how is substantial evidence different? Does the court require a higher burden from the plaintiff, or does it simply look for a stronger factual basis for the claim? How does the ‘substantial evidence’ test interact with the evidence presented by expert witnesses?
Answer:
**Q: In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* the Fifth Circuit says the jury’s infringement verdict was not “supported by substantial evidence.”  
What does the “substantial evidence” standard really mean, how does it differ from the normal preponderance standard, and how is it applied when plaintiffs rely on expert witnesses?**

---

### 1. Where the standard comes from

The Fifth Circuit applies a **de novo** standard of *substantial evidence* when it reviews a jury’s factual findings in patent‐infringement cases.  
The opinion cites *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022):

> “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a *reasonable jury* could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.”  
>  (cf. *Apple* – internal citations omitted.)

This is the test the court uses to decide whether a jury verdict can stand.

---

### 2. How it differs from the preponderance standard

| Aspect | Preponderance (trial burden) | Substantial evidence (appellate review) |
|--------|------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Who sets it?** | The trial court, under the general burden of proof in civil cases. | The appellate courts (in the Fifth Circuit). |
| **Whose standard?** | The proving party (plaintiff in infringement cases). | The appellate court, evaluating the *juror’s* verdict. |
| **Threshold** | “More likely than not” – a little over 50 % probability. | “A reasonable jury could have concluded it.” – effectively **higher** than preponderance but less than clear‑and‑convincing. |
| **Purpose** | To decide whether the evidence is enough for the party to prove its case. | To determine whether the **jury’s factual basis** is sufficient for a *reasonable* juror to reach the same conclusion. |
| **Effect on the parties** | The plaintiff carries the usual burden at trial. | No shift in the plaintiff’s burden; but the appellate court can reverse a verdict that fails the *substantial evidence* test. |

So, while the plaintiff still has a preponderance burden at trial, the appellate court looks for a *stronger factual foundation*—enough that a *reasonable jury* could have found infringement.

---

### 3. Interaction with expert testimony

The court’s “substantial evidence” review is **content‑based**; it examines the *totality* of the evidence that the jury considered, including expert affidavits and testimony.

In *Finesse* the court found Dr. Wells’ evidence to be “confusing and contradictory” (J.A. 15010‑19; J.A. 10‑11, 19‑22).  The reasoning:

1. **Mapping to claim limitations** – Dr. Wells was required to show that the radio’s RF ADC sampled *both* “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (claim 1[ a]).  
   *The court concluded he never mapped the two input signals (x1 and x2) to the claim‑required signals.*  J.A. 10‑11, 19‑22.

2. **Consistency** – His testimony shifted from “a single RF ADC input” to “two separate signals,” then back, with no clear reconciliation.  
   *The jury could not have inferred from this testimony that the limitation was met.*  J.A. 15010‑19.

3. **Overall sufficiency** – Because these contradictions undermine the factual matrix the jury needed, the evidence was deemed not “substantial.”  (Hence the reversal on JMOL.)  

The court did not merely apply a “weight‑of‑expert” standard; it asked whether the **combination** of expert opinions, documents, and other evidence provided a factual basis a *reasonable jury* could accept.  Contradictory or vague expert testimony fails that test.

---

### 4. Bottom line for practitioners

- **Preponderance** remains the proving‑party burden at trial.  
- **Substantial evidence** is a *higher* appellate bar that judges use to decide whether a jury’s infringement verdict stands.  
- Under that bar, the **quality** of expert evidence matters more than its mere weight. Clear, consistent, and credibly mapped testimony is required; contradictions or lack of mapping can invalidate a verdict even if the evidence may have been “preponderant.”  

In *Finesse*, the jury’s verdict was reversed because, after a de novo review, the appellate court found that the evidence—including the expert testimony—did **not** provide a **reasonable juror** with a factual basis for infringement. The standard therefore functions as an additional filter over the preponderance burden, ensuring that only verdicts grounded in a substantive and coherent evidentiary record survive appellate scrutiny.

### How does the Fifth Circuit apply an abuse‑of‑discretion standard when reviewing a district court’s denial of a new trial on damages in a patent case?
Question:
The opinion mentions that the court reviewed the denial of a new trial on damages for abuse of discretion. I’m not clear on what that standard looks like—what does the appellate court examine? Must the district court have made a clear error, or is any misstep enough? Does this standard apply the same way when damages are decided by a judge versus a jury?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
In *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility*, the Fifth Circuit treats a district court’s denial of a new trial on damages the same way it treats any other denial of a new trial: the appellate court applies an **abuse‑of‑discretion** review.  The court simply asks whether the denial was “reasonable” and “not arbitrary or capricious.”  If the district court’s decision is supported by the evidence and the law, no reversal will occur.  A “misstep” does not in itself trigger the standard; the misstep must be serious enough to make the decision unreasonable.  The same standard applies whether the damages were awarded by a judge or by a jury, because the appellate court reviews the trial‑court decision, not the process that produced it.

---

### 1.  The Fifth Circuit’s standard expressed in the opinion  

> *“We review a district court’s denial of a new trial under regional circuit law.  Wi‑LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016).  The Fifth Circuit reviews a denial of a new trial for abuse of discretion.”*  

The **abuse‑of‑discretion** test is de‑facto the same as the Fifth Circuit’s review of other new‑trial denials.  The appellate court grants the trial court “wide latitude” so long as its ruling is **reasonably grounded** in the evidence.  A denial is reversible only if the trial court’s decision is **clearly erroneous, arbitrary, or unsupported by the record** (cf. *Wi‑LAN*, 811 F.3d at 461; *Finesse*, 2025‑1039).

### 2.  How the appellate court applies the test  

The court examines the record for:

| What the appellate court looks for | Why it matters |
|-----------------------------------|----------------|
| **Evidence supporting the denial** | The court must have a rational basis. |
| **Misapplication of the law** | If the court applies the wrong statute or case law, the denial is likely abused. |
| **Failure to consider material evidence** | Ignoring evidence that would favor a new trial signals an abuse. |
| **Unreasonable weighting of evidence** | A decision that assigns disproportionate weight to unreliable testimony is reviewable. |

If any of these issues exist, the appellate court asks whether the denial was **“reasonable”**.  The standard is *deferential*; minor or isolated errors do **not** rise to abuse.  Only an error that leads the court to make a decision that a reasonable court could not have made is reversible.

### 3.  Misstep vs. clear error  

The opinion does not use a “clear‑error” bar, but it implicitly requires the misstep to be **gross** enough to render the denial unreasonable.  It is not sufficient that the trial court made an arbitrary correction or that two experts disagree.  The appellate court must be satisfied that the denial was **clearly wrong** in view of the evidence.  Thus:

* **Any minor slip** (e.g., a lapsus in a witness’s recollection) does **not** trigger reversal.  
* **A substantive legal mistake** (e.g., applying the wrong damages standard) **does** constitute abuse of discretion.

### 4.  Judge‑decided vs. jury‑decided damages  

The Five‑Circuit law treated damages the same way it treats any other factual finding.  The opinion says only that the denial is subject to an abuse‑of‑discretion review; it does not distinguish between a judge or a jury.  Consequently, the appellate court reviews:

* The **district judge’s findings of damages** (most common)  
* Or the **jury’s verdict on damages** (less common but equally subject to the same standard)

The key figure remains the same: is the denial supported by the evidence, or is it an unreasonable outcome?

### 5.  Bottom line for your case  

Because *Finesse* vacated the damages award after finding the district court’s refusal to grant a new trial was an abuse of discretion, you can see that the Fifth Circuit will be willing to reverse such a denial **only** when the denial is demonstrably unreasonable or unsupported by the record.  A mere “misstep” without an underlying unreasonable outcome will not suffice.

---

**Citation**  
*Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2025‑1039, 2025 Fed. Cir. 52‑73 (Moore, C.J.), at *II‑Damages* (review of denial of a new trial under an abuse‑of‑discretion standard).  The standard is set out in *Wi‑LAN, Inc.* 811 F.3d 455, 461.

### What is the difference between internal and external passive intermodulation (PIM) in the context of the ‘134’ patent, and why is that distinction significant?
Question:
The background section explains that internal PIM stems from inside the radio and external PIM comes from outside sources. I don’t understand how that distinction affects the infringement analysis. Does it change what signals must be sampled, or what claim elements are required? Why does the court focus on the ADC sampling both the signal of interest and the interference‑generating signals?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility* the ‘134 patent was read as a method that

> “over‑samples, at a desired frequency, a *passband of received signals* to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include **signals of interest** and **interference‑generating signals** … to isolate those two groups … and cancel the interference‑generating signals” (see *§ 134*, claim 1, ¶ a‑e, J.A. 1–6).

The court’s discussion of *internal* versus *external* passive intermodulation (PIM) is found in the background (J.A. 15223–24):

> *“Internal PIM is caused by mechanical issues inside the radio, while external PIM is caused by sources outside the radio.”* (J.A. 15223–24)

The key to infringement is whether the **RF ADC** actually samples a passband that contains **both** the signals of interest **and** the interference‑generating signals. The ‘134’ claim’s first limitation therefore requires that the interference‑generating signals be *received* and *sampled* by the ADC (J.A. 1–6).  

---

### 1. Internal vs. External PIM

| Feature | Internal PIM | External PIM |
|---------|--------------|--------------|
| **Origin** | Mechanical defects *inside* the radio (e.g., loose connectors, faulty antenna brackets) | Passive obstacles *outside* the radio (e.g., metal fences, cable runs nearby) |
| **When does it form?** | When the receiver’s internal signals mix inside the circuitry | When the receiver’s transmitted signals reflect and mix externally before reaching the receiver |
| **Sampling by the ADC?** | Yes – the interference‑generating path is part of the RF signal that enters the ADC, so it can be isolated, digitized, and canceled (J.A. 15223‑24) | No – the interference is generated *after* the RF stage; the ADC never sees it as an input signal. |

Because the claim’s language uses *“received signals”* and *“interference‑generating signals”*, the court naturally treated *internal PIM* as the relevant interference source: the interference is already part of the tunable RX *passband* that the ADC is mandated to sample (see the court’s reliance on Dr. Wells’ testimony that the RF ADC “samples the *modeled PIM path*” – see J.A. 14914–21, J.A. 15010–11).

---

### 2. Why the distinction matters for infringement

1. **Requirement of the first limitation**  
   The claim explicitly requires the device to *over‑sample* a passband that **includes** both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals (J.A. 1–6). If the interference‑generating signals are *internal*, they can be present in that passband and thus satisfy the limitation; if they are *external*, they are not present in the passband that the ADC samples, and the limitation is not met.  

2. **ADC as the point of capture**  
   The court focused on the ADC because it is the receiver component that actually *samples* the passband. The question is whether the *modeled PIM path*—representing the internal interference—was part of that sample. The court ultimately found that “the RF ADC in the accused radios samples both the *signals of interest* and *interference‑generating signals*” (J.A. 7–16, 19–22), and this evidence was deemed sufficient to sustain the jury verdict. This would not have been the case had the interference have been *external* and thus not captured by the ADC.

3. **Interpretation of “interference‑generating signals”**  
   Internal PIM originates *inside* the path that is being over‑sampled, meaning the *interference‑generating signals* are literally part of the signals the ADC receives. External PIM, being an after‑effect of environmental scattering, does not arise *inside* that same path; consequently, labeling external PIM signals as “interference‑generating signals” that the ADC samples would be inconsistent with the claim’s dependence on an *actual* sample.  

---

### 3. Bottom line

The distinction is significant because *only internal PIM can satisfy the claim’s first limitation on the grounds that the interference‑generating signals must be sampled by the RF ADC*. The court thus prioritized the ADC sampling evidence when deciding infringement: a device that samples both signals of interest and the internal PIM path can perform the isolation, estimation, and cancellation steps recited by the claims; a device that does not sample the interference‑generating signals (because they are external) would fail to meet that limitation.

In *Finesse*, the jury’s verdict was based on the (albeit disputed) finding that the accused radios’ RF ADC indeed sampled the internal interference‑generating signals. The court affirmed that the distinction between internal and external PIM is pivotal to the infringement analysis because it determines whether the device actually “samples” the interference‑generating signals required by the claims.

### How does the remaining life of a patent factor into lump‑sum damages, and does a new trial on damages require a new infringement trial?
Question:
The jury awarded $166,303,391 in lump‑sum damages based on the remaining life of the patents. I’m unsure how the court connects patent life to damages. Also, if the appellate court vacates the damages award and orders a new trial on damages, must the infringement verdict also be revisited, or can damages be recalculated based on the same finding of infringement?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The jury’s lump‑sum award simply multiplied an estimated annual‐value of the infringement (most often a “reasonable royalty” or cost‑based figure) by the number of full years the patents were still in force when the infringement was found.  The District Court therefore treated the “remaining life” of each patent as a multiplier in the calculation of damages, which is the standard way courts apply § 284 when a patent has not yet expired.  Because the appellate court reversed the district court’s denial of a judgment‑as‑law‑of‑moral‑right (JMOL) of non‑infringement, the underlying infringement finding was set aside.  Damages cannot be awarded absent an infringement finding, so the appellate court vacated the entire damages award.  A new trial on damages can be held without a separate infringement trial **only if the infringement finding is still valid**; where that finding has been vacated (as here), a new infringement determination must be made first—either in the same combined trial or in a new trial on infringement and damages together.

---

### 1.  How the “remaining life” of a patent is used in a lump‑sum damages award

The jury’s award of $166,303,391 was expressly “for the remaining life of the ’134 and ’775 patents” (J.A. 169–71).  In a § 284 damages calculation the court typically estimates the amount of damages that would accrue for each year of the patent’s remaining term and then multiplies that annual figure by the number of years until the patent expires.  The “remaining life” therefore serves as the time‑period over which the estimated annual royalty or loss‑profit figure is applied.

This methodology is the one most commonly used in post‑Grant infringement cases—because § 284 requires damages to equal the “amount of damages that would be imposed if the infringement had continued until the patent expired or was otherwise terminated” (see, e.g., the statutory text, which the court applied implicitly).  The opinion does not spell out the precise arithmetic used in the calculation, but it confirms that the jury deliberately tied the lump‑sum award to the pending expiration dates of the two patents.

---

### 2.  Whether a new trial on damages requires a new infringement trial

The appellate court’s order reads:

> “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.” (…).  The reversal of the denial of JMOL means the court has set aside the jury’s finding that the patents were infringed.

Damages are proportional to the infringement – they can be calculated only after an infringement finding survives.  As the appellate court has **reversed** that finding, the entire damages award is vacated, not merely recalculated.  The court therefore does **not** simply allow the district court to re‑calculate damages using the same infringement verdict.

If the parties seek a *new trial on damages*, that trial must rest on a finding of infringement that is valid for that trial.  In practice this means the district court will likely remand for a new infringement trial (or for a combined trial in which infringement and damages are re‑determined together).  A new damages trial that relies on the earlier infringement verdict would be impossible because that verdict has been set aside by the appellate court.

In other words:

| Situation | Infringement finding? | Damages trial possible? |
|-----------|-----------------------|------------------------|
| Infringement still valid (not vacated) | Yes | Yes – new damages can be calculated on that same finding |
| Infringement vacated | No | No – damages cannot be determined without a fresh infringement finding |

### Bottom line

- The “remaining life” of the patents was used as a multiplier in the court’s lump‑sum damages calculation.  
- Because the appellate court set aside the infringement finding, the damages award is vacated.  
- To award damages again, the court must first re‑establish infringement—either in the same trial or in a separate trial—before any new damages can be imposed.

This is exactly what the appellate order indicates: the damages award was vacated solely because the infringement finding was reversed.  Consequently, any new damages trial must also involve a new infringement determination.
