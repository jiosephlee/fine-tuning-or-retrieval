Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Inside the Frequency Maze: Internal vs. External PIM and the Anatomy of Interference Claims

Punctured by a single mistake, an entire patent litigation can unravel.  In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* the Federal Circuit set out to untangle a knot built of high‑frequency troubleshooting, patent claim construction, and the thin line between a claim’s “signals of interest” and the “interference‑generating signals.”  The decision is more than a footnote in the history of PIM technology; it is a textbook illustration of how the precise language of a claim, the testimony of an expert, and the procedural posture of a lawsuit combine to determine whether a jury verdict should stand.

## Why Internal vs. External PIM Matters

Passive intermodulation (PIM) is not a trivial software glitch—it is a fundamental physics problem that can cause a smartphone or base‑station radio to drown in the very signals it is meant to hear.  When two sufficiently strong signals coexist on the same frequency band, their interaction in a passive component (copper wire, solder joint, or an antenna feed line) can produce new spurious tones called intermodulation products (IMPs).  If those IMPs fall within the useful band, they can degrade reception or even cause severe interference.  The *Adding‑intermodulation* problem is why makers of mobile handsets and base‑stations invest heavily in PIM‑cancellation techniques.

When a device’s own receive path can generate PIM—what the Federal Circuit calls “internal PIM”—the remedy differs from dealing with “external PIM,” generated outside the radio.  Internal PIM is a property of the device’s own hardware and firmware, whereas external PIM is caused by the environment in which the device operates.  The distinction mattered in *Finesse* because the defendants claimed that the patented PIM‑cancellation methods were performed either before or after the radio’s analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC) had sampled the incoming signal.  Whether the claimed devices had actually sampled a passband of signals that included both the “signals of interest” and the “interference‑generating signals” hinged on whether the PIM‑generating phenomena were internal (generated inside the device) or external (generated elsewhere).

## Claim Construction: The Heart of the Decision

The crux of the case rested on the Fifth Circuit's standard for judging a motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL).  The court reiterated that it reviews such motions de novo, asking whether, when the evidence is viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, it points so strongly and overwhelmingly toward one party that a reasonable jury could not have arrived at anything else.  In the *Finesse* district‑court trial, the jury found every claim of the two patents infringed and awarded $166 million in damages.  The Federal Circuit reversed that verdict by finding that the evidence presented failed to meet that stringent threshold of “substantial evidence” for two separate sets of claims.

### For the ’134 Patent

Claim 1 of the ’134 patent is centered around a method of separating the “signals of interest” from the “interference‑generating signals” before the RF ADC samples them, then generating an estimate of each in‑band IMP and canceling it.  The central limitation of “over‑sampling… to create a bit stream” required that the receiver’s ADC actually sample the full band—including both the desired signal and whatever has the potential to produce a PIM within that band.

Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, told the jury that the Nokia technical documents showed the accused radios’ RF ADC sampling an “interference‑generating” path that was modeled *after* the ADC had already finished sampling the signal.  When the cross‑examining counsel pointed out that a modeled path could not be “generated” before the ADC sampled, Wells pivoted, insisting that “the two signals on the red path” were the needed “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.”  The court was not convinced that his shifting testimony sufficed.  It found that he essentially asserted the modeled PIM path was an “interference‑generating signal” **while, in fact, the path was constructed only after sampling**—a clear contradiction that undermined the evidence standard.  The court therefore reversed the JMOL denial, vacating the damages that had been awarded for the ’134 claims.

### For the ’775 Patent

The ’775 patent’s hurdle was even sharper.  A claim limitation required the accused receiver to produce four complex multipliers—seven third‑order multiplications—based on *three* separately identifiable signals.  The defense argued that the accused radios used only two distinct transmitter signals (denoted x1 and x2) and a copy of one of them, which would not satisfy the "three separate" requirement.  The jury, persuaded otherwise, found infringement. 

Finesse relied on a Nokia technical document that listed only three distinct multiplications involving the two signals and their conjugates and magnitudes: x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2′, and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|.  The court held that these three multiplications were **not** the seven claimed ones.  Because the Nokia document did not disclose any mapping from the two physical signals into *seven* distinct third‑order multiplications, no reasonable jury could find the limit satisfied.  The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL, again vacating the damages award for the ’775 claims.

## Dr. Wells’s Testimony: A Case Study in Expert Ambiguity

At each step, the court confronted an expert whose testimony wavered between assertion and technical uncertainty.  In the ’134 case, Wells spoke of an “interference‑generating” path that, by definition, could not be captured by the ADC because it did not exist at the time of sampling.  His insistence on a PIM‑model path in a receiver that, in theory, was not part of the sampled mix illustrated the danger of translating engineering diagrams into legal arguments without a disciplined mapping to the claim elements.

In the ’775 case, Wells again floundered.  He strung together a story that sprinkled the required number of multiplications across a document that only gave three, misreading the mathematical expressions—especially complex‑conjugate operations—as equivalent to the claimed seventh multiplication.  The court concluded that the evidence was too thin to support the jury’s verdict.

Both episodes reinforce the principle that patent litigation is not a science experiment on its own terms; it is a legal narrative that must survive the judge’s scrutiny of credibility, consistency, and relevance to the claim language.

## Drafting Lessons: What Patent Attorneys Picked Up

1. **Define the “Signals of Interest” and “Interference‑Generating Signals” by Concrete Microarchitecture**  
   The claims here dragged the court into a debate over whether a modeled, post‑ADC path could be treated as an “interference‐generating signal.”  Precise claim language that ties “interference‑generating” to physically present path segments that are *present* at the time of sampling—and that uses operative verbs that do not ambiguously allow imbedded post‑processing—helps avoid courts interpreting the elements on unintended fodder.

2. **Be Careful With “Three Separate Signals”**  
   The court sent a clear message: a claim that requires “three separately identifiable signals” does not automatically imply the same physical signal cannot serve more than one role.  However, it does preclude a lone signal.  Literal claim drafting and enumeration of the scenarios where a single signal may be replicated or recombined must adhere to the spirit of “separate.”

3. **Avoid Ambiguous Cross‑Examining Techniques**  
   Assertions that a given path is “generated” after a critical action may be a red flag, especially if that action is a transducer (like an ADC).  Expert testimony should include verifiable timing diagrams and a clarity that won't be thrown out as contradictory.

4. **Amenditions as “Surrender”?**  
   The court concluded that the amendment to phrase the claim as “digitally multiplying three signals” did not razor‑edge the scope away from two signals.  The court explicitly noted the amendment’s lack of “clear and unambiguous” disavowal.  When you amend claims to overcome prior‑art rejections, a more thorough disclosure—perhaps an illustrative block diagram—could demonstrate to a court the exact range of admissible technical configurations.

5. **Document Assurance & Expert Isolation**  
   The appellate court noted that Finesse never presented to the jury a mapping from the Nokia ILR (illustrated logical representation) to the seven multiplications.  This highlights the importance of ensuring any expert mapping is available for the jury and not merely revealed to the panel during appellate brief preparation.

## Doctrinal and Practical Implications

*Finesse* adds to the Fifth Circuit’s body of work regarding substantial evidence and the judicial interpretation of “internal” vs. “external” PIM.  It reinforces that:

- **Claim construction depends not just on the words but on the *implementation*** of the claimed method. “Substantially similar” injection of signals must be physically plausible, not merely mathematically modeled within the software.  
- **Expert testimony that shifts from one technical concept to another without transparency is unreliable**.  Authors of technical patents can no longer rely on the doctrine of “best engineering practice” as a crutch; each claim element must be demonstrable.  
- **JMOL is a high bar**.  Unlike a negative, a motion for JMOL requires overwhelming evidence.  A jury verdict that can be undone by a single technical misinterpretation or gap in the evidence will flush at appellate review.  
- **Litigation over PIM may give patent attorneys a new battleground**.  Plaintiffs must provide unambiguous mapping between circuit diagrams and the claim elements that need to be proven; defendants, in turn, can use expert testimony to challenge such mapping.

On the technical front, the case signals that internal PIM mitigation can be patent‑protected, but proving infringement requires careful segregation of *observed* path elements and their required timing order.  The distinction between “interference‑generating signals” that exist pre‑ADC versus modeled after-sampling is not merely semantic; it can hinge the outcome of a starry‑sphere suit.

## Conclusion

The fine print of a patent claim is more than legal jargon; it is a map that translates the labyrinthine engineering of a radio into a jurisdictional boundary.  *Finesse* reminds us that any attempt to skirt this boundary—by making ambiguous promises of "separate signals" or implying post‑sampling modeled paths—generates a room for the court to snatch down.  The dollar‑staggering damages award lost to the reversal indicates the stakes are high: the quality of claim construction, the reliability of expert testimony, and the coherence of the technical story can decide between a win and a costly reversal.

For the practitioner, the lesson is simple: draft with explicitness, shepherd your expert’s testimony through the rigors of evidence, and remember that the court will not accept an engineering theory that cannot be consistently mapped to the claim language under scrutiny.  Whether you’re protecting an internal PIM cancellation algorithm or dissecting external interference, the intersection of law and physics remains a place where precision is indispensable.
