Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Parties, Patents, and Trial Facts

The first step in understanding the appellate opinion is to lay out the parties involved, the patents at issue, the alleged infringement, and the outcome of the trial that prompted a federal‑circuit review.

## Parties

Plaintiff in this action is **Finesse Wireless LLC** (“Finesse”). The defendants are **AT&T Mobility LLC** (“AT&T”) and **Nokia of America Corporation** (“Nokia”). Nokia intervened in the litigation after the defendant coalition had decided to sue, following the traditional practice that an alternate manufacturer that supplies the same kind of device may have a legitimate interest in the case. The suit ultimately centered on the alleged use of Nokia’s product line in AT&T’s wireless service.

## Underlying Patents

The patents under dispute are:

* **U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134** (“the ’134”) – a method of mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio receivers by separating signals of interest from interference‑generating signals and cancelling the resulting intermodulation products.  
* **U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775** (“the ’775”) – a method for reducing IMP interference by digitally multiplying three signals in a precise set of seven multiplications that generate cancellation signals for passive intermodulation products.

Both patents address the same technical problem – the unwanted mixing of received radio signals that can produce new, out‑of‑band frequency components called “intermodulation products” (IMPs). In the context of mobile radio, these products are often caused by passive obstacles in the transmission environment, and the patents claim technical solutions to null those unwanted signals.

## Allegations

Finesse sued AT&T for infringement of **claims 1–3** of the ’134 patent and **claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36** of the ’775 patent. The allegations focused on AT&T’s use of Nokia‑manufactured phones that incorporated a “PIM‑cancellation” feature described in a Nokia technical documentation set. Finesse asserted that the Nokia phones performed the patented methods and therefore infringed the above‑named claims when supplied by AT&T to its customers.

## Trial Verdict

The case proceeded to trial at the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas. A jury was empaneled and found that:  
* All the claims pleaded by Finesse were **valid**; and  
* Each of those claims was **infringed** by the Nokia phones sold by AT&T.

Following the infringement verdict, the jury awarded **$166,303,391** in lump‑sum damages calculated for the remaining life of the two patents. That award, like the verdict, was the last order of the district court and thus gave rise to the appellate action.

## Appellants’ Motions

After the jury’s judgment, AT&T and Nokia – collectively referred to in the opinion as “Appellants” – filed motions under Rule 50(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. The motions sought:

1. **Judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement** for each of the asserted claims, challenging the sufficiency of the evidentiary basis for the jury’s infringement conclusion.  
2. **A new trial on damages**, arguing that the amount of liquidated damages was not supported by the evidence presented at trial.

The district court rejected both motions, allowing the infringement verdict and the damages award to stand. The defendants therefore appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.

## Jurisdiction

The appellate case is certified to the Federal Circuit under **28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)**, which allows a party to seek appellate review of the final judgment of a United States district court. Consequently, the Federal Circuit’s review of the district court’s denial of JMOL and new‑trial motions is jurisdictionally appropriate, and the parties move forward with an examination of the legal and factual bases that undergird the court’s decision.

This foundational framework—plaintiff, defendants, patents, allegations, trial outcome, and appellate posture—sets the stage for the deeper analysis that follows in subsequent chapters of this textbook.

# Legal Standards Governing JMOL, Substantial Evidence, and Claim Construction

The appellate process in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* illustrates how a Fifth Circuit court applies its procedural and substantive standards when reviewing (1) a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), (2) a jury’s factual findings of infringement, (3) a claim‑construction hearing, and (4) a denial of a new trial on damages. The court’s analysis—drawn from *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.* and *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN, Inc.*—shows the interplay among claim construction, infringement determination, and damages calculation.

## Fifth Circuit JMOL Standard

The Fifth Circuit tests denials of JMOL de novo. As held in *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, the appellate court examines the evidence “in the light most favorable to the verdict” and may reverse the district court only if the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that **reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion**. This standard places a high evidentiary threshold on the material that the district court used to reach its judgment and protects the jury’s fact‑finding function. In *Finesse*, the district court denied JMOL of noninfringement for the ’134 and ’775 patents; the court now must decide whether the evidence—chiefly the expert testimony of Dr. Wells and the Nokia technical documents—meet this “strong and overwhelming” standard.

## Substantial Evidence Standard for Infringement

When a jury finds infringement, the appellate court applies the substantial‑evidence standard, derived in *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN, Inc.* The test asks whether a **reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial**. If the evidence is adequate to support the verdict, the appellate court will not reverse; if the record contains only a slim or ambiguous basis for the jury’s conclusion, the court may rule that no reasonable jury could have reached that verdict. In *Finesse*, the appellate court scrutinized Dr. Wells’s testimony that the accused Nokia radios “sample a passband of signals that include both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals” and the claim that the radios perform the requisite seven multiplications. The court concluded the evidence was insufficient to sustain the jury’s infringement ruling, thus reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL.

## Claim Construction: De Novo “Clear and Unambiguous” Review

Claim construction is a separate, de novo proceeding. The Fifth Circuit adopts a **“clear and unambiguous” standard**: the claims and specification are interpreted to provide a determinate sense of scope. Extrinsic evidence (expert testimony, technical documents, industry usage) may be considered *only under the superimposition test* when it can be believed to reflect the inventor’s intent and is consistent with intrinsic evidence. In *Finesse*, the court reviewed the ’134 and ’775 patents, drawing on the claims, abstract, and specification, and considered Dr. Wells’s mapping of the “modeled PIM path” onto the interference‑generating signal and the Nokia documents that purported to show the radios performed the seven required multiplications. The court’s refusal to find clear evidence that the radios satisfied each claim limitation established a basis for JMOL.

## Damages Review: Abuse of Discretion Standard

A district court’s denial of a new trial on damages is reviewed for **abuse of discretion** under the controlled‑abuse framework the Fifth Circuit follows (*Wi‑LAN, Inc.*). The appellate court assesses whether the district court’s decision was arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to law or precedent. In *Finesse*, the district court denied a new trial on damages after finding infringement and awarding a lump‑sum award. Because the appellate court reversed the JMOL decisions, it found the district court’s denial of damages was premised on an erroneous infringement analysis and thus vacated the award.

## Interplay among Claim Construction, Infringement Analysis, and Damages

The procedural rules create a sequential relationship:  

1. **Claim construction** defines the protected subject matter.  
2. **Infringement analysis** determines whether the accused product falls within that scope.  
3. **Damages** are contingent upon an infringement finding.  

In *Finesse*, the appellate court’s reversal of the JMOL findings—grounded in the lack of substantial evidence and the failure to properly construct the claims—eliminates the substrate for the damages award. Consequently, the court vacated the $166.3 million lump‑sum award, emphasizing that damages cannot be realized in the absence of infringement. 

By applying the Fifth Circuit’s de novo JMOL standard, the substantial‑evidence review, the clear‑and‑unambiguous claim‑construction doctrine, and the abuse‑of‑discretion test for damages, the court systematically dismantled the district court’s findings and restored the integrity of the appellate review process.

# Analysis of the ’134 Patent Infringement and JMOL

## Structure of Claim 1 of the ’134 Patent

Claim 1 of the ’134 patent describes a method for mitigating intermodulation (IMP) interference in a receiver. The method is broken down into seven distinct steps: first, the radio over‑samples a passband of received signals that contains both *signals of interest* and *interference‑generating signals*; next, it isolates the signals of interest from the interference‑generating signals using decimating filters; then, the isolated interference‑generating signals are used to compute an estimate of each resultant IMP; the radio then cancels the in‑band IMPs by applying the estimated signals; and finally, it performs closed‑loop phase and amplitude adjustment on the estimates. The claim therefore hinges on a particular signal‑sampling architecture (the RF ADC) and on the successive isolation, estimation, and cancellation steps that follow. The appellant’s case centered on whether the accused Nokia radios’ RF ADC satisfied this architecture.

## Limitation 1[A] – The Need to Sample Both Signal Types

Limitation 1[a] explicitly requires that the RF analogue‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC) sample a *passband* in which *both* signals of interest and interference‑generating signals are present. In plain terms, the capture step must provide the raw data on which later stages can isolate, estimate, and cancel the IMPs. If a device’s RF ADC samples only one of those signal sets, or if the interference‑generating signals are not present at the sampling stage, then the device would not satisfy this limitation. The importance of this limitation was echoed by the district court when it noted that “the RF ADC must sample a passband of received signals that includes both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals” (J.A. 28:1‑6).

## Finesse’s Burden of Proof and the Reliance on Dr. Jonathan Wells

Because the plaintiff (Finesse) carried the burden of proving infringement, it was required to demonstrate that the accused radios sampled a passband containing both signal types. Finesse’s approach hinged on expert testimony by Dr. Jonathan Wells, a signal‑processing specialist. Dr. Wells relied heavily on a Nokia technical drawing (J.A. 26421) that illustrated the internal signal flow of the Nokia radios. He testified that the RF ADC, depicted in blue, captured both the downstream downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and a “modeled PIM path” (shown on a red path in the diagram). He claimed that these two signals corresponded directly to the *signals of interest* and the *interference‑generating signals* required by limitation 1[a] (J.A. 14918‑19, 15009). By analogy, Dr. Wells positioned the PIM model—an internal construct used to estimate the IMPs—as the interference‑generating signal that the RF ADC must capture.

## Dr. Wells’s Testimony on Signal Mapping

Dr. Wells’s testimony consistently exercised the mapping between the Nokia diagram and the claim limits:

1. The DL(TX) reference (a real transmit waveform) was mapped to the *signal of interest*.
2. The modeled PIM path—essentially a pre‑computed estimate of the interference contributions—was mapped to the *interference‑generating signal* (J.A. 14920‑21, 15009‑14).

He reiterated this mapping in his expert report, stating that “the DL(TX) reference signal … meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’ … and the modeled PIM signal is the ‘interference generating signal.’” (J.A. 4476‑77). The jury, persuaded by these statements, found that the limitation was satisfied and held that all the Plaintiffs’ claims infringed.

## Conflict in Testimony and the Misimpression Claim

During cross‑examination, Appellants highlighted a logical problem: the modeled PIM path is *created* within the radio’s digital logic after the RF ADC has performed its sampling exit. Because the RF ADC cannot sample a signal that does not yet exist, Dr. Wells’ mapping was mathematically impossible. Appellants asked whether the modeled PIM path was actually an “interference‑generating signal” in the sense required by the claim, and the trial court noted that the evidence “suggested that the modeled PIM path is generated in the GROOT FPGA” and that the recitation of the modeled PIM path as an interference‑generating signal could not satisfy limitation 1[a] (J.A. 19‑22).

Finesse argued that Dr. Wells was “labouring under the misimpression that x1 and x2 correspond to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path.” (Oral Arg. at 31:01‑32:20). The court, however, recognized that Dr. Wells had not provided an explicit mapping of the internal signals x1 and x2 (the physical wires into the RF ADC) to the claim elements. His testimony was a patchwork of statements taken from different portions of the record and was notably self‑contradictory.  The evidence was punctuated by Dr. Wells’ repeated comment that the modeled PIM path, generated downstream, could not satisfy the sampling requirement (J.A. 15010‑19). In short, the jury’s verdict rested on testimony that did not unambiguously demonstrate that the RF ADC sampled a passband containing the interference‑generating signal.

## Appellants’ Argument That the Modeled PIM Path Is Generated After Sampling

Appellants’ core argument for granting JMOL leaned on the scientific fact that the modeled PIM path is synthesized after the RF ADC has completed its analog‑to‑digital conversion. Thus, the claim limitation—requesting that the RF ADC sample *both* signal types—cannot be satisfied if the interference signal is only produced later in the chain. The district court accepted this reasoning when it concluded that the jury had sufficient evidence for infringement (J.A. 19‑22) but did so while ignoring the evidence of Dr. Wells’ contradictory testimony. The appellate court, applying the Fifth Circuit “strong and overwhelming” standard (Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016)), found that the evidence did not satisfy that threshold.

## Appellate Court’s Reasoning: Evidence Fails the “Strong and Overwhelming” Test

The appellate court began by outlining the procedural framework. Under the Fifth Circuit rules, a denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo, and the court reverses only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion” (Austin, p. 461). Here, the principal evidence was Dr. Wells’s testimony, which the court described as “confusing and unclear” (J.A. 15010‑19). Because he never sustained that the x1 and x2 signals entering the RF ADC were the *signals of interest* and *interference‑generating signal*, respectively, the court deemed the evidence to be incomplete and contradictory.

The court bemoaned that the jury “piece‑together[ed] the testimony over ninety pages apart” (J.A. 30–31) and spoke of “self‑contradictory” testimony that did not provide a clear explanation of how the measured signals mapped onto the claim elements (John s Hopkins v. Datascope, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008)). The court held that because the expert’s testimony was not reliable, “no reasonable jury could have found that the accused device literally met this limitation” (J.A. 33). Consequently, it reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL and vacated the judgment for the ’134 patent. The reverse was based on the assertion that the evidence failed to meet the “strong and overwhelming” standard and was therefore insufficient to support the jury’s infringement finding.

## Conclusion

The appellate court’s decision to reverse the denial of JMOL for the ’134 patent rests on a clear reading of the claim’s sampling requirement, the failure of the plaintiff’s expert testimony to satisfy that requirement in an unequivocal manner, and the application of the Fifth Circuit’s standard for JMOL. The court recognized that the RF ADC could not sample a post‑generated modeled PIM path, a fact that undermined the plaintiff’s central argument. Because the evidence, particularly the expert’s contradictory statements, did not meet the “strong and overwhelming” threshold, the appellate court correctly reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL and vacated the judgment accordingly.

# Analysis of the ‘775 Patent Infringement and JMOL  

## Claim Language and Essential Elements  
Claim 1 of the ’775 patent recites a “method for performing interference cancellation…by digitally multiplying…three signals S1, S2, S3 in seven third‑order multiplications” (J.A. 15‑19).  The claim further limits the multiplications to those that generate intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals, and the method is performed in a receiver that shares a transmitter (co‑locus).  For a device to infringe, the jury must find that the accused system physically implements the construction of claim 1: it must (1) use three signals, (2) multiply those signals in exactly seven distinct third‑order terms, and (3) produce cancellation signals that suppress passive IMPs.  The district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) “presupposed” that the jury had sufficient evidence of infringement under this doctrine.

## District‑Court Claim Construction  
The district court construed “three signals” as “separately identifiable” without requiring that each signal be unique.  The court emphasized that the intrinsic evidence did not limit the term to unambiguous signals and accorded the inventor’s extrinsic testimony that the signals could be the same or different (J.A. 109‑14).  The court, therefore, accepted that using two distinct signals, x1 and x2, together with a copy of one of those signals, satisfied the construction because the three “separately identifiable” signals could be represented by the two physical inputs plus a duplicate.  This interpretation entered the jury’s work‑paper (J.A. 38).  The court’s construction is reviewed de novo by the Federal Circuit; the appellate court affirmed the construction because it was supported by the record.

## Appellants’ Argument and Claim Amendment  
Appellants contended that “S1, S2, S3” had to be unique signals, that the opposition to the “three signals” construction relied on the claim amendment, and that Finesse had surrendered scope by amending the claim during prosecution to recite “three signals” rather than “two or three signals” in order to defeat the Filipovic prior‑art rejection.  The appellate court rejected this view.  It noted that the examiner’s rejection of the earlier “two or three signals” claim, which was based on Filipovic, was overruled only after Finesse explained that Filipovic taught only active IMP mitigation.  The amendment, therefore, “does not rise to the level of a clear and unambiguous disavowal of claim scope over products…with two unique input signals” (J.A. 968‑69).  The court held that the Amendment still required three *separately identifiable* signals but made no definitive statement about uniqueness, so Appellants could not rely on that argument at the point of a JMOL motion.

## Expert Testimony and Evidence Gap  
The core of the infringement analysis rested on Dr. Joshua Wells’s testimony.  Wells used a Nokia technical document to identify three distinct third‑order multiplications performed by the accused radios (J.A. 14966‑70).  He asserted that those three distinct multiplications could be mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications (J.A. 28416‑17).  The district court accepted this mapping, reasoning that each of the three distinct terms “corresponds to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications” (J.A. 38).  However, the evidence did not support this claim.  The Nokia document listed only the three multiplications

1. \(x_1\cdot|x_1|\cdot|x_1|\),  
2. \(x_1\cdot x_1\cdot x_2^{\prime}\), and  
3. \(x_1\cdot|x_2|\cdot|x_2|\)

and made no reference to the seven explicit third‑order products required by the claim (J.A. 26485‑86).  There was no proof that the same signal appeared in a complex conjugate or modulus form, nor was there an explicit demonstration that any input signals were multiplied twice or thrice to produce the full set of seven products.  Furthermore, the appellate court noted that on appeal Finesse made no new evidence: “Nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications” (J.A. 15025‑29).  With that gap, the alleged mapping was unsupported, and the jury’s finding of infringement was not “supported by substantial evidence” (Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN; J.A. 169).

## Appellate Review of JMOL  
The Federal Circuit reviews a denial of JMOL de novo, but “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion” (Wi‑LAN v. Apple; J.A. 1‑16).  The appellate court found that Dr. Wells’s contradictory testimony and the absence of a concrete mapping between the actual multiplications and the seven claimed ones produced evidence “insufficient to satisfy that standard.”  The court applied the standard for a finding of infringement based on substantial evidence, concluding that no reasonable jury could have found infringement given the evidence presented (J.A. 169).

## Conclusion  
Because the appellate court determined that the jury’s verdict of infringement was not supported by substantial evidence—specifically, that the accused radios did not demonstrably perform the seven third‑order multiplications of the ’775 patent and that the “three signals” were not proven to be unique—the denial of JMOL for the ’775 claims was reversed.  The vacated damages award consequently removed the monetary stakes that had otherwise been awarded to Finesse.

# Damages Review, Conclusion, and Significance

## Infringement–Damages Nexus

Damages in a patent action are inextricably linked to the factual determination of infringement. The Fifth Circuit affirms that a court may award damages only where it finds that the patented invention was infringed. Accordingly, once the district court’s infringement verdict is reversed—as the Court does on both the ’134 and ’775 patents—the foundation for any damages award collapses. In Finesse Wireless, the Supreme Court’s precedent on infringement and damages is implicit: a judgment for infringement “connotes a liability for damages” (Finesse, J.A. 169‑71). The Court therefore holds that reversing the infringement findings automatically triggers a dismissal of the damages award, because the statutory basis for collecting against a defendant ceases when the infringement element is negated.

## District Court’s Denial of JMOL on Damages and Denial of a New Trial Were Vacated

The district court denied both a motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on damages and a motion for a new trial on damages, premised on the jury’s finding of infringement. After the appellate court reversed the infringement determinations, those district‑court rulings became contradicted by the uncertain value of the evidence supporting infringement. The appellate court explicitly vacated the damages award in the concluding judgment, citing that a reversal of infringement renders the prior joint denial “unenforced” (Finesse, J.A. 1‑72). Under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), the appellate court’s correction of the infringement findings rationally entails the vacatur of any non­insolvent claims that depend upon them, including the damages judgment.

## Fifth Circuit Review of Damages

Damages in a criminal or civil trial are reviewed by a higher court on a standard of abuse of discretion. The Court explicitly states that the Fifth Circuit reviews a district court’s denial of a new trial for damages “for abuse of discretion” (Finesse, see Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d at 461). This standard is considerably deferential; the appellate court merely evaluates whether the lower court’s decision was “justifiable.” In Finesse, having vacated the infringement findings, the district court’s denial of JMOL on damages and new trial is rendered vacuous. Since the appellate court found no error or abuse in the district court’s previously vacated decisions, it affirms that the reversal of infringement sufficiently eliminates the basis for any damages award without requiring the appellate court to revisit the merits of the damages claim itself. The Court therefore finds that its own denial of the damages award is not a matter for reconsideration; instead, that award is automatically voided by the underlying infringement reversal.

## Outcome

The court orders that the jury’s lump‑sum award of $166,303,391 be vacated. In addition, the court awards costs to the defendants‑appellants. The finding that the inverted findings void the damages award is procedural and factual: the impairment of the underlying claim causes the monetary judgment to lose its enforceability. The holding restores the defendants’ ignorance for the right to enforce the patent claims, eliminating the punitive monetary burden that had been accrued from the jury’s determinations.

## Implications

The decision underscores the peril to plaintiffs when expert testimony fails to establish an unambiguous link between the accused device’s operation and the literal claim language. The Court’s analysis illustrates that even a robust patent claim, if the evidence for infringement is insufficiently substantiated, can be reversed, nullifying a significant damages award. For defendants, the ruling demonstrates the practical effectiveness of JMOL motions in the damages context; by challenging a weak infringement finding, a party can eliminate both the probability of infringement and the corresponding monetary liability. The case also demonstrates the distinguished roles of claim construction and infringement findings as foundations for damages. Crucially, it accentuates the Fifth Circuit’s de‑novo claim‑construction standard—exceedingly “strong and overwhelmingly” evidence is required for JMOL—and the limited scope for appellate reviews of damages.

## Take‑away

Finesse Wireless reinforces essential doctrines in patent litigation: (1) damages cannot be awarded where infringement is not established; (2) a successful JMOL denial on infringement automatically voids any associated damages award; (3) the Fifth Circuit reviews a denial of a new trial on damages for abuse of discretion, a deferential standard; (4) expert testimony must be precise, consistent, and directly tied to the claim limitations; and (5) the strategic use of JMOL and the de‑novo claim‑construction standard remain pivotal tools for parties to manage liability in complex technical disputes. The Court’s ruling, therefore, operates both as a cautionary tale for plaintiffs relying on ambiguous technical evidence and as a reinforcement of procedural safeguards that protect defendants from unwarranted monetary exposure.
