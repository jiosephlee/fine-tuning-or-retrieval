Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 3: # Analysis of the ’134 Patent Infringement and JMOL

## Structure of Claim 1 of the ’134 Patent

Claim 1 of the ’134 patent describes a method for mitigating intermodulation (IMP) interference in a receiver. The method is broken down into seven distinct steps: first, the radio over‑samples a passband of received signals that contains both *signals of interest* and *interference‑generating signals*; next, it isolates the signals of interest from the interference‑generating signals using decimating filters; then, the isolated interference‑generating signals are used to compute an estimate of each resultant IMP; the radio then cancels the in‑band IMPs by applying the estimated signals; and finally, it performs closed‑loop phase and amplitude adjustment on the estimates. The claim therefore hinges on a particular signal‑sampling architecture (the RF ADC) and on the successive isolation, estimation, and cancellation steps that follow. The appellant’s case centered on whether the accused Nokia radios’ RF ADC satisfied this architecture.

## Limitation 1[A] – The Need to Sample Both Signal Types

Limitation 1[a] explicitly requires that the RF analogue‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC) sample a *passband* in which *both* signals of interest and interference‑generating signals are present. In plain terms, the capture step must provide the raw data on which later stages can isolate, estimate, and cancel the IMPs. If a device’s RF ADC samples only one of those signal sets, or if the interference‑generating signals are not present at the sampling stage, then the device would not satisfy this limitation. The importance of this limitation was echoed by the district court when it noted that “the RF ADC must sample a passband of received signals that includes both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals” (J.A. 28:1‑6).

## Finesse’s Burden of Proof and the Reliance on Dr. Jonathan Wells

Because the plaintiff (Finesse) carried the burden of proving infringement, it was required to demonstrate that the accused radios sampled a passband containing both signal types. Finesse’s approach hinged on expert testimony by Dr. Jonathan Wells, a signal‑processing specialist. Dr. Wells relied heavily on a Nokia technical drawing (J.A. 26421) that illustrated the internal signal flow of the Nokia radios. He testified that the RF ADC, depicted in blue, captured both the downstream downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and a “modeled PIM path” (shown on a red path in the diagram). He claimed that these two signals corresponded directly to the *signals of interest* and the *interference‑generating signals* required by limitation 1[a] (J.A. 14918‑19, 15009). By analogy, Dr. Wells positioned the PIM model—an internal construct used to estimate the IMPs—as the interference‑generating signal that the RF ADC must capture.

## Dr. Wells’s Testimony on Signal Mapping

Dr. Wells’s testimony consistently exercised the mapping between the Nokia diagram and the claim limits:

1. The DL(TX) reference (a real transmit waveform) was mapped to the *signal of interest*.
2. The modeled PIM path—essentially a pre‑computed estimate of the interference contributions—was mapped to the *interference‑generating signal* (J.A. 14920‑21, 15009‑14).

He reiterated this mapping in his expert report, stating that “the DL(TX) reference signal … meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’ … and the modeled PIM signal is the ‘interference generating signal.’” (J.A. 4476‑77). The jury, persuaded by these statements, found that the limitation was satisfied and held that all the Plaintiffs’ claims infringed.

## Conflict in Testimony and the Misimpression Claim

During cross‑examination, Appellants highlighted a logical problem: the modeled PIM path is *created* within the radio’s digital logic after the RF ADC has performed its sampling exit. Because the RF ADC cannot sample a signal that does not yet exist, Dr. Wells’ mapping was mathematically impossible. Appellants asked whether the modeled PIM path was actually an “interference‑generating signal” in the sense required by the claim, and the trial court noted that the evidence “suggested that the modeled PIM path is generated in the GROOT FPGA” and that the recitation of the modeled PIM path as an interference‑generating signal could not satisfy limitation 1[a] (J.A. 19‑22).

Finesse argued that Dr. Wells was “labouring under the misimpression that x1 and x2 correspond to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path.” (Oral Arg. at 31:01‑32:20). The court, however, recognized that Dr. Wells had not provided an explicit mapping of the internal signals x1 and x2 (the physical wires into the RF ADC) to the claim elements. His testimony was a patchwork of statements taken from different portions of the record and was notably self‑contradictory.  The evidence was punctuated by Dr. Wells’ repeated comment that the modeled PIM path, generated downstream, could not satisfy the sampling requirement (J.A. 15010‑19). In short, the jury’s verdict rested on testimony that did not unambiguously demonstrate that the RF ADC sampled a passband containing the interference‑generating signal.

## Appellants’ Argument That the Modeled PIM Path Is Generated After Sampling

Appellants’ core argument for granting JMOL leaned on the scientific fact that the modeled PIM path is synthesized after the RF ADC has completed its analog‑to‑digital conversion. Thus, the claim limitation—requesting that the RF ADC sample *both* signal types—cannot be satisfied if the interference signal is only produced later in the chain. The district court accepted this reasoning when it concluded that the jury had sufficient evidence for infringement (J.A. 19‑22) but did so while ignoring the evidence of Dr. Wells’ contradictory testimony. The appellate court, applying the Fifth Circuit “strong and overwhelming” standard (Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016)), found that the evidence did not satisfy that threshold.

## Appellate Court’s Reasoning: Evidence Fails the “Strong and Overwhelming” Test

The appellate court began by outlining the procedural framework. Under the Fifth Circuit rules, a denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo, and the court reverses only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion” (Austin, p. 461). Here, the principal evidence was Dr. Wells’s testimony, which the court described as “confusing and unclear” (J.A. 15010‑19). Because he never sustained that the x1 and x2 signals entering the RF ADC were the *signals of interest* and *interference‑generating signal*, respectively, the court deemed the evidence to be incomplete and contradictory.

The court bemoaned that the jury “piece‑together[ed] the testimony over ninety pages apart” (J.A. 30–31) and spoke of “self‑contradictory” testimony that did not provide a clear explanation of how the measured signals mapped onto the claim elements (John s Hopkins v. Datascope, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008)). The court held that because the expert’s testimony was not reliable, “no reasonable jury could have found that the accused device literally met this limitation” (J.A. 33). Consequently, it reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL and vacated the judgment for the ’134 patent. The reverse was based on the assertion that the evidence failed to meet the “strong and overwhelming” standard and was therefore insufficient to support the jury’s infringement finding.

## Conclusion

The appellate court’s decision to reverse the denial of JMOL for the ’134 patent rests on a clear reading of the claim’s sampling requirement, the failure of the plaintiff’s expert testimony to satisfy that requirement in an unequivocal manner, and the application of the Fifth Circuit’s standard for JMOL. The court recognized that the RF ADC could not sample a post‑generated modeled PIM path, a fact that undermined the plaintiff’s central argument. Because the evidence, particularly the expert’s contradictory statements, did not meet the “strong and overwhelming” threshold, the appellate court correctly reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL and vacated the judgment accordingly.
