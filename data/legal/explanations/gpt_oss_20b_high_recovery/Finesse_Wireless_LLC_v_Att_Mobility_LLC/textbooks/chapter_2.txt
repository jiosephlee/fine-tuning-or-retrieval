Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # Legal Standards Governing JMOL, Substantial Evidence, and Claim Construction

The appellate process in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* illustrates how a Fifth Circuit court applies its procedural and substantive standards when reviewing (1) a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), (2) a jury’s factual findings of infringement, (3) a claim‑construction hearing, and (4) a denial of a new trial on damages. The court’s analysis—drawn from *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.* and *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN, Inc.*—shows the interplay among claim construction, infringement determination, and damages calculation.

## Fifth Circuit JMOL Standard

The Fifth Circuit tests denials of JMOL de novo. As held in *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, the appellate court examines the evidence “in the light most favorable to the verdict” and may reverse the district court only if the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that **reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion**. This standard places a high evidentiary threshold on the material that the district court used to reach its judgment and protects the jury’s fact‑finding function. In *Finesse*, the district court denied JMOL of noninfringement for the ’134 and ’775 patents; the court now must decide whether the evidence—chiefly the expert testimony of Dr. Wells and the Nokia technical documents—meet this “strong and overwhelming” standard.

## Substantial Evidence Standard for Infringement

When a jury finds infringement, the appellate court applies the substantial‑evidence standard, derived in *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN, Inc.* The test asks whether a **reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial**. If the evidence is adequate to support the verdict, the appellate court will not reverse; if the record contains only a slim or ambiguous basis for the jury’s conclusion, the court may rule that no reasonable jury could have reached that verdict. In *Finesse*, the appellate court scrutinized Dr. Wells’s testimony that the accused Nokia radios “sample a passband of signals that include both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals” and the claim that the radios perform the requisite seven multiplications. The court concluded the evidence was insufficient to sustain the jury’s infringement ruling, thus reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL.

## Claim Construction: De Novo “Clear and Unambiguous” Review

Claim construction is a separate, de novo proceeding. The Fifth Circuit adopts a **“clear and unambiguous” standard**: the claims and specification are interpreted to provide a determinate sense of scope. Extrinsic evidence (expert testimony, technical documents, industry usage) may be considered *only under the superimposition test* when it can be believed to reflect the inventor’s intent and is consistent with intrinsic evidence. In *Finesse*, the court reviewed the ’134 and ’775 patents, drawing on the claims, abstract, and specification, and considered Dr. Wells’s mapping of the “modeled PIM path” onto the interference‑generating signal and the Nokia documents that purported to show the radios performed the seven required multiplications. The court’s refusal to find clear evidence that the radios satisfied each claim limitation established a basis for JMOL.

## Damages Review: Abuse of Discretion Standard

A district court’s denial of a new trial on damages is reviewed for **abuse of discretion** under the controlled‑abuse framework the Fifth Circuit follows (*Wi‑LAN, Inc.*). The appellate court assesses whether the district court’s decision was arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to law or precedent. In *Finesse*, the district court denied a new trial on damages after finding infringement and awarding a lump‑sum award. Because the appellate court reversed the JMOL decisions, it found the district court’s denial of damages was premised on an erroneous infringement analysis and thus vacated the award.

## Interplay among Claim Construction, Infringement Analysis, and Damages

The procedural rules create a sequential relationship:  

1. **Claim construction** defines the protected subject matter.  
2. **Infringement analysis** determines whether the accused product falls within that scope.  
3. **Damages** are contingent upon an infringement finding.  

In *Finesse*, the appellate court’s reversal of the JMOL findings—grounded in the lack of substantial evidence and the failure to properly construct the claims—eliminates the substrate for the damages award. Consequently, the court vacated the $166.3 million lump‑sum award, emphasizing that damages cannot be realized in the absence of infringement. 

By applying the Fifth Circuit’s de novo JMOL standard, the substantial‑evidence review, the clear‑and‑unambiguous claim‑construction doctrine, and the abuse‑of‑discretion test for damages, the court systematically dismantled the district court’s findings and restored the integrity of the appellate review process.
