Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does the remaining life of a patent factor into lump‑sum damages, and does a new trial on damages require a new infringement trial?
Question:
The jury awarded $166,303,391 in lump‑sum damages based on the remaining life of the patents. I’m unsure how the court connects patent life to damages. Also, if the appellate court vacates the damages award and orders a new trial on damages, must the infringement verdict also be revisited, or can damages be recalculated based on the same finding of infringement?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The jury’s lump‑sum award simply multiplied an estimated annual‐value of the infringement (most often a “reasonable royalty” or cost‑based figure) by the number of full years the patents were still in force when the infringement was found.  The District Court therefore treated the “remaining life” of each patent as a multiplier in the calculation of damages, which is the standard way courts apply § 284 when a patent has not yet expired.  Because the appellate court reversed the district court’s denial of a judgment‑as‑law‑of‑moral‑right (JMOL) of non‑infringement, the underlying infringement finding was set aside.  Damages cannot be awarded absent an infringement finding, so the appellate court vacated the entire damages award.  A new trial on damages can be held without a separate infringement trial **only if the infringement finding is still valid**; where that finding has been vacated (as here), a new infringement determination must be made first—either in the same combined trial or in a new trial on infringement and damages together.

---

### 1.  How the “remaining life” of a patent is used in a lump‑sum damages award

The jury’s award of $166,303,391 was expressly “for the remaining life of the ’134 and ’775 patents” (J.A. 169–71).  In a § 284 damages calculation the court typically estimates the amount of damages that would accrue for each year of the patent’s remaining term and then multiplies that annual figure by the number of years until the patent expires.  The “remaining life” therefore serves as the time‑period over which the estimated annual royalty or loss‑profit figure is applied.

This methodology is the one most commonly used in post‑Grant infringement cases—because § 284 requires damages to equal the “amount of damages that would be imposed if the infringement had continued until the patent expired or was otherwise terminated” (see, e.g., the statutory text, which the court applied implicitly).  The opinion does not spell out the precise arithmetic used in the calculation, but it confirms that the jury deliberately tied the lump‑sum award to the pending expiration dates of the two patents.

---

### 2.  Whether a new trial on damages requires a new infringement trial

The appellate court’s order reads:

> “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.” (…).  The reversal of the denial of JMOL means the court has set aside the jury’s finding that the patents were infringed.

Damages are proportional to the infringement – they can be calculated only after an infringement finding survives.  As the appellate court has **reversed** that finding, the entire damages award is vacated, not merely recalculated.  The court therefore does **not** simply allow the district court to re‑calculate damages using the same infringement verdict.

If the parties seek a *new trial on damages*, that trial must rest on a finding of infringement that is valid for that trial.  In practice this means the district court will likely remand for a new infringement trial (or for a combined trial in which infringement and damages are re‑determined together).  A new damages trial that relies on the earlier infringement verdict would be impossible because that verdict has been set aside by the appellate court.

In other words:

| Situation | Infringement finding? | Damages trial possible? |
|-----------|-----------------------|------------------------|
| Infringement still valid (not vacated) | Yes | Yes – new damages can be calculated on that same finding |
| Infringement vacated | No | No – damages cannot be determined without a fresh infringement finding |

### Bottom line

- The “remaining life” of the patents was used as a multiplier in the court’s lump‑sum damages calculation.  
- Because the appellate court set aside the infringement finding, the damages award is vacated.  
- To award damages again, the court must first re‑establish infringement—either in the same trial or in a separate trial—before any new damages can be imposed.

This is exactly what the appellate order indicates: the damages award was vacated solely because the infringement finding was reversed.  Consequently, any new damages trial must also involve a new infringement determination.
