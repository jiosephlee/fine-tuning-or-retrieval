Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### Which factors does a court consider when determining if consent was voluntarily given?
Question:
The opinion cites Bumper, Han, and several other cases that use a 'totality of the circumstances' test to assess voluntariness. What specific factors are weighed in that calculation, and how do they interact to produce a conclusion that the consent was 'free and voluntary'?  Also, how does the presence of a police officer influence that assessment?
Answer:
**Short Answer**  
The Seventh Circuit tests “free and voluntary” consent by balancing a set of factual prongs that, taken together, show whether a person could have reasonably refused.  In *United States v. Han*, 105 F.4th 986 (7th Cir. 2024) and *United States v. Figueroa‑Espana*, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing *Santiago*, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05) the court lists the following factors:

1. The person’s age, intelligence, and education.  
2. Whether the person was advised of his constitutional rights.  
3. How long he was detained or bound before giving consent.  
4. Whether the consent was immediate, or prompted by repeated requests by the authorities.  
5. Whether any physical coercion was used.  
6. Whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.

The court weighs *all* of these together; no single factor is determinative.  In *Coleman* the district court found that none of these factors weighed against voluntariness.  Lisa was an adult with no extraordinary vulnerability noted, she was not told her rights, she was not detained (she remained free‑to‑leave), she granted consent next after only a brief exchange and not after repeated, pressure‑filled requests, no physical coercion was evident, and she was not in custody.  The police’s tone was calm, and their request to “check on the children” was framed as concern rather than threat.  The district court therefore concluded that the consent was “free and voluntary.”

**How the factors interact**  
The court treats the factors as a summary of the “strength” of coercion or voluntariness.  If the person is young or mentally impaired, or has been told of her rights, or if she was detained for minutes, the burden to show voluntariness shifts.  Conversely, if the person is an adult, not detained, and not subjected to repeated or forceful requests, the weight shifts toward voluntariness.  The factors are not scored one‑by‑one; instead, the court asks whether *any* combination of them could have produced a “reasonable persuasion” to comply.  In *Coleman* the record supplied no indication that the reunion of the “how long,” “repeated requests,” “physical coercion,” or “custody” facts combined to override the other factors.

**Effect of a police officer’s presence**  
The presence of a police officer is a double‑edged sword.  On its own it does not make consent involuntary; what matters is whether that presence creates coercion.  The court therefore gives particular weight to

* factor 6 (custody): a person in custody has a gravely diminished capacity to refuse, and consent is presumptively involuntary.  
* factor 4 (repeated requests): a police officer’s repeated invocations of a right to enter can produce a reasonable expectation of coercion.  

In *Coleman* the woman was neither in custody nor had the officers persisted beyond a single, polite request.  The court explicitly noted that the officer’s use of “need” was not coercive and that Lisa still “had the ability to deny consent.”  The record did not show the officer’s presence to have impaired her decision‑making, so the presence of the police officers did not negate voluntariness.

In sum, the “totality of circumstances” test forces the court to look at every detail that could influence voluntariness.  In *Coleman* the combination of factors—adult age, lack of detention, no coercion or repeated pressure, and a calm, concern‑oriented tone—leads the court to conclude that Lisa’s consent was free and voluntary.
