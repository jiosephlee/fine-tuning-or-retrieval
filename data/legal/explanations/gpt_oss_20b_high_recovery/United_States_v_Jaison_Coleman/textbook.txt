Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Case Background & Key Facts

The central facts of *United States v. Jaison Coleman* arise from a 911 call reporting a domestic‑violence emergency, the police officers’ arrival and interaction with the homeowner, and the police a‑post‑entry activities that ultimately led to the recovery of firearms. The facts set the stage for the Fourth‑Amendment inquiry regarding consensual entry and the scope of that consent.

## The 911 call: threat against Lisa Coleman and the circumstances of domestic violence

At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, a 911 dispatcher received a call reporting that Jaison Coleman, the stepfather of Lisa Coleman, had threatened to kill her and was still inside the home.  The caller indicated that the suspect was present and that the situation warranted immediate police assistance.  The call established that the police were responding to an active domestic‑violence incident in which the suspect had allegedly threatened violence against the homeowner.

## Officer Jamie Kizer’s arrival: announcement, knocking, and initial communication protocol

Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department arrived on the scene promptly. He knocked on the front door and announced himself to the occupant.  The officer’s initial approach followed standard protocol: he identified himself, explained the purpose of his visit, and sought to verify the occupant’s identity.  Body‑camera footage captured his demeanor, tone, and the content of the conversation, later used to assess the voluntariness of consent.

## Lisa Coleman’s statements about children, husband, and dogs during the first encounter

When Officer Kizer first spoke with Lisa Coleman, she explained that her son and daughter were inside.  She hesitated when asked whether her children were safe and, in her initial response, said only that they were in her bedroom.  Officer Kizer repeated the question, requesting confirmation that the children were “okay.”  She again offered a non‑committal answer, mentioning that she “hope[d] so.”  The officer then asked for the location of her husband, to which Lisa replied that she did not know.  In each exchange, Lisa displayed a degree of uncertainty and reluctance to provide a definitive response.

A brief interlude ensued: Lisa told Officer Kizer she needed to go inside to retrieve her shoes, after which she returned briefly to the doorway in the absence of the officer’s presence.  He then cracked the door open and called out again, asking if she was safe, which prompted her to step outside again to converse.

Lisa’s final remarks during this first encounter included a statement about her dogs: “You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you,” followed by “They’re not gonna bite.”  These comments were repeated as Officer Kizer requested clarification about the children’s safety and where Jaison Coleman had been positioned.

## The initial refusal of entry and the officer’s second request for consent

At the outset, Lisa literally refused entry.  When Officer Kizer asked “Could I come in?” she paused and replied, “um … I’d prefer you not.”  This initial denial indicated a refusal to allow the officer to cross the threshold.  After a short break, and at a later point when the officer had again reiterated the importance of checking on the children’s well‑being, Lisa consented.  She responded “Okay” to the officer’s question, “Do we need to come inside to make sure the kids are okay?”  The attorney’s transcripts capture the exchange, with Officer Kizer using the phrase “we almost need to come inside.”  This moment marks the turning point in the factual record: consent for entry was given.

## The moment Lisa gives consent and the officers’ subsequent cautionary entry

Following Lisa’s affirmative response, she opened the front door to Officer Kizer and his colleagues, allowing them entry.  As she led them into the house, she brushed her dogs away and guided the officers toward a back bedroom.  She pointed out the locations of her children and expressly confirmed that the dogs would not pose harm.  The officers, therefore, entered the home with a focused purpose: to ascertain the safety of the children and assess whether Jaison Coleman posed an imminent threat.

## Police actions inside the home: child interviews, pat‑down, canvassing for Coleman

Once inside, Officer Kizer quickly canvassed the residence.  The officers located Jaison Coleman in a separate bedroom, where he permitted a protective pat‑down.  The children were questioned, revealing that they had witnessed their father point a gun at their mother and threatening to shoot her, as well as to set the house on fire.  These interviews helped cement the officers’ assessment of danger and the need for further search.

## Discovery of firearms and the decision to secure a search warrant

The officers subsequently seized a warrant to search the Coleman home, based on information gleaned during the initial entry.  The warrant search uncovered several firearms, which became the evidence for the federal indictment charging Jaison Coleman with unlawful possession as a convicted felon under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).  The discovery of these firearms set up the suppression motion that would later be litigated.

## Summary of facts as identified by the magistrate judge and district court

The magistrate judge, relying on the body‑camera evidence and witness statements, found that Lisa Coleman had consented to the officers’ entry and that the officers did not exceed the scope of that consent while inside the home.  The district court adopted the magistrate’s findings: consent was voluntary, the officers’ actions were within the permissible scope, and consequently the recovered firearms were admissible.  These factual findings constitute the factual matrix upon which the appellate court ultimately affirmed the conviction.

# Procedural Posture & Motion to Suppress  

## District Court’s Review of the Magistrate Judge’s Report on Consent  

The jurisdictional step in docket No. 24‑3051 involved a magistrate‐judge’s post‑incident report.  The magistrate examined the body‑camera footage and, applying the precedential standard that consent must be “freely and voluntarily” given (see *United States v. Banks*, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968)), determined that Lisa Coleman had consented to the officers’ entry.  The magistrate further noted that her consent was neither the product of coercion nor impermissible limitation on the officers’ investigative purpose; it fell within the “objective reasonableness” test for the scope of a consented search (*United States v. Saucedo*, 688 F.4th 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012)).  The district court, in its hearing, reviewed this report and fully adopted the magistrate’s findings.  The court’s brief pronouncement that “the magistrate judge’s report finding that Lisa did consent” served as the legal base for the officers’ subsequent entry and search.  

## The Defendant’s Motion to Suppress—Argument that No Consent Was Given  

Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms recovered from his residence, contending that Lisa had never actually granted permission for the officers to enter the home.  In his brief, he asserted that the language used by Officer Kizer (“We almost need to come inside”) conveyed coercive pressure and that Lisa’s intermittent hesitancy indicated a lack of clear, voluntary consent.  He further argued that the officers had no substantive authority, as the stepfather’s status—being a convicted felon—rendered any third‑party consent insufficient under the Fourth Amendment (“a third party may give consent to search a place ... and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party” – *United States v. Cellitti*, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004)).  His relief sought would have required the district court to find that the officers’ entry was otherwise unlawful and that the firearms must be excluded from trial.  

## District Court’s Denial of the Motion and its Findings on Consent and Scope  

Upon consideration of the motion, the district court expressly rejected Coleman’s contentions.  The court reiterated that Lisa’s consent was both voluntary and within the scope of the officers’ investigative purpose.  The district court particularly emphasized that the officers’ dialogue—committed to explicit concern for Lisa’s children—did not amount to ‘coercion’ and that the officers remained within “objective reasonableness” by focusing their search on preserving child welfare and verifying the presence of a dangerous firearm.  The court held that the officers did not overstep the limits the plaintiff’s consent had implied, a conclusion that directly supported the admissibility of the firearms as evidence.  By granting the magistrate’s recommendation, the district court thereby denied the suppression request.  

## The Defendant’s Guilty Plea with Reservation of Appellate Rights  

Following the denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea to the federal firearm offense under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).  He expressly reserved the right to challenge the appellate court’s decision on the suppression ruling.  This reservation maintained his conviction for the underlying misdemeanor while allowing him to assert on appeal that the evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment.  The plea effectively closed the trial phase and ushered the case into appellate review.  

## Appellate Focus: Evaluating the Correctness of the District Court’s Finding of Voluntariness and Scope  

The Seventh Circuit’s primary review focus in this appeal is whether the district court erred in its factual findings about consent and its legal assessment of scope.  The Court applies a “clear‑error” standard, giving deference to the district court unless the appellate tribunal finds the lower court’s conclusions not only erroneous but “clear” of any merit.  In doing so, the Court checks whether the officer’s request for entry was truly “freely and voluntarily” given in light of the “totality of circumstances” standard (see *United States v. Han*, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024); *United States v. Figueroa‑Espana*, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007)).  It also scrutinizes the officers’ intrusiveness against the objective‑reasonableness framework for scope (*United States v. Saucedo*, 688 F.4th 863, 865).  The appellate court therefore examines the body‑camera evidence for any indication of coercion or overreach, and whether the officers stayed within the confines that Lisa’s consent permitted.  

## Standard of Review for Appellate Courts: Question of Clear Error  

Under Seventh Circuit precedent, appellate review of a district court’s determination of consensual entry is conducted on a “question of clear error” basis.  The appellate court must first establish the standard of voluntariness, which mandatorily requires the “totality of the circumstances” test, and then the standard of scope, requiring objective reasonableness.  The Court reviews whether the district court misapplied those standards or drew facts from the record that are plainly wrong.  If the appellate judge finds no factual misapprehensions or misapplication of the legal principles, it will defer to the district court’s conclusions and affirm the prosecution’s use of the evidence.  In “United States v. Jaison Coleman,” the appellate court concluded that the district court had correctly applied both the voluntariness and scope tests and therefore found no clear error in its findings, leading to the affirmation of the conviction.

# Legal Framework: Consent Searches of the Home

The Sixth and Seventh Circuits have repeatedly refashioned the balance between homeowners’ Fourth Amendment protections and the police need to respond rapidly to alarms of violence. The doctrine that voluntary consent removes the warrant requirement is rooted in the Supreme Court, but the precise conditions that render consent “voluntary” and the boundaries of what a consenting party may allow are clarified through a body of case law developed by the Seventh Circuit. This chapter reviews that framework and shows how the court applied it to the facts of United States v. Jaison Coleman.

## Consent exception under the Fourth Amendment: Supreme Court precedent

The Supreme Court’s stare decisis establishes that a court may rely on a third‑party’s consent to search a dwelling, provided that consent is valid and that the search is limited to the scope of that consent. In *Schneckloth v. Bustamante*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), the Court held that the presence of a sign telling a person that the officer was “allowed to enter” was sufficient for a “consent exception” because no proof of coercion or lack of voluntariness was necessary. The Court further clarified that consent need not rest on any affirmative statement as long as it is "free and voluntary" and that the Fourth Amendment does not require the officer to counsel the person about the rights associated with consent. The *Schneckloth* framework thus permits officers to re‑enter a residence after a preliminary egress; the key is the presence of a tangible, uncoerced clause that grants the officer access.

## Voluntary consent: Bumper v. North Carolina and its influence on Seventh Circuit analysis

The voluntariness of a given consent is determined by a totality‑of‑circumstances inquiry, an approach adopted explicitly in *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543 (1968). The Court held that the voluntariness of a third‑party’s consent is a question of fact, and, absent clear evidence of a coercive or suspicious means, courts should presume an affirmative consent was freely given. Seventh‑Circuit cases have adopted that line precisely, insisting that an affirmative “yes” drawn from a calm and aware citizen is generally regarded as voluntary. In *United States v. Han*, 105 F.4th 986 (7th Cir. 2024), the court again weighed factors—including age, prior knowledge, presence of coercive language, and the length of the interaction—to rebut a claim that a homeowner’s consent was tainted by force. In *Coleman*, the district court found Lisa's affirmative statement “okay” to be a voluntary consent, and the appellate court reaffirmed that finding by comparing the fact pattern to *Bumper*: Lisa was not detained, was fully aware of the situation, and issued the consent without a repeated, aggressive prompt from the officer.

## Totality‑of‑circumstances factors used to assess voluntariness

The Seventh Circuit has identified a catalog of facts that may weigh either way on voluntariness: the age, intelligence, and education of the consenting party; whether the officer explicitly advised the individual of the rights involved; how long the individual was detained or observed before giving consent; whether the consent was immediate or delayed; the presence of physical or verbal pressure; and whether the person was in police custody at the time of consent. In *United States v. Figueroa‑Espana*, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007), the court elaborated that no single factor is determinative; rather, the totality “of the circumstances” must be judged. Importantly, the court also recognized that a brief, repeated request can materially affect the perception of voluntariness. In *Coleman*, the officer’s first request was declined, but on a second, subdued ask—without any demonstrable coercion—the homeowner said “okay.” The magistrate judge and district court appropriately concluded that the overall context satisfied the *Bumper* threshold: No custodial doubt, no delay to question the validity of the consent, and an unpressured environment.

## Clear‑error standard for appellate review of district‑court findings on voluntariness

Appellate courts defer to district courts on findings of fact but review those decisions for clear error. In *United States v. Jones*, 22 F.4th 667 (7th Cir. 2022), the appellate panel explained that voluntariness is a matter of inquiry that is not prone to “blunders” by a trial court, and that could only be reversed if the appellate court found a “clear error.” Because the evidence in *Coleman* included a fully intact video record that demonstrated Lisa’s calm demeanor, a lack of physical coercion, and her spontaneous assent, the appellate court found no clear-error. The *Jones* decision also obliged the court to apply the same standard when reevaluating the district court’s findings in *Coleman*, which it did, thereby affirming the lower court’s ruling.

## Scope of consent: objective‑reasonableness test and precedent

The question of whether a police search exceeded the consenting party’s scope is a question of objective reasonableness. The Seventh Circuit calls on *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248 (1991), to provide that courts should ask: “What would a typical reasonable person have believed was authorized by the consenting party?” In *Saucedo*, 688 F.3d 863 (7th Cir. 2012), the court reinforced that the question is always a “reasonable person” standard, not a subjective one. In *Coleman*, the officers explained that they “almost need to come inside to make sure that the kids are okay.” The subpoenaed officers then inspected the child’s bedroom, located the children, talked to the parents about safety, and later, after securing a warrant, searched the home. That activity stays squarely within the reasonable expectations set by Lisa’s statement. Under *Saucedo*, a top‑down limit on what a reasonable resident would understand requires that the search remain limited to the act of ensuring child safety, nothing beyond. Because the officers adhered to this scope, the appellate court found that there was no overstep. *Hicks*, 539 F.3d 566 (7th Cir. 2008) further clarified that the use of the phrase “almost need” was not under the effect of legal or cultural coercion, confirming that the extent of the search was consistent with the consent’s limited purpose.

## Discussion of exigent circumstances & inevitable discovery and their inapplicability here

The government may sometimes assert that exigent circumstances justify entry despite lack of consent, or that they could have discovered the evidence inevitably through other legal means. In *Coleman*, the district court explicitly noted that the third party’s consent and the officers’ following the standard of *Jimeno* and *Saucedo* satisfied the Fourth Amendment. The appellate court finds the exigent‑circumstance doctrine unnecessary because the police had no imminent danger of loss of evidence, no evidence that the occupants could flee, and no prior history that would justify the assumption that the firearms must be found promptly. Likewise, inevitable‑discovery would have required that the officers have no other feasible ways to obtain a warrant prior to the entry; but since they immediately secured a warrant based on the children’s testimony, the court determined that they had the option to wait for a warrant and did not need an exigent exception. Consequently, the court dismissed those lines of argument as inapplicable, reaffirming that the consent exception was the proper basis.

## Relevance of Seventh Circuit guiding cases

A tapestry of Seventh‑Circuit precedent frames the debate on consent searches: *United States v. Banks*, 60 F.4th 386 (7th Cir. 2023) explicitly endorsed using the voluntariness standard developed at *Bumper* for third‑party consent; *United States v. Cellitti*, 387 F.3d 618 (7th Cir. 2004) made clear that a spouse’s consent may be valid even if the defendant cannot object; *United States v. Jones*, 22 F.4th 667 (7th Cir. 2022) clarified appellate standards for reviewing volatily findings; *United States v. Han*, 105 F.4th 986 (7th Cir. 2024) expanded the totality‑of‑circumstances factor list; *United States v. Figueroa‑Espana*, 511 F.3d 696 (7th Cir. 2007) emphasized the role of custodial status; and *United States v. Hicks*, 539 F.3d 566 (7th Cir. 2008) highlighted language nuances in plea of need. All these cases are reflected in the *Coleman* decision. The court’s reasoning consistently advanced the idea that a spouse’s voluntary, uncoerced consent may be honored and that the scope of that consent is bounded by what a reasonable resident would accept. In sum, *Coleman* illuminates both the breadth and the limits of the consent doctrine and demonstrates how Seventh‑Circuit jurisprudence provides a structured checklist for judges and attorneys to evaluate each element in a consent search.

# Court’s Reasoning, Findings, and Holding

The appellate court’s analysis centers on whether the district court erred in concluding that Lisa Coleman voluntarily consented to Officer Kizer’s entry and that the officers’ subsequent actions were within that consent’s scope. The court applied the well‑established voluntariness test, the objective‑reasonableness inquiry for scope, and rejected the government’s alternative doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. The findings firmed the lower court’s decisions and thereby affirmed Coleman’s conviction.

## Video evidence evaluation: officer’s demeanor, tone, and use of “need”

The body‑camera footage was the linchpin of the appellate court’s factual determination. In examining Officer Kizer’s demeanor, the court noted that he introduced himself calmly, asked questions in a matter‑of‑fact tone, and repeatedly signaled concern for the safety of Lisa’s children. The phrases “almost need to come inside to make sure of that” and “I’m sensing we almost need to come inside” were deemed expressions of urgency but not coerced demands. The court contrasted these with the observation that Lisa consistently responded with “Okay” after each request, indicating her willingness to allow entry without being forced. On the record, no physical pressure or threats were evident; the officer’s use of the word “need” did not, in the court’s view, transform an ordinary request into a coercive one under the fourth‑amendment voluntariness standard.

## Assessment of Lisa’s capacity to deny consent and her subsequent acquiescence

The court emphasized that consent must be given by a person who has both the authority to do so and the practical ability to refuse. Lisa’s early refusal when Officer Kizer asked whether her children were safe demonstrated that she possessed that authority and was not in any way restricted. The second request came after a brief pause and after Lisa had explored the possibility of allowing them to enter the house to retrieve her shoes. At that time, she again said “okay,” indicating that she was not compelled but voluntarily altered her stance. The court noted that neither the duration nor the manner of the conversation gave rise to coercion; it was simply a brief exchange in which Lisa had chosen to grant grounds for entry.

## Absence of coercion, no custody, brief interaction, and lack of physical force

For consent to be considered voluntary, the person giving it must not be subject to any form of physical or psychological pressure. The appeal court observed that Lisa was never in police custody, she never heard a threat over the speaker, and, at no point, did Officer Kizer or colleagues attempt to force her or use threats of force. The interaction lasted only a few minutes—a brief period that court recognised could not engender a claim of duress. The “totality of circumstances” framework was thus satisfied: there was no indication of a longer detention or any pattern of repeated coercive requests, and no physical implication was present.

## Determining voluntariness: totality of circumstances factors and the court’s conclusions

Based on the evidence, the court reiterated the Seventh Circuit’s endorsement of “the totality of the circumstances” test for voluntariness (see United States v. Hal, 105 F.4th 986). The relevant factors—Lisa’s sober and immediate choice, lack of custody, the rapid exchange, and the officer’s tone—pointed to an uncoerced consent. Consequently, the appellate court determined that the district court’s finding of voluntary consent was “beyond a reasonable doubt” and did not suffer from a “clear error.” The fact that the word “need” appeared in the officer’s statement was deemed peripheral and not material to the voluntariness analysis.

## Scope of consent: objective‑reasonableness regarding the officers’ welfare‑of‑children focus

The court turned next to the scope inquiry. Under the objective‑reasonableness standard (Florida v. Jimeno, U.S. 1991), the officers’ actions must be those a reasonable person would understand as permissible within the granted consent. The court focused on the officer’s repeated references to the children’s safety and the child‑witness accounts of the suspect’s threats. The officers’ primary objective was to confirm the children’s safety and locate the defendant, a limited and narrowly tailored activity. They did not conduct a general sweep; their search was restricted to locating the suspect and verifying that no weapon was present or threatening the household. The court therefore found that the officers stayed within the confines of the scope that a reasonable person would have understood to have been granted.

## Rejection of alternative doctrines (exigent circumstances, inevitable discovery)

The government argued that an exigent circumstance justified entry and that inevitable discovery would preserve the search’s validity. The appellate court found neither doctrine to apply. No imminent danger was demonstrated: the suspect was not in the house at the moment of entry, and there was no evidence that failure to grant immediate entry would endanger anyone. Furthermore, the officers obtained a warrant after they entered; thus, the weapons discovered were a product of a lawful warrant search, not an inevitable discovery that could salvage an unconstitutional entry. Consequently, the court dismissed these alternative defenses as irrelevant to the consent issue.

## Final holding: affirmation of conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm

After affirming that Lisa’s consent was voluntary and that the officers’ conduct post‑entry stayed within that consent’s scope, the court affirmed the district court’s denial of the suppression motion. Because the firearms were recovered pursuant to a lawful procedure, the indictment for unlawful possession of a firearm pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) stands. The court thereby upheld Coleman’s conviction for possession of a firearm as a convicted felon.
