Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 2: # Procedural Posture & Motion to Suppress  

## District Court’s Review of the Magistrate Judge’s Report on Consent  

The jurisdictional step in docket No. 24‑3051 involved a magistrate‐judge’s post‑incident report.  The magistrate examined the body‑camera footage and, applying the precedential standard that consent must be “freely and voluntarily” given (see *United States v. Banks*, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968)), determined that Lisa Coleman had consented to the officers’ entry.  The magistrate further noted that her consent was neither the product of coercion nor impermissible limitation on the officers’ investigative purpose; it fell within the “objective reasonableness” test for the scope of a consented search (*United States v. Saucedo*, 688 F.4th 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012)).  The district court, in its hearing, reviewed this report and fully adopted the magistrate’s findings.  The court’s brief pronouncement that “the magistrate judge’s report finding that Lisa did consent” served as the legal base for the officers’ subsequent entry and search.  

## The Defendant’s Motion to Suppress—Argument that No Consent Was Given  

Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms recovered from his residence, contending that Lisa had never actually granted permission for the officers to enter the home.  In his brief, he asserted that the language used by Officer Kizer (“We almost need to come inside”) conveyed coercive pressure and that Lisa’s intermittent hesitancy indicated a lack of clear, voluntary consent.  He further argued that the officers had no substantive authority, as the stepfather’s status—being a convicted felon—rendered any third‑party consent insufficient under the Fourth Amendment (“a third party may give consent to search a place ... and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party” – *United States v. Cellitti*, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004)).  His relief sought would have required the district court to find that the officers’ entry was otherwise unlawful and that the firearms must be excluded from trial.  

## District Court’s Denial of the Motion and its Findings on Consent and Scope  

Upon consideration of the motion, the district court expressly rejected Coleman’s contentions.  The court reiterated that Lisa’s consent was both voluntary and within the scope of the officers’ investigative purpose.  The district court particularly emphasized that the officers’ dialogue—committed to explicit concern for Lisa’s children—did not amount to ‘coercion’ and that the officers remained within “objective reasonableness” by focusing their search on preserving child welfare and verifying the presence of a dangerous firearm.  The court held that the officers did not overstep the limits the plaintiff’s consent had implied, a conclusion that directly supported the admissibility of the firearms as evidence.  By granting the magistrate’s recommendation, the district court thereby denied the suppression request.  

## The Defendant’s Guilty Plea with Reservation of Appellate Rights  

Following the denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea to the federal firearm offense under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).  He expressly reserved the right to challenge the appellate court’s decision on the suppression ruling.  This reservation maintained his conviction for the underlying misdemeanor while allowing him to assert on appeal that the evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment.  The plea effectively closed the trial phase and ushered the case into appellate review.  

## Appellate Focus: Evaluating the Correctness of the District Court’s Finding of Voluntariness and Scope  

The Seventh Circuit’s primary review focus in this appeal is whether the district court erred in its factual findings about consent and its legal assessment of scope.  The Court applies a “clear‑error” standard, giving deference to the district court unless the appellate tribunal finds the lower court’s conclusions not only erroneous but “clear” of any merit.  In doing so, the Court checks whether the officer’s request for entry was truly “freely and voluntarily” given in light of the “totality of circumstances” standard (see *United States v. Han*, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024); *United States v. Figueroa‑Espana*, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007)).  It also scrutinizes the officers’ intrusiveness against the objective‑reasonableness framework for scope (*United States v. Saucedo*, 688 F.4th 863, 865).  The appellate court therefore examines the body‑camera evidence for any indication of coercion or overreach, and whether the officers stayed within the confines that Lisa’s consent permitted.  

## Standard of Review for Appellate Courts: Question of Clear Error  

Under Seventh Circuit precedent, appellate review of a district court’s determination of consensual entry is conducted on a “question of clear error” basis.  The appellate court must first establish the standard of voluntariness, which mandatorily requires the “totality of the circumstances” test, and then the standard of scope, requiring objective reasonableness.  The Court reviews whether the district court misapplied those standards or drew facts from the record that are plainly wrong.  If the appellate judge finds no factual misapprehensions or misapplication of the legal principles, it will defer to the district court’s conclusions and affirm the prosecution’s use of the evidence.  In “United States v. Jaison Coleman,” the appellate court concluded that the district court had correctly applied both the voluntariness and scope tests and therefore found no clear error in its findings, leading to the affirmation of the conviction.
