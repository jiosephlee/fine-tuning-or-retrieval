Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Case Background and Procedural History

This chapter walks through the factual and procedural backdrop that led the Jimenez family to litigate before the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit.  It traces the family’s political involvement in Colombia, the threats and violence they endured, their decision to relocate to the United States without legal admission, and the successive immigration‑court and appellate proceedings that culminated in a petition for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252.

---

## Jimenez Family Political Background and Key Incidents of Intimidation

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, along with their two minor sons, hail from Bogotá, Colombia.  Prior to the family’s move to the United States, they worked in a local furniture factory and were active members of the Colombian Liberal Party, joining that party in 2012.  Their political engagement intensified during the 2022 presidential campaign, when they campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, the Liberal candidate.

Around the end of May 2022, Jimenez received threatening phone calls that were repeated once or twice each week.  Similar threats were directed at other Liberal Party affiliates and colleagues.  In early June, a campaign colleague was kidnapped and murdered despite prior reports of such threats.  These intimidation incidents set the stage for a sustained campaign of harassment, culminating in a series of violent encounters tied to the family’s political activities.

---

## Involvement of the Liberal Party and the 2022 Colombian Presidential Campaign

During the 2022 election season, the Jimenez family undertook door‑to‑door canvassing to garner support for Gutierrez in several local neighborhoods.  Their patriotic commitment to the Liberal Party, however, made them visible targets for rivals in the political arena.  The threats continued throughout 2022, and the family’s political stance was repeatedly cited as a justification by malevolent actors who sought to disrupt the Liberal Party’s campaign.

---

## Threats from Political Opponents and the Narcotrafficking Group Don Rafa

While Jimenez worked as a provisional law‑student‑and‑intern for Colombia’s National Prosecutor’s Office, he became involved in a property dispute.  The property in question was under a contested lease, where the tenant was revealed to be Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros—better known as “Don Rafa,” a prominent narcotrafficking figure aligned with the then‑president Gustavo Petro.  In June 2022, Jimenez attempted to mediate between the tenant and the property owner, discovering that the property had been shut down by health authorities for suspected human‑trafficking and prostitution activities.

The discovery of Jimenez’s investigative role triggered a series of escalating threats from individuals associated with Don Rafa.  The family was warned to desist from “snitching” and promised harm if they continued.  One night, men confronted Jimenez at the factory, threatened that they would kill him if he remained in Bogotá, and cautioned that the order came from Don Rafa.  The family was further intimidated when, while out of town, the same men targeted a factory employee, demanding information on Jimenez’s whereabouts and again referencing Don Rafa’s potential retribution.

---

## Property Dispute and Resultant Violence Experienced by the Family

The property dispute generated direct violence against Jimenez.  On a separate occasion, a man approached the family’s son as he exited school and forced his hand over the boy’s mouth, urging him to return to his father.  Jimenez intervened, pushing the assailant and ramming their two‑way sex between the boy and the attacker.  This confrontation, coupled with the cumulative battery of threats, convinced the family that the threats posed a tangible risk to their safety.

In the weeks following these violent episodes, Jimenez contacted a police patrol officer.  The officer admitted that no effective action could be taken and advised the family to leave Bogotá.  Following this conversation, the Jimenez family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the countryside for a brief period before ultimately deciding to emigrate to the United States.

---

## Forced Migration to the United States Without Admission or Parole

In late December 2022, the Jimenez family moved to the United States.  They entered the country without being admitted or paroled through any immigration‑law officer.  Their presence in the United States was therefore deemed “illegal” under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

Three months after arrival—so in late March 2023—the Department of Homeland Security issued each family member an individual Notice to Appear, charging them with removal as inadmissible under the Act.  The family’s case was placed before an immigration judge (IJ), giving them an opportunity to challenge the removal direction by filing asylum and withholding‑of‑removal applications.  Jimenez and Duarte testified that they sought protection due to the danger they faced in Colombia and that their minor children, as derivative beneficiaries, were at equal risk.  The IJ denied the asylum and withholding claims, citing the absence of any evidence that the family had experienced past persecution, that it would receive protection from the Colombian authorities, or that they were unlikely to relocate safely within Colombia.

---

## Administrative Process: Immigration Judge Denial, BIA Affiliation, and Appellate Filing

After the IJ denial, the Jimenez family appealed the removal order to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA).  The BIA, reviewing the case under 8 U.S.C. § 1471, affirmed the IJ’s decision, finding again no basis for asylum or withholding.  The family’s anticipatory questions regarding the due‑process of the IJ’s proceedings were likewise dismissed.

The family’s next step was to petition the federal appellate court for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, which permits the Tenth Circuit to review a final order of removal and related matters such as the denial of asylum relief.  The petition, filed in October 2025, was denied by the court; the court held that the family had failed to demonstrate past persecution, a nexus to a protected ground, or incapability of the Colombian government to protect them, and that the record did not support either asylum or withholding of removal.

In sum, the administrative pathway of the Jimenez family followed the sequence: No lawful admission → Notice to Appear → IJ denial of asylum and removal order → Appeal to the BIA → BIA affirmation → Petition for review by the Tenth Circuit → Denial of petition.  This procedural history, along with the factual allegations of political opposition and narcotrafficking‑related intimidation, frames the larger narrative of why the Court ultimately declined to grant the family relief under the asylum or withholding statutes.

# Legal Framework and Standards of Review

## Statistical Overview of Asylum as Discretionary Relief Under the INA

Asylum remains a discretionary gateway for noncitizens seeking protection from violence or persecution.  The Department of Homeland Security’s 2023 *Immigration Statistics* report shows that roughly 38 % of the 720,000 applications filed that year were granted, while 4 % were denied and the remainder were diverted to other relief categories.  These figures illustrate the tight control the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) places on asylum: less than one‑third of applicants ultimately receive protection.  The high denial rate underscores the ASA's stringent evidentiary standards and the legal framework that requires applicants to satisfy multiple interlocking burdens.  The parties in *Jimenez v. Bondi* were tested against these same thresholds, and the court examined whether their record met the statutory and policy constraints that govern discretionary admission into this pool of relief.

## Definition and Enumerated Protected Grounds in § 1101(a)(42)(A)

Section 1101(a)(42)(A) defines a “refugee” as someone who, outside any country of nationality, is unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.  This list is the sole statutory basis for asylum and withholding relief.  The Court’s language in *Niang v. Gonzales* emphasizes that the INA’s protection does not extend to non‑statutory categories such as “economic hardship” or “family reunification.”  Consequently, the Jimenez petitioners claimed that their persecution was based on political opinion—a ground the statute explicitly protects—and the adjudicators had to determine whether the facts they presented satisfied the statutory definition.

## Burden of Proof and the Preponderance Standard for Asylum

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1), the burden lies with the applicant to establish that he or she is a refugee.  The court adopts a preponderance of the evidence standard for the asylum trilemma: the applicant must convince the adjudicator that, on the balance of probabilities, all three elements of past persecution interlock.  In *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder* the court reiterated that the “onus is on the applicant to prove that he is a refugee by a preponderance of the evidence.”  The Jimenez case relied heavily on this standard; the immigration judge and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) found that the evidence did not meet the preponderance threshold, and the appeals panel upheld that finding.

## The Three‑Element Test for Past Persecution (Incidents, Protected Ground, State Involvement)

Past persecution is a factual determination that requires the presence of three co‑synchronous elements:

1. **An Incident or Incidents that Rise to the Level of Persecution** – The courts require that the experiences “compromise a fundamental right, entail personal threat to life or liberty, or exceed mere restrictions or threats” (*Wiransane v. Ashcroft*).  Threats alone rarely suffice (*Karki v. Holder*).
2. **Protected Ground** – The adverse action must be on account of one of the five enumerated grounds in § 1101(a)(42)(A).  *Rivera‑Barrientos* has made it clear that the persecution must be “on account of a protected characteristic” and not merely “incidentally related.”
3. **State Involvement or Complicity** – The persecutor must be the government or a force linked to it that either commits the acts directly or fails to protect the applicant from private violence (including “affiliation” or “influence” by a state actor).

In *Jimenez*, the Court evaluated the record under this trilemma.  The petitioners presented a series of phone threats, an assault, and a property dispute, but the adjudicators concluded that those incidents did not rise to the level of persecution and that the state was neither a direct actor nor demonstrably complicit.  The court repeatedly pointed to the distinction between “mere threats” and “persecution that harms or threatens life and liberty” to emphasize the narrowness of the past‑persecution standard.

## Well‑Founded Fear of Future Persecution and its Relation to the Asylum Standard

A “well‑founded fear of future persecution” is a matter of law derived from the evidence presented regarding past persecution and the government’s capacity to protect the applicant.  In *Niang*, the Court explained that an applicant who does not establish past persecution may still satisfy the precedent if he can show “detrastic fear that does not require state cooperation.”  Conversely, if past persecution is established, “the evaluator may presume that the applicant will be protected by the state,” and the applicant must rebut that presumption by proving the state is unwilling or unable to intervene (*Singh v. Bondi*).  This presumption collapses when the applicant can demonstrate that the state has taken specific action or has, by policy, failed to protect persons on the same protected ground.  The Jimenez petitioners contended that the Colombian government was ineffective, but the adjudicators found the evidence insufficient to rebut the presumption of protection, specifically noting the lack of formal complaint and the isolated interactions with individual officers.

## Withholding of Removal: Requirement of Satisfying Asylum Elements

Withholding relief is governed by 8 U.S.C. § 1155(a).  The Court has consistently held that “withholding of removal is a discretionary form of relief that requires satisfaction of the same factual trilemma that applies to asylum” (*Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*).  Only when the applicant meets the asylum criteria can he claim withholding; otherwise, the “preventive” standard is denied.  In Jimenez, the admonition that the could not establish asylum “consequen­tly fails the higher standard” demonstrates the precise legal logic: failure to meet asylum equals automatic denial of withholding.

## Standard of Review Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and the Substantial‑Evidence Test

The Tenth Circuit reviews BIA decisions under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A).  The standard “is substantial evidence” – the BIA’s findings are conclusive unless the record shows that **any reasonable adjudicator** would conclude to the contrary (*Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*).  The court also may consult the Immigration Judge’s record for clarity, but it cannot replace the BIA’s findings.  In *Jimenez*, the appellate panel evaluated the IJ’s findi­ngs of prejudice and the BIA’s denial of asylum under this standard and concluded that “the record supports a substantial‑evidence basis” for the BIA’s denial.  The appellate court affirmed the BIA because it rejected the petitioner’s contention that evidence was insufficient or unsubstantiated.

## Appellate Review Scope Over the Immigration Judge and BIA Findings

Section 1252(a)(1) and (b)(9) grant jurisdiction to appellate review of “final orders of removal” and “consolidated asylum claims.”  The Court stresses that “the scope of review is limited to the record upon which the removal order was based” (*Singh v. Bondi*).  Accordingly, the appellate court focused primarily on the BIA’s findings and the IJ’s supporting record.  The court explicitly rejected the petitioners’ attempt to reopen due‑process arguments—those were extrinsic to the review of the affirmative claim of asylum and withholding.  The plan of the appellate review is restricted to “whether the record would support the finding that the applicant was a refugee” and “whether the BIA erred in interpreting the law” (*Uanreroro v. Gonzales*).

## Dismissal of the Petitioners’ Due‑Process Claim as a Matter of Scope

The Jimenez petitioners also advanced a due‑process argument, challenging the IJ’s handling of counsel.  Under the review scope language in § 1252(b), the appellate court is not required to consider “issues adjudicated in a separate proceeding” unless they were “inherent to the denial of relief.”  The Court treated the due‑process claim as outside the panel’s purview because it was not raised on the merits at the IJ or BIA level and did not “directly affect weel‑founded fear or past persecution.”  In *Osorio‑Mora­les v. Garland*, the Fourth Circuit made this point precisely: “an appellate court need not revisit that claim unless it implicates the adjudicative standards directly.”  Accordingly, the decision dismissed the due‑process argument as beyond the scope of the petition for review.

***

In sum, this chapter has traced the statutory underpinnings and evidentiary thresholds that shaped the *Jimenez v. Bondi* decision, highlighted how the three‑element past‑persecution test operates, and clarified the appellate review framework that ultimately governed the court’s denial.  The intersection of these doctrines—discretionary asylum, the “well‑founded fear” standard, withholding’s higher bar, and the substantial‑evidence review—provides the lens through which law students should analyze this and similar immigration‑law decisions.

# Past Persecution Analysis

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court’s central task was to determine whether the petitioners had proven “past persecution” in a manner sufficient to satisfy the statutory requirements for asylum. The review focused on whether the incidents described in the record—numerous threatening phone calls, men at the family’s factory, and a confrontation on a school street—raised to the level of persecution. The court applied the established threshold, the cumulative‑effect doctrine, and the evidentiary test for physical, psychological, and emotional harm.  In doing so, it relied on the Ninth and Tenth‑Circuit precedent cited in the opinion, particularly *Karki*, *Brandy*, *Carias‑Mejia*, and *Chhetri*, to reach the conclusion that the petitioners’ experiences fall short of past persecution.

## Threshold for past persecution: more than mere threats or restrictions

The First Amendment microscope has long required that a finding of persecution be based on more than isolated threats or restrictions.  The court reiterated that “a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm ... that is offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty” (*Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893).  Accordingly, the standard is twofold: (1) the applicant must have endured treatment that is offensive to a reasonable person, and (2) that treatment must go beyond mere intimidation.  The petitioners reported only threats—brief phone calls, a brochure, and violent intimidation at the factory.  None of these isolated acts, in and of themselves, satisfy the threshold for persecution because they do not yet amount to “offensive” harm.

## Cumulative effect of multiple incidents in asylum analysis

When evaluating aggregated incidents, courts look at the cumulative impact that a series of events may have on the applicant.  The cumulative‑effect doctrine is tested against the preponderance‑of‑the‑evidence standard: an individual facts‑watcher must see enough to conclude that a reasonable adjudicator would find past persecution.  Though the petitioners faced several incidents, the court found that, in sum, they amounted to threat and intimidation without a sustained pattern of physical or psychological harm.  The incidents were “sporadic” and not linked to a broader campaign of abuse.  Thus, their accumulation did not cross the line into persecution, as the court noted that “the cumulative effect of these threats does not elevate the situation to persecution.”

## Analysis of physical, psychological, and emotional harm claims

The petitioners’ only substantive claim of harm was psychological.  *Brandy v. Holder* (590 F. App’x 744, 747) clarified that an emotional or psychological response must be “more than a mere restriction or sporadic threat.”  In *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland* (860 F. App’x 121, 124), the court held that threats with a weapon, coupled with repeated robbery, did not, on their own, amount to persecution without accompanying physical harm.  Similarly, *Chhetri v. Rosen* (844 F. App’x 23, 27) found that four telephone threats and an ambiguous in‑person threat fell short of past persecution due to a lack of substantive injury.

Applying those precedents to the record, the court found that:

* **Physical harm**: The petitioners experienced no bodily injury.  This absence precludes meeting the “more than mere restrictions” bar when combined with psychological descriptors alone.

* **Psychological harm**: The petitioners’ parents claimed sleep disturbances and panic attacks, yet the court treated these as “psychological distress” that, individually or cumulatively, remains “more than a mere restriction or sporadic threat.”  The distress level did not square with the standard that demands a qualitatively significant, ongoing ordeal.

* **Emotional harm**: This overlaps with psychological harm; without the backing of more concrete evidence of lasting harm, emotional distress alone could not be deemed sufficient.

Because threats alone, even when repeated, cannot substitute for real or substantial harm, the court rejected the petitioners’ claim that the experiences rose to persecution.

## Court’s application of precedent: Karki, Brandy, Carias‑Mejia, and Chhetri

The court explicitly used the cited cases to delimit the analysis:

* **Karki v. Holder** (715 F.3d 792, 805) emphasized that threats do not constitute persecution in the meaningful sense required by the INA unless accompanied by other injurious conduct.

* **Brandy v. Holder** demanded a higher threshold for psychological distress; the court found the petitioners’ reported stress insufficient.

* **Carias‑Mejia v. Garland** and **Chhetri v. Rosen** both spotlighted the need for a closer nexus between threat and physical or extreme psychological injury—something absent in the petitioners’ record.

By carrying these doctrinal standards into the record, the court concluded that the evidence failed to meet the threshold.

## Rationale for deeming threats alone insufficient for persecution

The court explained that while a sequence of threats may, in some contexts, be “dealing towards persecution,” the petitioners’ threats were “isolated, sporadic, and not accompanied by sustained or escalating danger.”  Therefore, the court applied the *Karki* and *Brandy* standards to decide that threats alone are a subtle and disquieting nuisance, not an egregious persecution episode.  The claim leveled against the petitioners involved intimidation by violent actors, but the petitioners failed to show that this intimidation threatened life or confined them under instruments that would jeopardize bodily integrity.

## Evidence supporting the BIA’s conclusion of absent past persecution

The court weighed the evidence in the record:

* No documented physical harm or injury suffered by Jimenez or Angelica.
* Threats were limited to phone calls, a brochure, kill‑when‑snitching verbal assault, and a single confrontation at the factory.  None of these produced tangible harm or a sustained pattern.
* The petitioners’ emotional arguments hinged on generalized anxiety and panic, unsupported by more decisive evidence (e.g., medical or treatment records).
* The BIA’s denial was grounded in the absence of tangible evidence of persecution; the court found that the administrative record “shows substantial evidence of lack of past persecution.”

Because the record supports the BIA’s conclusions, the appellate court, applying the substantial‑evidence standard for findings of fact, affirmed that the petitioners did not establish past persecution.

In sum, the court disciplined the petitioners’ claims by insisting on a markedly high bar: more than isolated threats, more than generalized anxiety, and more than sporadic intimidation.  The influence of *Karki*, *Brandy*, *Carias‑Mejia*, and *Chhetri* sharpened that bar, and the court concluded unanimously that the petitioners’ experiences failed to pass the threshold for past persecution.

# Nexus, Government Protection, and Future Persecution

## Nature of the Nexus Requirement and Central‑Reason Test

The asylum nexus—whether past persecution was “on account of” a protected ground—constitutes the second element of the refugee definition. In *Niang* v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187 (10th Cir. 2005), the court reiterated that a claimant must prove that past persecution was *at least* one central reason for the harm, not merely an incidental or tangential factor. The central‑reason test is anchored in *Rivera‑Barrientos* v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641 (10th Cir. 2012), which instructs review of the Board’s nexus findings only to see whether the record “supports” that at least one protected ground was a central reason for the persecution. A claimant who demonstrates that a persecutor’s motive was a *mix* of reasons must show that the protected ground was *at least as important* as the other motives. That standard—expressed in *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i))—operates as a “substantial‑evidence” check: if the Board’s findings rest on data that, if viewed from a reasonable perspective, could have led to the opposite conclusion, the appellate court must reverse.

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Board’s nexus analysis rested on the allegation that the petitioners’ Latin‑American political engagement—supporting a presidential candidate—rendered them targets of credible threats and intimidation. The Board and the IJ recognized three elements under the nexus test:

1. *Past persecution*; 2. *Persecution on account of a protected ground*; 3. *The threat of future persecution* (irrelevant for the nexus analysis but relevant for the overall asylum grant).

Only the second element—whether the persecution was on account of a protected ground—was at issue in this chapter; the court found that the Board’s conclusion was supported by the record as it turned out that *political opinion* was not a **central** reason for the persecution. The refusal to satisfy the central‑reason test effectively negated the petitioner’s answer to the *Niang* burden, and made the case inoperative for further scrutiny of *future persecution* or *withholding of removal*.

## Distinguishing Political Opinion from Criminal Affiliation in Asylum Cases

The asylum statute protects political opinion, defined as beliefs or attitudes “regarding politics,” such as beliefs about the functioning, direction, or composition of a government (see *Niang*). By contrast, criminal affiliation—being a member of a criminal organization—cannot be a basis for asylum, except in the limited “crime‑related” relief constructions addressed in *Reagan* and *Karyoliv* (which are outside the scope of the present opinion). A mischaracterization of a violent or criminal context as purely political often leads to a factual error. The court in *Jimenez* distinguished political opinion from a criminal affiliation by examining the nature of the persecutors and the context of the violence. 

The petitioners’ argument that they were targeted because of their political support for an opposition candidate was countered by a series of facts: the threats and aggressions stemmed from a narcotrafficking organization, led by Don Rafa, rather than a purely political adversary. The court underscored that the group’s primary interest was the alleged interference of Jimenez in a property dispute and subsequent allegations of law‑enforcement action at the property. No evidence suggested that since the beginning of the persecution, the claimants’ political affiliation was *at least as important* as the criminal motive. Hence, the petitioners’ attempts to subsume the kind of violence they endured under “political persecution” failed to meet the *central‑reason* requirement.

## Role of Don Rafa and the Narcotrafficking Group in the Alleged Persecution

Don Rafa, a known narcotrafficking leader aligned with the current Colombian president, was the principal actor behind the harassment and violence against the petitioners. The facts show that:

- Jiménez’s investigation of a tenant’s property—an ex‑husband affiliated with Don Rafa—triggered a series of threats and physical confrontations.
- Victims of the group generally comment on threats to be “snitches for police” and “police collaborator” rather than political opponents.
- The property at issue was a known narcotrafficking base, closed by the health department for illicit activities.

Therefore, the threats and violence fall into the realm of criminal coercion rather than political intimidation. Because the primary motive of the persecutors lies within the criminal nexus, it is not reasonable to infer that the petitioners’ political views were the central reason for the persecution. 

## Court’s Reasoning that Jiménez’s Political Activity Was Not the Primary Motive

The court noted that the *central‐reason* test requires political opinion to play a *major* role in the persecution, not marginal or superficial. In *Jiménez*, the court considered two strands of evidence of political activity and of criminal motive. In the first step, the court examined the record for any indication that the terrorist group’s threats directly referenced the petitioners’ political affiliation. The only political mention was found after Jiménez’s involvement in the property dispute. This chronology suggested that the group’s hostility was retaliatory for a legal and possibly law‑enforcement related action, not for political campaigning. 

The court also weighed the testimony that the petitioners continued campaigning while under threat in May and June 2022, and that the group intensified its aggression only after Jiménez confronted Don Rafa. As *Rivera‑Barrientos* clarifies, a single motive—with the claim that it was central—must be supported by “reasonably enough evidence.” The court concluded that there was no *sufficient* evidence that Jiménez’s political motivation was *at least as important* as the criminal motive. Accordingly, the Board’s denial of the nexus found that, as the court said, “Petitioners fail to establish that requirement because there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA and IJ’s findings that Petitioners were primarily targeted because Jimenez threatened Don Rafa’s revenue and security, not because of their political opinion.”

## Government Unwillingness/Unavailability Standard: Notice, Response, and Systemic Failure

The “unable or unwilling” standard is triggered when an appellant claims the host government is *incompetent* or *unwilling* to protect them from past persecution. The *IMM* statutory definition (8 U.S.C. § 1105‑2) instructs that *notice* of the persecution and *response* from the government are key factors. In *Jiménez* the court followed *Aviles‑Gonzalez* v. Garland (2024) and *Osorio‑Mora* v. Garland (2023) to distinguish between *local* police apathy and *systemic* failure. 

### Notice

The petitioners’ record contains extremely limited attempts at reporting. The fact that they spoke with a single police officer who dismissed them does not constitute serious *notice* of the government to the broader public order or law‑enforcement system. The court observed that “Petitioners’ attempts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any.” (See *Jiménez*, discussion D). Therefore the court could not conclude that the officials had sufficient knowledge of the persecution events to adjust a protective response.

### Response

The record shows a single citizen‑lawyer (Mr. Jiménez) and his father, both “currently work for the government in Colombia,” unresponsive to the persecution. The court considered that “the record does not show that Petitioners sought help outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jiménez’s law‑enforcement contacts.” The lack of a tangible government undertaking to protect the petitioners renders the claim of unwillingness or unavailability untenable.

### Systemic Failure

An essential element in the *government unwillingness* inquiry is establishing that the government consistently fails to respond to widespread or pervasive anti‑victim actions. *Jiménez* cited the reasoning that a *one‑off* encounter with negligent officers “does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” The barangue in *Osorio‑Mora* notes that a “systemic failure” is required. Here, the lack of any credible evidence of government action and the inevitable ‘local’ obstruction are insufficient. Accordingly, the court could not find a *systemic* lack of government protection.

## Evaluation of Petitioners’ Attempts to Seek Protection and Their Limited Scope

Petitioners’ claim that they could not obtain police assistance is undermined by the nature of the attempted contact. The record includes:

- A single conversation with a police officer that was dismissive and non‑formal;
- No complaint lodged with a municipal or national law‑enforcement agency;
- No evidence that the petitioners engaged law‑enforcement beyond that initial contact.

Translating these facts into the *government protection* element, the court concluded that “Petitioners’ efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any.” (See *Jiménez*, Discussion, bullet “Evaluation of petitioners’ attempts to seek protection…”) The court further highlighted that “Petitioners failed to show that the Colombian government would be” *unable* or *unwilling* to intervene, which is the vital requirement. In short, there is a *lack of adequate notice* and *response* which render the petitioners’ proximate sense of futility unsubstantiated.

## Assessment of the Claim that the Government Would Be Unable or Unwilling to Intervene

While the petitioners argued that the “persecutors are closely aligned and associated with the Colombian government, which prevents safe internal relocation,” the court rejected this notion. Evidence shows that:

- The Colombian government was aware of the illicit activity at the property via health‑department sanctions and attempted searches;
- Don Rafa’s ties to the president were long‑standing, but this relationship does not automatically make the government absent from *law‑enforcement* responsibilities;
- Without concrete evidence that the government actively *permitted* or *supported* the persecutors, the court relied on *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, holding that “without such reporting… we have no way of knowing how police would respond.”

The court applied the *unwilling/unavailable* analysis positing that “there is no reliance on a single episode of lackluster police conduct to infer systemic unwillingness.” The board’s findings were supported by the record that the authorities did not provide any credible evidence that they were unable or unwilling in a systemic sense.

## Court’s Conclusion that Insufficient Evidence Exists for a Well‑Founded Fear of Future Persecution

Once the court had established that (1) the nexus fails and (2) the government is neither unable nor unwilling to provide protection, the only remaining basis for an asylum grant would be a *well‑founded fear of future persecution*. However, the asylum standard requires:

1. *Past persecution* (already denied);
2. *Well‑founded fear of future persecution* (absent due to nexus failure and government inability).

Because the *nexus* element is a precondition, the denial is final. The court reaffirmed that, since the petitioners cannot satisfy any one of the three absolute prerequisites for 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A) (persecution, nexus, well‑founded fear), the Board’s denial is not reversible. The court’s summary reasoning—“there is insufficient evidence to support the BIA’s finding that Petitioners have a well‑founded fear of future persecution”—warrants unconditional dismissal of the petitioner’s claim for asylum and, consequently, the *withholding of removal* claim.

---

**Bottom Line for Students:** In *Jiménez*, the court methodically applied the asylum nexus test (central‑reason) and found political motivation peripheral. It distinguished criminal affiliation from political opinion by focusing on the nature of the persecutors and the events that precipitated the threat. When evaluating *government unwillingness*, the court required substantial evidence of systemic failure, which the petitioners did not provide. Finally, because *at least one* of the three core asylum premises was lacking, the entire claim collapsed, leaving the petitioner denied and the appellate review dismissed.

# Safe Relocation, Final Holding, and Significance

## Safe Relocation Standard Under Asylum Law and Regulatory Guidance

The safe‑relocation doctrine is one of the key safeguards that protects victims of persecution from automatic asylum grants when a credible risk of future persecution can be mitigated by internal relocation. The statutory framework is found in 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1) and 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A). Where an applicant has limited evidence of a well‑founded fear of future persecution, the asylum adjudicating officer may refuse relief if the applicant can show that relocation to another part of the country would likely be “safe and reliable” (see 8 CFR § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)). When an applicant _can_ take the step of relocation, the presumption of a well‑founded fear is rebutted under a substantial‑evidence standard. The Supreme Court has held that this standard is a “protective shield” reliable for parties who have not been able to substantiate the direct link between the state and a specific victim. The Tenth Circuit follows this approach, emphasizing that the burden rests on the government or the applicant to demonstrate internal relocation is reasonably attainable.

In the present case the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and the Immigration Judge (IJ) concluded that Petitioners could relocate within Colombia, thereby rebutting a presumption of a well‑founded fear under § 1158(b). The appellate court thus reviewed that finding under the same substantial‑evidence standard.

## Evidence Indicating Possible Relocation Away from the Threatened Area

Petitioners met“filming state by the presence of a home in the countryside, their connection to the federal government's criminal‑justice system,” and the fact that they had lived with Jiménez’s mother for several weeks prior to emigrating. The record specifically indicated that the family had continued to reside in parts of Bogotá and the surrounding countryside, experiencing few or no threats outside the specific factory area. The alleged escalation in threats and violence – notably the gun‑point intimidation and kidnapping attempts – were tied to Don Rafa’s territory in the Bogota center. Petitioners themselves acknowledged that the men who threatened them had offered a “stay‑in‑the‑country” admonition.

The BIA’s safety analysis was not based on the abstract: Petitioners had "family and relatives who remained in Colombia" and had been able to seek help from local authorities with minimal success. The IJ found no evidence that they had rejected internal relocation efforts because of the threat. Importantly, the record also shows that the family was cared for during the three‑week period in the countryside and that there were no subsequent requests for relocation or denial of such requests by local authorities. Consequently, the court was credibly satisfied that Petitioners could reasonably move to rural or suburban areas without leaving Colombia, thereby undermining the presumption of a future threat.

## Evaluation of the Family’s Ability to Relocate Within Colombia and Mitigate Risk

The Court considered the “bidirectional” nature of safe relocation: the claimant must show that relocation would _protect_ the claimant from persecution, and that the relocation is _appropriate_ and appears readily achievable. This evaluation requires the same weighty evidence that a real threat of persecution exists, but the courts also examine whether all the government’s information is known and whether there are clear evidence of systemic impunity.

The BIA’s and IJ’s findings were substantially supported by court‑approved criteria. The authors noted that _Petitioners’ pro‑government contacts_ – although “clergy‑government” – did not translate into guarded protection because the “officials who advised them to stay out of trouble were individual officers who could not shield the family.” These officers were “deemed unwilling or lacking the capacity” to provide protective custody or an effective threat response. Nonetheless, the court found that the lack of such a response did not preclude the possibility of safe relocation because the applicants had not yet sought any formal government assistance that might demonstrate a systemic obstacle.

Moreover, in litigation such as *Rio Vegetales v. Snyder*, a Tenth Circuit precedent, the court emphasized that internal relocation is more credible when an applicant has documented family ties to a safe region. The aggressive presence of the Colombian state in Bogotá, as evidenced by prior attempts at property seizures and internal investigations, did not prove that petitioners could not shift to a safer part of the country. Accordingly, the court concluded that the “risk of both domestic and cross‑border persecution could have been minimized by safe relocation.”

## Linkage Between Failed Asylum Claim and Withholding of Removal Denial

Asylum and withholding of removal share a single factual foundation under the INA: a "well‑founded fear of future persecution." The court’s holding that the Petitioners could safe‑relocate thereby rebutting a presumption of a well‑founded fear is, in the law, identical for both remedies. The BIA's rejection of the asylum claim under the political‑opinion nexus standard automatically entailed that the petitioners also failed to meet the threshold for withholding. Indeed, in *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*, the Tenth Circuit had already affirmed that the same standard applies. *Jimenez v. Bondi* reaffirms this link: the denial of asylum under the acceptable political‑opinion criteria is the basis for dismissal of the withholding claim. Failure to meet the safe‑relocation threshold directly undermines the “internal relocation” element required to rebut the presumption.

## Overall Holding: Denial of Petition for Review and Reaffirmation of BIA Decision

The Court, citing *Singh v. Bondi*, declined to review issues beyond the BIA’s findings. Under the limited jurisdiction granted by 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and the review standard, the Court affirmed the BIA’s conclusions regarding both asylum and withholding. The underlying reasoning was:

1. Petitioner’s alleged threats and emotional distress *did not aggregate to persecution*. The standard requires more than “sp(heme) threats or psychological harm”; it requires actual physical harm or a persistent, demonstrable threat.
2. The supposedly political‑opinion motive was *tangential* to the actual motive (real‑world grey‑market enforcement) because the threat was triggered by a property dispute, not the electoral activity itself.
3. Petitioners had recourse to internal relocation, as indicated by a lack of documented unsuccessful attempts at seeking official protection and by their capacity to remain in a safe region of Colombia.

In light of those findings, the Court denied the petition for review and thereby upheld the BIA’s denials of asylum and withholding of removal. The Court emphasized that “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety” (Peters’ Brief 18), but that the court would not “grant asylum based on hypothetical hardships” absent concrete evidence.

## Legal Implications for Future Asylum Applicants

The case reinforces three critical thresholds:

1. **Persecution Standard** – Threats alone, when not accompanied by palpable physical harm or a sustained pattern of coercion, are insufficient. Applicants should provide evidence of *actual* harm or credible, *systemic* persecution.
2. **Nexus to Protected Ground** – The state must demonstrate that a protected ground (here, political opinion) is an *independent*, central factor in the persecution, not a superficial or secondary motive. Mixed‑motive claims must weigh heavily on the protected ground to the satisfaction of the adjudicating officers.
3. **Safe Relocation** – Even in the face of credible threats, applicants can defeat the presumption of a well‑founded fear through *circumstantial* evidence that safe relocation is practicable. Demonstrating family ties to other regions, having previously lived in safe zones, and showing that no systematic impediment exists to relocation can all support a dismissal of the asylum claim.

Failure on any one of these troika—past persecution, nexus, and safe relocation—renders an asylum claim moot. Moreover, because the same standards underlie the withholding of removal, applicants must meet them in order to secure either remedy.

## Significance of the Case for Tenth Circuit Jurisprudence on Asylum and Withholding

*Jimenez v. Bondi* is a textbook illustration of the Circuit’s consistent emphasis on **substantial evidence** as the gatekeeper in asylum adjudication and the binding *two‑step* analysis of past persecution and safe relocation. It distinguishes itself by reaffirming the litigant’s burden in providing *cumulative* evidence of persecution and a *central* nexus to a protected ground in political‑opinion cases. As *Singh v. Bondi* clarified oversight of numerous final orders of removal, this decision further delineates the court’s jurisdictional reach and demonstrates the limited scope of appellate review under § 1252.

Furthermore, the case signals to future applicants that the judicial apparatus closes a ***well‑founded fear* conclusion swiftly when the existential risk can be mitigated by safe relocation*, indeed rendering the asylum denial a direct precursor to withholding denial. The Tenth Circuit thereby articulates, through concrete facts, the *legal appetite* for rigorous evidence that a claimant truly meets all three prongs of the asylum standard, or risk being denied on both fronts.
