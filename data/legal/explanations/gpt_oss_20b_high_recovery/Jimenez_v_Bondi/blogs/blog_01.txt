Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# When Threats Fall Short: Jimenez v. Bondi and the Tenth Circuit’s Reassessed Standard for Past Persecution  

## Why This Case Matters  

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit reasserted the bar on asylum claims built on a trail of threats that never escalated to violence. The opinion, penned by Judge Tymkovich, stakes its ground in the 1986 Immigration and Nationality Act’s definition of “past persecution” and in a federally established standard that requires more than merely intimidating words. The decision is a reminder that, for the last two decades, courts have consistently demanded hard, tangible evidence of harm before granting the protection of a refugee. For Latin American applicants—whose stories are often peppered with intimidation rather than overt violence—*Jimenez* signals that the threshold remains unforgiving.

## A Brief Fact Pattern  

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, along with their two sons, arrived in the United States in December 2022 after a year of political campaigning in Bogotá. They had been active members of the Colombian Liberal Party and had even volunteered at the National Prosecutor’s office, helping domestic abuse victims. In late 2022, they faced a spate of phone calls, a threatening brochure, and subsequent armed intimidation incidents tied to a narcotics‑affiliated gang leader named “Don Rafa” who had political ties to the Colombian president. The attackers explicitly linked Jiménez’s perceived cooperation with law enforcement to their threats. Despite these harrowing encounters, Jiménez and his family were not physically harmed.

The Immigration Judge (IJ) denied their asylum and withholding of removal applications; the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) upheld the denial. A petition for review on § 1252 was filed but was dismissed by the Tenth Circuit as the parties had failed to demonstrate “past persecution, nexus, or government protection” under the asylum framework.

## A Rigorous Standard for Past Persecution  

The Oval authored the court’s doctrinal backdrop: “*We have held that a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm…more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty*” (Tymkovich, 2025). The court relies on the classic tripartite test from *Niang v. Gonzales*: (1) an event(s) that rise to the “level” of persecution; (2) on account of a statutorily protected ground; (3) committed by a persecutor whom the government cannot or will not control. In addition, a priori, “the record must show that a substantive *harm*—not merely a threat—occurred” (*Jimenez*, 2025).

Judge Tymkovich explicitly draws a line at “threats alone,” distinguishing them from a “cumulative pattern of actual harm.” She notes the Ninth Circuit’s precedent that threats, though unnerving, do not "constitute persecution" when the applicant has been “not physically harmed” (Karki v. Holder, 2013; *Jimenez*, 2025). She further emphasizes that a past “peaceful” campaigner faces a higher burden if the threats do not translate into tangible damage. Hence, the court upheld the *substantial‑evidence* standard and found no sufficient damage to meet the threshold.

## The Nexus and the “Political Opinion” Clause  

Even after the bulk of the decision, the court turned to the “nexus” prong, the “most complex” of the asylum inquiry’s three components. Here, Tymkovich writes that applicants “must establish that** the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for their persecution” ( *Jimenez*, 2025). The petitioner’s narrative — that they campaigned for a rival candidate — was eclipsed by evidence that their direct conflict with Don Rafa stemmed from a property dispute and Jiménez’s role as a “provisional lawyer” aiding a former partner of the gang. Consequently, the nexus “collapsed” because the aggression “was not at least one central reason” for targeting the family but was instead a “ritual” of intimidation over property and alleged “snitching” ( *Jimenez*, 2025).

This allocation clarifies that even when a persecutor is indeed aligned with a state actor, the tortuous interplay of motives matters. The court, underscoring *Dallakoti v. Holder*, reaffirms that a protected ground must be a *central* element, not a peripheral one.

## Government Protection and Safe Relocation  

The “unable or unwilling” prong is effectively a winnowing filter, and the court found the record too thin. The litigants' own admissions—“we did not report to the Colombian police”—combined with brief, fruitless conversations with “unhelpful” officers, said the court, were “not enough to situs this status.” Even though the government had attempted some law‑enforcement action against Don Rafa’s property, the court pointed out that the *record did not show that they had *aware* of the individual threats or that they had a specific duty to step in.*

Safe relocation, the court’s concluding point, was deemed “reasonable” because the family could have moved within Colombia and had relatives who remained free of harm. A few desperate weeks spent at the family’s rural house proved that any perceived danger was local and not a physical threat to the entire country ( *Jimenez*, 2025).

## My Take: A Sticky Hierarchy of Evidence  

Judge Tymkovich’s decision is consistent with a 10‑year line of Tenth Circuit precedent that privileges *actual* harm over *innocuous* intimidation. The doctrine is unambiguously both *inclusive* and *exclusive*; it retains the right to protect those seriously harmed, yet refuses to extrapolate protection from the pattern of threat alone. This raises a palpable question: In a climate where “terrorizing” squads pose daily threats to opposition figures without ever drawing a bullet, are we—by design or by oversight—failing an international public‑health crisis in the form of unarmed violence? The court does not answer that hotter policy question here, but the ruling does underline that asylum’s political‑opinion nexus remains an interpretive bottleneck.

The main doctrinal take‑away is the *central‑reason rule* for the nexus: a single protected ground cannot be subservient to other motives if the deficiency hence is too marginal. Combined with the admission that *past persecution* has to be “more than a threat,” the decision closes a narrow but important window of opportunity for those that only have a “feared” future beyond revived political campaigns.

## Downstream Effects for Latin Amerindian Asylum Seekers  

For potential Angelica Duarte, the family, and the broader Colombian diaspora, *Jimenez* sends a noting message. If an applicant cannot document physical harm, the asylum clock starts to tick in a different direction. Even with the *cumulative‑effects* rationale, the judge’s mapping demonstrates that a “pattern” of threats is not enough. Thus, future requests—especially from Latin Americans where threats from political gang affiliates proliferate without the resulting violence—are likely to face the same fatalistic micro‑analysis: a threat cannot become persecution without tangible, specific harm. 

Moreover, the decision reinforces the idea that *safe relocation* in the home country is a first‑tier solution: just obtaining proof that one could move to a safer region is entirely sufficient to defeat an asylum claim. The bar for the “unable or unwilling” standard is, as the court stressed, high. Applicants cannot rely on the plain fact that “the government is jacked up” without documented attempts at protection. The result? An asylum claim built on a trophy of intimidation is, above all, probably a footnote, with the ILO in its peacetime battles and the families fighting to escape them forced along more limited legal avenues.

## Final Takeaway  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* does not dramatically shift asylum law. The court echoes a lineage of decisions that hold “past persecution” to the degree of *material* harm. The outcome gives the Tenth Circuit’s left‑handed, “threat‑doesn’t‐count” rule a kind of reaffirmation. For Latin American applicants a pattern of intimidation—together with tenuous governmental failures—remains an uphill battle. Navigating asylum amid political violence that management tools or vehement threats can provide but cannot inflict a bullet may well remain outside the reach of the domestic asylum mechanism. That is the simple, hopeful-to‑some, and frankly disheartening side of *Jimenez*—a supremely rational, but still deeply human, application of the INA’s refugee standards.
