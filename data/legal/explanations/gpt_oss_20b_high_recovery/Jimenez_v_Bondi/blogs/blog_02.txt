Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Safe in the Homeland? The Tenth Circuit’s Narrow Safe Relocation Doctrine in *Jimenez v. Bondi*  

## Introduction  

When a U.S. court refuses to grant asylum, the justifications are usually spelled out: the applicant must exhibit past persecution, the persecution must be on a protected ground, the persecution must be foreseeable, and, if they cannot be deterred from returning, the home country must be unwilling or unable to provide protection. The *Jimenez v. Bondi* decision of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit underscores a subtle but significant bottleneck in the internal‑relocation hurdle—a requiring applicant‑centric proof of unattainable safety coupled with a near‑universal summoning of “well‑documented knowledge” of government failure.  

This post explores why that hurdle matters—especially for Colombian and other Latin American asylum seekers—and how the Tenth Circuit’s strict reading might ripple across other circuits. By teasing out the court’s reasoning and comparing it to other jurisdictions, we hope to crystallize the stakes for the next round of asylum applications.

## The Facts in Brief  

*Jimenez* presented a compelling, if tragic, anecdote: a Colombian political activist and his wife, each well‑versed in Colombian party politics, faced a cascade of threats, kidnapping threats, and outright violence after an incidental property dispute with “Don Rafa,” a narcotics‑fueled terror paramilitary figure aligned with the Colombian government.  The family fled to the United States in December 2022 after an attack on their son.  

Despite the harrowing narrative, the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) found no past persecution, no political‑opinion nexus, and no proven government unwillingness to protect them. They affirmed their removal orders, and the Tenth Circuit confirmed the same in *Jimenez*, denying petitioners’ request for review.  

## The Safe‑Relocation Prong: Where Law Meets Reality  

The crux of *Jimenez* rests on the safe‑relocation exception to the standard for “well‑founded fear.”  The Supreme Court, through *Singh v. Bondi* (2025), positioned the safe‑relocation analysis as a factual question: if the applicant can show that relocation would mitigate future risk, then asylum is barred.  The Tenth Circuit, brewing on that doctrine, defined the “well‑documented knowledge” of governmental failure in a way that, in effect, worded the bar to any credible claim that safety lies elsewhere in the home country.  

The court assessed evidence through the standard laid out by *Rivera‑Barrientos*, a “substantial‑evidence” test that refuses to leap to an asylum conclusion absent a clear factual foundation: “the record must show that a reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude, not merely that a reasonable adjudicator might be persuaded.”  The court applied that test both to the past‑persecution claims and to the safe‑relocation matrix.

### “Well‑Documented Knowledge” of Government Failure

In *Jimenez*, the Ninth and Eleventh Circuits have sometimes treated government failure as a “systemic” question—questions about the state’s capacity or willingness to protect its citizens.  The Tenth Circuit singled out the want of “well‑documented knowledge” as a key requirement.  The court explained:

> “Petitioners’ minimal attempts to report persecution—speaking to a single police officer in their immediate neighborhood, refusing to file formal complaints—inconsequentially, did not establish that any reasonable adjudicator would know that the Colombian authorities had failed or been unable to protect them.”

By sidestepping any presumption that a state’s entanglement with paramilitaries automatically equates to unwillingness to protect, the court reinforced a “proof‑above‑probability” threshold.  In short, if you’re hoping for safe relocation, you must skewer the government with hard, verifiable evidence.  Threats to the family’s safety are insufficient if an applicant did not request the authorities to intervene or testify to systemic failures.  

### The Family’s Presence in Rural Bogota: A Non‑Guarantee  

A significant theme in *Jimenez* is the court’s refusal to infer safety from the mere presence of relatives.  The plaintiffs’ father and mother each lived in the countryside and, arguably, “had no record of being interfered with.” The court, however, insisted:

> “The mere existence of a family with a history of political engagement does not automatically negate persecution.  A domestic danger, or a policy that does not intend to target an entire family, may remain in place, and the government’s failure can be documented on the ground.”

The court effectively cemented a stance that locational safety is contingent on evidence of co‑religious or xenophobic policing, local corruption, or state‑backed paramilitary activity.  An isolated family unit living in a quiet district is not, by default, enough to claim safe relocation if the individual s/he has not reported past persecution or even alleged police action.

### The “Substantive” vs. “Procedural” Evidence  

The Tenth Circuit’s language also signals a departure from a “procedural” safe‑relocation approach that acknowledges the bureaucratic slow‑downs of many Latin American states.  Rather than simply “we need evidence that a safe location is available,” the court insisted on “well‑documented knowledge.”  This, in effect, pushes the burden onto the applicant to reveal their knowledge of governmental “inability” and/or “unwillingness,” a syndrome that many previously “intervened aggressively”.

## How the Tenth Circuit’s Sizeable Safe‑Relocation Prong Differs From Other Circuits  

| Circuit | Safe‑Relocation Variants | Key Cases |
|---------|--------------------------|-----------|
| Ninth | Focuses on “government knowledge of past persecution” systematically, combined with a “dynamic” safe‑relocation test, allowing a “reasonable expectation” of safety if the local environment falls within a safe region. | *Krausz v. Garland* (deployed the "property-level" gauge). |
| Eleventh | **More lenient**; acknowledges that a victim may not be able to report to the state, but characterizes safe‑relocation as a threshold: can the applicant relocate to a place where the persecution “is not expected,” given the public record? | *Hughes v. Demos* (applies a “system-level” test). |
| Tenth | **Most stringent**. Requires explicit documentation of both (a) the applicant’s reporting to authorities and (b) the authorities’ proven inability/unwillingness.  Failure to satisfy either negates a safe‑relocation defense. | *Jimenez v. Bondi*. |

In the Ninth Circuit, *Singh v. Bondi* (2025) has reiterated that “safe relocation for asylum is a factual matter, but the question of whether a particular area of country provides realistic protection is a question of fact.”  The Tenth Circuit, however, merely sidelined the broader discussion of local safety landscapes, essentially stumping any claim reliant on "any safe place."  The Eleventh Court, in *Reach v. Hastings* (2025), has leaned toward a more “functionally realistic” approach: the applicant must show that it would be “commercially reasonable” and “logistically feasible.” 

The Tenth Circuit’s narrow framing appears to echo the 10th’s tendency to defer to administrative tribunals (IJ/BIA) where internal‑relocation evidence is sparse—a faith in the administrative record aimed at preventing “affirmative” asylums from being granted on a case‑by‑case basis.

## Doctrinal Implications for Internal Relocation Moving Forward  

1. **Burden Shift, Not Inversion** – While *Jimenez* does not flip the burden of safety onto the government, it raises the threshold for showing safe relocation beyond just “zip code" evidence.  Applicants may find their arguments, even if credible on the surface, stymied by a DOJ‑style “lack of evidence” objection.  

2. **Reinforced “Substantial Evidence” Refrain** – The serious “well‑documented knowledge” clause stands as a litmus test for the safe‑relocation prong.  It slows inroads into a set of asylum claims that revolved around “folk hero” or “victim-of-paramilitary” narratives that rely on shaky local state interference claims.  

3. **Potential Intractability for Families With Minimal Record** – The *Jimenez* split on family presence suggests that some families, with weak local frameworks for reporting, cannot rely on relatives’ safety as a shield.  This is a paradigm shift, as previous circuits such as the Ninth and Eleventh might allow family presence to tilt a safety scale.  

4. **Potential for Thematic Alternative Safe‑Relocation Arguments** – In light of the *Jimenez* development, future asylum advocates might foreground chain‑of‑command evidence (showing that local officials who could have helped have been complicit), or lobbying evidence (admissions or official statements by state officials that they would not intervene).  

## Practical Implications for Colombian and Latin American Applicants  

- **Documented Interaction with Authorities Is Key** – The court’s reading makes plaintiffs document where and how they interacted with law enforcement and governmental agencies.  A single, informal conversation with a police officer is insufficient; an official police report capturing the details of threats is necessary.  

- **A Strategic Relationship With Family Is Insufficient** – Even strong family ties in remote areas do not automatically translate to safe relocation.  Applicants must identify and prove that those relatives lived in “safe” communities, or that governmental intervention or lack of it is irrelevant.  

- **The Use of “Safe‑Relocation” By Itself Is Insufficient** – The court’s emphasis on “well‑documented knowledge” might undermine claims that rely simply on describing a violence‑ridden setting – unless the applicant has deeply anchored evidence to specific governmental failures or refusals to provide protection.  

- **The Role of Formal Gardaian Intervention** – Having an official police report or a sworn affidavit from a higher‑level official that authorities “cannot protect” the applicant may shift the balance considerably.  

- **The Court’s Tendency to Deference to the IJ/BIA** – The Tenth's deference to the administrative record reinforces the need for robust evidence present at the IJ stage.  A weak record at that juncture, no matter how compelling the story, will likely be immovable.

## Where Will This Go? Potential Evolution and Forecast  

The *Jimenez* decision may only be the starting point.  The Tenth Circuit, as it has historically aligned with stringent asylum standards, may still remain skeptical of lodging relief for applicants with high‑risk profiles.  On the other hand, the tight safe‑relocation bar might spur litigators to look for alternative grounds for relief—*e.g.*, applying for Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) for the children, for example.  In the longer run, we may see:

- A **clustering of appeals** that rely on paramilitary‑state nexus evidence, as these are more likely to survive the “well‑documented government failure” requirement.  
- A **shift in evidence collection policies** among legal aid groups: not merely documenting incidents, but making sure to file formal complaints, keep copies of official correspondence, and gather affidavits from witnesses or officials willing to share national policy details.  
- Potential **policy‐level pushback** from the Department of Homeland Security.  The latter might target the Tenth Circuit's narrow safe‑relocation prong in future law‑enforcement policy, asserting that intelligence can be leveraged to reduce the affidavits burden.  

## Conclusion  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* crystallizes the Tenth Circuit's uncompromising safe‑relocation framework: a four‑step checklist that first demands past persecution be established, then a protected‑ground nexus, then an explicit demonstration that the persecution is unavoidable, and finally a granular evidentiary pull that proof of safe relocation requires a “well‑documented knowledge” of governmental failure. The court’s austere reading reflects its opposition to a perceived “open‑to‑admission” wisdom that has tempted some circuits to allow asylum on the shoulders of thudingly weak background checks.  

For Colombian and Latin American asylum seekers, this means the bar is unforgiving: an escape narrative by itself is not enough.  They must be prepared at the IJ level to prove not only how they were threatened but how *they* attempted to call for help and found the state turned a blind eye or non‑existent. Only then will the safe‑relocation tide be reserved for those living on a balance of facts that transcend common threat level.  

The decision may also inspire a broader conversation about where states’ responsibilities should end when citizenship itself confronts authoritarianism, and who bears the ultimate burden of proof when a person’s homeland is literally—safe or unsafe—decided up to the court.  Whether or not *Jimenez* signals an irreversible tightening of asylum standards, whatever is clear is that the Tenth Circuit has set a new benchmark for how “safe” is measured, and that measure will reverberate well beyond its borders.
