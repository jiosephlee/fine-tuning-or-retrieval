Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Political Opinion and Mixed Motive Harassment: The Tenth Circuit’s Tightened Nexus Requirement in *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why This Opinion Matters

The Ninth and Eleventh Circuits, among others, have long wrestled with the question of how strictly a protected ground must be linked to persecution in an asylum claim. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit cut a sharpened line: political persecution must be a *central* reason for the harm. It rejected a more flexible “mixed‑motive” view that had allowed a political motivation to stand alongside criminal or personal animus, and dismissed the claim because the protesters’ peril stemmed chiefly from a property dispute, not from Jimenez’s campaign. The decision sends a clear signal to both advocates and adjudicators that a provenance of threat that can be extracted from an unrelated conflict will not suffice to prove a political nexus.

## The Case in Brief

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, a provisional lawyer and longtime member of the Colombian Liberal Party, and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, travelled to the United States in late 2022 after a series of threats, kidnappings, and confrontations with a narco‑trafficking gang aligned with the current Colombian presidency. The gang’s leader, “Don Rafa,” had allegedly recruited imprisoned men and operated an illegal rental base at a property owned by a client Jiménez represented. When Jiménez tried to secure the property, Don Rafa’s men threatened the family at a local factory, abducted an employee, and, after a public confrontation, threatened the family with death. The family ended up fleeing to the United States for safety.

In New York, Jiménez and Duarte petitioned for asylum and withholding of removal. An immigration judge found them credible but could not prove past persecution, a nexus to a protected ground, or that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them. The Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed. The Tenth Circuit denied the appeal, holding that the alleged persecution was “tangential” to their political opinion and, therefore, failed the nexus test.

## The Court’s Reading of “Central Reason”

The crux of the decision lies in the court’s application of the “central‑reason” requirement that has long governed the nexus analysis. The court quotes *Niang* and *Rivera‑Barrientos*, noting that “the political opinion must be *at least one central reason* for the persecution,” or else the claim fails. In *Jiménez*, the petitioners showed a chain of intimidation that began with political activism. Yet the court made a salient distinction: the repeated, escalated threats came *after* Jiménez’s involvement in a property dispute with Don Rafa. At that point, the hunters’ primary motive—according to the record—was to punish the lawyer for “talking Santa” and undermining Don Rafa’s revenue. The court concluded that this commercial–criminal motive was the real driver of the harassment, relegating the political allegations to “tangent” concern.

By treating political grounds that do not lie at the heart of the persecution as insufficient, the Tenth Circuit removes the “mixed‑motive” flex that had appeared in earlier decisions. The court’s reasoning is not that the political motive is irredeemably weak; rather, it is that, in evaluating past persecution, the focus must be on the motive that the persecutor claimed—here, the property dispute—rather than on an incidental ideological element that could not be discerned as a primary reason from the record.

## How Jimenez Evolved the Precedent

Prior Tenth Circuit rulings—*Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*, *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, and *Dallakoti v. Holder*, to name a few—had entertained the idea that a protected ground could be present even if it was among several motives. Those decisions emphasized a “substantial‑evidence” threshold and left room for a political angle to be acknowledged, so long as it contributed to the persecution. *Jiménez* pares that latitude down to a matter of causal centrality. Where *Carias‑Mejia* accepted a claim that a political opinion was one of multiple motives behind a threat, *Jiménez* demands that motive be the one that the threat functions as the “anchor.” If the chain of evidence can equally satisfy a non‑political explanation, the political claim lapses.

The court’s current language—“not a central reason” and “tangential”—clears up the earlier policy ambiguity. It aligns the Tenth Circuit’s standard more closely with the literal reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i), which requires that a political opinion “is *at least one central reason* for the persecution.” The decision is a reminder that the reference to “central” is not merely a matter of emphasis; it has teeth.

## Practical Ramifications for Attorneys

The judgment forces law firms to retool the way they structure asylum petitions for politically engaged clients, particularly those who have been involved in business or property disputes that could cloud the origins of any threat.

1. **Clear Causal Mapping**: Counsel can no longer treat a political activity as a safety net for unrelated threats. The filing must include a clean narrative that shows how the prosecution’s motive was the political stance itself. If the client is also embroiled in a business quarrel or other conflict, evidence must demonstrate that the persecutor’s official or “public” motive was political in nature or that the political motivation was the primary recourse in the violence.

2. **Strategic Avoidance of Mixed Motives**: The court’s tightening demands a “single dose” approach to the nexus. For instance, if a paramilitary gang threatens a defendant for both abducting a rival and for belonging to a political group, the client should either dismiss the gang–related motives or prove that the gang’s political grievance was pivotal in triggering the prize.

3. **Deeper Unwilling‑Uncertain Evidence**: A client’s failure to seek aide from law‑enforcement operators might not be fatal if the client proves that police engagement would not have protected them from a gang-linked threat. But as seen in *Jiménez*, a lukewarm “talk to the officer” anecdote may be interpreted as a lack of knowledge of any meaningful protective mechanism and, in turn, as evidence of ineffective government protection.

4. **Risk Assessment in Behind‑closed‑door Diplomacy**: The case situates paramilitary violence in the context of a politically sympathetic state apparatus—an area that previously had ambiguous jurisprudence. The barrier to convincing an immigration judge that the persecutor was an extension of the state is higher than before. Attorneys may need to bring independent assessments of executive failure or collusion rather than simply citing arrests, taking the question beyond a handful of police statements.

In sum, *Jiménez* forces counsel to move the political claim to the forefront, not hide it behind the dust‑cloud of a commercial quarrel or a criminal feud. For practitioners, it becomes a lesson in causality: the “why” must be crystal‑clear in the statutory mind.

## Is a New Doctrine Emerging?

The Tenth Circuit is not alone in tightening the nexus. The Fifth, Seventh, and Ninth Circuits have occasionally stressed a need for a “central” reason or shown a willingness to reject claims where the political ground is only one of several motives. *Jiménez* clarifies the Tenth Circuit’s approach but does not optical‑ly preempt a stay of precedent. Still, the opinion is likely to be persuasive in other circuits, especially one that shares the same sense of federalism that frees circuits from each other’s sovereignty. Immigrants with unclear motive disputes will likely find the bar higher.

## Bottom Line

*Jiménez* does not strip asylum seekers of the possibility to claim a political network. Instead, it pins the standard of proof tighter. The court emphatically states: when a persecutor’s motive can be explained by an alternative, less visible factor—such as a property dispute or personal vendetta—the political claim has the same /*unprofitable*/ foot in the door as a piece of extra‑credit. The decision cuts the goodwill that attorneys have sometimes relied on when fighting for a client who is both a political activist and a registered lawyer. If you represent a politically engaged Chilean, Venezuelan, or Colombian facing threats, be prepared to draw a clean line between the modus operandi of the threat and the political motive in your filing. The Tenth Circuit has made that line straighter, not just for Jiménez but for the constellation of future asylum claims.
