Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What standard of review does the 10th Circuit apply to a BIA’s denial of asylum under 8 U.S.C. § 1252?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi the court reviewed the BIA’s denial of asylum and stated that the review is limited to the "administrative record on which the order of removal is based" (citing § 1252(b)(4)(A)). The court also notes that the BIA’s findings are subject to the "substantial‑evidence" standard. I am confused about how the appellate court treats the IJ’s explanation of the same facts, whether it gives the conclusion de‑novo, and whether the review is "pre‑ponderance of the evidence" or something else. What exactly is the standard the appellate court uses, and how does it treat the record of the IJ versus the BIA?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the 10th Circuit reviewed the BIA’s denial of asylum under § 1252(b)(4)(A) – i.e., *the administrative record on which the order of removal is based* – and applied the *substantial‑evidence* standard to the BIA’s factual findings. The court also noted that it is “not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds,” but it does **not** re‑weigh the evidence or give the IJ its findings de novo. The IJ’s record is treated simply as part of the administrative record that the appellate court evaluates for substantial evidence.

---

### How the court applies the standard

| Step | What the court says | Practical effect |
|------|--------------------|------------------|
| 1.  Scope | “Our review of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’” 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A) (See *Jimenez*, 2025 WL 6000). | Only the IJ, BIA, and other pre‑removal evidence are considered; new evidence is excluded. |
| 2.  Factual findings | “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard. Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” (*Jimenez*, 2025 WL 6000, at *4‑5*; citing *Rivera‑Barrientos*). | The appellate court does not apply a *preponderance of the evidence* test. Instead, it asks whether the record contains *substantial evidence* supporting the BIA’s conclusions. If it does, the findings stand. If it does not, the BIA is found to have erred. |
| 3.  Legal conclusions | The court treats the BIA’s **legal** rulings as subject to de novo review (the usual rule for § 1252 cases). | Only factual findings are protected by “substantial evidence”; legal arguments are reviewed afresh. |
| 4.  Use of the IJ’s record | “We are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” (*Jimenez*, 2025 WL 6000, at *3*; citing *Uanreroro*). | The IJ’s explanation is part of the record and is used to decide if the evidence is substantial. It does **not** give the IJ an independent factual basis that the appellate court must separately evaluate. |

---

### Why the “substantial‑evidence” standard is key

*Substantial evidence* is a *deferential* standard: the appellate court gives the original adjudicator (the BIA) the benefit of the doubt and will not reverse unless **any** reasonable adjudicator could have arrived at a different conclusion. It is **not** the same as “preponderance of the evidence” (the standard a trial judge uses when deciding G. v. K.) or “beyond a reasonable doubt,” and it provides a higher bar for reverse than a preponderance test would. The court’s statement that the BIA’s findings “are conclusive unless the record demonstrates…a contrary conclusion” makes this clear.

---

### How the IJ’s record is treated

- The IJ’s explanation of the same facts is **consulted** but *not* treated as a separate factual determination.  
- The appellate court looks to see whether that explanation, combined with all other administrative evidence, creates *substantial evidence* for the BIA’s conclusions.  
- If it does, the BIA’s findings are upheld. If it doesn’t, the court declares the BIA wrong.  
- The IJ’s record is therefore part of the evidence the appellate court reviews under the substantial‑evidence standard; it does not create a new “de‑novo” factual analysis.

---

### Bottom line

In *Jimenez* the 10th Circuit applies:

1. **Substantial‑evidence** standard to the BIA’s factual findings.  
2. **De novo** review to the BIA’s legal conclusions.  
3. The IJ’s record is treated as part of the same administrative record and is used only to determine whether substantial evidence exists—no new factual weighing is performed.

That is the standard the court uses when reviewing a BIA denial of asylum under § 1252.

### Can threats alone be considered past persecution under the INA?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi the court held that threats are not sufficient on their own unless accompanied by actual physical harm, citing Karki v. Holder, Chhetri v. Rosen, and others. I would like to understand what qualifies as "persecution" under § 1101(a)(42)(A) and whether psychological or emotional harm can substitute for physical harm. Also, are there statutory criteria that differentiate mere threats from persecution? Please clarify the standards and provide examples.
Answer:
**No.  In the …**  
The Courts have made it clear that *threats alone—whether verbal, written, or a single phone call—do not meet the statutory threshold for “past persecution” under § 1101(a)(42)(A). The requirements are higher; the law tests whether the applicant has actually suffered “suffering or harm” that is more than a mere restriction or threat of violence.  The case law the question cites—**Karki v. Holder** (10th Cir. 2013), **Chhetri v. Rosen** (10th Cir. 2021), and **Carias‑Mejia v. Garland** (10th Cir. 2021)—all consistently held that a solitary or even a handful of threats fails to establish persecution unless they culminate in actual physical harm or are part of a credible, imminent assault.

---

## 1.  Statutory framework

1. **§ 1158(b)(1)** – The 1986 definition of “refugee” requires that a non‑citizen be “unable or unwilling” to return because of **persecution** or a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground—race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.  
2. **Past persecution** is the factual element the applicant bears the burden of proving “by a preponderance of the evidence.”  
3. The Constitution of “persecution” is not provided in the statute, but the Courts have elaborated that it requires the infliction of **suffering or harm that is offensive and more than a mere threat or restriction** (see *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004); further discussed in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 1 F.4th 936, 944‑45 (10th Cir. 2025)).

The statutory sheet does not contain specific “threat clauses”; instead, it requires the *actual* infliction of harm.

---

## 2.  Judicial treatment of threats

| Case | Threats described | Outcome |
|------|-------------------|---------|
| **Karki v. Holder** | A single menacing phone call warning of potential death | *Not persecution.*  The Court held that the threat, without a corresponding act of violence or credible imminent danger, did not rise to the level of “suffering or harm.” |
| **Chhetri v. Rosen** | Four telephone threats plus one vague in‑person threat | *Not persecution.*  The Court reiterated that “threats alone … are insufficient to constitute persecution.” |
| **Carias‑Mejia v. Garland** | Threats with machetes, repeated robberies | *Not persecution.*  The Court found that when the threats were “contingent on compliance” and did not actually inflict harm, they did not meet the statutory standard. |
| **Jimenez v. Bondi** | Repeated phone threats, a property dispute, two gun‑point encounters, and a near‑physical abduction attempt | *Not persecution.*  The Court stressed that “threats alone are insufficient … Petitioner conceded no lasting physical harm.” It also noted that the Court has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (¶ 63‑64) |

The common thread: **mere threats, even when repeated or in the context of a politically motivated background, fail to satisfy “persecution” unless accompanied by actual violence, a credible immediate danger, or a pattern of intimidation that culminates in physical harm.**

---

## 3.  Psychological / emotional harm

The law affirms that **psychological or emotional harm can be part of persecution**—if it constitutes “suffering or harm” that is as serious as a physical injury.  However, the Court in *Jimenez* stressed that the standard is not met by mere anxiety or fear stemming from threats:

> “We have rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.”  
> ( *Jimenez*, 1 F.4th 944‑45)

In practice, the Court has accepted extreme psychological trauma—such as PTSD following repeated abductions or systematic intimidation—as *suffering* that can meet the threshold.  But isolated emotional distress caused by a threat is **not** enough.

---

## 4.  How the Court distinguishes threats from persecution

| Element | What the Court looks for | This case’s analysis |
|---------|--------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Actual harm** | Physical injury, kidnapping, or credible immediacy of violence | • All decisions that rejected a threat‑only claim noted the absence of physical harm. |
| **Severity | Suffering must be “offensive” and “more than a restriction.” | • In *Jimenez*, the Court said threats “must entail more than just restrictions or sporadic threats.” |
| **Nexus to protected ground** | Must be a “protected” ground that is a **central** reason for the persecution (e.g., political opinion). | • *Jimenez* found the prosecution was centered on a personal conflict with a criminal associate, not on political opinion. |
| **State or state‑controlled perpetrator** | Must be the government itself, a public official, or a person the state can control. | • The Court reviewed whether the perpetrator was tied to the Colombian state or a private criminal group. |

If a threat satisfies those criteria—especially if it’s tied to a state actor and accompanied by imminent physical danger—then it can cross the threshold into “persecution.”  Otherwise, it remains “dangerous but not persecutory.”

---

## 5.  Bottom line for a student

*Threats alone do **not** constitute past persecution under § 1101(a)(42)(A).*  
- **Necessity of suffering/harm**: The threat must (directly or indirectly) cause real injury, or be part of a credible, imminent assault.  
- **Psychological harm** can only substitute for physical harm when the harm is **severe** and **documented** (e.g., PTSD, chronic intrusive memories).  
- **Statutory differentiation**: The INA does not provide a separate clause for “threats”; it requires “suffering or harm.” Courts have used that standard to differentiate a mere threat from persecution.  
- **Significant exceptions**: A threat that accompanies actual violence, is issued by a state actor, or is part of a systematic pattern culminating in physical attacks may be deemed persecution.  
- **Contrast**: In **Jimenez v. Bondi** the Court found that “threats…without physical harm” were insufficient, echoing **Karki** and **Chhetri.**  

When drafting or arguing an asylum petition, always pair a threat with evidence of:
1. **Physical harm, or credible imminent violence**;  
2. **Tied to a protected ground**; and  
3. **Committed by, or with knowledge of, a state actor.**

Only then does the threat move from the realm of “intimidation” to the legal threshold of “persecution.”

### How is the ‘central reason’ test applied to political opinion claims under asylum law?
Question:
The court referenced Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti, stating that the political opinion must be "at least one central reason" for the persecution, not incidental or subordinate. In Jimenez v. Bondi the court found the threats were primarily due to Jimenez’s involvement with Don Rafa, not his political opinion. Could you explain how a court determines the centrality of political opinion, what evidence is needed to prove it, and how courts treat cases with "mixed motives"?
Answer:
**Answer – Top‑valued style**

The “at least one central reason” test is the heart of the *nexus* analysis for political‑opinion claims.  In *Rivera‑Barrientos* and *Dallakoti* the Court made it plain that the applicant must show that the protected ground was *not* merely incidental or subordinate to another motive.  In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Ninth Section applied that rule to a case where the record showed a mix of motives, and the Court rejected the claim because the political opinion was not the primary cause of the threat.  Below is a step‑by‑step guide to how a court applies the test and what sort of evidence matters.

---

### 1.  What the central‑reason test requires

1. **Political opinion as the pillar of persecution:**  The petitioner must prove that *on its face* the political opinion was “at least one central reason” the persecutor targeted him or his family (cf. *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646; *Dallakoti*, 619 F.3d at 1268).  
2. **Not an add‑on or side‑effect:**  The opinion may not be in the background, or used only to *justify* a different motive.  It must be a standing reason—appearing, for example, in the perpetrator’s own words or in a pattern that shows the victim was singled out because of that opinion.

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Court reiterated that a *mixed‑motive* scenario does not solve the central‑reason problem:  
> “Even so, in such mixed‑motive cases, the persecutor must be motivated by at least one protected ground that is a central reason for the inflicted harm.”  
> *Jimenez*, p. 28, at 51 (citing *O.C.V. v. Bondi*, 2025 WL 2447603, at 6).

Thus the test is a *weighing* of evidence to decide what the predominant motive was.

---

### 2.  How the Court in *Jimenez* determined centrality

| Evidence examined | What it shows | Court’s determination |
|------------------|--------------|------------------------|
| **Timeline of threats** | Threats began after a **property dispute** with Don Rafa; prior political‑campaign threats were less serious. | Courts noted the escalation “after Jimenez got involved in a property dispute with Don Rafa” (*Jimenez*, p. 28, at 45). |
| **Content of threats** | Perpetrators explicitly tied the men’s hostility to the “snitch” accusation and to Don Rafa’s *revenues* and *security* (*Jimenez*, p. 28, at 48). | The court labeled the political opinion “merely tangential” because the men “accused Jimenez of working for the prosecutor’s office” – an action unrelated to any political expression. |
| **Perpetrator statements** | They said they were acting on Don Rafa’s orders and that the motive was retaliation (“the order came from Don Rafa”). | The Court accepted that the *political opinion* was not a “central reason” for the threat (*Jimenez*, p. 28, at 49). |
| **Lack of independent political motive evidence** | No documentation that the perpetrators targeted him because of his Liberal Party membership, beyond incidental war‑like remarks about “snitches.” | The Court found the political opinion was “**incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate**” to the retaliation motive (*Jimenez*, p. 28, at 47). |

The Court’s conclusion was that the *primary* motive was Mr. Jiménez’s personal involvement with Don Rafa and the property dispute, **not** his political opinion.

---

### 3.  Evidence needed to prove a political opinion was central

- **Explicit statements of motive** that tie the persecution directly to the victim’s political views (e.g. “because you are a Liberal”.)  
- **Pattern of persecution**: multiple incidents or a broader campaign against that party or ideology.  
- **Contextual timing**: incidents that occur against the backdrop of political activity, not after unrelated disputes.  
- **Corroboration**: third‑party reports or documents showing that the political affiliation, not another fact, prompted the threat.  
- **Absence of a better explanation**: the record should not reveal a more plausible motive that explains the actions equally well (or better).

In *Jimenez* none of these satisfied the Court because the threats could be explained wholly by retaliation for the property dispute.

---

### 4.  “Mixed motives” and the central‑reason test

The Court acknowledges that a persecutor can have *several* motives.  
> “...the persecutor must be motivated by at least one protected ground that is a central reason for the inflicted harm.”  
> *Jimenez*, p. 28, at 51.

Hence, if a case contains a *demonstrably* strong political motive *and* a secondary motive (e.g., personal revenge), asylum can still be granted on the grounds of political opinion *provided the political motive was central*.

But when the record shows that the *primary* reason for the threat is something else—as in *Jimenez*—the political opinion claim fails, even though it might still be present as a factor.

---

### 5.  Practical takeaway for applicants

1. **Characterize the threatening incident as politically **driven** (not collateral).**  
2. **Document, whenever possible, that the perpetrator’s motive was political, right from the start.**  
3. **Demonstrate a pattern or context that links the violent or threatening conduct to the applicant’s party membership or viewpoint.**  
4. **Show that the threat would not have occurred without the political opinion** – a sort of *exclusionary* argument that other motives are insufficient to explain the claim.  

If those points cannot be proven, the Court, as it did in *Jimenez*, will likely find the political opinion to be *subordinate* and deny asylum on that ground.

---

**Bottom line:** The central‑reason test is not satisfied unless the record presents evidence that *on its face* the political opinion was the main driving force behind the persecution.  The *Jimenez* decision illustrates that even with mixed motives, the Court will focus on the predominant motive, and will not be satisfied by a tangential or incidental connection.

### How significant is a single police officer’s statement in the government‑protection assessment for asylum?
Question:
In the case, the plaintiffs only spoke to a lone police officer who told them not to seek trouble, and the court said this is not enough to show that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them. I want to understand how the "notice" and "reporting" requirement is applied and how much weight a single officer’s attitude carries. Could you explain the guidelines or precedent for evaluating such evidence?
Answer:
**Short answer** – In the 10th Circuit’s view, a *single* police officer’s dismissive comment does not, by itself, satisfy the “notice‐and‑reporting” element of the “government‑unwilling or –unable” exception in an asylum case.  The court requires that the applicant either show that the government had *systemic* knowledge of the persecution or that repeated, tangible evidence shows the police (or another governmental body) failed to act.  One chat with an officer who merely told the applicant to “stay away” is treated as “minimal reporting” and is weighted very lightly, unless it is part of a broader pattern that reveals a government’s inability or unwillingness to protect the applicant.

---

## 1.  The “notice‑and‑reporting” framework

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1) and the 10th Circuit precedent, an asylum applicant **may rely on the government‑unwilling or –unable** exception only if:

1. **The government had notice** of the applicant’s past persecution, *and*
2. **The government offered protection** (e.g., protective custody, active intervention) *or* took actions that *indicated* it was willing to control the persecutors.

See Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861 (citing Singh, 130 F.4th at 861).  
“If the applicant has no notice*, we cannot know how the police would have acted” (ibid.). “Notice is a prerequisite to proving that the government was unwilling or unable.”

In practice, the burden is on the applicant to demonstrate that the Indonesian—or Colombian, in Jimenez—government knew about the persecution and, faced with that knowledge, either failed to act or did not offer protection.  The court therefore pays close attention to **any evidence that the applicant reported the persecution** to the authorities.

---

## 2.  What the Jimenez opinion says about a single officer’s remark

Jimenez emphatically notes that the applicants’ minimal interaction with law‑enforcement “was **not enough**” to meet the notice requirement:

> “Petitioners’ efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any… the record does **not** show that Petitioners sought help… through Jimenez’s law‑enforcement contacts.”  
> —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62

The opinion goes further:

> “The applicant may satisfy the government‑protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous.”  
> —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861

and

> “Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond… a one‑off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect a victim.”  
> —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861


### Key take‑aways

* **“Notice” ≠ “one conversation.”** The court requires that the author of the notice (the police department, the city, the national government) had *systemic* knowledge of the persecution before any protective action was attempted.
* **“Minimal reporting” is treated with skepticism.** A single, informal encounter that offers no follow‑up (such as a police report filed, a summons, an investigation, a protective‑custody action, or a credible threat of retaliation for reporting) is insufficient.
* **A pattern of unhelpful or hostile officers isn’t automatically enough.** The court prefers *public evidence* (media reports, court filings, documented police negligence, or testimony that indicates a police department routinely ignores such complaints). “That same men… told the Petitioners the police officers were contributing to the problem” speaks to individual attitudes, but without documentary evidence of the department’s overall response or lack thereof the court cannot infer a government‑wide incapacity.

---

## 3.  How courts weigh evidence of the government’s willingness or inability

The 10th Circuit’s standard is designed to tie the denial of asylum to an objective, *substantial‑evidence* standard:

1. **Substantial evidence** – The BIA’s findings stand unless another reasonable adjudicator would necessarily reach the opposite conclusion.  
   > “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard… The BIA’s findings are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator… would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.”  
   —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 851

2. **Evidence of police action** – Police reports, arrest records, or official responses to complaints are the most persuasive.  
   *Lack of such evidence, even if the applicant asserts personal conversations, weighs in favor of the BIA’s finding that the government was *not* unwilling or unable.*  
   —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62

3. **Pattern or systemic failure** – The *evidence must suggest a systemic lack of protection*, not a single anecdotal incident. The court refers to *Osorio‑Morales v. Garland*, 72 F.4th at 743, for this rationale:
   > “we often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people.”  

4. **Alternative explanations** – Courts also consider whether reporting would have been futile or dangerous. *Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland*, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2, strengthens the notion that *failure to report* is not fatal unless the decision can be shown to be due to the unavailability or unwillingness of the police to help the applicant.  

Taken together, a one‑off conversation with a single policeman can be reasonable **only** when the broader record eliminates other possibilities. If the applicant can show, for example, that the police department conducted *no* investigations into the alleged threats, that the department has a documented policy of turning away political activists, or that the police were visibly threatened or intimidated by the perpetrators, then the single conversation becomes part of a weighty pattern. Absent any such corroborating evidence, the conversation is treated as a weak or “unable‑to‑verify” piece of evidence and **does not tip the balance**.

---

## 4.  Practical guidance for applicants and practitioners

| Step | What to prove | What the Opinion says | Typical evidence that will carry the most weight |
|------|---------------|-----------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| 1 | **Past persecution** is *not merely threats* – must be “offensive” harm. | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 852‑54. | Physical injury, credible, documented police arrest, evidence of the threat being imminent or actualized. |
| 2 | **Notice** – the government *knew* about the persecution. | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62; *Osorio‑Morales* | Police reports filed, summons to appear in court, documented attempts by police to investigate, media coverage of employee or police statements. |
| 3 | **Reporting** – the applicant *told the police* or other governmental body about the persecution. | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62; *Aviles‑Gonzalez* | Written complaint, affidavit, recorded conversation that was later transcribed. |
| 4 | **Evidence of failure to act** – the government was *unable or unwilling* to protect. | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62 | Police failure to investigate, failure to provide protective custody, chain of “unresponsive” police when other victims received help, or a clear statement that the police would not intervene because of connection to perpetrators. |
| 5 | **Pattern or systemic issue** – not just a “single conversation.” | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62; *Osorio‑Morales* | Multiple reports filed across different departments, repeated refusals or warnings and no subsequent action, official documentation or expert testimony that law‑enforcement is shut down against political activists. |

---

## 5.  Bottom line

In **Jimenez v. Bondi**, the court expressly rejected the applicants’ reliance on a handful of brief, unprotected conversations with two police officers. The court held that those conversations did **not** satisfy the notice‑and‑reporting requirement, and that the **absence** of substantive, corroborated evidence of police or governmental failure meant the applicants could not establish that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them. The decision makes it clear that a *single* officer’s statement, without further evidence of systemic police failure or inside evidence that the police were aware and unhelpful, is too weak to bear on the asylum government‑protection prong.

**For practice:** Whenever you argue that police were not helpful, demonstrate that the department actually *knew* about the persecution (through reports, investigative records, or a documented policy) and failed to act. If you can only present a single informal conversation, the court will treat it as inconclusive, and the government‑protection exception will almost certainly fail.

### What standard governs determining if an applicant can safely relocate within their country?
Question:
Jimenez v. Bondi says the applicant must show that they could mitigate the threat by internal relocation based on a preponderance of the evidence, citing Rivera‑Barrientos. I’m confused about the evidence required: Does the applicant need to produce a relocation plan, proof of safe areas, or simply that family resides safely? How does the court evaluate "reasonable" relocation?
Answer:
**Standard of review**

Under U.N.C.L.E. 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i) and the Ninth‑Circuit precedent that the Ninth Circuit mis‑states the standard here, the “safe‑relocation” branch of the *asylum* analysis is itself evaluated **by a preponderance of the evidence**.  The government may rebut the presumption that a refugee carries a well‑founded fear of future persecution if it shows *by a preponderance of the evidence* that “the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.”  (See *Rivera‑Barrientos* v. Holder, 666 F.3d at 646; *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 244170, at *13*).  

Because asylum and with­holding are discretionary forms of relief, the same factual proof‑standard that applies to the threat and nexus elements applies here: the applicant must show, not merely speculate, that relocation would be **safe and practicable**.

---

### What evidence is required?

The opinion in *Jimenez* does not prescribe an exhaustive “relocation plan” but requires *facts* that that relocation would actually reduce the risk the applicant faces.  In practice courts look for:

| Evidence | Why it satisfies the standard |
|----------|------------------------------|
|**Presence of a living, safe family member** | If a relative can provide shelter and economic support in a location outside the area of threat, that demonstrates the possibility of “moving” to a safer spot (*Jimenez*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 244170, at *14*). |
|**Recent stays in safe areas** | The appellants lived with Jimenez’s mother in the countryside for three weeks before deciding to depart the country.  The court treated this as evidence that a “brief relocation” was *possible and did not provoke retaliation* (*Jimenez*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 244170, at *14*). |
|**Affidavits or testimony that no overt threat exists in the new area** | The state’s response—here, “players did not threaten the family to leave the country” and “the threatened men focused on a neighborhood, not on a national escape” – signals that the intrabound threat is local, not nationwide (*Jimenez*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 244170, at *14*). |
|**Information on the area’s safety (statistics, official reports, prior police work, etc.)** | Courts watch for evidence that the authorities had *attempted* to address the implicated area’s criminal activities: “the government had attempted several searches and seizures at the property” (*Jimenez*, § E). This shows that an external threat is not systemic and can be avoided by moving (*Jimenez*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 244170, at *14*). |
|**Practical ability to relocate** | In the appellate opinion it is enough that the claimant *could* move—there is no requirement to show *routine* resources, but *there must be no evidential obstacle* (e.g., “no economic hardship” or “no legal impediment”).  The court noted that having a relative in the country does *not* automatically make relocation impossible; it is the *absence of a “fundamental change” or obstacles* that matters (*Jimenez*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 244170, at *14*). |

No statutory language requires a formal SOP or "plan," but a **minimum fact‑based outline** is the expectation: where the applicant would move, how they would go there, and whether a threat would persist.

---

### How does the court understand “reasonable”?

*“Reasonable” is a *factual* term, not a purely policy one.*  
The judge in *Jimenez* compared the applicant’s circumstances to the government’s knowledge and response.  Because the government attempted to police the property in question and no wider threat was evident, the court concluded that the applicant could “re‑locate and avoid persecution” – the reasoning that the threats were *localized* and *not government‑wide*.  The court uses the *preponderance* lens that it discerns even when evidence is sparse, but the evidence must still show:

1. **The threat is controllable** – it would not follow the applicant to any safe area; the threat was tied to the specific neighborhood and the “Don Rafa” gang (*Jimenez*, § E).
2. **The applicant has the capability** – they have relatives in safe parts of the country, have lived there temporarily, and there's no prohibition from the state blocking such moves.
3. **The state cannot be expected to intervene** – the state has "unwillingness or inability" to protect them *only if* this is shown otherwise, which the applicants failed to do (*Jimenez*, § E).

In sum: the court says “this is a *preponderant* fact that the applicant could abandon the threat zone, stay elsewhere, and that the chance of future persecution is reduced.”  The standard does not demand a full relocation plan but does demand **factual, contextual evidence** that the move is *both possible and likely to put the applicant in a safer location.*

---

### Bottom line

The standard you must meet is the same “preponderance of the evidence” test applied to all asylum elements.  To satisfy the *safe‑relocation* requirement you need to offer **fact‑based evidence** that:

- there is a *specific, safe area* outside the threat zone;  
- you are able to *move there* (family, economic support, prior residence, contact with officials, etc.); and  
- the threat would *not* continue once you relocate.

An abstract “family is alive in Colombia” is *not* enough; your record must show that the family member is in an area that is demonstrably safe and that you can actually relocate there without hindrance or additional risk.  The court in *Jimenez* treated the evidence that the family and father were uninjured, the presence of a mother in a safe countryside location, and the fact that no broader threat was observed as satisfying the safe‑relocation standard—yet that was insufficient to rebut the lack of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.

So, in practice, keep your evidence tight and factual: document a safe destination, prove you can actually go there, show that the threat would be neutralized, and demonstrate that the state has no practical capacity to enforce the threat across that area. That is what the court expects when it asks whether internal relocation “is safely and reasonably available.”

### How does the legal definition of 'refugee' under 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A) interact with the requirement of a well‑founded fear of future persecution?
Question:
In the opinion, the Court said asylum requires proof of either past persecution or a well‑founded fear of future persecution, but "well‑founded fear" applies in the withholding context too. I’m uncertain how the statutory definition and the standard of proof differ between asylum and withholding, especially with reference to the threshold evidence required for each. Please explain the relationship.
Answer:
**Short answer**

*Both asylum and withholding of removal use the same statutory definition of “refugee.”  The difference is the *threshold* of evidence that must allow the refugee definition to be satisfied.  
* For **asylum** a preponderance of evidence that the applicant is a refugee is enough – past persecution automatically gives a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.  
* For **withholding** the applicant must still prove they are a refugee, but the “well‑founded fear” must survive two extra hurdles:  

  1. **Government security** – the Colombian government must be *unable* or *unwilling* to protect the applicant.  
  2. **Safe relocation** – the applicant must be unable to relocate safely within Colombia.  

If the asylum claim fails, the withholding claim fails automatically because the higher bar has not been met.  (See J. Jiménez v. Bondi, 130 F. 4th at 861–66, 864‑65; Carias‑Mejia v. Garland, 860 App’x at 125.)**

---

### 1.  Statutory definition – what “refugee” means

The INA (8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A)) defines a *refugee* as:

> “any person **outside the country of his nationality**…who *is unable or unwilling to return to, … because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution* on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.”

The Court makes it clear that the statutory definition is the same for both reliefs:

> “To be eligible for a discretionary grant of asylum…the applicant bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that he is a refugee…8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1).”  
> – *Jiménez* (para. B)

> “Because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well‑founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.”  
> – *Jiménez* (para. III)

So the *same* six elements need to be satisfied in both contexts.

---

### 2.  Asylum – how “well‑founded fear” is used

* **Burden** – Preponderance of the evidence that the applicant is a refugee.  
* **Past persecution → presumption** – A showing of past persecution gives rise to a *rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution* (Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194–95).  
* **“Well‑founded fear” alone** – If past persecution is absent, the applicant still must demonstrate a *well‑founded fear of future persecution*, which can be based on threats, a hazardous national context, or credible evidence of future harm.  
* The Court in *Jiménez* noted that “threats alone are insufficient” but can contribute to a *cumulative* or *emotional/psychological* component of persecution.  
* If the asylum claim is denied, the applicant cannot automatically get withholding because the inverse relationship is not mechanically drawn; the applicant must satisfy a **higher bar**.

---

### 3.  Withholding of removal – the higher bar

Withholding requires the same **refugee definition** but adds the following **extra evidence**:

| Requirement | Asylum | Withholding | Why it matters |
|-------------|--------|-------------|----------------|
| **Well‑founded fear** | Satisfies refugee definition. | Must be *credible* AND **coupled** with *government unwilling/unable* protection. | Failure to show government incapacity defeats withholding. |
| **Government protection** | Not expressly required (unless to rebut safe relocation). | Must **prove** that Colombian authorities are unwilling or unable to protect the applicant. | As *Jiménez* shows, lack of police assistance or systemic failure does not automatically establish unavailability; specific evidence of *unwillingness* or *ineffectiveness* is needed. |
| **Safe relocation** | Only considered when a removal order is issued. | Must **prove** that safe relocation is **impossible** within Colombia. | *Jiménez* notes that internal relocation is a “reset” test; if the applicant could relocate safely, withholding is barred. |

The opinion says:

> “Because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well‑founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. (Carias‑Mejia, 860 App’x at 125)”

Thus, the threshold for withholding is *strictly higher* than for asylum: you need the same refugee definition *plus* evidence that the host government cannot protect and that safe relocation is unavailable.

---

### 4.  Practical takeaway for a law‑student

1. **Start with the refugee definition.**  *Both* asylum and withholding hinge on proving the statutory “refugee.”  
2. **Show past persecution first.**  If you have past persecution, you get a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.   
3. **If past persecution is absent,**  you must present a *well‑founded fear of future persecution* that is credible and based on the status of the host country.  
4. **For withholding**,  in addition to the above, supply strong evidence that the government is *unable* or *unwilling* to protect you and that no safe relocation is available.  
5. **Failure in asylum** translates to an automatic **failure in withholding** because the withholding standard subsumes the asylum standard but adds the evidenced government and relocation hurdles.

---

**Bottom line:** *Well‑founded fear* is the hinge point in both reliefs.  Under asylum, it suffices to satisfy the refugee definition, especially when past persecution is shown.  For withholding, the same fear must be accompanied by incontrovertible proof that the host government cannot, and the applicant cannot, find a safe place in the home country.  The Court’s language in *Jiménez* makes that distinction crystal‑clear—you do not get “well‑founded fear” for withholding just because you get it for asylum.*

### Do asylum applicants need to invoke the 'particular social group' ground if they are claiming political opinion?
Question:
Jimenez v. Bondi notes that the plaintiffs did not brief a particular social group; they only alleged persecution for political opinion. I’m confused whether it’s possible to argue asylum denial under the political opinion ground without also addressing the particular social group requirement. Is it necessary to include the particular social group argument if the applicant only believes that the government is targeting them on political grounds? Clarify.
Answer:
**No, an applicant does not have to plead a Particular Social Group (PSG) claim just because the claim is based on political opinion.**  

Under the INA an applicant may rely on *any one* of the five protected bases (race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or PSG). The grounds are mutually exclusive – you can plead one, two, or all five, but each has its own standard. In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the petitioners chose only the political‑opinion ground and never raised a PSG claim.

The court made that distinction clear in the discussion of the **nexus** requirement:

> “Petitioners, however, did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group nor did they establish their membership in such a group. … In arguing the nexus requirement, Petitioners allege that they were persecuted by Don Rafa because of their political opinion.”  
> *Jimenez v. Bondi*, § D

The court then went on to apply the *political‑opinion* standard—specifically the “central reason” test from *Rivera‑Barrientos*—and found that the alleged persecution was *not* driven by the petitioners’ opinion. The fact that they did not also argue a PSG claim was irrelevant to that analysis; the PTO ground itself was unsupported.

In other words:

| Ground | What must be shown for asylum |
|--------|------------------------------|
| **Political opinion** | Persecution is *at least one central reason* for the harm (or fear thereof). |
| **Particular social group** | Persistent oppression of a defined group that is *cohesive* and *impreactable.* |

If the asylum seeker believes the government is targeting them **only because of their political views**, that allegation is sufficient as an independent basis for asylum. The PSG ground is not automatically “attached” to a political‑opinion claim; it would have to be asserted and supported separately.

*Jimenez* illustrates this cleanly: the petitioners’ failure to show a central political‑opinion nexus was the decisive factor; the absence of a PSG claim was merely an observation, not a punch‑line. An applicant can, of course, raise both bases if the facts support each, but an omission of one does **not** render the other invalid.

**Bottom line:**  
- You may argue asylum on *political opinion* alone.  
- You need not invoke a PSG argument unless you intend to rely on that ground.  
- The court will evaluate each ground independently, applying its own constitutional and statutory standards.
