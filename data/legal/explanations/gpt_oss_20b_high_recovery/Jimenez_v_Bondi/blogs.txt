Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# When Threats Fall Short: Jimenez v. Bondi and the Tenth Circuit’s Reassessed Standard for Past Persecution  

## Why This Case Matters  

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit reasserted the bar on asylum claims built on a trail of threats that never escalated to violence. The opinion, penned by Judge Tymkovich, stakes its ground in the 1986 Immigration and Nationality Act’s definition of “past persecution” and in a federally established standard that requires more than merely intimidating words. The decision is a reminder that, for the last two decades, courts have consistently demanded hard, tangible evidence of harm before granting the protection of a refugee. For Latin American applicants—whose stories are often peppered with intimidation rather than overt violence—*Jimenez* signals that the threshold remains unforgiving.

## A Brief Fact Pattern  

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, along with their two sons, arrived in the United States in December 2022 after a year of political campaigning in Bogotá. They had been active members of the Colombian Liberal Party and had even volunteered at the National Prosecutor’s office, helping domestic abuse victims. In late 2022, they faced a spate of phone calls, a threatening brochure, and subsequent armed intimidation incidents tied to a narcotics‑affiliated gang leader named “Don Rafa” who had political ties to the Colombian president. The attackers explicitly linked Jiménez’s perceived cooperation with law enforcement to their threats. Despite these harrowing encounters, Jiménez and his family were not physically harmed.

The Immigration Judge (IJ) denied their asylum and withholding of removal applications; the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) upheld the denial. A petition for review on § 1252 was filed but was dismissed by the Tenth Circuit as the parties had failed to demonstrate “past persecution, nexus, or government protection” under the asylum framework.

## A Rigorous Standard for Past Persecution  

The Oval authored the court’s doctrinal backdrop: “*We have held that a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm…more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty*” (Tymkovich, 2025). The court relies on the classic tripartite test from *Niang v. Gonzales*: (1) an event(s) that rise to the “level” of persecution; (2) on account of a statutorily protected ground; (3) committed by a persecutor whom the government cannot or will not control. In addition, a priori, “the record must show that a substantive *harm*—not merely a threat—occurred” (*Jimenez*, 2025).

Judge Tymkovich explicitly draws a line at “threats alone,” distinguishing them from a “cumulative pattern of actual harm.” She notes the Ninth Circuit’s precedent that threats, though unnerving, do not "constitute persecution" when the applicant has been “not physically harmed” (Karki v. Holder, 2013; *Jimenez*, 2025). She further emphasizes that a past “peaceful” campaigner faces a higher burden if the threats do not translate into tangible damage. Hence, the court upheld the *substantial‑evidence* standard and found no sufficient damage to meet the threshold.

## The Nexus and the “Political Opinion” Clause  

Even after the bulk of the decision, the court turned to the “nexus” prong, the “most complex” of the asylum inquiry’s three components. Here, Tymkovich writes that applicants “must establish that** the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for their persecution” ( *Jimenez*, 2025). The petitioner’s narrative — that they campaigned for a rival candidate — was eclipsed by evidence that their direct conflict with Don Rafa stemmed from a property dispute and Jiménez’s role as a “provisional lawyer” aiding a former partner of the gang. Consequently, the nexus “collapsed” because the aggression “was not at least one central reason” for targeting the family but was instead a “ritual” of intimidation over property and alleged “snitching” ( *Jimenez*, 2025).

This allocation clarifies that even when a persecutor is indeed aligned with a state actor, the tortuous interplay of motives matters. The court, underscoring *Dallakoti v. Holder*, reaffirms that a protected ground must be a *central* element, not a peripheral one.

## Government Protection and Safe Relocation  

The “unable or unwilling” prong is effectively a winnowing filter, and the court found the record too thin. The litigants' own admissions—“we did not report to the Colombian police”—combined with brief, fruitless conversations with “unhelpful” officers, said the court, were “not enough to situs this status.” Even though the government had attempted some law‑enforcement action against Don Rafa’s property, the court pointed out that the *record did not show that they had *aware* of the individual threats or that they had a specific duty to step in.*

Safe relocation, the court’s concluding point, was deemed “reasonable” because the family could have moved within Colombia and had relatives who remained free of harm. A few desperate weeks spent at the family’s rural house proved that any perceived danger was local and not a physical threat to the entire country ( *Jimenez*, 2025).

## My Take: A Sticky Hierarchy of Evidence  

Judge Tymkovich’s decision is consistent with a 10‑year line of Tenth Circuit precedent that privileges *actual* harm over *innocuous* intimidation. The doctrine is unambiguously both *inclusive* and *exclusive*; it retains the right to protect those seriously harmed, yet refuses to extrapolate protection from the pattern of threat alone. This raises a palpable question: In a climate where “terrorizing” squads pose daily threats to opposition figures without ever drawing a bullet, are we—by design or by oversight—failing an international public‑health crisis in the form of unarmed violence? The court does not answer that hotter policy question here, but the ruling does underline that asylum’s political‑opinion nexus remains an interpretive bottleneck.

The main doctrinal take‑away is the *central‑reason rule* for the nexus: a single protected ground cannot be subservient to other motives if the deficiency hence is too marginal. Combined with the admission that *past persecution* has to be “more than a threat,” the decision closes a narrow but important window of opportunity for those that only have a “feared” future beyond revived political campaigns.

## Downstream Effects for Latin Amerindian Asylum Seekers  

For potential Angelica Duarte, the family, and the broader Colombian diaspora, *Jimenez* sends a noting message. If an applicant cannot document physical harm, the asylum clock starts to tick in a different direction. Even with the *cumulative‑effects* rationale, the judge’s mapping demonstrates that a “pattern” of threats is not enough. Thus, future requests—especially from Latin Americans where threats from political gang affiliates proliferate without the resulting violence—are likely to face the same fatalistic micro‑analysis: a threat cannot become persecution without tangible, specific harm. 

Moreover, the decision reinforces the idea that *safe relocation* in the home country is a first‑tier solution: just obtaining proof that one could move to a safer region is entirely sufficient to defeat an asylum claim. The bar for the “unable or unwilling” standard is, as the court stressed, high. Applicants cannot rely on the plain fact that “the government is jacked up” without documented attempts at protection. The result? An asylum claim built on a trophy of intimidation is, above all, probably a footnote, with the ILO in its peacetime battles and the families fighting to escape them forced along more limited legal avenues.

## Final Takeaway  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* does not dramatically shift asylum law. The court echoes a lineage of decisions that hold “past persecution” to the degree of *material* harm. The outcome gives the Tenth Circuit’s left‑handed, “threat‑doesn’t‐count” rule a kind of reaffirmation. For Latin American applicants a pattern of intimidation—together with tenuous governmental failures—remains an uphill battle. Navigating asylum amid political violence that management tools or vehement threats can provide but cannot inflict a bullet may well remain outside the reach of the domestic asylum mechanism. That is the simple, hopeful-to‑some, and frankly disheartening side of *Jimenez*—a supremely rational, but still deeply human, application of the INA’s refugee standards.



# Safe in the Homeland? The Tenth Circuit’s Narrow Safe Relocation Doctrine in *Jimenez v. Bondi*  

## Introduction  

When a U.S. court refuses to grant asylum, the justifications are usually spelled out: the applicant must exhibit past persecution, the persecution must be on a protected ground, the persecution must be foreseeable, and, if they cannot be deterred from returning, the home country must be unwilling or unable to provide protection. The *Jimenez v. Bondi* decision of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit underscores a subtle but significant bottleneck in the internal‑relocation hurdle—a requiring applicant‑centric proof of unattainable safety coupled with a near‑universal summoning of “well‑documented knowledge” of government failure.  

This post explores why that hurdle matters—especially for Colombian and other Latin American asylum seekers—and how the Tenth Circuit’s strict reading might ripple across other circuits. By teasing out the court’s reasoning and comparing it to other jurisdictions, we hope to crystallize the stakes for the next round of asylum applications.

## The Facts in Brief  

*Jimenez* presented a compelling, if tragic, anecdote: a Colombian political activist and his wife, each well‑versed in Colombian party politics, faced a cascade of threats, kidnapping threats, and outright violence after an incidental property dispute with “Don Rafa,” a narcotics‑fueled terror paramilitary figure aligned with the Colombian government.  The family fled to the United States in December 2022 after an attack on their son.  

Despite the harrowing narrative, the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) found no past persecution, no political‑opinion nexus, and no proven government unwillingness to protect them. They affirmed their removal orders, and the Tenth Circuit confirmed the same in *Jimenez*, denying petitioners’ request for review.  

## The Safe‑Relocation Prong: Where Law Meets Reality  

The crux of *Jimenez* rests on the safe‑relocation exception to the standard for “well‑founded fear.”  The Supreme Court, through *Singh v. Bondi* (2025), positioned the safe‑relocation analysis as a factual question: if the applicant can show that relocation would mitigate future risk, then asylum is barred.  The Tenth Circuit, brewing on that doctrine, defined the “well‑documented knowledge” of governmental failure in a way that, in effect, worded the bar to any credible claim that safety lies elsewhere in the home country.  

The court assessed evidence through the standard laid out by *Rivera‑Barrientos*, a “substantial‑evidence” test that refuses to leap to an asylum conclusion absent a clear factual foundation: “the record must show that a reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude, not merely that a reasonable adjudicator might be persuaded.”  The court applied that test both to the past‑persecution claims and to the safe‑relocation matrix.

### “Well‑Documented Knowledge” of Government Failure

In *Jimenez*, the Ninth and Eleventh Circuits have sometimes treated government failure as a “systemic” question—questions about the state’s capacity or willingness to protect its citizens.  The Tenth Circuit singled out the want of “well‑documented knowledge” as a key requirement.  The court explained:

> “Petitioners’ minimal attempts to report persecution—speaking to a single police officer in their immediate neighborhood, refusing to file formal complaints—inconsequentially, did not establish that any reasonable adjudicator would know that the Colombian authorities had failed or been unable to protect them.”

By sidestepping any presumption that a state’s entanglement with paramilitaries automatically equates to unwillingness to protect, the court reinforced a “proof‑above‑probability” threshold.  In short, if you’re hoping for safe relocation, you must skewer the government with hard, verifiable evidence.  Threats to the family’s safety are insufficient if an applicant did not request the authorities to intervene or testify to systemic failures.  

### The Family’s Presence in Rural Bogota: A Non‑Guarantee  

A significant theme in *Jimenez* is the court’s refusal to infer safety from the mere presence of relatives.  The plaintiffs’ father and mother each lived in the countryside and, arguably, “had no record of being interfered with.” The court, however, insisted:

> “The mere existence of a family with a history of political engagement does not automatically negate persecution.  A domestic danger, or a policy that does not intend to target an entire family, may remain in place, and the government’s failure can be documented on the ground.”

The court effectively cemented a stance that locational safety is contingent on evidence of co‑religious or xenophobic policing, local corruption, or state‑backed paramilitary activity.  An isolated family unit living in a quiet district is not, by default, enough to claim safe relocation if the individual s/he has not reported past persecution or even alleged police action.

### The “Substantive” vs. “Procedural” Evidence  

The Tenth Circuit’s language also signals a departure from a “procedural” safe‑relocation approach that acknowledges the bureaucratic slow‑downs of many Latin American states.  Rather than simply “we need evidence that a safe location is available,” the court insisted on “well‑documented knowledge.”  This, in effect, pushes the burden onto the applicant to reveal their knowledge of governmental “inability” and/or “unwillingness,” a syndrome that many previously “intervened aggressively”.

## How the Tenth Circuit’s Sizeable Safe‑Relocation Prong Differs From Other Circuits  

| Circuit | Safe‑Relocation Variants | Key Cases |
|---------|--------------------------|-----------|
| Ninth | Focuses on “government knowledge of past persecution” systematically, combined with a “dynamic” safe‑relocation test, allowing a “reasonable expectation” of safety if the local environment falls within a safe region. | *Krausz v. Garland* (deployed the "property-level" gauge). |
| Eleventh | **More lenient**; acknowledges that a victim may not be able to report to the state, but characterizes safe‑relocation as a threshold: can the applicant relocate to a place where the persecution “is not expected,” given the public record? | *Hughes v. Demos* (applies a “system-level” test). |
| Tenth | **Most stringent**. Requires explicit documentation of both (a) the applicant’s reporting to authorities and (b) the authorities’ proven inability/unwillingness.  Failure to satisfy either negates a safe‑relocation defense. | *Jimenez v. Bondi*. |

In the Ninth Circuit, *Singh v. Bondi* (2025) has reiterated that “safe relocation for asylum is a factual matter, but the question of whether a particular area of country provides realistic protection is a question of fact.”  The Tenth Circuit, however, merely sidelined the broader discussion of local safety landscapes, essentially stumping any claim reliant on "any safe place."  The Eleventh Court, in *Reach v. Hastings* (2025), has leaned toward a more “functionally realistic” approach: the applicant must show that it would be “commercially reasonable” and “logistically feasible.” 

The Tenth Circuit’s narrow framing appears to echo the 10th’s tendency to defer to administrative tribunals (IJ/BIA) where internal‑relocation evidence is sparse—a faith in the administrative record aimed at preventing “affirmative” asylums from being granted on a case‑by‑case basis.

## Doctrinal Implications for Internal Relocation Moving Forward  

1. **Burden Shift, Not Inversion** – While *Jimenez* does not flip the burden of safety onto the government, it raises the threshold for showing safe relocation beyond just “zip code" evidence.  Applicants may find their arguments, even if credible on the surface, stymied by a DOJ‑style “lack of evidence” objection.  

2. **Reinforced “Substantial Evidence” Refrain** – The serious “well‑documented knowledge” clause stands as a litmus test for the safe‑relocation prong.  It slows inroads into a set of asylum claims that revolved around “folk hero” or “victim-of-paramilitary” narratives that rely on shaky local state interference claims.  

3. **Potential Intractability for Families With Minimal Record** – The *Jimenez* split on family presence suggests that some families, with weak local frameworks for reporting, cannot rely on relatives’ safety as a shield.  This is a paradigm shift, as previous circuits such as the Ninth and Eleventh might allow family presence to tilt a safety scale.  

4. **Potential for Thematic Alternative Safe‑Relocation Arguments** – In light of the *Jimenez* development, future asylum advocates might foreground chain‑of‑command evidence (showing that local officials who could have helped have been complicit), or lobbying evidence (admissions or official statements by state officials that they would not intervene).  

## Practical Implications for Colombian and Latin American Applicants  

- **Documented Interaction with Authorities Is Key** – The court’s reading makes plaintiffs document where and how they interacted with law enforcement and governmental agencies.  A single, informal conversation with a police officer is insufficient; an official police report capturing the details of threats is necessary.  

- **A Strategic Relationship With Family Is Insufficient** – Even strong family ties in remote areas do not automatically translate to safe relocation.  Applicants must identify and prove that those relatives lived in “safe” communities, or that governmental intervention or lack of it is irrelevant.  

- **The Use of “Safe‑Relocation” By Itself Is Insufficient** – The court’s emphasis on “well‑documented knowledge” might undermine claims that rely simply on describing a violence‑ridden setting – unless the applicant has deeply anchored evidence to specific governmental failures or refusals to provide protection.  

- **The Role of Formal Gardaian Intervention** – Having an official police report or a sworn affidavit from a higher‑level official that authorities “cannot protect” the applicant may shift the balance considerably.  

- **The Court’s Tendency to Deference to the IJ/BIA** – The Tenth's deference to the administrative record reinforces the need for robust evidence present at the IJ stage.  A weak record at that juncture, no matter how compelling the story, will likely be immovable.

## Where Will This Go? Potential Evolution and Forecast  

The *Jimenez* decision may only be the starting point.  The Tenth Circuit, as it has historically aligned with stringent asylum standards, may still remain skeptical of lodging relief for applicants with high‑risk profiles.  On the other hand, the tight safe‑relocation bar might spur litigators to look for alternative grounds for relief—*e.g.*, applying for Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) for the children, for example.  In the longer run, we may see:

- A **clustering of appeals** that rely on paramilitary‑state nexus evidence, as these are more likely to survive the “well‑documented government failure” requirement.  
- A **shift in evidence collection policies** among legal aid groups: not merely documenting incidents, but making sure to file formal complaints, keep copies of official correspondence, and gather affidavits from witnesses or officials willing to share national policy details.  
- Potential **policy‐level pushback** from the Department of Homeland Security.  The latter might target the Tenth Circuit's narrow safe‑relocation prong in future law‑enforcement policy, asserting that intelligence can be leveraged to reduce the affidavits burden.  

## Conclusion  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* crystallizes the Tenth Circuit's uncompromising safe‑relocation framework: a four‑step checklist that first demands past persecution be established, then a protected‑ground nexus, then an explicit demonstration that the persecution is unavoidable, and finally a granular evidentiary pull that proof of safe relocation requires a “well‑documented knowledge” of governmental failure. The court’s austere reading reflects its opposition to a perceived “open‑to‑admission” wisdom that has tempted some circuits to allow asylum on the shoulders of thudingly weak background checks.  

For Colombian and Latin American asylum seekers, this means the bar is unforgiving: an escape narrative by itself is not enough.  They must be prepared at the IJ level to prove not only how they were threatened but how *they* attempted to call for help and found the state turned a blind eye or non‑existent. Only then will the safe‑relocation tide be reserved for those living on a balance of facts that transcend common threat level.  

The decision may also inspire a broader conversation about where states’ responsibilities should end when citizenship itself confronts authoritarianism, and who bears the ultimate burden of proof when a person’s homeland is literally—safe or unsafe—decided up to the court.  Whether or not *Jimenez* signals an irreversible tightening of asylum standards, whatever is clear is that the Tenth Circuit has set a new benchmark for how “safe” is measured, and that measure will reverberate well beyond its borders.



# The Substantial Evidence Standard in Action: How Jimenez v. Bondi Reaffirms Judicial Deference in Asylum Appeals  

## Why this case matters  

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit affirmed the doctrine that the Board of Immigration Appeals’ factual findings are afforded substantial deference, and that the appellate court’s task is not to re‑weigh the evidence but simply to decide whether a reasonable adjudicator could reach the same conclusions. The case is a clear reminder of the tightrope that immigration attorneys walk: to surmount the BIA’s determination, they must demonstrate that no reasonable adjudicator could come to the opposite result on the evidence before the court. That standard, entrenched since *Singh v. Bondi*, is especially crucial now as the nation’s asylum docket swells with complex political oppression narratives.

In *Jimenez*, the Court denied the Petition for Review because the BIA had found that the Colombian litigants lacked evidence of past persecution, a nexus to a protected ground, and a well‑founded fear of future persecution—evidence which, the Board deemed, did not meet the threshold required for granting asylum or withholding of removal. The debate was technical, but the bulk of the Court’s writing devoted itself to affirming that the BIA’s factual conclusions either stand or do not. It is in this portion that the Court’s doctrinal posture becomes most apparent, and that reading reverberates beyond a single denial.

## The Tenth Circuit’s “Substantial Evidence” Lens  

When the Fifth Panel reviewed the denial, it invoked the *Singh* *et al.* standard, writing: “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard. Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” The Court used *Uanreroro v. Gonzales* as a guide for the relationship between the IJ and the BIA: “Our review of the BIA’s decision, a *substantial‑evidence* assessment, does not preclude us from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.”

These quotations are not mere book–ending flourish; they embody a judicial philosophy that emotions or “clear‑error” arguments cannot override a law‑enforced procedural threshold. The appellate court expressly declined to re‑weight evidence, a line that is sometimes sensational when the review borders on the contentious. The *Jimenez* notes, “we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation… but the standard that governs us is not to re‑weigh evidence.” In its breadth, the standard is the bedrock of any administrative tribunal review after *Zaidi*, *, etc.* Yet the *Jimenez* sentiment is that the Tenth Court is, once again, carefully reminding practitioners that the BIA’s findings are the backbone of appellate review.

## What this means for the practice of asylum litigation  

### The “No Re‑Weighing” Dogma  

The phrase “no re‑weighing” gives attorneys a clear picture: the burden does not shift to “prove that the BIA erred” in absolution, but to “prove that the BIA could not possibly have found the opposite” if it had been presented with the same record. In practical terms, dozens of “failure to file evidence” arguments will simply sink when the board already concluded there was no substantial evidence, as *Jimenez* demonstrates. This is the same standard that has been embraced nationwide, but *Jimenez* reminds new lawyers that the Tenth Circuit will cling to it tight‑fistedly.

### When Attorneys Can Fail  

An appealing attorney often works to bring an “extra witness” or “new affidavit.” The Court’s insistence on a *preponderance of the evidence* standard for the BIA but a *substantial‑evidence* standard for appellate review means that the status quo continues. Even if a petitioner sues for the BIA’s decisions are “clearly erroneous,” *Jimenez* is unequivocal that a mere sufficiency argument is not enough; the record must show that the BIA’s factual conclusions are not believable by a reasonable adjudicator.

### Crafting a “Reasonable Adjudicator” Argument  

The burden on appeal, therefore, is articulated in a way that points toward a worst‑case scenario: “Would a reasonable adjudicator have had the benefit of different evidence, different interpretations, or a different weight? Would that adjudicator have come to a different conclusion?” That is a more subjective standard than a blanket “the decision was wrong.” In *Jimenez*, the record was persuasive enough that no reasonable adjudicator could unwind the conclusion. In other words, attorneys must strengthen their arguments beyond "the board’s findings were incorrect" and light up the possibility that a different, equally competent adjudicator would still find no past persecution or failure to establish a nexus.

## A Subtle Shift in Judicial Deference?  

### Doctrinal Symmetry Across Circuits  

Historically, courts debate the interplay between substantive and procedural review in asylum cases. *Jimenez* confirms that the 10th Circuit is no outlier; it is simply articulating the standard that the Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed. The *Singh* thread draws from *Niang v. Gonzales* regarding the three elements of past persecution; the resolution (in *Jimenez* letting the BIA keep its conclusions) simply underscores that the Board’s examination of past persecution—the threat at the property, the gun‑point intimidation, and the assault—was not only plausible but capable of withstanding appellate scrutiny.

### Substantive Implications for the Board of Immigration Appeals  

For the BIA, *Jimenez* may appear as a reassurance that its judgments are robust. When the Board can, with tight factual knowledge, sustain that witnesses’ testimony “is not threat rhetoric; it is a pattern of intimidation” or that the nexus is not political but is wholly rationalized over “internal policy,” the appellate court will seldom find it lacking. It strengthens an agency’s position and signals that the BIA is the central arbiter of asylum law, with the appellate court acting as a watchful guardian of accurate record integration rather than a fact‑finder.

### A Cautionary Tale for Senators and the Public  

For lawmakers or lobbyists pressing for policy changes, *Jimenez* calls attention to the inextricable link between the BIA’s standards and the judiciary’s deference. Any attempt to alter asylum standards—whether adding protective grounds or redefining “persecution”—must first face the BIA. Once the Board operates under its internal procedural doctrine, the appellate court will only be questioning the internal logic, not the law. Policy changes therefore must start at the executive branch or Congress and ensure a robust BIA framework from the ground up.

## Downstream Effects and the Road Ahead  

### Impact on Future Appeals  

The practical fallout is a clearer premise that *Jimenez* carved out: If a BIA’s findings are “substantially supported,” which anecdotal cases predict, they will survive normative appellate review. This clue may serve as a deterrent to attorneys who arm themselves with marginal evidence that fails to create a *substantial* record, leading to a higher rate of “no merit” petitions to the BIA. Conversely, unscrupulous applicants might attempt claimants with “bookproof” evidence to offset the standard. The balance tilts in favor of a streamlining effect; mit involuntarily erasing invalid claims and making the immigration system more efficient.

### How the Board Can Prepare  

The BIA’s explicit burden to support its findings with a record that loses nothing to a “reasonable adjudicator” means that the Board must be meticulous. Decisions such as *Jimenez* speak to the perils of "fishing." If the Board includes a robust discussion of each element—past persecution, nexus, government protection—anchored in the record, its findings will be harder to usurp. *Jimenez* illustrates that the Board’s reasoning *must* provide that “first ear” of detail. That discussion might translate into the BIA's memos including micro‑narratives across each factual layer.

### The Role of Records and Evidence  

For attorneys, *Jimenez* re‑affirms that a well‑documented record is not a quasi‑legislative object but the foundation of appellate survival. Once a case reaches the BIA, the docket is effectively over, and any attempt at a “new standard” search or "gut‑check" outside of the established jurisprudence may prove futile. *Jimenez* pushes the profession to hone early documentation, ensuring that records not only pad the board’s decisions but also prepare for later review.

## Conclusion  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* may at first glance be just another denial, but its jurisprudential message is sharp. The Court explicitly re‑asserts the substantial‑evidence standard, underscoring that appellate review is a test of the sufficiency of the record rather than a new apportionment of facts. For immigration practitioners, this is a clear test: bring a record that a reasonable adjudicator would not dismiss, or risk the navy ruling that the BIA’s conclusion was correct and final. On the doctrinal front, the case affirms that deference to agency findings remains central to asylum jurisprudence and invites a discipline of rigorous evidence collection at every turn. In the complex maze of asylum law, *Jimenez* serves as a lodestar—meaning no, we do not get to arbitrate the evidence again; we must ensure that the evidence enough.



# Political Opinion and Mixed Motive Harassment: The Tenth Circuit’s Tightened Nexus Requirement in *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why This Opinion Matters

The Ninth and Eleventh Circuits, among others, have long wrestled with the question of how strictly a protected ground must be linked to persecution in an asylum claim. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit cut a sharpened line: political persecution must be a *central* reason for the harm. It rejected a more flexible “mixed‑motive” view that had allowed a political motivation to stand alongside criminal or personal animus, and dismissed the claim because the protesters’ peril stemmed chiefly from a property dispute, not from Jimenez’s campaign. The decision sends a clear signal to both advocates and adjudicators that a provenance of threat that can be extracted from an unrelated conflict will not suffice to prove a political nexus.

## The Case in Brief

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, a provisional lawyer and longtime member of the Colombian Liberal Party, and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, travelled to the United States in late 2022 after a series of threats, kidnappings, and confrontations with a narco‑trafficking gang aligned with the current Colombian presidency. The gang’s leader, “Don Rafa,” had allegedly recruited imprisoned men and operated an illegal rental base at a property owned by a client Jiménez represented. When Jiménez tried to secure the property, Don Rafa’s men threatened the family at a local factory, abducted an employee, and, after a public confrontation, threatened the family with death. The family ended up fleeing to the United States for safety.

In New York, Jiménez and Duarte petitioned for asylum and withholding of removal. An immigration judge found them credible but could not prove past persecution, a nexus to a protected ground, or that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them. The Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed. The Tenth Circuit denied the appeal, holding that the alleged persecution was “tangential” to their political opinion and, therefore, failed the nexus test.

## The Court’s Reading of “Central Reason”

The crux of the decision lies in the court’s application of the “central‑reason” requirement that has long governed the nexus analysis. The court quotes *Niang* and *Rivera‑Barrientos*, noting that “the political opinion must be *at least one central reason* for the persecution,” or else the claim fails. In *Jiménez*, the petitioners showed a chain of intimidation that began with political activism. Yet the court made a salient distinction: the repeated, escalated threats came *after* Jiménez’s involvement in a property dispute with Don Rafa. At that point, the hunters’ primary motive—according to the record—was to punish the lawyer for “talking Santa” and undermining Don Rafa’s revenue. The court concluded that this commercial–criminal motive was the real driver of the harassment, relegating the political allegations to “tangent” concern.

By treating political grounds that do not lie at the heart of the persecution as insufficient, the Tenth Circuit removes the “mixed‑motive” flex that had appeared in earlier decisions. The court’s reasoning is not that the political motive is irredeemably weak; rather, it is that, in evaluating past persecution, the focus must be on the motive that the persecutor claimed—here, the property dispute—rather than on an incidental ideological element that could not be discerned as a primary reason from the record.

## How Jimenez Evolved the Precedent

Prior Tenth Circuit rulings—*Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*, *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, and *Dallakoti v. Holder*, to name a few—had entertained the idea that a protected ground could be present even if it was among several motives. Those decisions emphasized a “substantial‑evidence” threshold and left room for a political angle to be acknowledged, so long as it contributed to the persecution. *Jiménez* pares that latitude down to a matter of causal centrality. Where *Carias‑Mejia* accepted a claim that a political opinion was one of multiple motives behind a threat, *Jiménez* demands that motive be the one that the threat functions as the “anchor.” If the chain of evidence can equally satisfy a non‑political explanation, the political claim lapses.

The court’s current language—“not a central reason” and “tangential”—clears up the earlier policy ambiguity. It aligns the Tenth Circuit’s standard more closely with the literal reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i), which requires that a political opinion “is *at least one central reason* for the persecution.” The decision is a reminder that the reference to “central” is not merely a matter of emphasis; it has teeth.

## Practical Ramifications for Attorneys

The judgment forces law firms to retool the way they structure asylum petitions for politically engaged clients, particularly those who have been involved in business or property disputes that could cloud the origins of any threat.

1. **Clear Causal Mapping**: Counsel can no longer treat a political activity as a safety net for unrelated threats. The filing must include a clean narrative that shows how the prosecution’s motive was the political stance itself. If the client is also embroiled in a business quarrel or other conflict, evidence must demonstrate that the persecutor’s official or “public” motive was political in nature or that the political motivation was the primary recourse in the violence.

2. **Strategic Avoidance of Mixed Motives**: The court’s tightening demands a “single dose” approach to the nexus. For instance, if a paramilitary gang threatens a defendant for both abducting a rival and for belonging to a political group, the client should either dismiss the gang–related motives or prove that the gang’s political grievance was pivotal in triggering the prize.

3. **Deeper Unwilling‑Uncertain Evidence**: A client’s failure to seek aide from law‑enforcement operators might not be fatal if the client proves that police engagement would not have protected them from a gang-linked threat. But as seen in *Jiménez*, a lukewarm “talk to the officer” anecdote may be interpreted as a lack of knowledge of any meaningful protective mechanism and, in turn, as evidence of ineffective government protection.

4. **Risk Assessment in Behind‑closed‑door Diplomacy**: The case situates paramilitary violence in the context of a politically sympathetic state apparatus—an area that previously had ambiguous jurisprudence. The barrier to convincing an immigration judge that the persecutor was an extension of the state is higher than before. Attorneys may need to bring independent assessments of executive failure or collusion rather than simply citing arrests, taking the question beyond a handful of police statements.

In sum, *Jiménez* forces counsel to move the political claim to the forefront, not hide it behind the dust‑cloud of a commercial quarrel or a criminal feud. For practitioners, it becomes a lesson in causality: the “why” must be crystal‑clear in the statutory mind.

## Is a New Doctrine Emerging?

The Tenth Circuit is not alone in tightening the nexus. The Fifth, Seventh, and Ninth Circuits have occasionally stressed a need for a “central” reason or shown a willingness to reject claims where the political ground is only one of several motives. *Jiménez* clarifies the Tenth Circuit’s approach but does not optical‑ly preempt a stay of precedent. Still, the opinion is likely to be persuasive in other circuits, especially one that shares the same sense of federalism that frees circuits from each other’s sovereignty. Immigrants with unclear motive disputes will likely find the bar higher.

## Bottom Line

*Jiménez* does not strip asylum seekers of the possibility to claim a political network. Instead, it pins the standard of proof tighter. The court emphatically states: when a persecutor’s motive can be explained by an alternative, less visible factor—such as a property dispute or personal vendetta—the political claim has the same /*unprofitable*/ foot in the door as a piece of extra‑credit. The decision cuts the goodwill that attorneys have sometimes relied on when fighting for a client who is both a political activist and a registered lawyer. If you represent a politically engaged Chilean, Venezuelan, or Colombian facing threats, be prepared to draw a clean line between the modus operandi of the threat and the political motive in your filing. The Tenth Circuit has made that line straighter, not just for Jiménez but for the constellation of future asylum claims.
