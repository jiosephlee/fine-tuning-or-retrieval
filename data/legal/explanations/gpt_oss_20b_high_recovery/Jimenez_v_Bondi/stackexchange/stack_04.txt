Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How significant is a single police officer’s statement in the government‑protection assessment for asylum?
Question:
In the case, the plaintiffs only spoke to a lone police officer who told them not to seek trouble, and the court said this is not enough to show that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them. I want to understand how the "notice" and "reporting" requirement is applied and how much weight a single officer’s attitude carries. Could you explain the guidelines or precedent for evaluating such evidence?
Answer:
**Short answer** – In the 10th Circuit’s view, a *single* police officer’s dismissive comment does not, by itself, satisfy the “notice‐and‑reporting” element of the “government‑unwilling or –unable” exception in an asylum case.  The court requires that the applicant either show that the government had *systemic* knowledge of the persecution or that repeated, tangible evidence shows the police (or another governmental body) failed to act.  One chat with an officer who merely told the applicant to “stay away” is treated as “minimal reporting” and is weighted very lightly, unless it is part of a broader pattern that reveals a government’s inability or unwillingness to protect the applicant.

---

## 1.  The “notice‑and‑reporting” framework

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1) and the 10th Circuit precedent, an asylum applicant **may rely on the government‑unwilling or –unable** exception only if:

1. **The government had notice** of the applicant’s past persecution, *and*
2. **The government offered protection** (e.g., protective custody, active intervention) *or* took actions that *indicated* it was willing to control the persecutors.

See Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861 (citing Singh, 130 F.4th at 861).  
“If the applicant has no notice*, we cannot know how the police would have acted” (ibid.). “Notice is a prerequisite to proving that the government was unwilling or unable.”

In practice, the burden is on the applicant to demonstrate that the Indonesian—or Colombian, in Jimenez—government knew about the persecution and, faced with that knowledge, either failed to act or did not offer protection.  The court therefore pays close attention to **any evidence that the applicant reported the persecution** to the authorities.

---

## 2.  What the Jimenez opinion says about a single officer’s remark

Jimenez emphatically notes that the applicants’ minimal interaction with law‑enforcement “was **not enough**” to meet the notice requirement:

> “Petitioners’ efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any… the record does **not** show that Petitioners sought help… through Jimenez’s law‑enforcement contacts.”  
> —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62

The opinion goes further:

> “The applicant may satisfy the government‑protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous.”  
> —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861

and

> “Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond… a one‑off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect a victim.”  
> —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861


### Key take‑aways

* **“Notice” ≠ “one conversation.”** The court requires that the author of the notice (the police department, the city, the national government) had *systemic* knowledge of the persecution before any protective action was attempted.
* **“Minimal reporting” is treated with skepticism.** A single, informal encounter that offers no follow‑up (such as a police report filed, a summons, an investigation, a protective‑custody action, or a credible threat of retaliation for reporting) is insufficient.
* **A pattern of unhelpful or hostile officers isn’t automatically enough.** The court prefers *public evidence* (media reports, court filings, documented police negligence, or testimony that indicates a police department routinely ignores such complaints). “That same men… told the Petitioners the police officers were contributing to the problem” speaks to individual attitudes, but without documentary evidence of the department’s overall response or lack thereof the court cannot infer a government‑wide incapacity.

---

## 3.  How courts weigh evidence of the government’s willingness or inability

The 10th Circuit’s standard is designed to tie the denial of asylum to an objective, *substantial‑evidence* standard:

1. **Substantial evidence** – The BIA’s findings stand unless another reasonable adjudicator would necessarily reach the opposite conclusion.  
   > “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard… The BIA’s findings are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator… would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.”  
   —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 851

2. **Evidence of police action** – Police reports, arrest records, or official responses to complaints are the most persuasive.  
   *Lack of such evidence, even if the applicant asserts personal conversations, weighs in favor of the BIA’s finding that the government was *not* unwilling or unable.*  
   —Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62

3. **Pattern or systemic failure** – The *evidence must suggest a systemic lack of protection*, not a single anecdotal incident. The court refers to *Osorio‑Morales v. Garland*, 72 F.4th at 743, for this rationale:
   > “we often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people.”  

4. **Alternative explanations** – Courts also consider whether reporting would have been futile or dangerous. *Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland*, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2, strengthens the notion that *failure to report* is not fatal unless the decision can be shown to be due to the unavailability or unwillingness of the police to help the applicant.  

Taken together, a one‑off conversation with a single policeman can be reasonable **only** when the broader record eliminates other possibilities. If the applicant can show, for example, that the police department conducted *no* investigations into the alleged threats, that the department has a documented policy of turning away political activists, or that the police were visibly threatened or intimidated by the perpetrators, then the single conversation becomes part of a weighty pattern. Absent any such corroborating evidence, the conversation is treated as a weak or “unable‑to‑verify” piece of evidence and **does not tip the balance**.

---

## 4.  Practical guidance for applicants and practitioners

| Step | What to prove | What the Opinion says | Typical evidence that will carry the most weight |
|------|---------------|-----------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| 1 | **Past persecution** is *not merely threats* – must be “offensive” harm. | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 852‑54. | Physical injury, credible, documented police arrest, evidence of the threat being imminent or actualized. |
| 2 | **Notice** – the government *knew* about the persecution. | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62; *Osorio‑Morales* | Police reports filed, summons to appear in court, documented attempts by police to investigate, media coverage of employee or police statements. |
| 3 | **Reporting** – the applicant *told the police* or other governmental body about the persecution. | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62; *Aviles‑Gonzalez* | Written complaint, affidavit, recorded conversation that was later transcribed. |
| 4 | **Evidence of failure to act** – the government was *unable or unwilling* to protect. | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62 | Police failure to investigate, failure to provide protective custody, chain of “unresponsive” police when other victims received help, or a clear statement that the police would not intervene because of connection to perpetrators. |
| 5 | **Pattern or systemic issue** – not just a “single conversation.” | Jimenez, 131 F.4th at 861‑62; *Osorio‑Morales* | Multiple reports filed across different departments, repeated refusals or warnings and no subsequent action, official documentation or expert testimony that law‑enforcement is shut down against political activists. |

---

## 5.  Bottom line

In **Jimenez v. Bondi**, the court expressly rejected the applicants’ reliance on a handful of brief, unprotected conversations with two police officers. The court held that those conversations did **not** satisfy the notice‑and‑reporting requirement, and that the **absence** of substantive, corroborated evidence of police or governmental failure meant the applicants could not establish that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them. The decision makes it clear that a *single* officer’s statement, without further evidence of systemic police failure or inside evidence that the police were aware and unhelpful, is too weak to bear on the asylum government‑protection prong.

**For practice:** Whenever you argue that police were not helpful, demonstrate that the department actually *knew* about the persecution (through reports, investigative records, or a documented policy) and failed to act. If you can only present a single informal conversation, the court will treat it as inconclusive, and the government‑protection exception will almost certainly fail.
