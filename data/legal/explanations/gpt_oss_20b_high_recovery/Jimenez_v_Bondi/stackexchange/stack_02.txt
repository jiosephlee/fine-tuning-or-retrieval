Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Can threats alone be considered past persecution under the INA?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi the court held that threats are not sufficient on their own unless accompanied by actual physical harm, citing Karki v. Holder, Chhetri v. Rosen, and others. I would like to understand what qualifies as "persecution" under § 1101(a)(42)(A) and whether psychological or emotional harm can substitute for physical harm. Also, are there statutory criteria that differentiate mere threats from persecution? Please clarify the standards and provide examples.
Answer:
**No.  In the …**  
The Courts have made it clear that *threats alone—whether verbal, written, or a single phone call—do not meet the statutory threshold for “past persecution” under § 1101(a)(42)(A). The requirements are higher; the law tests whether the applicant has actually suffered “suffering or harm” that is more than a mere restriction or threat of violence.  The case law the question cites—**Karki v. Holder** (10th Cir. 2013), **Chhetri v. Rosen** (10th Cir. 2021), and **Carias‑Mejia v. Garland** (10th Cir. 2021)—all consistently held that a solitary or even a handful of threats fails to establish persecution unless they culminate in actual physical harm or are part of a credible, imminent assault.

---

## 1.  Statutory framework

1. **§ 1158(b)(1)** – The 1986 definition of “refugee” requires that a non‑citizen be “unable or unwilling” to return because of **persecution** or a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground—race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.  
2. **Past persecution** is the factual element the applicant bears the burden of proving “by a preponderance of the evidence.”  
3. The Constitution of “persecution” is not provided in the statute, but the Courts have elaborated that it requires the infliction of **suffering or harm that is offensive and more than a mere threat or restriction** (see *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004); further discussed in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 1 F.4th 936, 944‑45 (10th Cir. 2025)).

The statutory sheet does not contain specific “threat clauses”; instead, it requires the *actual* infliction of harm.

---

## 2.  Judicial treatment of threats

| Case | Threats described | Outcome |
|------|-------------------|---------|
| **Karki v. Holder** | A single menacing phone call warning of potential death | *Not persecution.*  The Court held that the threat, without a corresponding act of violence or credible imminent danger, did not rise to the level of “suffering or harm.” |
| **Chhetri v. Rosen** | Four telephone threats plus one vague in‑person threat | *Not persecution.*  The Court reiterated that “threats alone … are insufficient to constitute persecution.” |
| **Carias‑Mejia v. Garland** | Threats with machetes, repeated robberies | *Not persecution.*  The Court found that when the threats were “contingent on compliance” and did not actually inflict harm, they did not meet the statutory standard. |
| **Jimenez v. Bondi** | Repeated phone threats, a property dispute, two gun‑point encounters, and a near‑physical abduction attempt | *Not persecution.*  The Court stressed that “threats alone are insufficient … Petitioner conceded no lasting physical harm.” It also noted that the Court has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (¶ 63‑64) |

The common thread: **mere threats, even when repeated or in the context of a politically motivated background, fail to satisfy “persecution” unless accompanied by actual violence, a credible immediate danger, or a pattern of intimidation that culminates in physical harm.**

---

## 3.  Psychological / emotional harm

The law affirms that **psychological or emotional harm can be part of persecution**—if it constitutes “suffering or harm” that is as serious as a physical injury.  However, the Court in *Jimenez* stressed that the standard is not met by mere anxiety or fear stemming from threats:

> “We have rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.”  
> ( *Jimenez*, 1 F.4th 944‑45)

In practice, the Court has accepted extreme psychological trauma—such as PTSD following repeated abductions or systematic intimidation—as *suffering* that can meet the threshold.  But isolated emotional distress caused by a threat is **not** enough.

---

## 4.  How the Court distinguishes threats from persecution

| Element | What the Court looks for | This case’s analysis |
|---------|--------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Actual harm** | Physical injury, kidnapping, or credible immediacy of violence | • All decisions that rejected a threat‑only claim noted the absence of physical harm. |
| **Severity | Suffering must be “offensive” and “more than a restriction.” | • In *Jimenez*, the Court said threats “must entail more than just restrictions or sporadic threats.” |
| **Nexus to protected ground** | Must be a “protected” ground that is a **central** reason for the persecution (e.g., political opinion). | • *Jimenez* found the prosecution was centered on a personal conflict with a criminal associate, not on political opinion. |
| **State or state‑controlled perpetrator** | Must be the government itself, a public official, or a person the state can control. | • The Court reviewed whether the perpetrator was tied to the Colombian state or a private criminal group. |

If a threat satisfies those criteria—especially if it’s tied to a state actor and accompanied by imminent physical danger—then it can cross the threshold into “persecution.”  Otherwise, it remains “dangerous but not persecutory.”

---

## 5.  Bottom line for a student

*Threats alone do **not** constitute past persecution under § 1101(a)(42)(A).*  
- **Necessity of suffering/harm**: The threat must (directly or indirectly) cause real injury, or be part of a credible, imminent assault.  
- **Psychological harm** can only substitute for physical harm when the harm is **severe** and **documented** (e.g., PTSD, chronic intrusive memories).  
- **Statutory differentiation**: The INA does not provide a separate clause for “threats”; it requires “suffering or harm.” Courts have used that standard to differentiate a mere threat from persecution.  
- **Significant exceptions**: A threat that accompanies actual violence, is issued by a state actor, or is part of a systematic pattern culminating in physical attacks may be deemed persecution.  
- **Contrast**: In **Jimenez v. Bondi** the Court found that “threats…without physical harm” were insufficient, echoing **Karki** and **Chhetri.**  

When drafting or arguing an asylum petition, always pair a threat with evidence of:
1. **Physical harm, or credible imminent violence**;  
2. **Tied to a protected ground**; and  
3. **Committed by, or with knowledge of, a state actor.**

Only then does the threat move from the realm of “intimidation” to the legal threshold of “persecution.”
