Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What standard of review does the 10th Circuit apply to a BIA’s denial of asylum under 8 U.S.C. § 1252?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi the court reviewed the BIA’s denial of asylum and stated that the review is limited to the "administrative record on which the order of removal is based" (citing § 1252(b)(4)(A)). The court also notes that the BIA’s findings are subject to the "substantial‑evidence" standard. I am confused about how the appellate court treats the IJ’s explanation of the same facts, whether it gives the conclusion de‑novo, and whether the review is "pre‑ponderance of the evidence" or something else. What exactly is the standard the appellate court uses, and how does it treat the record of the IJ versus the BIA?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the 10th Circuit reviewed the BIA’s denial of asylum under § 1252(b)(4)(A) – i.e., *the administrative record on which the order of removal is based* – and applied the *substantial‑evidence* standard to the BIA’s factual findings. The court also noted that it is “not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds,” but it does **not** re‑weigh the evidence or give the IJ its findings de novo. The IJ’s record is treated simply as part of the administrative record that the appellate court evaluates for substantial evidence.

---

### How the court applies the standard

| Step | What the court says | Practical effect |
|------|--------------------|------------------|
| 1.  Scope | “Our review of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’” 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A) (See *Jimenez*, 2025 WL 6000). | Only the IJ, BIA, and other pre‑removal evidence are considered; new evidence is excluded. |
| 2.  Factual findings | “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard. Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” (*Jimenez*, 2025 WL 6000, at *4‑5*; citing *Rivera‑Barrientos*). | The appellate court does not apply a *preponderance of the evidence* test. Instead, it asks whether the record contains *substantial evidence* supporting the BIA’s conclusions. If it does, the findings stand. If it does not, the BIA is found to have erred. |
| 3.  Legal conclusions | The court treats the BIA’s **legal** rulings as subject to de novo review (the usual rule for § 1252 cases). | Only factual findings are protected by “substantial evidence”; legal arguments are reviewed afresh. |
| 4.  Use of the IJ’s record | “We are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” (*Jimenez*, 2025 WL 6000, at *3*; citing *Uanreroro*). | The IJ’s explanation is part of the record and is used to decide if the evidence is substantial. It does **not** give the IJ an independent factual basis that the appellate court must separately evaluate. |

---

### Why the “substantial‑evidence” standard is key

*Substantial evidence* is a *deferential* standard: the appellate court gives the original adjudicator (the BIA) the benefit of the doubt and will not reverse unless **any** reasonable adjudicator could have arrived at a different conclusion. It is **not** the same as “preponderance of the evidence” (the standard a trial judge uses when deciding G. v. K.) or “beyond a reasonable doubt,” and it provides a higher bar for reverse than a preponderance test would. The court’s statement that the BIA’s findings “are conclusive unless the record demonstrates…a contrary conclusion” makes this clear.

---

### How the IJ’s record is treated

- The IJ’s explanation of the same facts is **consulted** but *not* treated as a separate factual determination.  
- The appellate court looks to see whether that explanation, combined with all other administrative evidence, creates *substantial evidence* for the BIA’s conclusions.  
- If it does, the BIA’s findings are upheld. If it doesn’t, the court declares the BIA wrong.  
- The IJ’s record is therefore part of the evidence the appellate court reviews under the substantial‑evidence standard; it does not create a new “de‑novo” factual analysis.

---

### Bottom line

In *Jimenez* the 10th Circuit applies:

1. **Substantial‑evidence** standard to the BIA’s factual findings.  
2. **De novo** review to the BIA’s legal conclusions.  
3. The IJ’s record is treated as part of the same administrative record and is used only to determine whether substantial evidence exists—no new factual weighing is performed.

That is the standard the court uses when reviewing a BIA denial of asylum under § 1252.
