Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the legal definition of 'refugee' under 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A) interact with the requirement of a well‑founded fear of future persecution?
Question:
In the opinion, the Court said asylum requires proof of either past persecution or a well‑founded fear of future persecution, but "well‑founded fear" applies in the withholding context too. I’m uncertain how the statutory definition and the standard of proof differ between asylum and withholding, especially with reference to the threshold evidence required for each. Please explain the relationship.
Answer:
**Short answer**

*Both asylum and withholding of removal use the same statutory definition of “refugee.”  The difference is the *threshold* of evidence that must allow the refugee definition to be satisfied.  
* For **asylum** a preponderance of evidence that the applicant is a refugee is enough – past persecution automatically gives a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.  
* For **withholding** the applicant must still prove they are a refugee, but the “well‑founded fear” must survive two extra hurdles:  

  1. **Government security** – the Colombian government must be *unable* or *unwilling* to protect the applicant.  
  2. **Safe relocation** – the applicant must be unable to relocate safely within Colombia.  

If the asylum claim fails, the withholding claim fails automatically because the higher bar has not been met.  (See J. Jiménez v. Bondi, 130 F. 4th at 861–66, 864‑65; Carias‑Mejia v. Garland, 860 App’x at 125.)**

---

### 1.  Statutory definition – what “refugee” means

The INA (8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A)) defines a *refugee* as:

> “any person **outside the country of his nationality**…who *is unable or unwilling to return to, … because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution* on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.”

The Court makes it clear that the statutory definition is the same for both reliefs:

> “To be eligible for a discretionary grant of asylum…the applicant bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that he is a refugee…8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1).”  
> – *Jiménez* (para. B)

> “Because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well‑founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.”  
> – *Jiménez* (para. III)

So the *same* six elements need to be satisfied in both contexts.

---

### 2.  Asylum – how “well‑founded fear” is used

* **Burden** – Preponderance of the evidence that the applicant is a refugee.  
* **Past persecution → presumption** – A showing of past persecution gives rise to a *rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution* (Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194–95).  
* **“Well‑founded fear” alone** – If past persecution is absent, the applicant still must demonstrate a *well‑founded fear of future persecution*, which can be based on threats, a hazardous national context, or credible evidence of future harm.  
* The Court in *Jiménez* noted that “threats alone are insufficient” but can contribute to a *cumulative* or *emotional/psychological* component of persecution.  
* If the asylum claim is denied, the applicant cannot automatically get withholding because the inverse relationship is not mechanically drawn; the applicant must satisfy a **higher bar**.

---

### 3.  Withholding of removal – the higher bar

Withholding requires the same **refugee definition** but adds the following **extra evidence**:

| Requirement | Asylum | Withholding | Why it matters |
|-------------|--------|-------------|----------------|
| **Well‑founded fear** | Satisfies refugee definition. | Must be *credible* AND **coupled** with *government unwilling/unable* protection. | Failure to show government incapacity defeats withholding. |
| **Government protection** | Not expressly required (unless to rebut safe relocation). | Must **prove** that Colombian authorities are unwilling or unable to protect the applicant. | As *Jiménez* shows, lack of police assistance or systemic failure does not automatically establish unavailability; specific evidence of *unwillingness* or *ineffectiveness* is needed. |
| **Safe relocation** | Only considered when a removal order is issued. | Must **prove** that safe relocation is **impossible** within Colombia. | *Jiménez* notes that internal relocation is a “reset” test; if the applicant could relocate safely, withholding is barred. |

The opinion says:

> “Because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well‑founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. (Carias‑Mejia, 860 App’x at 125)”

Thus, the threshold for withholding is *strictly higher* than for asylum: you need the same refugee definition *plus* evidence that the host government cannot protect and that safe relocation is unavailable.

---

### 4.  Practical takeaway for a law‑student

1. **Start with the refugee definition.**  *Both* asylum and withholding hinge on proving the statutory “refugee.”  
2. **Show past persecution first.**  If you have past persecution, you get a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.   
3. **If past persecution is absent,**  you must present a *well‑founded fear of future persecution* that is credible and based on the status of the host country.  
4. **For withholding**,  in addition to the above, supply strong evidence that the government is *unable* or *unwilling* to protect you and that no safe relocation is available.  
5. **Failure in asylum** translates to an automatic **failure in withholding** because the withholding standard subsumes the asylum standard but adds the evidenced government and relocation hurdles.

---

**Bottom line:** *Well‑founded fear* is the hinge point in both reliefs.  Under asylum, it suffices to satisfy the refugee definition, especially when past persecution is shown.  For withholding, the same fear must be accompanied by incontrovertible proof that the host government cannot, and the applicant cannot, find a safe place in the home country.  The Court’s language in *Jiménez* makes that distinction crystal‑clear—you do not get “well‑founded fear” for withholding just because you get it for asylum.*
