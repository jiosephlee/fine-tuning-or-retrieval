Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 5: # Safe Relocation, Final Holding, and Significance

## Safe Relocation Standard Under Asylum Law and Regulatory Guidance

The safe‑relocation doctrine is one of the key safeguards that protects victims of persecution from automatic asylum grants when a credible risk of future persecution can be mitigated by internal relocation. The statutory framework is found in 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1) and 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A). Where an applicant has limited evidence of a well‑founded fear of future persecution, the asylum adjudicating officer may refuse relief if the applicant can show that relocation to another part of the country would likely be “safe and reliable” (see 8 CFR § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)). When an applicant _can_ take the step of relocation, the presumption of a well‑founded fear is rebutted under a substantial‑evidence standard. The Supreme Court has held that this standard is a “protective shield” reliable for parties who have not been able to substantiate the direct link between the state and a specific victim. The Tenth Circuit follows this approach, emphasizing that the burden rests on the government or the applicant to demonstrate internal relocation is reasonably attainable.

In the present case the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and the Immigration Judge (IJ) concluded that Petitioners could relocate within Colombia, thereby rebutting a presumption of a well‑founded fear under § 1158(b). The appellate court thus reviewed that finding under the same substantial‑evidence standard.

## Evidence Indicating Possible Relocation Away from the Threatened Area

Petitioners met“filming state by the presence of a home in the countryside, their connection to the federal government's criminal‑justice system,” and the fact that they had lived with Jiménez’s mother for several weeks prior to emigrating. The record specifically indicated that the family had continued to reside in parts of Bogotá and the surrounding countryside, experiencing few or no threats outside the specific factory area. The alleged escalation in threats and violence – notably the gun‑point intimidation and kidnapping attempts – were tied to Don Rafa’s territory in the Bogota center. Petitioners themselves acknowledged that the men who threatened them had offered a “stay‑in‑the‑country” admonition.

The BIA’s safety analysis was not based on the abstract: Petitioners had "family and relatives who remained in Colombia" and had been able to seek help from local authorities with minimal success. The IJ found no evidence that they had rejected internal relocation efforts because of the threat. Importantly, the record also shows that the family was cared for during the three‑week period in the countryside and that there were no subsequent requests for relocation or denial of such requests by local authorities. Consequently, the court was credibly satisfied that Petitioners could reasonably move to rural or suburban areas without leaving Colombia, thereby undermining the presumption of a future threat.

## Evaluation of the Family’s Ability to Relocate Within Colombia and Mitigate Risk

The Court considered the “bidirectional” nature of safe relocation: the claimant must show that relocation would _protect_ the claimant from persecution, and that the relocation is _appropriate_ and appears readily achievable. This evaluation requires the same weighty evidence that a real threat of persecution exists, but the courts also examine whether all the government’s information is known and whether there are clear evidence of systemic impunity.

The BIA’s and IJ’s findings were substantially supported by court‑approved criteria. The authors noted that _Petitioners’ pro‑government contacts_ – although “clergy‑government” – did not translate into guarded protection because the “officials who advised them to stay out of trouble were individual officers who could not shield the family.” These officers were “deemed unwilling or lacking the capacity” to provide protective custody or an effective threat response. Nonetheless, the court found that the lack of such a response did not preclude the possibility of safe relocation because the applicants had not yet sought any formal government assistance that might demonstrate a systemic obstacle.

Moreover, in litigation such as *Rio Vegetales v. Snyder*, a Tenth Circuit precedent, the court emphasized that internal relocation is more credible when an applicant has documented family ties to a safe region. The aggressive presence of the Colombian state in Bogotá, as evidenced by prior attempts at property seizures and internal investigations, did not prove that petitioners could not shift to a safer part of the country. Accordingly, the court concluded that the “risk of both domestic and cross‑border persecution could have been minimized by safe relocation.”

## Linkage Between Failed Asylum Claim and Withholding of Removal Denial

Asylum and withholding of removal share a single factual foundation under the INA: a "well‑founded fear of future persecution." The court’s holding that the Petitioners could safe‑relocate thereby rebutting a presumption of a well‑founded fear is, in the law, identical for both remedies. The BIA's rejection of the asylum claim under the political‑opinion nexus standard automatically entailed that the petitioners also failed to meet the threshold for withholding. Indeed, in *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*, the Tenth Circuit had already affirmed that the same standard applies. *Jimenez v. Bondi* reaffirms this link: the denial of asylum under the acceptable political‑opinion criteria is the basis for dismissal of the withholding claim. Failure to meet the safe‑relocation threshold directly undermines the “internal relocation” element required to rebut the presumption.

## Overall Holding: Denial of Petition for Review and Reaffirmation of BIA Decision

The Court, citing *Singh v. Bondi*, declined to review issues beyond the BIA’s findings. Under the limited jurisdiction granted by 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and the review standard, the Court affirmed the BIA’s conclusions regarding both asylum and withholding. The underlying reasoning was:

1. Petitioner’s alleged threats and emotional distress *did not aggregate to persecution*. The standard requires more than “sp(heme) threats or psychological harm”; it requires actual physical harm or a persistent, demonstrable threat.
2. The supposedly political‑opinion motive was *tangential* to the actual motive (real‑world grey‑market enforcement) because the threat was triggered by a property dispute, not the electoral activity itself.
3. Petitioners had recourse to internal relocation, as indicated by a lack of documented unsuccessful attempts at seeking official protection and by their capacity to remain in a safe region of Colombia.

In light of those findings, the Court denied the petition for review and thereby upheld the BIA’s denials of asylum and withholding of removal. The Court emphasized that “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety” (Peters’ Brief 18), but that the court would not “grant asylum based on hypothetical hardships” absent concrete evidence.

## Legal Implications for Future Asylum Applicants

The case reinforces three critical thresholds:

1. **Persecution Standard** – Threats alone, when not accompanied by palpable physical harm or a sustained pattern of coercion, are insufficient. Applicants should provide evidence of *actual* harm or credible, *systemic* persecution.
2. **Nexus to Protected Ground** – The state must demonstrate that a protected ground (here, political opinion) is an *independent*, central factor in the persecution, not a superficial or secondary motive. Mixed‑motive claims must weigh heavily on the protected ground to the satisfaction of the adjudicating officers.
3. **Safe Relocation** – Even in the face of credible threats, applicants can defeat the presumption of a well‑founded fear through *circumstantial* evidence that safe relocation is practicable. Demonstrating family ties to other regions, having previously lived in safe zones, and showing that no systematic impediment exists to relocation can all support a dismissal of the asylum claim.

Failure on any one of these troika—past persecution, nexus, and safe relocation—renders an asylum claim moot. Moreover, because the same standards underlie the withholding of removal, applicants must meet them in order to secure either remedy.

## Significance of the Case for Tenth Circuit Jurisprudence on Asylum and Withholding

*Jimenez v. Bondi* is a textbook illustration of the Circuit’s consistent emphasis on **substantial evidence** as the gatekeeper in asylum adjudication and the binding *two‑step* analysis of past persecution and safe relocation. It distinguishes itself by reaffirming the litigant’s burden in providing *cumulative* evidence of persecution and a *central* nexus to a protected ground in political‑opinion cases. As *Singh v. Bondi* clarified oversight of numerous final orders of removal, this decision further delineates the court’s jurisdictional reach and demonstrates the limited scope of appellate review under § 1252.

Furthermore, the case signals to future applicants that the judicial apparatus closes a ***well‑founded fear* conclusion swiftly when the existential risk can be mitigated by safe relocation*, indeed rendering the asylum denial a direct precursor to withholding denial. The Tenth Circuit thereby articulates, through concrete facts, the *legal appetite* for rigorous evidence that a claimant truly meets all three prongs of the asylum standard, or risk being denied on both fronts.
