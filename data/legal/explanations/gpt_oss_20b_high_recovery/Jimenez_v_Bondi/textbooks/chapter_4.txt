Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 4: # Nexus, Government Protection, and Future Persecution

## Nature of the Nexus Requirement and Central‑Reason Test

The asylum nexus—whether past persecution was “on account of” a protected ground—constitutes the second element of the refugee definition. In *Niang* v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187 (10th Cir. 2005), the court reiterated that a claimant must prove that past persecution was *at least* one central reason for the harm, not merely an incidental or tangential factor. The central‑reason test is anchored in *Rivera‑Barrientos* v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641 (10th Cir. 2012), which instructs review of the Board’s nexus findings only to see whether the record “supports” that at least one protected ground was a central reason for the persecution. A claimant who demonstrates that a persecutor’s motive was a *mix* of reasons must show that the protected ground was *at least as important* as the other motives. That standard—expressed in *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i))—operates as a “substantial‑evidence” check: if the Board’s findings rest on data that, if viewed from a reasonable perspective, could have led to the opposite conclusion, the appellate court must reverse.

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Board’s nexus analysis rested on the allegation that the petitioners’ Latin‑American political engagement—supporting a presidential candidate—rendered them targets of credible threats and intimidation. The Board and the IJ recognized three elements under the nexus test:

1. *Past persecution*; 2. *Persecution on account of a protected ground*; 3. *The threat of future persecution* (irrelevant for the nexus analysis but relevant for the overall asylum grant).

Only the second element—whether the persecution was on account of a protected ground—was at issue in this chapter; the court found that the Board’s conclusion was supported by the record as it turned out that *political opinion* was not a **central** reason for the persecution. The refusal to satisfy the central‑reason test effectively negated the petitioner’s answer to the *Niang* burden, and made the case inoperative for further scrutiny of *future persecution* or *withholding of removal*.

## Distinguishing Political Opinion from Criminal Affiliation in Asylum Cases

The asylum statute protects political opinion, defined as beliefs or attitudes “regarding politics,” such as beliefs about the functioning, direction, or composition of a government (see *Niang*). By contrast, criminal affiliation—being a member of a criminal organization—cannot be a basis for asylum, except in the limited “crime‑related” relief constructions addressed in *Reagan* and *Karyoliv* (which are outside the scope of the present opinion). A mischaracterization of a violent or criminal context as purely political often leads to a factual error. The court in *Jimenez* distinguished political opinion from a criminal affiliation by examining the nature of the persecutors and the context of the violence. 

The petitioners’ argument that they were targeted because of their political support for an opposition candidate was countered by a series of facts: the threats and aggressions stemmed from a narcotrafficking organization, led by Don Rafa, rather than a purely political adversary. The court underscored that the group’s primary interest was the alleged interference of Jimenez in a property dispute and subsequent allegations of law‑enforcement action at the property. No evidence suggested that since the beginning of the persecution, the claimants’ political affiliation was *at least as important* as the criminal motive. Hence, the petitioners’ attempts to subsume the kind of violence they endured under “political persecution” failed to meet the *central‑reason* requirement.

## Role of Don Rafa and the Narcotrafficking Group in the Alleged Persecution

Don Rafa, a known narcotrafficking leader aligned with the current Colombian president, was the principal actor behind the harassment and violence against the petitioners. The facts show that:

- Jiménez’s investigation of a tenant’s property—an ex‑husband affiliated with Don Rafa—triggered a series of threats and physical confrontations.
- Victims of the group generally comment on threats to be “snitches for police” and “police collaborator” rather than political opponents.
- The property at issue was a known narcotrafficking base, closed by the health department for illicit activities.

Therefore, the threats and violence fall into the realm of criminal coercion rather than political intimidation. Because the primary motive of the persecutors lies within the criminal nexus, it is not reasonable to infer that the petitioners’ political views were the central reason for the persecution. 

## Court’s Reasoning that Jiménez’s Political Activity Was Not the Primary Motive

The court noted that the *central‐reason* test requires political opinion to play a *major* role in the persecution, not marginal or superficial. In *Jiménez*, the court considered two strands of evidence of political activity and of criminal motive. In the first step, the court examined the record for any indication that the terrorist group’s threats directly referenced the petitioners’ political affiliation. The only political mention was found after Jiménez’s involvement in the property dispute. This chronology suggested that the group’s hostility was retaliatory for a legal and possibly law‑enforcement related action, not for political campaigning. 

The court also weighed the testimony that the petitioners continued campaigning while under threat in May and June 2022, and that the group intensified its aggression only after Jiménez confronted Don Rafa. As *Rivera‑Barrientos* clarifies, a single motive—with the claim that it was central—must be supported by “reasonably enough evidence.” The court concluded that there was no *sufficient* evidence that Jiménez’s political motivation was *at least as important* as the criminal motive. Accordingly, the Board’s denial of the nexus found that, as the court said, “Petitioners fail to establish that requirement because there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA and IJ’s findings that Petitioners were primarily targeted because Jimenez threatened Don Rafa’s revenue and security, not because of their political opinion.”

## Government Unwillingness/Unavailability Standard: Notice, Response, and Systemic Failure

The “unable or unwilling” standard is triggered when an appellant claims the host government is *incompetent* or *unwilling* to protect them from past persecution. The *IMM* statutory definition (8 U.S.C. § 1105‑2) instructs that *notice* of the persecution and *response* from the government are key factors. In *Jiménez* the court followed *Aviles‑Gonzalez* v. Garland (2024) and *Osorio‑Mora* v. Garland (2023) to distinguish between *local* police apathy and *systemic* failure. 

### Notice

The petitioners’ record contains extremely limited attempts at reporting. The fact that they spoke with a single police officer who dismissed them does not constitute serious *notice* of the government to the broader public order or law‑enforcement system. The court observed that “Petitioners’ attempts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any.” (See *Jiménez*, discussion D). Therefore the court could not conclude that the officials had sufficient knowledge of the persecution events to adjust a protective response.

### Response

The record shows a single citizen‑lawyer (Mr. Jiménez) and his father, both “currently work for the government in Colombia,” unresponsive to the persecution. The court considered that “the record does not show that Petitioners sought help outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jiménez’s law‑enforcement contacts.” The lack of a tangible government undertaking to protect the petitioners renders the claim of unwillingness or unavailability untenable.

### Systemic Failure

An essential element in the *government unwillingness* inquiry is establishing that the government consistently fails to respond to widespread or pervasive anti‑victim actions. *Jiménez* cited the reasoning that a *one‑off* encounter with negligent officers “does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” The barangue in *Osorio‑Mora* notes that a “systemic failure” is required. Here, the lack of any credible evidence of government action and the inevitable ‘local’ obstruction are insufficient. Accordingly, the court could not find a *systemic* lack of government protection.

## Evaluation of Petitioners’ Attempts to Seek Protection and Their Limited Scope

Petitioners’ claim that they could not obtain police assistance is undermined by the nature of the attempted contact. The record includes:

- A single conversation with a police officer that was dismissive and non‑formal;
- No complaint lodged with a municipal or national law‑enforcement agency;
- No evidence that the petitioners engaged law‑enforcement beyond that initial contact.

Translating these facts into the *government protection* element, the court concluded that “Petitioners’ efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any.” (See *Jiménez*, Discussion, bullet “Evaluation of petitioners’ attempts to seek protection…”) The court further highlighted that “Petitioners failed to show that the Colombian government would be” *unable* or *unwilling* to intervene, which is the vital requirement. In short, there is a *lack of adequate notice* and *response* which render the petitioners’ proximate sense of futility unsubstantiated.

## Assessment of the Claim that the Government Would Be Unable or Unwilling to Intervene

While the petitioners argued that the “persecutors are closely aligned and associated with the Colombian government, which prevents safe internal relocation,” the court rejected this notion. Evidence shows that:

- The Colombian government was aware of the illicit activity at the property via health‑department sanctions and attempted searches;
- Don Rafa’s ties to the president were long‑standing, but this relationship does not automatically make the government absent from *law‑enforcement* responsibilities;
- Without concrete evidence that the government actively *permitted* or *supported* the persecutors, the court relied on *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, holding that “without such reporting… we have no way of knowing how police would respond.”

The court applied the *unwilling/unavailable* analysis positing that “there is no reliance on a single episode of lackluster police conduct to infer systemic unwillingness.” The board’s findings were supported by the record that the authorities did not provide any credible evidence that they were unable or unwilling in a systemic sense.

## Court’s Conclusion that Insufficient Evidence Exists for a Well‑Founded Fear of Future Persecution

Once the court had established that (1) the nexus fails and (2) the government is neither unable nor unwilling to provide protection, the only remaining basis for an asylum grant would be a *well‑founded fear of future persecution*. However, the asylum standard requires:

1. *Past persecution* (already denied);
2. *Well‑founded fear of future persecution* (absent due to nexus failure and government inability).

Because the *nexus* element is a precondition, the denial is final. The court reaffirmed that, since the petitioners cannot satisfy any one of the three absolute prerequisites for 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A) (persecution, nexus, well‑founded fear), the Board’s denial is not reversible. The court’s summary reasoning—“there is insufficient evidence to support the BIA’s finding that Petitioners have a well‑founded fear of future persecution”—warrants unconditional dismissal of the petitioner’s claim for asylum and, consequently, the *withholding of removal* claim.

---

**Bottom Line for Students:** In *Jiménez*, the court methodically applied the asylum nexus test (central‑reason) and found political motivation peripheral. It distinguished criminal affiliation from political opinion by focusing on the nature of the persecutors and the events that precipitated the threat. When evaluating *government unwillingness*, the court required substantial evidence of systemic failure, which the petitioners did not provide. Finally, because *at least one* of the three core asylum premises was lacking, the entire claim collapsed, leaving the petitioner denied and the appellate review dismissed.
