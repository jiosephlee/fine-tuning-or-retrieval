Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 2: # Legal Framework and Standards of Review

## Statistical Overview of Asylum as Discretionary Relief Under the INA

Asylum remains a discretionary gateway for noncitizens seeking protection from violence or persecution.  The Department of Homeland Security’s 2023 *Immigration Statistics* report shows that roughly 38 % of the 720,000 applications filed that year were granted, while 4 % were denied and the remainder were diverted to other relief categories.  These figures illustrate the tight control the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) places on asylum: less than one‑third of applicants ultimately receive protection.  The high denial rate underscores the ASA's stringent evidentiary standards and the legal framework that requires applicants to satisfy multiple interlocking burdens.  The parties in *Jimenez v. Bondi* were tested against these same thresholds, and the court examined whether their record met the statutory and policy constraints that govern discretionary admission into this pool of relief.

## Definition and Enumerated Protected Grounds in § 1101(a)(42)(A)

Section 1101(a)(42)(A) defines a “refugee” as someone who, outside any country of nationality, is unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.  This list is the sole statutory basis for asylum and withholding relief.  The Court’s language in *Niang v. Gonzales* emphasizes that the INA’s protection does not extend to non‑statutory categories such as “economic hardship” or “family reunification.”  Consequently, the Jimenez petitioners claimed that their persecution was based on political opinion—a ground the statute explicitly protects—and the adjudicators had to determine whether the facts they presented satisfied the statutory definition.

## Burden of Proof and the Preponderance Standard for Asylum

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1), the burden lies with the applicant to establish that he or she is a refugee.  The court adopts a preponderance of the evidence standard for the asylum trilemma: the applicant must convince the adjudicator that, on the balance of probabilities, all three elements of past persecution interlock.  In *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder* the court reiterated that the “onus is on the applicant to prove that he is a refugee by a preponderance of the evidence.”  The Jimenez case relied heavily on this standard; the immigration judge and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) found that the evidence did not meet the preponderance threshold, and the appeals panel upheld that finding.

## The Three‑Element Test for Past Persecution (Incidents, Protected Ground, State Involvement)

Past persecution is a factual determination that requires the presence of three co‑synchronous elements:

1. **An Incident or Incidents that Rise to the Level of Persecution** – The courts require that the experiences “compromise a fundamental right, entail personal threat to life or liberty, or exceed mere restrictions or threats” (*Wiransane v. Ashcroft*).  Threats alone rarely suffice (*Karki v. Holder*).
2. **Protected Ground** – The adverse action must be on account of one of the five enumerated grounds in § 1101(a)(42)(A).  *Rivera‑Barrientos* has made it clear that the persecution must be “on account of a protected characteristic” and not merely “incidentally related.”
3. **State Involvement or Complicity** – The persecutor must be the government or a force linked to it that either commits the acts directly or fails to protect the applicant from private violence (including “affiliation” or “influence” by a state actor).

In *Jimenez*, the Court evaluated the record under this trilemma.  The petitioners presented a series of phone threats, an assault, and a property dispute, but the adjudicators concluded that those incidents did not rise to the level of persecution and that the state was neither a direct actor nor demonstrably complicit.  The court repeatedly pointed to the distinction between “mere threats” and “persecution that harms or threatens life and liberty” to emphasize the narrowness of the past‑persecution standard.

## Well‑Founded Fear of Future Persecution and its Relation to the Asylum Standard

A “well‑founded fear of future persecution” is a matter of law derived from the evidence presented regarding past persecution and the government’s capacity to protect the applicant.  In *Niang*, the Court explained that an applicant who does not establish past persecution may still satisfy the precedent if he can show “detrastic fear that does not require state cooperation.”  Conversely, if past persecution is established, “the evaluator may presume that the applicant will be protected by the state,” and the applicant must rebut that presumption by proving the state is unwilling or unable to intervene (*Singh v. Bondi*).  This presumption collapses when the applicant can demonstrate that the state has taken specific action or has, by policy, failed to protect persons on the same protected ground.  The Jimenez petitioners contended that the Colombian government was ineffective, but the adjudicators found the evidence insufficient to rebut the presumption of protection, specifically noting the lack of formal complaint and the isolated interactions with individual officers.

## Withholding of Removal: Requirement of Satisfying Asylum Elements

Withholding relief is governed by 8 U.S.C. § 1155(a).  The Court has consistently held that “withholding of removal is a discretionary form of relief that requires satisfaction of the same factual trilemma that applies to asylum” (*Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*).  Only when the applicant meets the asylum criteria can he claim withholding; otherwise, the “preventive” standard is denied.  In Jimenez, the admonition that the could not establish asylum “consequen­tly fails the higher standard” demonstrates the precise legal logic: failure to meet asylum equals automatic denial of withholding.

## Standard of Review Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and the Substantial‑Evidence Test

The Tenth Circuit reviews BIA decisions under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A).  The standard “is substantial evidence” – the BIA’s findings are conclusive unless the record shows that **any reasonable adjudicator** would conclude to the contrary (*Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*).  The court also may consult the Immigration Judge’s record for clarity, but it cannot replace the BIA’s findings.  In *Jimenez*, the appellate panel evaluated the IJ’s findi­ngs of prejudice and the BIA’s denial of asylum under this standard and concluded that “the record supports a substantial‑evidence basis” for the BIA’s denial.  The appellate court affirmed the BIA because it rejected the petitioner’s contention that evidence was insufficient or unsubstantiated.

## Appellate Review Scope Over the Immigration Judge and BIA Findings

Section 1252(a)(1) and (b)(9) grant jurisdiction to appellate review of “final orders of removal” and “consolidated asylum claims.”  The Court stresses that “the scope of review is limited to the record upon which the removal order was based” (*Singh v. Bondi*).  Accordingly, the appellate court focused primarily on the BIA’s findings and the IJ’s supporting record.  The court explicitly rejected the petitioners’ attempt to reopen due‑process arguments—those were extrinsic to the review of the affirmative claim of asylum and withholding.  The plan of the appellate review is restricted to “whether the record would support the finding that the applicant was a refugee” and “whether the BIA erred in interpreting the law” (*Uanreroro v. Gonzales*).

## Dismissal of the Petitioners’ Due‑Process Claim as a Matter of Scope

The Jimenez petitioners also advanced a due‑process argument, challenging the IJ’s handling of counsel.  Under the review scope language in § 1252(b), the appellate court is not required to consider “issues adjudicated in a separate proceeding” unless they were “inherent to the denial of relief.”  The Court treated the due‑process claim as outside the panel’s purview because it was not raised on the merits at the IJ or BIA level and did not “directly affect weel‑founded fear or past persecution.”  In *Osorio‑Mora­les v. Garland*, the Fourth Circuit made this point precisely: “an appellate court need not revisit that claim unless it implicates the adjudicative standards directly.”  Accordingly, the decision dismissed the due‑process argument as beyond the scope of the petition for review.

***

In sum, this chapter has traced the statutory underpinnings and evidentiary thresholds that shaped the *Jimenez v. Bondi* decision, highlighted how the three‑element past‑persecution test operates, and clarified the appellate review framework that ultimately governed the court’s denial.  The intersection of these doctrines—discretionary asylum, the “well‑founded fear” standard, withholding’s higher bar, and the substantial‑evidence review—provides the lens through which law students should analyze this and similar immigration‑law decisions.
