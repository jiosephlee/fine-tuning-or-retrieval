Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural History

This chapter walks through the factual and procedural backdrop that led the Jimenez family to litigate before the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit.  It traces the family’s political involvement in Colombia, the threats and violence they endured, their decision to relocate to the United States without legal admission, and the successive immigration‑court and appellate proceedings that culminated in a petition for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252.

---

## Jimenez Family Political Background and Key Incidents of Intimidation

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, along with their two minor sons, hail from Bogotá, Colombia.  Prior to the family’s move to the United States, they worked in a local furniture factory and were active members of the Colombian Liberal Party, joining that party in 2012.  Their political engagement intensified during the 2022 presidential campaign, when they campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, the Liberal candidate.

Around the end of May 2022, Jimenez received threatening phone calls that were repeated once or twice each week.  Similar threats were directed at other Liberal Party affiliates and colleagues.  In early June, a campaign colleague was kidnapped and murdered despite prior reports of such threats.  These intimidation incidents set the stage for a sustained campaign of harassment, culminating in a series of violent encounters tied to the family’s political activities.

---

## Involvement of the Liberal Party and the 2022 Colombian Presidential Campaign

During the 2022 election season, the Jimenez family undertook door‑to‑door canvassing to garner support for Gutierrez in several local neighborhoods.  Their patriotic commitment to the Liberal Party, however, made them visible targets for rivals in the political arena.  The threats continued throughout 2022, and the family’s political stance was repeatedly cited as a justification by malevolent actors who sought to disrupt the Liberal Party’s campaign.

---

## Threats from Political Opponents and the Narcotrafficking Group Don Rafa

While Jimenez worked as a provisional law‑student‑and‑intern for Colombia’s National Prosecutor’s Office, he became involved in a property dispute.  The property in question was under a contested lease, where the tenant was revealed to be Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros—better known as “Don Rafa,” a prominent narcotrafficking figure aligned with the then‑president Gustavo Petro.  In June 2022, Jimenez attempted to mediate between the tenant and the property owner, discovering that the property had been shut down by health authorities for suspected human‑trafficking and prostitution activities.

The discovery of Jimenez’s investigative role triggered a series of escalating threats from individuals associated with Don Rafa.  The family was warned to desist from “snitching” and promised harm if they continued.  One night, men confronted Jimenez at the factory, threatened that they would kill him if he remained in Bogotá, and cautioned that the order came from Don Rafa.  The family was further intimidated when, while out of town, the same men targeted a factory employee, demanding information on Jimenez’s whereabouts and again referencing Don Rafa’s potential retribution.

---

## Property Dispute and Resultant Violence Experienced by the Family

The property dispute generated direct violence against Jimenez.  On a separate occasion, a man approached the family’s son as he exited school and forced his hand over the boy’s mouth, urging him to return to his father.  Jimenez intervened, pushing the assailant and ramming their two‑way sex between the boy and the attacker.  This confrontation, coupled with the cumulative battery of threats, convinced the family that the threats posed a tangible risk to their safety.

In the weeks following these violent episodes, Jimenez contacted a police patrol officer.  The officer admitted that no effective action could be taken and advised the family to leave Bogotá.  Following this conversation, the Jimenez family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the countryside for a brief period before ultimately deciding to emigrate to the United States.

---

## Forced Migration to the United States Without Admission or Parole

In late December 2022, the Jimenez family moved to the United States.  They entered the country without being admitted or paroled through any immigration‑law officer.  Their presence in the United States was therefore deemed “illegal” under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

Three months after arrival—so in late March 2023—the Department of Homeland Security issued each family member an individual Notice to Appear, charging them with removal as inadmissible under the Act.  The family’s case was placed before an immigration judge (IJ), giving them an opportunity to challenge the removal direction by filing asylum and withholding‑of‑removal applications.  Jimenez and Duarte testified that they sought protection due to the danger they faced in Colombia and that their minor children, as derivative beneficiaries, were at equal risk.  The IJ denied the asylum and withholding claims, citing the absence of any evidence that the family had experienced past persecution, that it would receive protection from the Colombian authorities, or that they were unlikely to relocate safely within Colombia.

---

## Administrative Process: Immigration Judge Denial, BIA Affiliation, and Appellate Filing

After the IJ denial, the Jimenez family appealed the removal order to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA).  The BIA, reviewing the case under 8 U.S.C. § 1471, affirmed the IJ’s decision, finding again no basis for asylum or withholding.  The family’s anticipatory questions regarding the due‑process of the IJ’s proceedings were likewise dismissed.

The family’s next step was to petition the federal appellate court for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, which permits the Tenth Circuit to review a final order of removal and related matters such as the denial of asylum relief.  The petition, filed in October 2025, was denied by the court; the court held that the family had failed to demonstrate past persecution, a nexus to a protected ground, or incapability of the Colombian government to protect them, and that the record did not support either asylum or withholding of removal.

In sum, the administrative pathway of the Jimenez family followed the sequence: No lawful admission → Notice to Appear → IJ denial of asylum and removal order → Appeal to the BIA → BIA affirmation → Petition for review by the Tenth Circuit → Denial of petition.  This procedural history, along with the factual allegations of political opposition and narcotrafficking‑related intimidation, frames the larger narrative of why the Court ultimately declined to grant the family relief under the asylum or withholding statutes.
