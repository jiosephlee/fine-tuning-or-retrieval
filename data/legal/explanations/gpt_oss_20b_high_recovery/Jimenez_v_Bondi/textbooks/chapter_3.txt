Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Past Persecution Analysis

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court’s central task was to determine whether the petitioners had proven “past persecution” in a manner sufficient to satisfy the statutory requirements for asylum. The review focused on whether the incidents described in the record—numerous threatening phone calls, men at the family’s factory, and a confrontation on a school street—raised to the level of persecution. The court applied the established threshold, the cumulative‑effect doctrine, and the evidentiary test for physical, psychological, and emotional harm.  In doing so, it relied on the Ninth and Tenth‑Circuit precedent cited in the opinion, particularly *Karki*, *Brandy*, *Carias‑Mejia*, and *Chhetri*, to reach the conclusion that the petitioners’ experiences fall short of past persecution.

## Threshold for past persecution: more than mere threats or restrictions

The First Amendment microscope has long required that a finding of persecution be based on more than isolated threats or restrictions.  The court reiterated that “a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm ... that is offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty” (*Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893).  Accordingly, the standard is twofold: (1) the applicant must have endured treatment that is offensive to a reasonable person, and (2) that treatment must go beyond mere intimidation.  The petitioners reported only threats—brief phone calls, a brochure, and violent intimidation at the factory.  None of these isolated acts, in and of themselves, satisfy the threshold for persecution because they do not yet amount to “offensive” harm.

## Cumulative effect of multiple incidents in asylum analysis

When evaluating aggregated incidents, courts look at the cumulative impact that a series of events may have on the applicant.  The cumulative‑effect doctrine is tested against the preponderance‑of‑the‑evidence standard: an individual facts‑watcher must see enough to conclude that a reasonable adjudicator would find past persecution.  Though the petitioners faced several incidents, the court found that, in sum, they amounted to threat and intimidation without a sustained pattern of physical or psychological harm.  The incidents were “sporadic” and not linked to a broader campaign of abuse.  Thus, their accumulation did not cross the line into persecution, as the court noted that “the cumulative effect of these threats does not elevate the situation to persecution.”

## Analysis of physical, psychological, and emotional harm claims

The petitioners’ only substantive claim of harm was psychological.  *Brandy v. Holder* (590 F. App’x 744, 747) clarified that an emotional or psychological response must be “more than a mere restriction or sporadic threat.”  In *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland* (860 F. App’x 121, 124), the court held that threats with a weapon, coupled with repeated robbery, did not, on their own, amount to persecution without accompanying physical harm.  Similarly, *Chhetri v. Rosen* (844 F. App’x 23, 27) found that four telephone threats and an ambiguous in‑person threat fell short of past persecution due to a lack of substantive injury.

Applying those precedents to the record, the court found that:

* **Physical harm**: The petitioners experienced no bodily injury.  This absence precludes meeting the “more than mere restrictions” bar when combined with psychological descriptors alone.

* **Psychological harm**: The petitioners’ parents claimed sleep disturbances and panic attacks, yet the court treated these as “psychological distress” that, individually or cumulatively, remains “more than a mere restriction or sporadic threat.”  The distress level did not square with the standard that demands a qualitatively significant, ongoing ordeal.

* **Emotional harm**: This overlaps with psychological harm; without the backing of more concrete evidence of lasting harm, emotional distress alone could not be deemed sufficient.

Because threats alone, even when repeated, cannot substitute for real or substantial harm, the court rejected the petitioners’ claim that the experiences rose to persecution.

## Court’s application of precedent: Karki, Brandy, Carias‑Mejia, and Chhetri

The court explicitly used the cited cases to delimit the analysis:

* **Karki v. Holder** (715 F.3d 792, 805) emphasized that threats do not constitute persecution in the meaningful sense required by the INA unless accompanied by other injurious conduct.

* **Brandy v. Holder** demanded a higher threshold for psychological distress; the court found the petitioners’ reported stress insufficient.

* **Carias‑Mejia v. Garland** and **Chhetri v. Rosen** both spotlighted the need for a closer nexus between threat and physical or extreme psychological injury—something absent in the petitioners’ record.

By carrying these doctrinal standards into the record, the court concluded that the evidence failed to meet the threshold.

## Rationale for deeming threats alone insufficient for persecution

The court explained that while a sequence of threats may, in some contexts, be “dealing towards persecution,” the petitioners’ threats were “isolated, sporadic, and not accompanied by sustained or escalating danger.”  Therefore, the court applied the *Karki* and *Brandy* standards to decide that threats alone are a subtle and disquieting nuisance, not an egregious persecution episode.  The claim leveled against the petitioners involved intimidation by violent actors, but the petitioners failed to show that this intimidation threatened life or confined them under instruments that would jeopardize bodily integrity.

## Evidence supporting the BIA’s conclusion of absent past persecution

The court weighed the evidence in the record:

* No documented physical harm or injury suffered by Jimenez or Angelica.
* Threats were limited to phone calls, a brochure, kill‑when‑snitching verbal assault, and a single confrontation at the factory.  None of these produced tangible harm or a sustained pattern.
* The petitioners’ emotional arguments hinged on generalized anxiety and panic, unsupported by more decisive evidence (e.g., medical or treatment records).
* The BIA’s denial was grounded in the absence of tangible evidence of persecution; the court found that the administrative record “shows substantial evidence of lack of past persecution.”

Because the record supports the BIA’s conclusions, the appellate court, applying the substantial‑evidence standard for findings of fact, affirmed that the petitioners did not establish past persecution.

In sum, the court disciplined the petitioners’ claims by insisting on a markedly high bar: more than isolated threats, more than generalized anxiety, and more than sporadic intimidation.  The influence of *Karki*, *Brandy*, *Carias‑Mejia*, and *Chhetri* sharpened that bar, and the court concluded unanimously that the petitioners’ experiences failed to pass the threshold for past persecution.
