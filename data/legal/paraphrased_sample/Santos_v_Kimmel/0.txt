Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos appeals a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims for copyright infringement and violations of state law brought against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), which broadcasts the late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent corporation, The Walt Disney Company. Santos initiated this lawsuit following Kimmel’s use of several pseudonyms to request personalized video messages from Santos via the Cameo platform. Santos completed each video request, which Kimmel subsequently broadcast on JKL in a series of satirical segments called “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the action pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, finding the copyright infringement claims barred by the fair use doctrine and determining that the state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise failed to allege a valid cause of action. As we concur with the District Court that the fair use doctrine precludes Santos’s copyright claims and also hold that each of Santos’s state law claims is insufficiently pled, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our analysis includes “the facts set forth in the complaint, any exhibits attached thereto, as well as documents that the complaint incorporates by reference.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as recorded in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In the context of copyright litigation, the fair use affirmative defense “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). When applying the Copyright Act, courts assess several non-exclusive factors to decide if fair use is present, namely: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” prong, our primary consideration is the extent to which the secondary use is transformative—specifically, whether it merely replaces the original work’s objectives or contributes something new with a different aim or nature. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This approach underscores that “fair use is a flexible doctrine whose contours shift based on contextual factors.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). On occasion, fair use may be “so plainly apparent from the face of a complaint as to warrant dismissal of a copyright claim” at the pleading stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends the use at issue lacks transformative qualities because Kimmel allegedly “prompted the creation of the original Works” and thus is the “designer[]” of their initial objectives. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s determination that the intent of the purportedly infringing use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, he maintains that this was also his motivation for producing the videos. However, the analysis of whether a secondary use is transformative depends on the perception of a reasonable observer, rather than the copyright owner’s or secondary user’s subjective motivations. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he user’s subjective intent (or the subjective view of a court) [does not] dictate the use’s purpose.”). According to Santos’s initial allegation, a reasonable observer would understand the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” instead of as an exhibition of willingness to say absurd things for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations constitute bad faith, which would negate the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are not persuaded by this argument. Although the Supreme Court remarked in Harper & Row that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (internal quotation marks omitted), the Court has since retreated from this position. Moreover, the allegations in Santos’s complaint do not support an inference that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the video content. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (internal quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint itself characterizes defendants as being driven by a (sarcastic) desire to criticize and comment. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of a fair use finding.

Santos’s objections to the District Court’s evaluation of the remaining elements are likewise unconvincing. Notably, the applicable copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos were both created and published on the same date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, it is evident from the allegations in Santos’s complaint that there is no market harm as understood under the fourth factor of the fair use analysis, since “the question is not whether the second work harms the market for the first (for example, by diminishing its value through parody or criticism), but whether it takes over the market for the original by supplying a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since our determination of “the question of fair use” requires only a comparison of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” as stated in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we agree with the District Court’s ruling that Santos’s copyright claims are barred by fair use even at the motion-to-dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s dismissal of his state law claims, specifically those alleging breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court dismissed the initial two claims, finding that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos counters this by asserting that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and thus should not be considered preempted, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, we do not need to address this preemption argument because, as a threshold matter, Santos’s state contract claims fail to sufficiently allege an entitlement to relief under applicable state law.

Turning first to Santos’s express breach of contract claim predicated on the Cameo Terms of Service, it is undisputed that Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service, which users such as Kimmel must accept and which Santos claims have been violated. Under Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service, there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). The implication that a contract covers third parties must be nearly tantamount to an express statement. Id. (quotation marks omitted). Here, Santos has not pointed to anything remotely approaching an “express declaration” indicating that the relevant parts of the Terms of Service are intended to benefit him or any non-signatory. In fact, the Terms of Service contain separate provisions expressly conferring third-party beneficiary rights, which underscores that, where the contracting parties wished to permit enforcement by third parties, they explicitly stated so.

With regard to New York law, which governs Santos’s implied contract claim, the analysis focuses on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to ascertain “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Santos’s complaint, however, does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (not setting forth the necessary elements of the alleged implied contract said to have been breached by Defendants). Nor does Santos adequately claim the existence of “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” whereby Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos takes issue with the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim for failure to allege an actual out-of-pocket loss, as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s finding that Santos failed to plead any actual out-of-pocket damages as a result of the alleged fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
