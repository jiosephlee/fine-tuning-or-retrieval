Case Name: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Jurisdiction: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Decided: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, which fell within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that infringed upon his right to a fair trial and erroneously imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) that raised the advisory guideline range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank roused his girlfriend, D.F., at gunpoint, aiming the weapon at her face. He compelled her into a closet while voicing paranoid allegations regarding supposed “listening devices” within the residence and claiming individuals outside intended him harm. According to D.F.’s testimony, Cutbank held her in the closet under threat for what seemed to her like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her on the face with the firearm. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake others sleeping in the household, specifically her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Subsequently, Cutbank departed, taking with him D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. then contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home stating that a male individual was present in the garage and was refusing to depart. Upon arrival, officers encountered Cutbank, who had barricaded himself inside the garage. Over the next several hours, Cutbank exhibited erratic behavior, made incoherent statements, and asserted that he possessed a weapon. When officers attempted to take him into custody, Cutbank actively resisted by biting an officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. During a search of the likely route Cutbank took between D.F.'s residence and the garage, officers discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had a shortened barrel and stock. Forensic testing confirmed that DNA found on the firearm belonged to Cutbank. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. testified that the weapon recovered was the same one they had seen in Cutbank’s possession prior to the incident. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm and determined that he had previously been convicted of at least three violent felonies, thereby making him subject to the enhanced sentencing provisions of § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court erred in several pretrial and trial rulings and improperly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents a number of procedural and evidentiary arguments, but offers virtually no supporting analysis or case authority. Each of these arguments is without merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their earlier observations of him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted improper propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government’s position that this evidence was intrinsic to the charge of possession and was relevant to showing that Cutbank continually possessed the firearm before the alleged event. We concur with this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank asked that the jury receive instructions specifying that, to prevent any hearsay or Confrontation Clause complications, D.F.’s statements to law enforcement could not be considered as direct evidence of Cutbank’s conduct unless D.F. herself provided testimony regarding those incidents. The prosecution maintained that these statements merely served to clarify the background for the officers’ actions and their understanding at the relevant times, asserted that the officers’ testimony would remain within the confines of hearsay regulations, and contended that a subsequent instruction from the judge, if deemed necessary, would be both standard practice and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to established precedent, a court is “not required to provide instructions to the jury about each piece of evidence at the time of its admission if a proper instruction is issued at the end of the trial.” Id. In this instance, the trial record shows that hearsay evidence was not admitted. Moreover, D.F. provided her own testimony. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Thus, there was no abuse of discretion in this case.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this testimony. Likewise, when C.F., a government witness, testified that she had previously seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice,” the court also rejected a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback does not point to any authority contesting the trial court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as to showing that Cutbank had maintained possession of the firearm before the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous statements that conflicted regarding the gun—including her claim of not recalling seeing a gun on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her prior statements were influenced by her unfamiliarity with firearms and her personal aversion to them, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The district court refused Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s testimony concerning her sister’s death by firearm. This particular testimony was elicited in response to cross-examination about D.F.’s inconsistent statements. Cutback does not identify any undue prejudice or juror confusion resulting from these remarks, nor is such prejudice or confusion evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., appearing as a government witness, also referred to D.F.’s sister being fatally shot, and the defense did not object. Thus, there is no indication that the district court exceeded the bounds of its wide discretion over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to cast doubt on C.F.’s testimony that she had previously seen the gun involved in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her about earlier statements she had made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries regarding C.F.’s statements to police, but it sustained the prosecution’s objections when defense counsel tried to read from the corresponding police report, since the proper foundation had not been established and C.F. was not the report’s author. Although Cutback did not directly address this ruling in his appeal, the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably on appellees who broaden appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless, this “claim,” preserved or not, fails. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The court did not abuse its discretion in demanding proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court does not disturb an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error is harmless. . . . Stated differently, reversal is warranted only where the jury’s decision could have been meaningfully impacted by the evidence improperly admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this burden. The government presented strong testimonial and physical proof. Consequently, the evidentiary decisions of the district court “either did not affect, or had at most a minimal effect, on the jury’s verdict.” Id. at 998. “We decline to set aside a conviction due to cumulative trial errors unless those errors caused significant prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the charge of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the increased penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26 based on the presence of a sawed-off firearm as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), as well as at least two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR further recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm's serial number being altered or obliterated, a two-level enhancement because Cutbank recklessly endangered others' lives or safety while fleeing law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in connection with the commission of another felony, resulting in a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the PSR calculated an advisory guideline range of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erroneously imposed the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense as provided by § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

The enhancement is applicable if the defendant either used or possessed a firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony under federal, state, or local law, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the PSR's factual account regarding Cutback’s prior felony convictions without objection. Consequently, the court did not err—let alone plainly err—in determining, based on the PSR, that the firearm was used in connection with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that imposing the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), even though it results in a guideline-range sentence, is inconsistent with the “core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” which requires that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” While the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies—thus making him subject to Armed Career Criminal Act sanctions—the Guideline for Armed Career Criminals would assign a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines specify that the higher offense level should be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When selecting a sentence, "a district court must start every sentencing hearing by accurately determining the relevant Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "An appellate court may only address the substantive reasonableness of a sentence after confirming that the district court did not commit a significant procedural error, such as neglecting to calculate or miscalculating the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates whether the factors enumerated in § 3553(a) justify either departing from or varying the advisory guidelines range sentence.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence that fell within the relevant guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable for purposes of appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court referenced its consideration of the PSR and sentencing briefs, and also addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted): “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” Moreover, according to United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted): “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” Cutback had almost twelve prior felony convictions, several of which were for violent acts and firearm offenses. The instant offense included domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s residence while her children were present, and causing injury to a police officer during resistance to arrest. By sentencing Cutback to a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guidelines, the district court did not exceed the bounds of its considerable discretion nor issue a sentence that was substantively unreasonable.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
