Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm, contrary to 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e); Cutbank has a significant history of criminal offenses. The district court imposed a sentence of 292 months’ imprisonment pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, which fell within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and that the court incorrectly imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), resulting in an increased guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her head, and forced her into a closet while expressing paranoid concerns regarding alleged “listening devices” inside the residence and individuals purportedly waiting outside to harm him. Cutbank held D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for a duration that D.F. described in her testimony as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened her life and struck her face with the firearm. At a certain point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake the other occupants of the house, including her two teenage sons and her roommate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank departed with D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. Following this, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, stating that an individual was in the garage and refused to leave. Upon their arrival, officers found Cutbank, who barricaded himself inside the garage and, during several hours, exhibited erratic behavior and claimed to possess a weapon. When officers tried to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted, biting an officer on the face and neck and seizing the officer's firearm. In searching the route that Cutbank was likely to have taken from D.F.'s residence to the garage, officers discovered D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA analysis of the firearm matched Cutbank. During the trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the same one they had seen Cutbank with prior to that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession, as charged, and determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, which subjected him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of § 924(e). Cutbank now appeals his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court committed abuse of discretion through various pretrial and trial rulings and improperly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in imposing an enhanced sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute inadmissible propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court, however, sided with the government, finding that this evidence was intrinsic to the offense charged and pertinent to establishing possession and ongoing control of the firearm leading up to the relevant event. We concur with this conclusion. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident at issue.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank moved for a jury instruction specifying that, to address potential hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns, D.F.’s statements to law enforcement could not be relied upon as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct unless D.F. herself later recounted the relevant events in her testimony. The government maintained that these statements would simply provide background for the actions and awareness of the police officers, that the officers’ recounting of such statements would not contravene hearsay principles, and that any necessary curative instruction could be given by the court at a later point, which is customary practice. As stated in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” The court is not obligated to instruct the jury on each evidentiary item as it is introduced, provided that an adequate instruction is given at the conclusion of trial. Id. In this matter, the record reflects that no hearsay evidence was admitted throughout the proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. did in fact provide testimony. According to United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted), the Confrontation Clause requirement is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Consequently, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident, describing it as a “handful of times.” When Cutback renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court denied it. Additionally, the court overruled another defense objection when C.F., a witness for the government, testified that she had seen the firearm used during the incident “once or twice” previously. On appeal, Cutback does not provide any authority challenging the court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating Cutbank’s continued possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about prior inconsistent statements concerning the firearm, including her statement that she did not recall seeing a gun on the night at issue, D.F. explained that certain statements were made because she was “not familiar with guns” and “did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s testimony regarding her sister’s death by firearm. This testimony was given in response to cross-examination probing D.F.’s inconsistent prior statements. Cutback fails to identify any undue prejudice or jury confusion resulting from this evidence, nor is any apparent from the record. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., a government witness, referenced the death of D.F.’s sister by gunfire without any objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed its considerable discretion in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s account that she had previously seen the gun “once or twice” by cross-examining her concerning earlier remarks she had made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. told law enforcement, but it upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel tried to read excerpts from the police report into evidence without adequate foundation, as C.F. was not the report’s author. While Cutback did not directly address this ruling on appeal, the government’s brief asserted that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably upon appellees who unnecessarily broaden the scope of appeal. Regardless, whether or not the argument was properly preserved, the “claim” lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion by insisting on proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not overturn an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error is harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only when the improperly admitted evidence could have had a significant impact on the jury.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government presented ample evidence, both through witness testimony and physical exhibits. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “either did not affect or had only a minimal effect on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not reverse a conviction based on the combined impact of errors at trial unless the defendant has suffered substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. The jury further determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced penalties set forth by the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended assigning a base offense level of 26, citing the fact that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and that Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also suggested a three-level increase due to the firearm’s serial number being altered or obliterated, a two-level enhancement based on Cutbank recklessly causing a risk of death or serious injury while fleeing law enforcement, and an additional four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with a separate felony offense. These enhancements brought the total offense level to 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, leading to an advisory sentencing guideline range of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. Cutbank challenges, on appeal, the district court’s application of the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

The enhancement is applicable when a defendant has used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony offense under federal, state, or local law, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the Presentence Report’s factual account of Cutback’s prior felony convictions without any objections. Therefore, the court did not commit error—clear or otherwise—by determining that the facts in the PSR demonstrate the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank instead contends that imposing the 4-level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) results in a guideline-range sentence that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” which requires that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury concluded that Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions—thereby activating the Armed Career Criminal Act penalties—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have assigned a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level must be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, “a district court must initiate sentencing by properly determining the applicable Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “Prior to a court of appeals evaluating the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it is necessary to verify that the district court has not made any material procedural mistakes, such as omitting to compute (or incorrectly computing) the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a deviation or variance from the advisory guidelines sentence.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence falling within the established guidelines range, which is considered presumptively reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court referenced its examination of the PSR and the parties’ sentencing memoranda, and also addressed the § 3553(a) factors that could justify a variance. “Therefore, the district court was cognizant of [Cutbank’s] contentions, leading us to presume it took them into account and declined them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To affirm the sentence, a district court need not recite the precise factors it evaluated; rather, it must be apparent from the record that the court did, in fact, consider the § 3553(a) factors when fashioning the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record included almost twelve previous felony convictions, many related to violent conduct and the use of firearms. The present conviction encompassed domestic violence, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s residence in front of her children, and harming a law enforcement officer during resistance to arrest. The district court, in selecting a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guideline range, did not overstep its broad sentencing authority nor deliver a substantively unreasonable sentence.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
