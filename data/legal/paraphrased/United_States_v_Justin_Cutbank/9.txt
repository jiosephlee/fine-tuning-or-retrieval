Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal background, guilty of possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, in contravention of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months of imprisonment, a term within the advisory guidelines range it had calculated. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that resulted in an unfair trial, and improperly imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby increasing his advisory guideline range. We affirm.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and compelled her into a closet, making paranoid allegations concerning "listening devices" present in the residence and individuals outside intending to harm him. While holding D.F. at gunpoint within the closet for what D.F. described in her testimony as several hours, he threatened to kill her and struck her face with the weapon. At one juncture, Cutbank instructed D.F. to awaken other individuals sleeping in the house, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank escaped with both D.F.'s cell phone and the firearm. Following his departure, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Police also received a report from a residence located half a mile from D.F.’s house, where it was alleged that a man had entered the garage and refused to exit. Upon their arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage and, throughout several hours, exhibited unpredictable behavior and stated he was armed. During attempts to take him into custody, Cutbank physically resisted, biting an officer's face and neck and attempting to take the officer's firearm. A search conducted along the likely route from D.F.’s home to the garage yielded D.F.’s mobile phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA collected from the firearm matched Cutbank’s profile. During trial testimony, both D.F. and C.F. identified the rifle as the same one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge, and further determined he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the increased penalty requirements of § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court abused its discretion in several pretrial and trial rulings and improperly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to impose a sentence enhancement.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from providing testimony that they had previously seen him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle. Cutbank contended that such evidence would constitute impermissible propensity evidence in violation of Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court, however, concurred with the government's position that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and to illustrating Cutbank's ongoing possession of the firearm preceding the incident. We concur with this determination. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident at issue.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank requested that the jury receive an instruction clarifying that D.F.’s statements to police could not be treated as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. personally testified to those events, in order to address potential hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns. In response, the government maintained that such statements would merely provide background for the officers’ actions and state of knowledge, that the officers’ testimony would remain within the bounds of hearsay rules, and that, if required, an instruction from the bench at a later point would be both standard practice and adequate. The standard of review for a district court’s decision to withhold a limiting instruction is abuse of discretion. United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not required to immediately instruct the jury about the evidentiary limitations as evidence is introduced, as long as an appropriate jury instruction is provided at the end of the trial. Id. In this case, the record indicates that no hearsay was admitted during the trial. Additionally, D.F. took the stand and gave testimony. The Confrontation Clause is deemed satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Consequently, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that prior to the event, she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions. The district court rejected Cutbank’s renewed objection to this testimony. Additionally, when government witness C.F. testified that she had previously seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice,” the court also overruled a defense objection. On appeal, Cutbank does not provide any authority challenging the court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and indicating Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm before the event in question. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier inconsistent statements concerning the firearm, such as not recalling having seen the gun on the night in question, D.F. explained that some of her statements were influenced by her unfamiliarity with firearms and her aversion to guns, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The district court overruled Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s testimony about her sister’s death involving a firearm. This evidence was offered in direct response to cross-examination about D.F.'s prior inconsistent statements. Cutback has not identified, nor does the record reflect, any undue prejudice or confusion from this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., a government witness, referenced the death of D.F.’s sister by gun without objection from the defense. Accordingly, the district court did not exceed its considerable discretion regarding evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to discredit C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the gun involved in the incident “once or twice,” by cross-examining her regarding previous statements she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted questions about C.F.’s communications with law enforcement but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel tried to read sections of the police report into evidence without laying a sufficient foundation, as C.F. was not the report’s author. On appeal, Cutback did not challenge this ruling directly, yet the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We discourage appellees from unnecessarily broadening the issues on appeal. Regardless, whether this issue was preserved or not, it lacks substance. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion by insisting on appropriate authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, "this court will not overturn an erroneous evidentiary decision if the mistake was harmless. . . . That is, reversal will occur only if the jury could have been significantly affected by the erroneously admitted evidence." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government presented strong testimonial and physical evidence. Accordingly, the district court's evidentiary decisions "did not influence the verdict or had only minimal impact." Id. at 998. "We do not reverse a conviction due to the combined effect of errors at trial absent a showing of significant prejudice to the defendant." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, which subjected him to the Armed Career Criminal Act's heightened sentencing provisions. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) assigned a base offense level of 26 on the grounds that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and that Cutbank had no fewer than two previous convictions for violent crimes. The PSR further recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm's altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly causing a risk of death or serious bodily harm during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement based on his possession of the firearm in connection with another felony offense, which brought the total offense level to 35. The PSR determined that Cutbank had a Criminal History Category of VI, which led to an advisory guidelines range of imprisonment between 292 and 365 months. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by applying the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant employs or possesses a firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony offense under federal, state, or local law, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the PSR’s factual recitations about Cutback’s earlier felony convictions without any opposition. Consequently, the court did not err—let alone clearly err—in determining, based on the PSR, that the firearm was used in association with another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank maintains that applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) leads to a sentence within the range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that the punishment “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions—which triggers penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would set a total offense level of 34. According to the Guidelines, the offense level that is higher applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, "a district court must start every sentencing hearing by accurately determining the relevant Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "Before a court of appeals evaluates whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, it must first verify that the district court did not commit any substantial procedural mistakes, such as neglecting to determine (or miscalculating) the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court examines if the factors set forth in § 3553(a) justify a sentence outside the recommended advisory guidelines range, either by way of a departure or variance.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the relevant guidelines range, which carries a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court stated that it had considered the PSR as well as sentencing memoranda, and addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “[t]hus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” Moreover, a district court is not required to mechanically list each factor it considered to affirm the sentence; it need only be evident from the record that the court took the § 3553(a) factors into account when determining the sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had close to twelve previous felony convictions, several of which were for violent crimes or involved firearms. The instant offense encompassed domestic violence, kidnapping, assault inside the victim’s residence in front of her children, and injuring a law enforcement officer during an arrest attempt. The district court properly exercised its broad discretion in sentencing and did not issue a substantively unreasonable sentence by imposing a 292-month prison term within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
