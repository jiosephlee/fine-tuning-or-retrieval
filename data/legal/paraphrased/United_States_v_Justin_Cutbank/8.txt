Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant prior criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, which fell within the calculated advisory guidelines range. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that resulted in an unfair trial and also incorrectly applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which raised his advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., and aimed a firearm at her face. He forced her into a closet, accusing her—apparently out of paranoia—of “listening devices” within the residence and asserting that individuals outside were intending to harm him. While holding D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described as feeling like several hours, Cutbank threatened to kill her and struck her face with the firearm. At one point, he instructed D.F. to wake up the other occupants in the home, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank departed with both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. Following this, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, stating that a man was present in the garage and refused to vacate the premises. Upon arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage and, over several hours, made incoherent statements and asserted that he possessed a weapon. When police attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted, biting an officer’s face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. During a search of the probable route between D.F.’s residence and the garage, police recovered D.F.’s cellphone, a butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a barrel and stock that had been sawed off. DNA testing revealed Cutbank’s genetic material on the firearm. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified this gun as the one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm, and also determined he had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the increased sentencing provisions of § 924(e). Cutbank now appeals both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court committed several discretionary errors before and during trial and improperly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute inadmissible propensity evidence in contravention of Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court sided with the government, determining that this testimony was intrinsic to the case and relevant to establishing the possession charge, as well as to showing that Cutbank maintained possession of the firearm prior to the event in question. We concur. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. provided testimony indicating that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident at issue.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought an instruction to the jury that would prevent them from considering D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct until after D.F. had testified regarding the events herself, citing concerns about hearsay and the Confrontation Clause. The government contended that these statements were relevant for explaining the officers’ actions and knowledge at the relevant time, that the officers’ testimony would remain within hearsay limits, and that any necessary instruction from the court could be given later and would be appropriate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The law does not require the court to instruct the jury on each piece of evidence at the moment it is admitted, so long as the court issues a suitable instruction at the end of the trial. Id. In this case, the trial record shows that hearsay evidence was not admitted. Further, D.F. took the stand and provided testimony. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Accordingly, the district court did not abuse its discretion.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, when C.F., appearing as a government witness, testified that she had witnessed the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” previously, the court overruled a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback does not reference any legal authority that contradicts the court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge and supported a showing of ongoing possession of the firearm before the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about her earlier inconsistent remarks concerning the gun, including her claim that she could not recall seeing it on the relevant night, D.F. explained that some of her statements stemmed from her lack of familiarity with firearms and her aversion to them, stating that her dislike was due to her sister having been killed by a gun. The district court declined Cutback’s request to strike the testimony concerning D.F.’s sister’s death by firearm. This statement was given in direct response to questions during cross-examination regarding D.F.’s inconsistent prior statements. Cutback does not identify any unfair prejudice or jury confusion arising from this testimony, nor does the record reveal any. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., who testified for the government, also referenced D.F.’s sister’s death by gun without any objection from the defense. The district court therefore acted well within its considerable discretion over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s statement that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her during cross-examination about earlier remarks she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into C.F.’s statements to law enforcement but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel attempted to read excerpts from the police report into evidence without establishing the necessary foundation, as C.F. was not the report’s author. While Cutback did not address this evidentiary ruling on appeal, the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove of appellees who unduly broaden the issues raised on appeal. Regardless, even assuming the argument was preserved, the “claim” lacks substance. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court did not err in insisting upon proper authentication.

Finally, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. . . . In other words, we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government presented significant testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm and further determined that he had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to enhanced sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) assigned a base offense level of 26 due to the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and because Cutbank had at least two prior violent crime convictions. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase because the firearm’s serial number was altered or obliterated, an additional two-level increase on account of Cutbank recklessly endangering lives while evading police, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony, culminating in a total offense level of 35. Cutbank’s criminal history was calculated at Category VI, which yielded an advisory guideline range of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court adopted the PSR’s factual recitations concerning Cutback’s previous felony convictions without any objection. Accordingly, the court did not commit error, let alone clear error, in concluding that the PSR’s facts demonstrate the firearm was used in relation to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, however, contends that the imposition of the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produces a sentence within the guideline range that is inconsistent with the foundational principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), which requires that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had three or more previous violent felony convictions—thereby subjecting him to penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act—the offense level under the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would be 34. According to the Guidelines, the higher offense level is to be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, "a district court must initially and accurately compute the applicable Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "A court of appeals, before evaluating the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, must first confirm that the district court did not commit a significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate or miscalculating the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Following this, the court evaluates whether the factors set forth in § 3553(a) justify either a departure from or a variance with the advisory Guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the applicable Guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court referenced its consideration of the PSR, the sentencing memoranda, and discussed § 3553(a) factors that could support a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” The law does not mandate that the district court recite each factor in detail; rather, it must be apparent from the record that the court took the § 3553(a) factors into account in deciding the sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal record includes nearly twelve prior felony convictions, several of which involved violent conduct and firearms. The current conviction pertains to acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assaulting the victim inside her home while her children were present, and causing injury to a police officer during the course of resisting arrest. Under these circumstances, the district court acted within its broad sentencing discretion and did not impose a substantively unreasonable sentence by ordering a 292-month term of imprisonment within the Guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
