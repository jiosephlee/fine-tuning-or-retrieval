Case Name: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date Decided: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon, given his significant criminal background. Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months' imprisonment, a term that fell within the advisory guideline range as calculated by the court. On appeal, Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made several errors in evidentiary rulings and procedure that undermined the fairness of his trial, and that the court improperly applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby raising the advisory guideline range. We affirm the district court's judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet, while expressing paranoid suspicions about "listening devices" being present in the home and individuals outside intending to harm him. He held D.F. in the closet under threat of the firearm for what D.F. later described as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the gun. At some juncture, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake other individuals sleeping in the house, including both her teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank departed the scene with both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and was refusing to leave. Upon arrival, the police found Cutbank, who barricaded himself in the garage and proceeded to make unpredictable statements over several hours, claiming possession of a weapon. When officers attempted to take him into custody, Cutbank resisted, biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. While searching the probable route between D.F.’s home and the garage, officers recovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both a sawed-off barrel and stock. DNA evidence collected from the rifle was matched to Cutbank. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession before that night. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge and determined he had prior convictions for at least three violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing requirements under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court erred in numerous pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple evidentiary and procedural objections but offers minimal analysis and cites no legal authority for his positions. All of these arguments lack merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from offering testimony that they had previously seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending such testimony constituted propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government’s position that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence material to the possession charge and served to illustrate Cutbank’s continuous possession of the firearm prior to the charged incident. We concur in this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. gave testimony that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of times” preceding the incident in question.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank asked the court to give the jury an instruction clarifying that D.F.’s statements to law enforcement should not be considered as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. testified personally about the underlying events, in order to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns. In response, the government contended that those statements were introduced to explain the background for the officers’ actions and state of knowledge at relevant times, that their testimony would not violate hearsay rules, and that a limiting instruction from the court later in the proceedings—if necessary—would be standard practice and adequately address any issue. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). It is not mandatory for a court to issue a jury instruction addressing each piece of evidence at the moment it is admitted, provided that a suitable instruction is given at the conclusion of the trial. Id. In the present case, the trial record reflects that hearsay evidence was not admitted. Further, D.F. took the stand and gave testimony. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Therefore, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection regarding this testimony. Additionally, when C.F., called as a witness by the government, testified that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice,” the court overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback does not present any authority disputing the district court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence relevant to the possession charge and to establish Cutback’s ongoing possession of the weapon before the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous statements about the gun—including her claim that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant evening—D.F. explained that certain remarks stemmed from her lack of familiarity with firearms and her aversion to them, noting, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court denied Cutback’s request to strike this portion of D.F.’s testimony concerning her sister's death by firearm. D.F.’s comments directly addressed matters raised during cross-examination relating to inconsistent statements. Cutback fails to identify, nor is there any clear, unfair prejudice or juror confusion caused by this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., who testified for the government, also referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister was shot and killed, and defense counsel did not object. The district court's considerable latitude in admitting evidence was not exceeded. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the firearm “once or twice” by cross-examining her concerning statements she had made earlier to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into what C.F. relayed to law enforcement but upheld the prosecution’s objections to defense counsel’s attempts to read from the police report without laying an adequate foundation, since C.F. was not the document’s author. Although Cutback did not directly address this ruling on appeal, the government’s brief contended that Cutback “next claim[ed] that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably upon appellees who needlessly broaden the issues on appeal. Regardless, preserved or not, such a “claim” lacks merit. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (citation omitted): “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The court therefore did not abuse its discretion in insisting on proper authentication.

As Cutback concedes, “this court will not set aside an incorrect evidentiary decision unless the mistake was not harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only if the jury’s verdict may have been materially affected by the wrongly admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this requirement. The prosecution presented substantial testimonial and physical evidence. Accordingly, the district court’s evidentiary rulings “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We decline to reverse a conviction on account of the cumulative effect of errors made at trial unless the defendant has suffered substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm, additionally determining that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, which resulted in enhanced penalties pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended assigning a base offense level of 26, since the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank had a minimum of two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also suggested an increase of three levels due to the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement because Cutbank recklessly created a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury while evading law enforcement, and an additional four-level increase for possession of the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These combined enhancements produced a total offense level of 35. According to the PSR, Cutbank’s Criminal History Category was VI, which established an advisory guideline range for imprisonment of 292 to 365 months. Cutbank contends on appeal that the district court erred in applying the four-level sentencing enhancement for possessing a firearm in relation to another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This sentencing enhancement is triggered when a defendant either uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted, without any challenge, the factual findings in the PSR concerning Cutback’s earlier felony convictions. Therefore, the district court did not make a mistake—let alone a clear one—by concluding, based on the PSR, that the firearm was involved with another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produces a sentence within the guidelines range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that a sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had three or more previous violent felony convictions, thereby activating penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the corresponding Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have established a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines stipulate that the higher offense level is to be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court must initially “begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this determination, the court evaluates whether the considerations outlined in § 3553(a) justify a departure or variance from the recommended Guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence falling within the correctly calculated guideline range, which is afforded a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the district court referenced its examination of the PSR and the parties’ sentencing memoranda, and addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a departure from the guidelines. As stated, “[t]hus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). The law does not require the district court to enumerate every factor weighed; rather, it suffices that “it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history consisted of nearly twelve prior felonies, several involving violent conduct and weapons offenses. The current conviction entailed domestic violence, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s home in the presence of her children, and inflicting injury on a law enforcement officer during resistance to arrest. Consequently, the district court did not exceed its broad sentencing authority nor did it impose a sentence that was substantively unreasonable by committing Cutback to a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guidelines.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
