Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a lengthy criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, falling within the advisory guideline range it calculated. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made a number of evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and wrongly imposed a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, which raised the applicable guideline range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank roused his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and compelled her into a closet, all the while expressing paranoia by alleging the presence of "listening devices" in the residence and asserting that individuals outside intended him harm. Cutbank detained D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described in her testimony as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her on the face with the gun. At a certain point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake the others who were sleeping in the residence, which included her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left, taking with him both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

The police were also notified by a resident living half a mile from D.F.’s home that a man had entered their garage and would not leave. Upon arrival, officers encountered Cutbank, who had barricaded himself inside the garage and, over several hours, exhibited unpredictable behavior and asserted he possessed a weapon. When officers tried to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted, biting an officer on the face and neck, and attempted to seize the officer’s firearm. In searching the route Cutbank was likely to have traveled from D.F.’s house to the garage, police discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a large kitchen knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had been modified with a shortened barrel and stock. DNA found on the rifle matched Cutbank. During trial, D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession that night. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense for being a felon in possession of a firearm, and determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the increased sentencing penalties under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court erred in various pretrial and trial decisions and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to aggravate his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents several challenges related to evidence and procedure, but offers minimal analysis and cites no legal authority in support of his claims. These arguments are unfounded.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute propensity evidence prohibited by Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government’s position that the testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showing Cutbank’s continued possession of the firearm prior to the event in question. We concur. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the relevant incident.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank sought an instruction to be given to the jury, intended to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns, specifying that the jury should not treat D.F.’s statements made to police as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. testified about those events herself. In response, the government contended that such statements would merely provide context for the actions and knowledge of police officers at the relevant time, that the officers’ testimony would remain within hearsay limits, and that any later bench instruction, if found necessary, would be both routine and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. The present record indicates that hearsay was not admitted during the trial proceedings. Moreover, D.F. did provide testimony. The Confrontation Clause is fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). There was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this portion of the testimony. Similarly, when C.F., who was a witness for the government, testified that she had seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” before, the court also rejected a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback fails to cite any authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge, as well as relevant to establishing his ongoing possession of the firearm preceding the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about her prior inconsistent statements concerning the firearm—including her claim that she could not recall seeing the weapon on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her earlier remarks resulted from her lack of familiarity with firearms and her personal aversion to them, adding that this aversion stemmed from the fact that her sister had been killed by a gun. The court refused Cutback's request to strike D.F.'s reference to her sister’s death by gunfire. The statement was made in response to questioning about inconsistencies in her previous testimony. Cutback has not identified any undue prejudice or jury confusion caused by this testimony, nor is any such effect evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., another government witness, mentioned that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun without any objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed its wide discretion in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s statement that she had seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” before, by cross-examining her about statements she previously gave to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into C.F.’s statements to law enforcement but upheld the prosecution’s objections when defense counsel tried to read from portions of the police report into evidence without first establishing an appropriate foundation, given that C.F. was not the report’s author. Although Cutback failed to reference this specific ruling in his appeal, the government’s brief asserted that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disfavor appellees who needlessly enlarge the scope of issues on appeal. Regardless, whether this “claim” was properly preserved or not, it lacks merit. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The court acted within its discretion in insisting upon proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not overturn an erroneous evidentiary decision if the mistake was harmless. . . . In other words, reversal is warranted only where the jury's verdict may have been significantly affected by the evidence that was improperly admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank is unable to meet this standard. The government introduced strong testimonial and physical evidence. Consequently, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not set aside a conviction for the cumulative effect of errors at trial unless there has been substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the increased penalties prescribed by the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26, reasoning that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as specified in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and that Cutbank had a minimum of two earlier convictions for violent crimes. The report recommended a further three-level increase due to the firearm's altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly placing others at risk of death or serious bodily harm while fleeing law enforcement, and an additional four-level increase because Cutbank possessed the firearm in relation to another felony, culminating in a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which yielded an advisory sentencing range under the guidelines of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by imposing the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing hearing, the district court adopted the PSR's factual recitation of Cutback’s previous felony convictions without any objection from the parties. Therefore, the district court did not err, let alone commit clear error, in determining that the PSR's facts support a finding that the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that imposing the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4-level enhancement leads to a sentence within the applicable range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” namely, that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby meeting the requirements for the Armed Career Criminal Act, the guideline applicable to Armed Career Criminals would set the total offense level at 34. According to the Guidelines, the higher offense level must be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, the district court must initially and accurately determine the relevant Guidelines range for the offense. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Furthermore, “[b]efore a court of appeals evaluates whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, it must first confirm that the district court did not commit any significant procedural mistakes, including a failure to compute the Guidelines range or an incorrect calculation thereof.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court assesses whether consideration of the § 3553(a) factors justifies either a departure from or a variance to the advisory guideline sentence.

In this case, the court imposed a sentence that fell within the properly calculated guidelines range, which we treat as presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceeding, the court indicated it had examined the PSR as well as the sentencing briefs, and considered the factors under § 3553(a) that might justify a different sentence. As stated, “the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). The law does not demand that a district court mechanically list all the factors it evaluated to uphold a sentence; rather, the record must show that the court actually took into account the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record included almost a dozen previous felony convictions, numerous ones involving violent conduct and firearms. The current conviction arose from conduct including domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s home in her children’s presence, and harming a law enforcement officer during his resistance to arrest. Under these circumstances, the district court did not exceed the wide latitude afforded it in sentencing, nor did it impose a sentence that was substantively unreasonable, by sentencing Cutback to a guidelines-range term of imprisonment of 292 months.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
