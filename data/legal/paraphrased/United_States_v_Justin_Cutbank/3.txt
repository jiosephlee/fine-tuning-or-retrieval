Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal background, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. The district court sentenced him pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act to 292 months in prison, a term falling within the calculated advisory guidelines range. On appeal, Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and that it erroneously applied a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement, thereby increasing his advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while making paranoid allegations regarding “listening devices” within the residence and individuals outside purportedly intent on harming him. He held D.F. inside the closet at gunpoint for what she later described in testimony as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the firearm. At a certain point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake up the other occupants in the home, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Cutbank ultimately departed the scene with D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. Afterward, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, indicating that an individual was present in the garage and refused to vacate the premises. Upon arrival, officers found Cutbank barricaded inside the garage, where he made disjointed remarks and asserted that he possessed a weapon during an hours-long standoff. Cutbank resisted officers’ efforts to detain him, during which he bit one officer on the face and neck and attempted to seize the officer’s firearm. Along the route that Cutbank likely traveled between D.F.’s residence and the garage, officers discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA testing of the rifle revealed a match to Cutbank. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. identified this firearm as one they had observed in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged felon-in-possession offense and determined that he had previously been convicted of at least three violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court erred in multiple pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to the trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their previous observations of him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government’s position, determining that this evidence was intrinsic and relevant to establishing the possession charge, as well as to showing Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm leading up to the event in question. We concur with this reasoning. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” At trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident under review.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought a jury instruction that would prohibit the jury, in order to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems, from considering D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. testified personally about the relevant events. The government maintained that these statements were being offered to provide background for the officers’ actions and state of mind, that the officers’ accounts would not violate hearsay restrictions, and that any necessary curative instruction from the court delivered subsequently would be standard practice and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to precedent, the district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In the present case, the record shows that no hearsay was admitted throughout the trial proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. did testify. The Confrontation Clause is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Consequently, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this testimony. Additionally, when C.F., testifying for the government, indicated that she had seen the firearm used during the incident “once or twice” previously, the court also overruled the defense's objection. On appeal, Cutback fails to reference any authority that challenges the court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showing ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements concerning the gun—including her claim of not recalling seeing the gun on the night at issue—D.F. explained that some of her remarks stemmed from her “not familiar[ity] with guns” and her dislike of them, noting that “[her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court refused Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s statement about her sister’s death by gun. This evidence was elicited in response to questioning about her inconsistent statements. Cutback fails to identify, and the record does not reflect, any unfair prejudice or confusion arising from this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., testifying for the government, also referenced D.F.’s sister having been killed by a gun without any objection from the defense. Therefore, the district court did not exceed its considerable discretion regarding evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the gun used in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her, during cross-examination, about earlier statements she had made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. had told the police but upheld the government’s objections to defense counsel reading excerpts from the corresponding police report into the record without establishing the necessary foundation, as C.F. was not the report’s author. On appeal, Cutback did not address this particular ruling, but the government, in its brief, contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove of appellees broadening the scope of appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless, whether or not the issue was properly preserved, the “claim” is unfounded. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion in demanding appropriate authentication.

Lastly, as Cutbank concedes, "this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. . . . In other words, we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government's case included significant testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court's evidentiary decisions "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict." Id. at 998. "We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to enhanced sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) suggested a base offense level of 26 because the case involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and because Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase due to the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase because Cutbank had recklessly endangered others by creating a risk of death or serious bodily harm during flight from police, and a four-level increase based on his possession of the firearm in connection with another felony offense, which yielded a total offense level of 35. According to the PSR, Cutbank’s Criminal History Category was VI, leading to an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by imposing the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is applicable when a defendant utilized or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing hearing, the district court accepted, without any objection, the Presentence Investigation Report’s factual representations about Cutback’s previous felony convictions. Therefore, the court did not err, let alone clearly err, by determining that the PSR’s facts demonstrated that the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (setting forth the standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which results in a guideline-range sentence, “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that a sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines stipulate that the higher offense level should be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, "the district court must first accurately determine the relevant Guidelines range at the outset of sentencing proceedings." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "Prior to assessing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, a court of appeals must confirm that the district court did not make a significant procedural mistake, including either a failure to compute, or an incorrect computation of, the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this step, the court evaluates whether the factors outlined in § 3553(a) justify a departure from, or a variance to, a sentence outside the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the appropriate guidelines range, and such a sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceeding, the court indicated that it had examined the PSR and sentencing memoranda, and also addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” Furthermore, a district court is not required “to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had almost twelve prior felony convictions, including several that entailed violence and firearm use. The crime of conviction included domestic abuse, kidnapping, an assault carried out in the victim’s residence in front of her children, and injuring a law enforcement officer during efforts to avoid arrest. The district court therefore acted within its wide discretion in sentencing and did not impose a substantively unreasonable sentence by ordering a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
