Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him under the Armed Career Criminal Act to 292 months in prison, a term that fell within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. On appeal, he challenges his conviction and sentence, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial, and also wrongly imposed a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement, which raised the advisory guidelines range. We affirm the district court’s judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a firearm at her head, and compelled her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions regarding alleged “listening devices” inside the residence and individuals supposedly waiting outside to harm him. Holding D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what she described as several hours, Cutbank threatened to kill her and struck her on the face with the weapon. At one stage, he instructed D.F. to wake up the other people sleeping in the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Eventually, Cutbank left the scene with both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home indicating that a man was present in the garage and refusing to leave. Upon police arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside and, during several hours, exhibited erratic behavior, making incoherent statements and asserting that he possessed a weapon. When officers sought to take him into custody, Cutbank resisted by biting an officer on the face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. Officers conducted a search of the route that Cutbank likely used traveling from D.F.’s house to the garage and recovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with both a shortened barrel and stock. The rifle’s DNA evidence matched Cutbank. During trial proceedings, D.F. and C.F. identified this firearm as the one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge and determined that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby subjecting him to the increased penalties of § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court erred in several pretrial and trial rulings and misapplied the sentence enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle on earlier occasions, contending such testimony constituted inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court sided with the government’s position that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge and relevant to establishing a pattern of continuous possession of the weapon preceding the alleged event. We concur with this reasoning. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. provided testimony that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested that the jury receive an instruction specifying that, to prevent hearsay issues and possible violations of the Confrontation Clause, they were not to regard D.F.’s statements to the police as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. personally testified regarding the events. In response, the government maintained that these statements would provide necessary background for the officers’ actions and their contemporaneous understanding, asserted that the officers’ testimony would comply with hearsay rules, and argued that any required bench instruction could properly be issued later, following standard practice. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). It is not mandatory for a district court to instruct the jury about each particular item of evidence at the moment it is introduced, so long as a suitable instruction is provided at the conclusion of trial. Id. In this case, the record shows the court did not allow the admission of hearsay during the proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. testified at trial. The Confrontation Clause is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Accordingly, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, the court dismissed a defense objection when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, indicated she had seen the weapon that was used during the event “once or twice” before. On appeal, Cutback fails to present any authority contradicting the district court’s pretrial determination that such evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession offense, as well as to proving that Cutback maintained continuous possession of the firearm leading up to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about her earlier inconsistent statements concerning the firearm, including whether she recalled observing the gun on the relevant evening, D.F. explained that some of her statements resulted from her lack of familiarity with firearms and her dislike of guns, stating, “as [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court overruled Cutback’s request to exclude D.F.’s mention of her sister’s death by gunshot. D.F.’s remark was made in direct response to questions in cross-examination about inconsistent statements she had made previously. Cutback does not identify, nor is there evident, any improper prejudice or confusion stemming from this statement. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., called by the government, referred to D.F.’s sister being shot during her own testimony, and the defense made no objection. The district court did not exceed its wide latitude in matters of evidence. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s statement that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” by cross-examining her with reference to earlier remarks she gave to a police investigator. The district court permitted questioning concerning C.F.’s statements to police but sustained the government’s objections to defense counsel’s attempts to introduce portions of the subsequent police report into evidence absent appropriate foundational showing, since C.F. was not the report’s author. Although Cutback did not raise this district court ruling specifically in his appellate argument, the government’s brief construed his “next claim” to assert that the district court “thwarted” his cross-examination of C.F. on the subject of firearm possession. We are not inclined to encourage appellees who broaden appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless, the “claim,” whether or not preserved, is without substance. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court did not exceed its discretion in insisting on proper authentication.

In conclusion, Cutback concedes that “this court will not reverse an evidentiary error unless it was not harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is appropriate only if the inadmissible evidence could have materially affected the jury’s decision.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this burden. The government offered considerable testimonial and physical proof. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “either did not impact or had only a minimal impact on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not set aside a conviction on the basis of multiple trial errors absent a showing of significant prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to increased penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. According to the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), a base offense level of 26 was recommended because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and because Cutbank had a minimum of two previous convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further proposed a three-level enhancement based on the firearm’s altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement due to Cutbank’s reckless creation of a risk of death or serious bodily harm while fleeing law enforcement, and an additional four-level enhancement for possession of the firearm in connection with another felony offense, bringing the total offense level to 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which produced an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. Cutbank, on appeal, challenges the district court’s application of the four-level sentencing enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant employs or has possession of any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing hearing, the district court accepted the Presentence Investigation Report’s factual descriptions concerning Cutback’s previous felony convictions, and there was no objection to these facts. Accordingly, the court did not commit error, let alone clear error, in determining that the PSR’s facts show that the firearm was used in connection with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank asserts that imposing the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produces a sentence within the advisory range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” namely that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback was guilty of three or more prior violent felony convictions, making him subject to penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, application of the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would yield a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the offense level that is higher applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, the district court must first accurately determine the relevant Guidelines range to initiate sentencing proceedings. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). An appellate court, before assessing whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, is required to confirm that the district court has not committed any significant procedural error, such as neglecting to compute or miscalculating the Guidelines range. See Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143–44 (2018). Following this, the court evaluates whether the considerations set out in § 3553(a) justify imposing a sentence outside the advisory guidelines range, either through a departure or a variance.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence falling within the relevant guideline range, which is presumed reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the district judge referenced a review of the PSR and sentencing briefs, as well as consideration of the § 3553(a) factors that could justify a variance. Because of this, the district court was apprised of Cutbank’s contentions, and we accordingly presume the court took those arguments into account and rejected them. See United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had almost twelve previous felony convictions, several of which related to violent offenses and the use of firearms. The instant offense involved acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assault committed in the victim’s residence while her children were present, and causing injury to a police officer during an attempt to evade arrest. The district court acted within its considerable discretion in sentencing and did not issue a substantively unreasonable sentence by imposing a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
