Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal record, guilty of possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term falling within the advisory guidelines range established by the court. Cutbank challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial, and that it wrongly imposed a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement which raised the guidelines range. We affirm the district court's judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., and, brandishing a firearm at her face, ordered her into a closet. He accused her in a paranoid manner of “listening devices” being present in the residence and alleged that individuals outside were waiting to harm him. While keeping D.F. confined in the closet at gunpoint for a duration that, according to D.F.'s testimony, seemed to last several hours, Cutbank threatened to kill her and struck her face with the firearm. At one point, he instructed D.F. to awaken others sleeping in the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the scene, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the gun with him. D.F. then contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was in the garage and refusing to depart. Upon the officers’ arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside and, during several hours, exhibited erratic behavior, made unusual statements, and asserted he possessed a weapon. When officers sought to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to take his firearm. While searching the route that Cutbank likely traveled from D.F.’s residence to the garage, officers discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a large butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA analysis revealed that Cutbank’s DNA was present on the firearm. During the trial, both D.F. and C.F. recognized the weapon as one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession prior to that incident. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm and determined that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby making him subject to the enhanced penalties of § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court erred by abusing its discretion in several pretrial and trial rulings and that it incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents several arguments concerning evidentiary and procedural matters, but offers virtually no legal authority or substantive analysis in their support. Each of these arguments lacks merit.

A

Prior to the trial, Cutbank sought to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending such testimony would constitute impermissible propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court, however, sided with the government, finding this evidence to be intrinsic and pertinent to the possession charge, as well as indicative of continued possession of the firearm before the relevant event. We concur with this conclusion. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions preceding the incident.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank requested that the jury receive an instruction—designed to mitigate hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns—directing it not to regard D.F.'s statements made to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank's conduct until D.F. provided direct testimony regarding the incidents in question. The government contended that these statements would serve solely to supply context regarding the officers’ conduct and their understanding at the relevant time; furthermore, they maintained that the officers’ accounts would not contravene hearsay principles, and asserted that any needed judicial instruction could customarily and adequately be provided later from the bench. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In the present matter, the record shows that no hearsay evidence was received during the proceedings. Moreover, D.F. testified at trial. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Accordingly, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident, describing it as a "handful of times." When Cutbank renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court denied the motion. Similarly, the court overruled a defense objection when government witness C.F. testified that she had seen the firearm involved in the incident "once or twice" beforehand. On appeal, Cutbank does not point to any authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence, which was pertinent to establishing the possession charge as well as continuous possession of the firearm before the incident occurred. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements concerning the gun—specifically, her claim that she did not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night—D.F. explained that certain statements were made because she was “not familiar with guns” and “did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court refused Cutback’s request to strike the testimony referencing the death of D.F.’s sister by gun. This testimony was given in response to questions about her inconsistent prior statements during cross-examination. Cutback fails to identify, and the record does not reveal, any undue prejudice or confusion stemming from this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, C.F., who testified for the government, referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun without any objection from the defense. The district court acted within its considerable latitude regarding evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to discredit C.F.'s assertion that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her, during cross-examination, about earlier statements she provided to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into C.F.’s statements to police but upheld the prosecution’s objections when defense counsel tried to read parts of the corresponding police report into evidence without laying the necessary foundation, as C.F. had not written the report. On appeal, although Cutback did not specifically address this court ruling, the government’s brief maintained that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably on appellees who broaden the issues on appeal without cause. Regardless, whether or not this matter was properly preserved, the claim fails. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The court acted within its discretion in insisting on appropriate authentication.

Ultimately, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not overturn an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. . . . In other words, reversal is warranted only when the jury could have been significantly influenced by the evidence that was improperly admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government introduced considerable testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions either did not affect the outcome or had only minimal impact on the verdict. Id. at 998. “We will not disturb a conviction on account of the cumulative impact of trial errors unless the defendant experiences substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. The jury also determined that he had three or more previous convictions for violent felonies, subjecting him to the enhanced penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26 because the case involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and because Cutbank had at least two earlier convictions for violent crimes. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase due to the firearm’s serial number being altered or obliterated, a two-level increase because Cutbank recklessly created a risk of death or serious injury while attempting to evade police, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in relation to another felony offense, reaching a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, resulting in an advisory sentencing range under the guidelines of 292 to 365 months in prison. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by applying the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

The enhancement is applicable when a defendant uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in connection with a separate federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court incorporated the Presentence Investigation Report’s undisputed factual assertions relating to Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Therefore, the court did not commit any error, let alone clear error, in determining that the facts as stated in the PSR demonstrate that the firearm was used in relation to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). However, Cutbank’s argument centers on the claim that applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) leads to a sentence within the Guideline range that allegedly “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)”—namely, that a sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Even though the jury determined Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions—which activates penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act—the total offense level under the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have been 34. Pursuant to USSG § 4B1.4(b), the Guidelines require use of the higher offense level.

When imposing a sentence, the district court is required to initially and accurately determine the relevant Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “Prior to reviewing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, a court of appeals must verify that the district court did not make a significant procedural error, including omitting to calculate, or incorrectly determining, the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates whether application of the § 3553(a) factors justifies a sentence that departs from or varies from the recommended advisory Guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the properly calculated guideline range, which is afforded a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court referenced its examination of both the presentence report (PSR) and the submitted sentencing memoranda, as well as consideration of the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. “Accordingly, the district court was cognizant of [Cutbank’s] contentions, and we consequently presume it addressed and denied them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “It is not necessary for a district court to list each factor it considered to sustain the sentence on review; it is sufficient if the record demonstrates that the court took account of the § 3553(a) factors when determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record includes almost twelve prior felony convictions, several of which involved violent conduct and use of firearms. The present conviction pertained to acts of domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s home in the presence of her children, and causing injury to a law enforcement officer during the course of resisting arrest. The district court, exercising its considerable discretion in sentencing, did not act unreasonably nor did it impose a substantively excessive sentence by committing Cutback to a term of 292 months’ imprisonment within the prescribed range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
