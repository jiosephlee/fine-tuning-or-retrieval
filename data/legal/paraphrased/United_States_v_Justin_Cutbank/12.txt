Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank—a felon with a significant criminal background—guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) for being a felon in possession of a firearm. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, which fell within the advisory guideline range it calculated. Cutbank challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and wrongly applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), resulting in a higher advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., at gunpoint, aimed the weapon at her face, and forced her into a closet, making paranoid claims about “listening devices” present in the residence and people outside intending to hurt him. He held D.F. in the closet under threat of the firearm for what she estimated to be several hours, during which he threatened her life and struck her across the face with the gun. At one point, Cutbank commanded D.F. to wake up other individuals sleeping in the house, specifically her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank escaped, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that an individual was present in the garage and refusing to leave. Upon arrival, officers found Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, where, for several hours, he made incoherent statements and asserted that he possessed a weapon. When officers tried to detain him, Cutbank resisted, biting one officer on the face and neck, and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. Officers conducting a search along the route that Cutbank likely took between D.F.’s home and the garage discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a large butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. The firearm yielded DNA evidence matching Cutbank. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the rifle as the same weapon they had seen in Cutbank’s possession prior to that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge, and further determined that he had previously been convicted of at least three violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the heightened sentencing provisions outlined in § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court abused its discretion in several pretrial and trial decisions, and erred in its application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) when enhancing his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their previous observations of him possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle. He contended that such testimony constituted inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court determined that, as argued by the government, this testimony would instead constitute intrinsic evidence that was pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showed ongoing possession of the firearm before the charged incident. We concur with this conclusion. As noted in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. stated that she had witnessed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested that the jury receive an instruction, designed to preempt hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns, directing that it should not rely on D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. testified to those events in court. The government countered by asserting that D.F.’s statements were offered to explain the circumstances underlying the police officers’ actions and their knowledge at relevant times, contending further that the officers’ testimony would remain within the boundaries of the hearsay rule and, if any instruction proved necessary, a standard curative instruction from the court after the fact would suffice. As articulated in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), appellate review of a district court’s refusal to deliver a limiting instruction is conducted for abuse of discretion. The court is not compelled to provide a limiting instruction addressing every piece of evidence as it is introduced so long as an appropriate overall instruction is given at the end of trial. Id. In this instance, the record indicates the court did not admit any hearsay evidence during the proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. took the stand and testified, which fulfills Confrontation Clause requirements, as satisfaction occurs “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). The district court thus did not abuse its discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. When Cutback renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court denied it. Additionally, when C.F.—called as a witness for the government—testified that she had seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” previously, the court overruled an objection from the defense. On appeal, Cutback does not cite any legal authority that undermines the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence relevant to establishing the possession charge and indicating continuous possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier statements that conflicted regarding the gun, including her claim that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night, D.F. explained that some of her statements were due to her unfamiliarity with firearms and her aversion to guns, adding that this aversion was because her sister had been killed by a gun. The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the testimony about D.F.’s sister’s death by firearm. D.F.'s testimony was elicited in response to questions during cross-examination concerning her inconsistent previous statements. Cutback does not identify, nor does the record show, any undue prejudice or confusion resulting from the testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, government witness C.F. also referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister had died by gun violence, and defense counsel made no objection. Accordingly, the district court did not exceed its wide discretion over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s statement that she had previously seen the gun involved in the incident “once or twice” by cross-examining her about earlier remarks she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries regarding C.F.’s statements to police but upheld the prosecution’s objections when defense counsel tried to read parts of the resulting police report into evidence without laying an adequate foundation, since C.F. was not the author of the report. Although Cutback’s appeal did not mention this specific ruling, the government’s brief responded to what it described as Cutback’s claim that the district court “thwarted” his attempts to cross-examine C.F. about her testimony related to the gun possession. We do not look favorably on appellees who unnecessarily broaden appellate issues. Regardless, whether this argument was preserved or not, it lacks merit. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court’s insistence on proper authentication did not constitute an abuse of discretion.

Lastly, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not overturn an evidentiary ruling unless the error affected the outcome. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only where the improperly admitted evidence may have had a substantial impact on the jury’s decision.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government introduced considerable testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. Furthermore, “[w]e will not set aside a conviction for the combined effect of multiple trial errors except in cases of significant prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of felon in possession of a firearm. The jury further determined that he had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (“PSR”) set a base offense level at 26, given that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and that Cutbank had at least two previous convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further recommended an increase of three levels for possessing a firearm with an altered or obliterated serial number, an additional two-level enhancement for recklessly causing a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for having the firearm in connection with another felony offense, leading to a combined offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the resulting advisory sentencing range was 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank asserts that the district court erred in imposing the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

The enhancement is triggered when a defendant uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the Presentence Investigation Report’s factual findings about Cutback’s earlier felony convictions without any challenge. Consequently, the court’s determination that the PSR supports the conclusion that the firearm was used in connection with another felony was not erroneous, let alone clearly erroneous. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank asserts that imposing the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4-level enhancement leads to a sentence within the guideline range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” specifically the requirement that the sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” While the jury determined that Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions, thereby triggering the penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, application of the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the offense level that is higher applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When determining an appropriate sentence, “a district court should begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). The court then considers whether the § 3553(a) factors warrant a departure or variance from the advisory guidelines range sentence.

Paraphrased:

When imposing a sentence, it is essential that the district court first properly determine the applicable Guidelines range to initiate sentencing proceedings. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). The appellate court cannot assess whether a sentence is substantively reasonable until it is satisfied that the district court did not commit any significant procedural error, such as neglecting to calculate or miscalculating the Guidelines range. See Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Following this, the court evaluates whether the factors outlined in § 3553(a) justify a deviation or variance from the sentence suggested by the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the applicable guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable upon appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court referenced its examination of the PSR and the sentencing briefs and addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a deviation. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). It is not necessary for a district court to expressly enumerate every factor it considered in order to support a sentence; the record must simply show that the court in fact took into account the § 3553(a) factors in its determination. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history included almost twelve prior felony convictions, several involving violence or the use of firearms. The instant offense was comprised of domestic violence, kidnapping, assaulting the victim in the presence of her children at her residence, and causing injury to a law enforcement officer in the course of resisting arrest. The district court exercised its wide latitude in sentencing appropriately and did not impose a sentence that was substantively unreasonable by sentencing Cutback to a guidelines-range term of imprisonment of 292 months.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
