Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), noting his lengthy criminal background. The district court sentenced him under the Armed Career Criminal Act to 292 months in prison, a sentence that fell within the recommended advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank now appeals both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and erred in applying a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement, which resulted in a higher advisory guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while making paranoid statements about “listening devices” in the residence and individuals outside allegedly intent on harming him. D.F. testified that Cutbank held her in the closet at gunpoint for what seemed to her like hours, during which time he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the weapon. At one stage, Cutbank compelled D.F. to wake other individuals in the house, specifically her two teenage sons as well as her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the premises taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. Afterwards, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

The police were additionally alerted by a report from a residence located half a mile from D.F.’s home, where it was stated that a man was inside the garage and refused to vacate the premises. Upon arrival, law enforcement found that Cutbank had barricaded himself in the garage. Over several hours, he displayed unpredictable behavior, made various statements, and asserted that he possessed a weapon. Cutbank resisted officers' attempts to apprehend him, during which he bit one officer on the face and neck and attempted to seize the officer’s firearm. Along the presumed route between D.F.’s residence and the garage, officers discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a shortened barrel and stock. DNA analysis of the firearm yielded a match to Cutbank. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. identified the weapon as the same one they had previously observed in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of felon in possession and determined he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, subjecting him to enhanced penalties under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court abused its discretion through multiple pretrial and trial rulings and erroneously applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments concerning evidentiary and procedural matters but fails to cite any authority or offer meaningful analysis in support. All of these claims are without merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their earlier observations of him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted propensity evidence in contravention of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court sided with the government, determining that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showing a continuous pattern of firearm possession leading up to the event in question. We concur with this reasoning. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” At trial, D.F. stated under oath that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle “a handful of times” prior to the underlying incident.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank moved for a jury instruction that would prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns by prohibiting the jury from treating D.F.’s police statements as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank's conduct until D.F. herself testified to the relevant events. The prosecution contended that these statements would solely provide context for the actions and knowledge of the police officers at the pertinent time, maintained that the officers' testimony would comply with hearsay rules, and argued that, should it become necessary, a subsequent bench instruction would suffice and was standard practice. The appellate standard is that “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). Furthermore, as set forth in Bennett, the court is not required to instruct the jury about each specific item of evidence at the time it is admitted, so long as a proper instruction is provided at the conclusion of the trial. In this case, the record indicates that no hearsay was introduced during the proceedings. Moreover, D.F. took the stand and gave testimony. The Confrontation Clause is deemed fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). The district court, therefore, did not abuse its discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this testimony. Additionally, when C.F., who testified for the government, recounted having seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” previously, the court also overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback does not present any authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that such evidence constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and supporting a finding of ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the event. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous statements that conflicted regarding the gun—including her claim that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her statements were due to her lack of knowledge about firearms and her aversion to them, as her “sister was killed by a gun.” The district court refused Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s testimony about her sister’s death by gunfire. This information was provided as an explanation in response to questions about her prior inconsistent statements. Cutback does not identify any specific risk of unfair prejudice or confusion caused by this testimony, nor is any such risk evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, C.F., a witness for the prosecution, also referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun, and the defense did not object. The district court thus remained within the wide latitude afforded to it in evidentiary decisions. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her about statements she had earlier made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries about C.F.’s communications with the police, but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel tried to read sections of the corresponding police report into evidence without first establishing the necessary foundation, since C.F. was not the report’s author. On appeal, Cutback did not address this specific ruling, but the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disfavor appellees who needlessly broaden the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, whether the argument has been preserved or not, it is unsubstantiated. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court acted within its discretion in demanding proper authentication.

Finally, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not disturb a flawed evidentiary decision if the mistake was harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal occurs only when there is a possibility that the jury’s verdict was significantly affected by evidence that was improperly admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government presented strong testimonial and physical evidence. Consequently, the district court's evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not set aside a conviction for the cumulative impact of trial errors unless they result in substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury determined that Cutbank was guilty of feloniously possessing a firearm. Additionally, it concluded he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the enhanced penalties outlined in the Armed Career Criminal Act. According to the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), a base offense level of 26 was appropriate due to the involvement of a sawed-off firearm defined under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank’s history of at least two previous convictions for violent crimes. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase because the firearm’s serial number was altered or obliterated, a two-level enhancement for recklessly endangering life or causing serious bodily harm while eluding law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement based on his possession of the firearm in connection with a separate felony. This resulted in a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory guidelines called for a sentencing range from 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank challenges the district court’s application of the four-level enhancement for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered if the defendant either used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony offense under federal, state, or local law, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court, with no objection raised, accepted the Presentence Report’s account of Cutback’s past felony convictions. Accordingly, the court did not err—and certainly did not commit clear error—when it determined, based on the PSR, that the firearm was connected to another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, instead, contends that applying the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which yields a sentence within the guideline range, is at odds with the “core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” namely, that a sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, invoking the Armed Career Criminal Act provisions, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have resulted in an overall offense level of 34. According to the Guidelines, the higher offense level is controlling. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing sentence, a district court is to initially and correctly compute the applicable Guidelines range. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to addressing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, an appellate court must first verify that the district court did not make any significant procedural mistakes, such as neglecting to calculate, or erroneously calculating, the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this determination, the court evaluates whether the factors outlined in § 3553(a) justify a departure or variance from the advisory guideline sentence.

In this case, the district court sentenced Cutbank within the established guideline range, a sentence presumed reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court acknowledged having examined the PSR, the parties’ sentencing memoranda, and also considered the relevant § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. Accordingly, the district court was aware of Cutbank’s contentions, which leads to the presumption that the court both considered and rejected them. United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “We do not insist upon a formal or rote listing of every factor by the district court to affirm the sentence; rather, it must be evident from the record that the district court gave genuine consideration to the § 3553(a) factors before imposing the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record included nearly a dozen previous felony convictions, many of which involved violence and the use of firearms. The conviction here involved domestic violence, kidnapping, assault within the victim's residence and in her children’s presence, and causing injury to a law enforcement officer in the process of resisting arrest. Therefore, the district court did not exceed its significant discretion in sentencing or impose a substantively unreasonable sentence when it ordered Cutback to serve a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
