Case Name: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Jurisdiction: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Decision Date: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a lengthy criminal background, guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm, in contravention of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, consistent with the advisory guidelines range. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes which resulted in an unfair trial, and improperly imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) that increased his advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night between November 19 and 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions regarding “listening devices” inside the residence and individuals outside purportedly intending to harm him. While confining D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described in her testimony as hours, he threatened to kill her and struck her face with the firearm. At one stage, Cutbank compelled D.F. to awaken other occupants of the house, including her teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the premises, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. Afterward, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence situated half a mile from D.F.'s home, where an individual in the garage allegedly refused to vacate the premises. Upon their arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, and over several hours he made unpredictable remarks and asserted that he was armed. When officers tried to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted, biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer's firearm. During a search along the likely route between D.F.'s residence and the garage, officers discovered D.F.'s cellular phone, a large butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with both barrel and stock sawed off. DNA analysis identified Cutbank as the source of genetic material on the rifle. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. recognized the firearm as one they had previously observed in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm and determined he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions set forth in § 924(e). Cutbank now seeks to overturn his conviction and sentence, contending on appeal that the district court committed several discretionary errors before and during the trial and improperly applied the sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents various arguments relating to evidentiary and procedural matters but fails to cite any authority or offer substantial analysis in support of these claims. Each of these arguments lacks merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from providing testimony that they had seen him previously in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle. He contended that such testimony would constitute inadmissible propensity evidence, in violation of Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court sided with the government, determining that this testimony was intrinsic evidence relevant to establishing the possession charge and showing Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm leading up to the charged incident. We concur with this assessment. “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015). At the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought an instruction to the jury that, in order to avoid hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns, it was prohibited from considering D.F.'s statements to police as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank's conduct until D.F. provided testimony about the events herself. The government contended that the statements served to provide the backdrop for law enforcement’s actions and what officers knew at the relevant time, asserting further that officers' testimony would not contravene hearsay restrictions and that, if necessary, a subsequent jury instruction would be typical and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to precedent, a court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this case, the record reveals that no hearsay was admitted throughout the trial proceedings. Additionally, D.F. took the stand and testified. The Confrontation Clause requirement is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). There is, therefore, no indication the district court abused its discretion.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident, describing it as a “handful of times.” The district court denied Cutback’s renewed objection to the admissibility of this testimony. Similarly, the court overruled a defense objection when C.F., who testified on behalf of the government, reported that she had seen the firearm at issue “once or twice” before the incident. On appeal, Cutback provides no legal authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating Cutbank’s continuous possession of the firearm leading up to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent remarks related to the firearm—such as not recalling seeing the firearm on the relevant night—D.F. explained that certain statements were due to her being "not familiar with guns" and her aversion to them because her sister "was killed by a gun." The court denied Cutback’s request to strike the testimony regarding D.F.'s sister's death by firearm. This evidence was presented in response to cross-examination concerning her prior inconsistent statements. Cutback does not identify any specific unfair prejudice or confusion caused by this testimony, nor is any evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, during C.F.'s testimony for the government, he also referenced the death of D.F.'s sister by firearm, and the defense raised no objection. The district court did not exceed its wide latitude in admitting this evidence. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to discredit C.F.’s statement that she had previously seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her during cross-examination about statements she gave to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into what C.F. communicated to law enforcement but upheld the government’s objections to defense counsel’s attempts to read from portions of the police report into evidence, citing a lack of foundation since C.F. was not the report’s author. On appeal, Cutback did not specifically address this evidentiary decision; nonetheless, the government responded in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We discourage appellees from broadening the appellate issues needlessly. Regardless of whether this issue was properly preserved, the “claim” lacks merit. As stated, “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court, therefore, did not err by insisting on proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not overturn an erroneous evidentiary decision unless the mistake was harmful. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only if the improperly admitted evidence could have significantly influenced the jury’s verdict.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this burden. The government presented significant testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “either did not affect or had only minimal effect on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We do not set aside a conviction due to the cumulative impact of trial errors unless the defendant suffered substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that Cutbank had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the heightened penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26, citing that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), along with Cutbank's minimum of two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase because the firearm’s serial number had been altered or obliterated, a two-level enhancement due to Cutbank’s reckless creation of a risk of death or serious bodily injury during a flight from law enforcement, and an additional four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in relation to another felony offense, cumulatively resulting in a total offense level of 35. According to the PSR, Cutbank was assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which produced an advisory Guidelines range of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court adopted without opposition the factual content of the PSR concerning Cutback’s previous felony convictions. Therefore, the court did not commit error, much less clear error, in concluding that the information in the PSR demonstrates that the firearm was linked to another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). However, Cutbank asserts that imposing the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produces a sentence within the range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” which requires that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions—making the Armed Career Criminal Act penalties applicable—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have assigned a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the offense level which is higher is to be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, “a district court should initiate every sentencing procedure by accurately determining the applicable Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “Prior to reviewing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, an appellate court must first verify that the district court did not make a significant procedural error, such as neglecting to calculate—or miscalculating—the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After that, the court assesses whether application of the § 3553(a) factors supports a departure from or variance to the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the recommended guidelines, which is considered presumptively reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceedings, the court acknowledged that it had reviewed the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and the parties' sentencing memoranda, and addressed the § 3553(a) factors relevant to any request for a variance. As noted in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” Moreover, as stated in United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted), “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” Cutback’s criminal history included nearly twelve felony convictions, several of which were for violent offenses and firearm-related crimes. The crime at issue comprised acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assault committed in the victim's residence and in front of her children, as well as injuring a law enforcement officer while resisting arrest. The district court did not act outside the bounds of its broad sentencing authority, nor did it impose a substantively unreasonable sentence in sentencing Cutback to a guidelines-range term of 292 months’ imprisonment.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
