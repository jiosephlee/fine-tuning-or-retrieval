Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant prior criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months of imprisonment, a term that falls within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, claiming that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural errors which denied him a fair trial, and that it wrongly imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), resulting in an increased guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while making paranoid allegations regarding “listening devices” in the residence and individuals outside waiting to attack him. D.F. testified that Cutbank held her at gunpoint inside the closet for what seemed to her an extended period, during which he threatened to kill her and hit her in the face with the gun. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake up others sleeping in the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Subsequently, Cutbank left, taking both the firearm and D.F.’s cell phone. D.F. then contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, stating that a man was present in the garage and would not vacate the premises. Upon their arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage, and over several hours, exhibited erratic behavior and asserted that he was armed. When officers attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting one officer's face and neck and attempting to seize his firearm. During a search along the probable route from D.F.'s residence to the garage, officers recovered D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA testing on the firearm returned a match to Cutbank. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had previously observed Cutbank possessing earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged crime—felon in possession of a firearm—and determined he had three prior convictions for violent felonies, invoking the enhanced sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court committed abuse of discretion through several of its rulings before and during the trial and that the court misapplied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to the trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude the testimony of D.F. and C.F. regarding their previous observations of him possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government’s assertion that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating the continuity of possession preceding the incident. We reach the same conclusion. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested that the jury receive an instruction clarifying that, in order to address concerns regarding hearsay and the Confrontation Clause, it was prohibited from treating D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself took the stand and described the incidents. The government contended that these statements would simply provide background for the officers’ actions and their state of knowledge at relevant times; it also asserted that the officers' testimony would not violate the hearsay rule and that, if necessary, a subsequent instruction from the court would be conventional and sufficient. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). A court is “not required to instruct the jury about every piece of evidence upon its admission if a suitable instruction is given at the end of the trial.” Id. In this case, the record shows that no hearsay was admitted into evidence during the trial. Moreover, D.F. gave testimony. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Thus, the district court did not abuse its discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this testimony. Additionally, the court rejected a defense objection during C.F.’s testimony for the government, wherein C.F. asserted she had previously seen the firearm used during the incident “once or twice.” On appeal, Cutback fails to reference any authority challenging the court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge and to showing Cutback’s ongoing possession of the firearm before the event. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements about the gun—specifically, that she could not recall seeing the gun on the night in question—D.F. explained that some of her statements were because she was "not familiar with guns" and did not "like guns," further explaining, "[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun." The district court rejected Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s testimony regarding her sister’s death by firearm. D.F.'s explanation directly addressed the cross-examination pertaining to her inconsistent prior statements. Cutback has not identified, nor is there any evident, unfair prejudice or confusion caused by this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Moreover, government witness C.F. also referred to D.F.'s sister being killed by a gun, without any objection by the defense. Consequently, the district court did not exceed its wide latitude in evidentiary decisions. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense aimed to challenge C.F.'s assertion that she had previously seen the firearm “once or twice” before the incident by cross-examining her regarding statements she had previously given to a police investigator. The district court permitted questioning about C.F.'s communications with police but upheld the government’s objections to defense counsel attempting to read excerpts from the corresponding police report into evidence without laying the necessary foundation, noting that C.F. was not the report’s author. Although Cutback failed to address this specific evidentiary ruling on appeal, the government’s brief asserted that his “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We are not favorably disposed toward appellees who unnecessarily enlarge the scope of issues on appeal. Regardless, whether or not this issue was properly preserved, the “claim” is not meritorious. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court did not err by insisting on proper authentication.

Finally, as Cutback concedes, "this court does not overturn an erroneous evidentiary decision if the error was harmless. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only if the jury could have been significantly affected by the evidence that was wrongfully admitted." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this burden. The government presented considerable testimonial and physical evidence. Consequently, the district court's evidentiary decisions "either did not impact or had only minimal impact on the verdict." Id. at 998. "A conviction will not be reversed on the basis of the cumulative effect of errors at trial unless the defendant suffered substantial prejudice." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It additionally determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26, as the crime involved a sawed-off firearm pursuant to 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and because Cutbank had two or more prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase due to the firearm bearing an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement as a result of recklessly endangering others while evading law enforcement, and a four-level increase on the grounds that the firearm was possessed in connection with another felony, culminating in a total offense level of 35. The PSR also assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, yielding an advisory guideline sentencing range of 292 to 365 months imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by imposing the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

The enhancement is triggered when a defendant has used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted as true—without objection—the PSR’s factual recitation concerning Cutback’s previous felony offenses. As a result, the court did not err, let alone plainly err, by concluding that the PSR’s facts demonstrate the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, however, contends that applying the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), resulting in a Guidelines-range sentence, is inconsistent with “the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that punishment must be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Even though the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions—thus activating the penalties associated with the Armed Career Criminal Act—the offense level under the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have been 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level must be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When determining a sentence, the district court is required to initially and accurately compute the relevant Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). A court of appeals may not assess the substantive reasonableness of a sentence unless it first verifies that the district court did not commit a notable procedural error, such as neglecting to calculate the Guidelines range or making an incorrect calculation. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court must evaluate whether the factors listed in § 3553(a) justify a departure or variance from the recommended guidelines sentence.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the appropriate guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court referenced its consideration of the PSR and sentencing memoranda, as well as an assessment of the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. As stated, “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). The law does not demand that a district court enumerate every factor it evaluates; it is sufficient if the record demonstrates the court gave due attention to the § 3553(a) factors when imposing the sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal record included almost a dozen previous felony convictions, several of which were violent and involved firearms. The offense at issue consisted of acts such as domestic abuse, kidnaping, assaulting the victim at her home in front of her children, and causing injury to a police officer while resisting arrest. The district court acted within its wide sentencing discretion and did not render a substantively unreasonable sentence by imposing a term of 292 months’ imprisonment within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
