Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a lengthy record of prior felonies, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term falling within the advisory guidelines range established by the court. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial, and also erred by applying a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement which increased the applicable advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet, expressing paranoid suspicions about "listening devices" present in the residence and alleging that individuals outside intended to harm him. He held D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what she described in her testimony as hours, threatening to kill her and striking her face with the firearm. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake up other occupants of the house, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the scene, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, where a male individual was said to be inside the garage and refusing to leave. Upon arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself within the garage, during which time he behaved erratically and asserted that he was armed. When officers attempted to take him into custody, Cutbank resisted arrest by biting an officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. While searching the route Cutbank was likely to have traveled between D.F.’s house and the garage, officers discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a large kitchen knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had been modified with a shortened barrel and stock. The firearm was found to contain DNA that matched Cutbank’s. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. testified and identified the firearm as one they had observed in Cutbank’s possession prior to that night. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge and determined that he had been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing requirements of § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court abused its discretion through several pretrial and trial rulings, and improperly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their earlier observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute improper propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court sided with the government, finding that the evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as to showing that Cutbank had ongoing possession of the firearm before the incident. We concur with this determination. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank requested that the jury receive an instruction clarifying that, in order to avoid hearsay and Confrontation Clause complications, D.F.’s statements to law enforcement could not be considered as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself provided testimony about those events. The government countered that these statements were intended only to illustrate the circumstances underlying the officers’ actions and their awareness at the relevant time, contending that the officers’ testimony would not violate hearsay principles and that, should a limiting instruction become necessary, it would be appropriate and customary for the court to provide one at the conclusion of the trial. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not obligated to instruct the jury on each piece of evidence as it is presented so long as a proper instruction is given at the end of the proceedings. Id. In this case, the trial record shows no hearsay was admitted. Moreover, D.F. did in fact testify. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Therefore, the district court did not abuse its discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court overruled Cutbank’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, when C.F. testified for the government that she had previously seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” before, the court again overruled a defense objection. On appeal, Cutbank does not provide any legal authority that contradicts the district court’s pretrial determination that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showing Cutbank’s continuous possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about prior inconsistent statements concerning the firearm, including her claim that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night, D.F. explained that certain of her statements resulted from her lack of familiarity with guns and her aversion to them, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The district court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister’s death by firearm. This testimony was elicited as part of D.F.’s explanation in response to questioning about inconsistent statements. Cutback does not identify any specific unfair prejudice or juror confusion stemming from this testimony, nor does the record reflect any. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, C.F., testifying for the prosecution, also referenced D.F.’s sister’s death by gun without any objection from the defense. The district court therefore acted within the broad scope of its evidentiary authority. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s testimony that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice,” by questioning her on cross-examination about earlier statements she had made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into what C.F. told the police but sustained the government's objections when defense counsel tried to read excerpts from the corresponding police report into evidence, finding that there was no adequate foundation since C.F. had not authored the report. Although Cutback did not raise this ruling on appeal, the government asserted in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We are disinclined to entertain appellees’ expansion of the issues presented on appeal beyond necessity. Regardless, even if this issue was preserved, the “claim” lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion in demanding proper authentication.

As Cutback concedes, “this court will not overturn an erroneous evidentiary decision if the mistake was harmless. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only if the jury’s decision may have been materially affected by the evidence that was admitted in error.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The prosecution presented strong testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “either did not impact or had only a minimal impact on the verdict.” Id. at 998. Furthermore, “[w]e will not vacate a conviction due to the aggregate impact of trial errors unless the defendant has suffered significant prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the charge of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further determined that he had at least three previous violent felony convictions, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) set forth a base offense level of 26, given that the offense concerned a sawed-off firearm as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and that Cutbank had two or more prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also recommended a three-level enhancement because the firearm's serial number was altered or obliterated, a two-level enhancement based on his reckless creation of a risk of death or serious injury while evading law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement due to possession of the firearm in connection with another felony offense, culminating in a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which produced an advisory sentencing guidelines range between 292 and 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant employs or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court incorporated, without any objection, the Presentence Investigation Report’s factual representations concerning Cutback’s previous felony convictions. Accordingly, the court did not make a mistake—much less a clear one—in determining that the PSR’s facts demonstrate the firearm was used in connection with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank asserts that imposing the 4-level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produces a guidelines-range sentence that is inconsistent with the fundamental principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, invoking penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, application of the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines dictate that the higher offense level controls. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court must initially determine the correct Guidelines range. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Before addressing whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, an appellate court must verify that the district court did not make any significant procedural errors, such as failing to compute the Guidelines range or miscalculating it. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates whether the factors set forth in § 3553(a) justify imposing a sentence outside the advisory Guidelines range.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence that fell within the appropriate guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court stated that it had reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda, as well as the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history included nearly twelve previous felony convictions, several of which involved violence or the use of firearms. The instant offense encompassed domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault committed inside the victim’s home in the presence of her children, and injuring a police officer during resistance to arrest. The district court did not exceed its broad discretion or render a sentence that was substantively unreasonable by imposing a within-guidelines term of 292 months’ imprisonment.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
