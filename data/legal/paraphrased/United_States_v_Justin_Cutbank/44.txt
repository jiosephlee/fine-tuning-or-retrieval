Case Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Jurisdiction: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Decision Date: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. The district court sentenced him in accordance with the Armed Career Criminal Act to 292 months in prison, a term falling within the advisory guidelines range established by the court. Cutbank appeals both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made various evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial, and erroneously imposed a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, thereby increasing the advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., held a firearm to her face, and forced her into a closet, while expressing paranoid suspicions regarding “listening devices” inside the residence and individuals outside intending to harm him. While holding D.F. at gunpoint within the closet for what D.F. described as hours, Cutbank threatened to kill her and hit her in the face with the gun. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake other individuals sleeping in the home, including her two teenage sons as well as her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the scene with both D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. Afterward, D.F. contacted the authorities.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, stating that a man was present in the garage and refusing to leave. Upon arrival, the police encountered Cutbank, who had barricaded himself inside the garage. Throughout several hours, Cutbank exhibited erratic behavior, made unpredictable statements, and asserted that he possessed a weapon. When officers attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted, biting one officer on the face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. During a search of the likely route between D.F.’s house and the garage, officers discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with its barrel and stock shortened. DNA recovered from the rifle matched that of Cutbank. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the same one they had observed Cutbank possessing prior to that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm and determined he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court erred in several pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) as a basis for sentencing enhancement.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple challenges regarding evidence and procedure, but offers virtually no legal authority or substantive analysis to bolster his claims. These arguments lack merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their earlier observations of him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such evidence constituted impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government's assertion that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating continuous possession of the firearm leading up to the incident. We concur with this reasoning. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” At trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested an instruction to the jury that, in order to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause complications, they were not to treat D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct until after D.F. had testified about the relevant events. The prosecution responded that these statements were necessary to clarify the circumstances surrounding the officers’ actions and what they understood at the relevant time, asserted that the officers’ testimony would not infringe upon hearsay prohibitions, and maintained that any necessary guidance from the bench could be given later and would be standard and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not obligated to give the jury an instruction on each individual piece of evidence as it is received if a proper instruction is provided when the trial concludes. Id. In this case, the record reflects that hearsay evidence was not admitted during the proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. did testify. The Confrontation Clause requirement is fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). There is, therefore, no indication of abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on a “handful of times” prior to the event at issue. When Cutback renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court denied it. Similarly, when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had seen the firearm involved “once or twice” prior to the incident, the court overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback does not reference any authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that this was intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and indicating ongoing possession of the firearm leading up to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

On cross-examination about her previous inconsistent statements concerning the firearm—including her claim that she did not recall seeing it on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her statements stemmed from her unfamiliarity with firearms and her dislike of them, noting, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court overruled Cutback’s request to exclude the testimony that D.F.’s sister had died due to a gun. This evidence was offered as an explanation in response to inquiries during cross-examination about her inconsistent prior statements. Cutback fails to identify any unfair prejudice or confusion caused by this testimony, nor is any such issue evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., testifying on behalf of the government, also referred to D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun without any objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed its wide latitude in evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s statement that she had observed the firearm “once or twice” before the incident by questioning her on prior remarks made to a police investigator during cross-examination. The district court permitted inquiries concerning what C.F. told police but upheld the prosecution’s objections to defense counsel attempting to read sections of the police report into evidence, citing a lack of foundation because C.F. had not written the report. Although Cutback did not address this court ruling in his appeal, the government's brief contended that his "next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not favor appellees who unnecessarily broaden the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, the “claim,” preserved or not, lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The court's insistence on proper authentication did not constitute an abuse of discretion.

In conclusion, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. . . . In other words, we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this burden. The government introduced ample testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s rulings regarding evidence “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury determined that Cutbank was guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further concluded that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the heightened penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) suggested setting the base offense level at 26, due to the fact that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as outlined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank had a minimum of two earlier convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also proposed a three-level increase because the firearm had its serial number altered or obliterated, a two-level enhancement for recklessly endangering lives or causing a risk of serious bodily injury during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level increase for using the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These factors combined for a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which resulted in an advisory sentencing guideline range between 292 and 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant has used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted, with no opposition, the Presentence Investigation Report’s factual assertions concerning Cutback’s past felony convictions. Therefore, the court did not commit error—let alone clear error—when it concluded that the PSR’s facts established that the firearm was connected to another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that imposing the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)—yielding a sentence within the Guidelines range—results in punishment that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)," namely that a sentence should be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection." Although the jury determined Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions, thereby invoking penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the Guideline provision for Armed Career Criminal would have set the total offense level at 34. The Guidelines state that the higher offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court is required to start the process by accurately determining the relevant Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to evaluating the substantive reasonableness of a sentence on appeal, a court of appeals must verify that the district court did not make a significant procedural error, including any failure to compute, or erroneous calculation of, the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this step, the court examines whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a departure from or variance to the advisory Guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the proper guidelines range, a circumstance we presume to be reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court referenced its consideration of the Presentence Report and the sentencing memoranda, as well as an evaluation of the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had almost twelve prior felony convictions, several related to violence and firearms. The instant offense included acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assault carried out in the presence of the victim’s children within her residence, and injury to a law enforcement officer during an attempt to evade arrest. Given these facts, the district court did not exceed its considerable discretion in sentencing, nor did it hand down a sentence that was substantively unreasonable when it imposed a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
