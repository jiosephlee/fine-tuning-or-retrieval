Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant record of felony convictions, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months of imprisonment under the Armed Career Criminal Act, placing the sentence within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and erroneously applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby increasing the advisory guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank roused his girlfriend, D.F., threatened her by pointing a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet, while making suspicious claims regarding alleged “listening devices” within the residence and individuals outside intending him harm. D.F. testified that Cutbank held her at gunpoint in the closet for what felt like several hours, during which he issued death threats and struck her face with the weapon. Subsequently, Cutbank demanded that D.F. wake the other occupants of the house, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. In due course, Cutbank escaped the scene, taking both D.F.’s cellphone and the firearm. D.F. then contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, indicating that an unidentified male was in the garage and refusing to exit. Upon arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, during which he exhibited unpredictable behavior over several hours, making unusual statements and asserting that he possessed a weapon. When officers sought to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted, including biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer's firearm. In a subsequent search along the route believed to have been taken by Cutbank from D.F.’s residence to the garage, officers located D.F.'s cellphone, a butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with both the barrel and stock sawn off. DNA analysis revealed Cutbank’s genetic material on the firearm. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as one they had previously observed in Cutbank's possession on the night in question. The jury returned a guilty verdict on the felon in possession charge, additionally finding that Cutbank had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, which invoked the sentencing enhancement provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending on appeal that the district court erred in multiple evidentiary and procedural determinations before and during the trial, and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents several arguments concerning evidence and procedure but offers little to no analysis or supporting authority. Each of these claims lacks merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, on the grounds that such testimony would constitute improper propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court determined, in accordance with the government’s position, that this testimony qualified as intrinsic evidence, pertinent to establishing the possession charge and to show Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm preceding the incident. We concur with this assessment. As articulated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. stated that she had witnessed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank sought a jury instruction aimed at preventing hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns by instructing that D.F.’s statements to law enforcement should not be regarded as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct unless D.F. herself testified about the incidents. The government contended that these statements served to provide background for the officers’ actions and their contemporaneous understanding, asserted that the officers’ testimony would comply with hearsay limitations, and maintained that, if necessary, a subsequent instruction from the judge would be both typical and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to precedent, the court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this instance, the record shows that no hearsay was allowed at trial. Moreover, D.F. took the stand. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Thus, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. When Cutback renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court overruled it. Similarly, the court also denied a defense objection when C.F., a witness for the government, recounted that she had previously seen the firearm used during the incident “once or twice.” On appeal, Cutback does not cite any authority contradicting the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and demonstrating Cutback’s ongoing possession of the firearm before the occurrence. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about inconsistencies in her earlier statements concerning the firearm—including claiming she did not recall observing the gun on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her statements were due to her unfamiliarity with firearms and her aversion to guns, since “her sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister’s death by firearm. D.F.'s comment addressed inquiries on cross-examination regarding her prior inconsistent statements. Cutback has failed to indicate any specific unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony, nor does any appear evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., who also testified for the government, referenced the death of D.F.’s sister by a gun without defense counsel raising an objection. The district court did not exceed its broad discretion in admitting this evidence. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the gun used in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her, during cross-examination, about statements she previously made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries about C.F.’s statements to police but upheld the government’s objections to the defense’s attempt to read excerpts from the police report into evidence, citing lack of adequate foundation since C.F. had not written the report herself. Although Cutback did not address this ruling in his appeal, the government’s brief maintained that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably upon appellees who broaden the scope of appellate issues without necessity. Regardless, even assuming this issue was properly raised, the claim lacks merit. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” (quotation omitted). The district court did not err in its discretion by insisting on proper authentication.

As Cutback concedes, “this court will not disturb an erroneous evidentiary ruling unless the mistake was prejudicial. . . . That is, we will only order reversal when it is possible that the jury’s verdict was materially affected by evidence improperly admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutback fails to meet this standard. The government presented ample testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “either did not affect or had only minimal impact on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “A conviction will not be set aside due to the collective effect of trial errors absent a showing of significant prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, which subjected him to the increased penalties mandated by the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26, noting that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and that Cutbank had two or more prior convictions for violent crimes. Additionally, the PSR recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm’s altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly endangering the lives or safety of others during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in furtherance of another felony, resulting in a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the resulting advisory sentencing guidelines range was calculated at 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in imposing the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This sentencing enhancement is applicable when a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with an additional federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted, without any challenge, the PSR’s factual recitation pertaining to Cutback’s previous felony convictions. Therefore, the court neither erred nor clearly erred in determining that the PSR’s facts establish the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank maintains that applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4-level increase yields a sentence within the Guidelines range that is “in conflict with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” requiring the sentence to “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury found Cutback had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies—activating penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act—the total offense level derived from the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have been 34. The Guidelines specify that the greater offense level must be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, the district court must initially and accurately determine the relevant Guidelines range, as directed by Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Furthermore, the appellate court must verify that the district court did not make a significant procedural mistake—such as neglecting to compute or miscalculating the Guidelines range—before it can assess whether the sentence is substantively reasonable. See Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Subsequently, the court evaluates whether the § 3553(a) factors justify departing from or varying the sentence imposed by the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the appropriate guidelines range, and we accordingly afford it a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court indicated it had examined the PSR and sentencing memoranda and addressed § 3553(a) considerations that might justify a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “We do not require district courts to make a formulaic recital of the considerations they weighed to sustain a sentence; what matters is that the record demonstrates the district court truly evaluated the § 3553(a) factors in selecting the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history included almost twelve previous felony convictions, several of which related to violence and firearm offenses. The current conviction involved conduct including domestic abuse, kidnapping, committing assault in the presence of the victim’s children inside her residence, and causing harm to a law enforcement officer during resistance to arrest. The district court acted within its broad sentencing authority and did not deliver a substantively unreasonable sentence by sentencing Cutback to 292 months’ imprisonment, consistent with the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
