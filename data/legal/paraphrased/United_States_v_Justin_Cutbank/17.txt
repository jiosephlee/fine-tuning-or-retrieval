Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, an individual with a significant record of prior convictions, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, with the sentence falling within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank now appeals both his conviction and sentence, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial, and that it incorrectly imposed a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, thereby increasing the advisory guidelines range. We uphold the district court’s judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her head, and compelled her to enter a closet while voicing paranoid suspicions about “listening devices” inside the residence and individuals outside plotting to harm him. Throughout the ordeal, Cutbank held D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what she described as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the gun. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake up others who were sleeping in the house, including her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the scene, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. then contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located half a mile from D.F.’s home, where it was stated that an individual was present in the garage and refusing to leave. Upon arriving at the scene, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, during which he behaved erratically for several hours, made unusual remarks, and asserted that he was armed. When officers attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted arrest, including biting an officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. Officers who examined the likely route from D.F.’s residence to the garage discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with both a shortened barrel and stock. DNA found on the rifle was determined to belong to Cutbank. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as one they had observed in Cutbank’s possession prior to the incident. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession, as charged, and further determined he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, which subjected him to the increased sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now appeals from his conviction and sentence, claiming that the district court committed various discretionary errors in its pretrial and trial rulings, as well as incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in determining his sentence enhancement.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from stating that they had seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle on earlier occasions, contending that such statements would constitute improper propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court sided with the government, determining that this evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to the possession charge, as well as relevant for showing that Cutbank continuously possessed the firearm leading up to the charged event. We concur with this reasoning. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” At trial, D.F. testified that she observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on a “handful of times” before the relevant incident.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought a jury instruction that, in order to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause violations, the jury should not treat D.F.’s police statements as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. provided her own testimony about the incidents. The government countered by contending that these statements merely served to provide background for the officers' actions and understanding at the pertinent times, that the officers’ account would not violate hearsay rules, and that, if necessary, an instruction from the bench at a later time would be both customary and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this instance, the record reveals no hearsay was admitted during the trial proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. took the stand and testified. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Therefore, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated under oath that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. Cutbank’s renewed objection to this evidence was rejected by the district court. Additionally, the court denied a defense objection when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice.” On appeal, Cutbank does not reference any case law that would challenge the district court’s earlier determination that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and to showing ongoing possession of the weapon preceding the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about her earlier inconsistent statements concerning the firearm, including her claim that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night, D.F. stated that some of her remarks were made because she was “not familiar with guns” and “did not like guns,” further explaining that this was due to her sister having been killed by a gun. The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the testimony about D.F.’s sister’s death by gunshot. This information was elicited as a response to questioning about inconsistencies in prior statements. Cutback does not identify any undue prejudice or confusion that might have arisen from this testimony, and none is evident. Refer to Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., called as a witness for the government, referenced D.F.’s sister being killed by a firearm as well, with no objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed the considerable latitude afforded it in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s statement that she had previously seen the gun used in the incident “once or twice,” by cross-examining her about prior remarks she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into C.F.’s statements to law enforcement but sustained the prosecution’s objections when defense counsel attempted to read portions of the related police report into the record without laying a proper foundation, noting that C.F. was not the author of the report. Cutback did not raise this evidentiary ruling on appeal; nevertheless, in its brief, the government asserted that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove of appellees who broaden the scope of appellate issues without necessity. Regardless, even if considered preserved, this “claim” lacks substance. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court did not exceed its discretion in insisting upon proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court does not overturn an incorrect evidentiary ruling if such an error is harmless. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only where it is possible that the jury’s verdict was significantly affected by the evidence improperly admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The prosecution presented strong testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s decisions regarding evidence “did not influence the verdict or had only minimal impact.” Id. at 998. “We do not set aside a conviction for the combined effect of multiple trial errors unless they result in substantial prejudice against the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury determined that Cutbank was guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further concluded that he had three or more previous convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the enhanced penalties mandated by the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26 on the grounds that the crime involved a sawed-off firearm as specified in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and that Cutbank possessed at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. Additionally, the PSR recommended a three-level increase because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase on the basis that Cutbank recklessly created a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense, producing an aggregate offense level of 35. The PSR determined his Criminal History Category to be VI, which yielded an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in imposing the four-level enhancement for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is applicable when a defendant either used or possessed a firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court, without objection, accepted the factual assertions in the PSR concerning Cutback’s prior felony offenses. Accordingly, the court’s determination that the PSR’s facts showed the firearm was used in connection with another felony was not erroneous, let alone clearly erroneous. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, instead, contends that imposing the four-level enhancement pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and the resultant guideline range, undermines the “core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Even though the jury determined that Cutback had three or more previous violent felony convictions and thus was subject to Armed Career Criminal Act sentencing provisions, the offense level calculated under the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have been 34. The Guidelines mandate application of the higher offense level. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court must initially ensure it accurately determines the applicable Guidelines range. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to a court of appeals reviewing a sentence’s substantive reasonableness, it must first confirm that the district court did not make a significant procedural mistake, including errors in calculating or failing to calculate the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates if the factors enumerated in § 3553(a) justify either a departure from, or a variance with, the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the court imposed a sentence that fell within the relevant guideline range, which is presumed to be reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceedings, the court referenced its consideration of the PSR, the parties’ sentencing memoranda, and specifically addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. “Accordingly, the district court was cognizant of [Cutbank’s] contentions, leading us to presume the district court both considered and declined them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “We do not mandate that a district court mechanically enumerate the factors weighed to sustain the sentence; it is sufficient if the record demonstrates the district court did, in fact, take the § 3553(a) factors into account in choosing the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record included almost twelve previous felony convictions, many of which related to violence and firearms offenses. The current conviction consisted of domestic abuse, kidnapping, assaulting the victim in her home with her children present, and causing injury to a law enforcement officer while resisting arrest. The district court did not exceed its broad discretion in sentencing nor did it impose a substantively unreasonable sentence by ordering a 292-month imprisonment term within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
