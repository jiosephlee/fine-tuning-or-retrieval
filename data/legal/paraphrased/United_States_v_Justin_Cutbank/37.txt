Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-1225  
Author: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant history of felony convictions, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison, a term that fell within the calculated advisory guidelines range. Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, claiming that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that infringed upon his right to a fair trial, and also wrongly imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), resulting in a higher guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a firearm at her head, and compelled her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions about the presence of “listening devices” inside the residence and individuals outside intending to cause him harm. He detained D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for a period that, according to D.F.’s testimony, seemed to last for several hours. During this time, he threatened to kill her and struck her across the face with the firearm. At one point, Cutbank demanded that D.F. wake up other people sleeping in the house, which included her two teenage sons as well as her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the gun with him. Afterward, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located half a mile away from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was inside the garage and refused to leave the premises. Upon police arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself within the garage and, over a period of several hours, made unpredictable statements and asserted that he possessed a weapon. When officers attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting an officer’s face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. During a search of the route that Cutbank was believed to have traveled from D.F.’s house to the garage, officers recovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had a sawed-off barrel and stock. DNA evidence on the firearm was identified as belonging to Cutbank. At trial, D.F. and C.F. recognized and identified the firearm as one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of being a felon in possession and further found that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced penalty provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court committed an abuse of discretion with respect to various pretrial and trial rulings and erroneously applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments regarding evidentiary and procedural matters, but offers virtually no analysis and cites no authority in support. These assertions are without merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from offering testimony that they had previously seen him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute propensity evidence prohibited under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government's position that this testimony was intrinsic to the charge, as it was pertinent to establishing the possession element and showed ongoing possession of the firearm leading up to the charged incident. We concur with this conclusion. As explained in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank sought an instruction to the jury to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause complications by advising that D.F.’s statements to police should not be considered substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. had provided her own account of the events on the witness stand. The prosecution contended that these statements were necessary to explain the context behind police officers’ actions and their contemporaneous knowledge, that the officers’ recounting would not violate hearsay rules, and that, should it be required, a subsequent instruction by the court would suffice and follow standard practice. As articulated in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), the standard for review of a district court’s refusal to give a limiting instruction is abuse of discretion. According to Bennett, it is not obligatory for the court to immediately instruct the jury on each piece of admitted evidence, so long as a suitable directive is given at the close of trial. The present record reveals that the court did not allow any hearsay evidence during trial. Furthermore, D.F. appeared and testified. The Confrontation Clause’s requirements are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Consequently, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Furthermore, the court dismissed a defense objection when C.F., serving as a witness for the prosecution, recounted seeing the firearm in question “once or twice” before the incident. On appeal, Cutback fails to present any authority that undermines the district court’s earlier ruling that deemed this testimony intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and indicating ongoing possession of the firearm before the incident occurred. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about prior inconsistent statements related to the gun—specifically her claim of not recalling having seen the gun on the relevant evening—D.F. explained that some of her earlier statements arose because she was “not familiar with guns” and “did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The trial court denied Cutback’s request to strike the portion of testimony referencing D.F.’s sister’s death by gun. This evidence was elicited in response to cross-examination probing inconsistent earlier statements. Cutback has failed to demonstrate, and none is evident, that this testimony caused unfair prejudice or confusion. See Fed. R. Evid. 403. Additionally, C.F., another government witness, also referred to the fact that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun, and defense counsel raised no objection. The district court did not exceed its substantial discretion in ruling on the evidentiary matter. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense aimed to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had observed the gun used in the incident “once or twice” before, by questioning her about previous statements given to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into what C.F. told the police, but upheld the government’s objections to defense counsel attempting to read selected parts of the related police report into evidence without establishing proper foundation, as the report was not written by C.F. Although Cutback did not address this particular ruling on appeal, the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably on appellees who expand appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless, whether this argument is properly before us or not, the “claim” lacks merit. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court acted within its discretion in insisting on appropriate authentication.

Lastly, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not reverse a mistaken evidentiary ruling when the error is harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only where there is a reasonable probability that the jury’s decision was significantly affected by the erroneously admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank does not meet this standard. The government presented ample testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s decisions regarding evidence admission “did not influence or had only minimal impact on the outcome.” Id. at 998. Furthermore, “We will not set aside a conviction due to the cumulative effect of trial errors unless the defendant suffers substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury determined that Cutbank was guilty of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. The jury further concluded that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the heightened penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act. According to the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), a base offense level of 26 was proposed, based on the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as defined under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank’s history of at least two previous crimes of violence. The PSR also recommended a three-level enhancement for possessing a firearm with an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly creating a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury during flight from law enforcement, and an additional four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in furtherance of another felony offense. These adjustments resulted in a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which produced an advisory sentencing guideline range of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank asserts that the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered if the defendant either used or had possession of any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony offense—federal, state, or local—“regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At sentencing, the district court accepted as true, and without opposition, the PSR’s factual assertions pertaining to Cutback’s previous felony convictions. Therefore, the court did not make an error—let alone a clear error—when it determined, based on the facts set forth in the PSR, that the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (discussing the standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, however, contends that imposing the 4-level enhancement pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produces a sentence within the guideline range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” which requires a sentence to be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury found Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions, thus making him subject to penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the corresponding Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded a total offense level of 34. Pursuant to the Guidelines, the higher offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When deciding on a suitable sentence, “a district court must first accurately determine the correct Guidelines range at the outset of any sentencing hearing.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Further, “an appellate court reviewing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence must preliminarily verify that the district court did not commit any material procedural mistake, such as neglecting to compute, or miscomputing, the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates whether the factors set forth in § 3553(a) justify either departing from or varying the sentence relative to the advisory guideline range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the relevant guidelines range, which creates a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court indicated it had considered the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) as well as the sentencing briefs, and had evaluated the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a departure. As held in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “[t]hus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” Furthermore, “[t]o uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history included almost twelve previous felony convictions, many of which involved violent acts and firearms offenses. The conduct resulting in the current conviction comprised domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault occurring within the victim’s home in front of her children, and causing injury to a law enforcement officer while attempting to evade arrest. The district court, therefore, acted within its broad discretion at sentencing and did not deliver a substantively unreasonable punishment by imposing a 292-month sentence within the guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
