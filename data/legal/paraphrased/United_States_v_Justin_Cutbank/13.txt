Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, aligning the sentence within the advisory guideline range. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and erred in applying a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which raised the advisory guideline range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet, while expressing paranoid concerns about “listening devices” within the residence and alleging individuals were outside intent on harming him. He held D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for a duration which, according to her testimony, seemed to last hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the firearm. At one point, Cutbank commanded D.F. to wake up the other occupants of the house, specifically her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the premises with both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. Afterward, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

The police were notified by a call from a residence located half a mile from D.F.'s home, where the caller reported that a man was present in the garage and refused to leave. Upon law enforcement’s arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage and, over several hours, exhibited erratic behavior and asserted that he was armed. When officers attempted to detain him, Cutbank resisted, biting one officer’s face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. Officers who searched the probable route that Cutbank would have traveled between D.F.’s house and the garage discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both a sawed-off barrel and stock. Cutbank’s DNA was detected on the rifle. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession earlier that night. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm and determined that he had been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies, thus subjecting him to enhanced sentencing under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court abused its discretion with several pretrial and trial rulings, and erroneously applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in imposing the sentencing enhancement.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to the trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute inadmissible propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the prosecution's position that the testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession offense and showing that Cutbank had continuous possession of the firearm leading up to the relevant incident. We concur with this reasoning. As held in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." During trial, D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be instructed that it could not treat D.F.’s statements to the police as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. herself took the stand to testify about the underlying events, citing concerns regarding hearsay and the Confrontation Clause. In response, the government contended that the statements in question were admissible to explain the circumstances surrounding police actions and the officers’ contemporaneous understanding, that their testimony would conform to hearsay rules, and that a limiting instruction delivered later—should it become necessary—would be both standard practice and sufficient. As stated in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), “[w]e review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” According to the same authority, a court is not obligated to provide a jury instruction on every piece of evidence as it is introduced, so long as an adequate instruction is delivered at the close of the trial. In the present case, the record establishes that hearsay evidence was not admitted during the trial. Moreover, D.F. provided testimony. The requirement of the Confrontation Clause is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). The court therefore did not abuse its discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to the admissibility of this testimony. Similarly, when C.F., appearing as a government witness, testified to having previously seen the firearm used in the incident "once or twice," the court overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback does not provide any legal authority challenging the district court’s pretrial conclusion that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating ongoing possession of the firearm before the incident occurred. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about her earlier inconsistent statements related to the firearm—including her claim that she could not recall observing the weapon on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her responses were attributable to her unfamiliarity with firearms and her aversion to them, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The district court declined Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister’s death by firearm. D.F.’s statements served as a direct reply to cross-examination probing her previous inconsistencies. Cutback does not identify any particular unfair prejudice or potential for juror confusion stemming from this testimony, and the record reveals none. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., a government witness, also referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun, and defense counsel did not object. Consequently, there is no indication that the district court exceeded its considerable discretion in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense aimed to challenge C.F.’s account that she had previously observed the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” by cross-examining her regarding earlier statements she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into what C.F. told law enforcement, but upheld the prosecution’s objections when defense counsel attempted to read excerpts from the police report into evidence without establishing an appropriate foundation, since C.F. had not authored the report. While Cutback’s appeal did not expressly address this ruling, the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disfavor appellees who needlessly broaden the scope of issues on appeal. Regardless, whether this argument was preserved or not, the “claim” does not succeed. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court did not exceed its discretion in requiring authentication.

Finally, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. . . . In other words, we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank does not meet this burden. The government introduced ample testimonial and physical evidence. Accordingly, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that he had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies, which subjected him to increased penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26, citing that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and that Cutbank had at least two earlier convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also recommended a three-level enhancement because the firearm’s serial number was altered or obliterated, a two-level increase for recklessly causing a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury while attempting to flee from law enforcement, and an additional four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in relation to another felony offense, resulting in a combined offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, producing an advisory sentencing guidelines range between 292 and 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred when it imposed the four-level enhancement for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is applicable when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the PSR’s recitation of facts about Cutback’s previous felony convictions without any objection from the parties. Therefore, the court did not err—let alone commit clear error—when it determined that the facts in the PSR showed the firearm was linked to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) four-level enhancement leads to a sentence within the advisory range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” requiring a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury concluded that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thus invoking the penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the Guideline for Armed Career Criminals would result in a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level controls. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, the district court must initially and correctly compute the relevant Guidelines range. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Following this, the court assesses whether application of the § 3553(a) factors justifies any departure from or variance to the advisory Guidelines sentence.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence falling within the proper guideline range, which creates a presumption of reasonableness for purposes of appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). At the sentencing hearing, the court indicated that it had examined the PSR, the sentencing memoranda, and reviewed the § 3553(a) factors potentially supporting a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “In order to affirm the sentence, a district court is not required to deliver a rote listing of the factors it considered; rather, the record must indicate that the § 3553(a) factors were actually weighed by the court in reaching its sentencing decision.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history included almost twelve previous felony convictions, several of which involved violent conduct and firearms. The instant conviction entailed acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assaults committed within the victim’s residence and witnessed by her children, and infliction of injury on a police officer during efforts to evade arrest. The district court exercised its broad sentencing discretion appropriately and did not deliver a sentence that was substantively unreasonable when it imposed a term of imprisonment of 292 months within the guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
