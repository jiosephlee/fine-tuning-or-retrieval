Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant prior criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court imposed a sentence of 292 months’ imprisonment, falling within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and his sentence, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural errors that denied him a fair trial, and that the court erroneously applied a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement, which raised the advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following alcohol consumption and methamphetamine use, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., held a firearm to her face, and forced her into a closet while making paranoid claims concerning “listening devices” in the residence and alleging people outside intended to harm him. While in the closet, D.F. testified that Cutbank held her at gunpoint for what seemed to be several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her across the face with the weapon. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake others in the home, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also responded to a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, where the caller stated that a man was present in their garage and refused to depart. Upon arrival, the police found Cutbank, who had barricaded himself inside the garage; throughout several hours, he made unpredictable statements and asserted possession of a weapon. When officers tried to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting one officer’s face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. In a search of the likely route between D.F.’s house and the aforementioned garage, officers discovered D.F.’s cellphone, a butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. The rifle bore Cutbank’s DNA. During the trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had seen in Cutbank’s possession prior to that night. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged felon-in-possession violation and determined he had been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies, invoking the enhanced penalties under § 924(e). He now challenges both his conviction and his sentence, contending the district court committed an abuse of discretion through several pretrial and trial rulings, as well as erred in applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple evidentiary and procedural objections, but offers little analysis and cites no supporting authority for his claims. Each of these arguments lacks merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government's position that this testimony qualified as intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showing Cutbank's continued possession of the firearm leading up to the event in question. We concur with this analysis. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), "[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the charged incident.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank sought an instruction for the jury, intended to preclude hearsay and address Confrontation Clause concerns, specifying that D.F.’s statements to law enforcement could not be treated as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself provided testimony about the matters in question. The prosecution responded that such statements would merely supply background for the officers’ conduct and their understanding at relevant times, that the officers’ testimony would comply with hearsay rules, and that any necessary limiting instruction from the court at a later stage would be standard practice and sufficient. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not required to instruct the jury about each particular piece of evidence at the point of its admission, as long as it delivers an adequate instruction at the end of the trial. Id. The current record reveals that the trial court did not allow any hearsay evidence. Furthermore, D.F. did testify in court. The Confrontation Clause requirement is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). No abuse of discretion is evident here.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question. The district court overruled Cutbank’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, the court rejected a defense objection when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” before. On appeal, Cutbank fails to reference any authority disputing the district court’s pretrial determination that this evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as illustrating ongoing possession of the weapon before the alleged event. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier inconsistent statements related to the firearm—including her inability to recall seeing the firearm on the relevant night—D.F. stated that certain remarks were made because she lacked familiarity with firearms and disliked them, as her “sister was killed by a gun.” The district court overruled Cutback’s request to strike the testimony concerning the death of D.F.’s sister by firearm. D.F.’s comments were made in response to the cross-examination about inconsistencies in her previous statements. Cutback fails to identify any resulting undue prejudice or confusion from this testimony, nor is any evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Further, C.F., a government witness, also referenced the death of D.F.’s sister by firearm without any objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed its wide discretion in matters of evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to cast doubt on C.F.'s statement that she had previously seen the firearm used during the event “once or twice,” by cross-examining her about prior comments she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries about C.F.’s statements to law enforcement, but upheld the government’s objections when the defense tried to read selected portions of the police report into evidence without adequate foundation, given that the report was not prepared by C.F. Although Cutback did not address this ruling in his appeal, the government maintained in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably on appellees who broaden the issues before us on appeal without necessity. Regardless, whether the issue was properly preserved or not, the “claim” has no merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court properly exercised its discretion by insisting on appropriate authentication.

Finally, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not overturn a mistaken evidentiary decision if the mistake was harmless. . . . In essence, reversal is appropriate only where the jury could have been significantly affected by the erroneously admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to satisfy this standard. The government presented strong testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the evidentiary rulings made by the district court “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not reverse a conviction on account of the collective impact of trial errors unless the defendant has suffered substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that he had three or more previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby invoking the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) advised a base offense level of 26, citing that the case involved a sawed-off firearm as specified in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and that Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR further recommended a three-level enhancement for possessing a firearm with an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily harm while evading law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possession of a firearm in relation to the commission of another felony offense. This resulted in a total offense level of 35. According to the PSR, his Criminal History Category was calculated at VI, producing an advisory guidelines range of imprisonment between 292 and 365 months. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant has used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing phase, the district court accepted, without any opposition, the PSR’s recitation of facts pertaining to Cutback’s previous felony convictions. Accordingly, the court did not err—let alone clearly err—in determining that the PSR’s facts demonstrate that the firearm was used in conjunction with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank asserts that imposing the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)—which yields a sentence within the advisory range—produces a sentence that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” specifically that a sentence be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Even though the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby invoking penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, application of the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have set the total offense level at 34. Pursuant to the Guidelines, the higher offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, "a district court must first accurately determine the relevant Guidelines range at the outset of any sentencing proceeding." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). As explained in Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018), "an appellate court cannot assess the substantive reasonableness of a sentence until it verifies that the district court avoided any significant procedural mistakes, including neglecting to compute, or incorrectly computing, the Guidelines range." Following this, the court evaluates whether the considerations outlined in § 3553(a) justify either departing from or varying the advisory guidelines sentencing range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the relevant guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable on appellate review. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing, the court indicated it had examined the PSR and sentencing briefs and addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. As stated, “the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “We do not require the district court to go through a rote listing of the factors it evaluated to affirm a sentence; rather, the record must make it apparent that the court in fact assessed the § 3553(a) factors in fashioning the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal record included nearly twelve prior felonies, several involving violent conduct and the use of firearms. The crime at issue entailed acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assault in the victim's residence while her children were present, and injuring a law enforcement officer during resistance to arrest. Given these circumstances, the district court did not exceed its broad discretion or impose a sentence that was substantively unreasonable by ordering a 292-month prison term within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
