Case: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Jurisdiction: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Decided: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank—who has a significant prior criminal record—guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) for being a felon in possession of a firearm. The district court imposed a sentence of 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, which fell within the calculated advisory guidelines range. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that resulted in an unfair trial, and incorrectly applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby raising the advisory guidelines range. We uphold the district court's judgment.

I. Background

On the night between November 19 and 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., at gunpoint, threatened her by aiming the firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while voicing paranoid suspicions about “listening devices” inside their residence and alleged threats from individuals outside intending to harm him. He held D.F. inside the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described as feeling like several hours, issuing threats to her life and striking her in the face with the gun. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake other occupants of the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank escaped with both D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. Afterward, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Police were also notified by a resident living approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home that a man was present in their garage and refusing to leave. Upon their arrival, officers encountered Cutbank, who barricaded himself in the garage and, over several hours, exhibited unstable behavior and asserted that he was armed. Cutbank resisted arrest, biting an officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. During a search along the likely route Cutbank would have traveled from D.F.’s house to the garage, police recovered D.F.’s mobile phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle modified with a sawed-off barrel and stock. DNA evidence taken from the firearm corresponded to Cutbank. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. recognized the weapon as the same one they had previously seen with Cutbank earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of being a felon in possession, and determined he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, making him subject to the enhanced sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court abused its discretion through various pretrial and trial rulings and erred in applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments regarding evidence and procedure, but fails to cite relevant case law or offer meaningful analysis to substantiate his claims. All of these arguments lack merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted improper propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government’s position, determining that this testimony would serve as intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating ongoing possession of the firearm leading up to the charged incident. We reach the same conclusion. “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015). During trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the events in question.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank asked that the jury be specifically instructed that, in order to prevent any hearsay or Confrontation Clause complications, they could not use D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. had personally recounted the events at trial. The prosecution countered that these statements were intended to demonstrate the background for the officers’ actions and awareness at that time, contending that the officers’ testimony would remain within the bounds of the hearsay prohibition and that, should an instruction later become necessary, the court’s standard practice of delivering such instructions from the bench would be adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to that authority, the court is not obligated to issue a limiting instruction regarding individual pieces of evidence at the point of admission provided that a suitable instruction is given at the end of the trial. Id. In this matter, the record reflects that hearsay evidence was not admitted at trial. Additionally, D.F. took the stand and testified. The Confrontation Clause requirements are fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). No abuse of discretion occurred.

C

During trial, D.F. stated she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a “handful of times” prior to the event in question. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this testimony. Additionally, when C.F. testified for the government that she had seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” before, the court overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback does not point to any authority challenging the district court’s pretrial decision that this evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating that Cutback had ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

On cross-examination, when questioned about previous inconsistent statements regarding the firearm—specifically her claim that she did not recall observing the gun on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her statements were made because she was "not familiar with guns" and "did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun." The district court denied Cutback’s request to strike D.F.'s testimony about her sister’s death. This testimony addressed issues raised during cross-examination about inconsistencies in D.F.'s earlier statements. Cutback does not identify, nor is there any indication of, unfair prejudice or juror confusion attributable to this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, C.F., called as a witness by the government, also referenced the fact that D.F.'s sister had been killed by a firearm, and the defense did not object. The district court did not exceed the considerable discretion it holds regarding evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s account that she had previously seen the firearm involved “once or twice” by cross-examining her regarding statements she had made earlier to a police investigator. The district court permitted questioning about what C.F. conveyed to law enforcement but upheld the government's objections when defense counsel sought to read excerpts from the ensuing police report into evidence without laying a proper foundation, as C.F. was not the author of that report. On appeal, Cutback did not specifically contest this ruling, but the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disfavor appellees who needlessly broaden the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, this “claim”—whether or not it has been preserved—is meritless. As established, “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court did not abuse its discretion in insisting on the requisite authentication.

Ultimately, as Cutbank concedes, “this court does not reverse an evidentiary error unless the mistake was not harmless. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only if the jury’s verdict could have been significantly affected by the erroneously admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to satisfy this standard. The government presented a robust case with both witness testimony and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s rulings regarding evidence “did not affect or only minimally affected the outcome.” Id. at 998. Furthermore, “we do not vacate a conviction based on the total effect of several trial errors unless the defendant experiences significant prejudice as a result.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thus subjecting him to the heightened penalties prescribed by the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) advised that the base offense level should be set at 26 due to the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as outlined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and because Cutbank had at least two previous convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further recommended enhancements: a three-level increase based on the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly endangering life or causing serious bodily injury during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level increase for possession of the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These recommendations resulted in a total offense level of 35. The report assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which yielded an advisory sentencing range of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment according to the guidelines. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in imposing the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

The enhancement in question is applicable when a defendant uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense, whether federal, state, or local, and “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing stage, the district court accepted the Presentence Investigation Report’s (PSR’s) factual descriptions concerning Cutback’s previous felony convictions, with no objections made. Accordingly, the court did not commit any error—let alone clear error—in determining that the information in the PSR showed the firearm was linked to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (setting forth the standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank’s challenge is that imposing the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4-level increase creates a guidelines sentence range that is inconsistent with the main requirement of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)—namely, that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had committed three or more prior violent felonies, which mandated penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the Guideline for Armed Career Criminals would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. The Sentencing Guidelines dictate that the higher offense level should apply. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court must initially “correctly calculat[e] the applicable Guidelines range” at the outset of any sentencing hearing. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). As established in Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018), an appellate court is required to first confirm the absence of “significant procedural error” by the district court—such as not computing, or incorrectly computing, the Guidelines range—prior to reviewing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence. Following this, the court evaluates if any of the factors outlined in § 3553(a) justify imposing a sentence above or below the advisory Guidelines range.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence that fell within the properly calculated guidelines range, which is presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceeding, the court referenced its examination of the PSR as well as the sentencing memoranda and discussed § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). The law does not require the district court to specifically list each factor considered, only that the record establishes the court actually took the § 3553(a) factors into account in imposing the sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had a criminal history with close to twelve previous felony convictions, including several offenses involving violence and use of firearms. The instant conviction included domestic abuse, kidnapping, an assault that occurred in front of the victim’s children within her residence, and causing injury to a law enforcement officer during the arrest. The district court acted within its broad discretion and did not impose a substantively unreasonable sentence by ordering a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
