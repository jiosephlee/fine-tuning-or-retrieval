Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term that falls within the advisory guidelines range as calculated by the court. On appeal, he challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and erroneously applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby increasing his advisory guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following the consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while making paranoid claims about “listening devices” within the residence and asserting that individuals outside intended to harm him. He detained D.F. inside the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. later described in testimony as feeling like hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her face with the firearm. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to awaken other people in the home, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the scene, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and refused to leave. Upon their arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage and, during several hours, made incoherent remarks and asserted that he was armed. When officers attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted arrest by biting an officer’s face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. Officers conducting a search along the route Cutbank was likely to have traveled between D.F.’s residence and the garage discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a shortened barrel and stock. DNA recovered from the firearm was consistent with Cutbank’s profile. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. recognized the firearm as one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession prior to that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of being a felon in possession and determined he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby invoking the heightened penalty provisions of § 924(e). Cutbank now contests both his conviction and sentence, asserting on appeal that the district court erred in various pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in imposing a sentence enhancement.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government's position that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence relevant to the possession charge and served to show that Cutbank maintained continuous possession of the firearm leading up to the event in question. We concur with this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank sought a jury instruction clarifying that, to address hearsay concerns and comply with the Confrontation Clause, the jury should refrain from treating D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself provided testimony about those events. In response, the government maintained that the statements were offered to establish the basis for the officers’ conduct and state of knowledge at relevant times, asserted that the officers’ testimony would remain within the bounds of hearsay rules, and contended that, if a limiting instruction were necessary, providing one from the bench at a later point would be appropriate and customary. As set forth in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), “[w]e review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” The precedent holds that a district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this instance, the record reflects that hearsay was not admitted during the proceedings. Moreover, D.F. took the stand and testified. The Confrontation Clause is deemed satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Accordingly, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection regarding this testimony. Additionally, the court overruled the defense’s objection when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” before. On appeal, Cutback does not reference any authority that challenges the court’s earlier determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements concerning the gun—including stating that she did not recall seeing the gun on the night in question—D.F. explained that certain statements stemmed from her lack of familiarity with firearms and her aversion to them, noting that her “sister was killed by a gun.” The district court denied Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister’s death by gun. This testimony was elicited in response to questioning about her inconsistent statements. Cutback fails to demonstrate any resulting unfair prejudice or confusion, and none is evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., who testified for the government, also referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun without any objection from the defense. The district court’s substantial discretion in evidentiary rulings was not abused in this instance. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to cast doubt on C.F.’s statement that she had previously observed the gun involved in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her during cross-examination about earlier remarks she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries about C.F.’s communications with police, but sustained the government’s objections to defense counsel’s attempt to read from sections of the police report into evidence without laying the necessary foundation, as C.F. was not the report’s author. Although Cutback did not mention this ruling in his appeal, the government responded in its brief by stating that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove when appellees broaden the appellate issues without necessity. Regardless, preserved or not, this “claim” lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The court did not act outside its discretion in insisting upon proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not overturn an evidentiary ruling unless the error affected the outcome. . . . That is, we reverse only where the improperly admitted evidence could have had a significant impact on the jury’s decision.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank does not meet this standard. The government presented strong testimonial and physical evidence. Accordingly, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “either had no effect or only a minimal effect on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We decline to reverse a conviction on account of the cumulative impact of trial errors unless the defendant suffered substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had three or more previous violent felony convictions, which subjected him to enhanced sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) advised a base offense level of 26, given that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as specified in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and that Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase because the firearm's serial number was altered or removed, a two-level increase for recklessly endangering lives or causing serious bodily injury while evading law enforcement, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense, resulting in a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which produced an advisory guideline imprisonment range of 292 to 365 months. On appeal, Cutbank challenges the district court's application of the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant has used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted, without opposition, the PSR’s factual recitation concerning Cutback’s previous felony convictions. Consequently, the district court did not err, let alone commit clear error, in determining that the facts in the PSR demonstrate that the firearm was used in connection with another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank’s argument instead focuses on his contention that applying the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) yields a sentence within the Guideline range that is inconsistent with the fundamental requirement of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury concluded that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thus making him subject to the Armed Career Criminal Act, application of the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level controls. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court must first properly compute the relevant Guidelines range, as required by Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Before an appellate court may review a sentence for substantive reasonableness, it must verify that the district court did not commit a significant procedural error, such as not calculating or miscalculating the Guidelines range, as stated in Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this, the court evaluates whether the § 3553(a) factors justify departing from or varying the sentence from the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the applicable guidelines range, which we treat as presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court acknowledged that it had considered the PSR, the parties’ sentencing memoranda, and the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. “Accordingly, the district court was cognizant of [Cutbank’s] contentions, and we therefore presume it took those arguments into account and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “We do not require the district court to recite each factor it considered in a formulaic manner in order to affirm the sentence; what is required is that the record reflects the court’s actual consideration of the § 3553(a) factors when determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had almost twelve prior felony convictions, several of which involved violence and firearms. The present offense involved domestic abuse, kidnapping, assaulting the victim in her residence with her children present, and inflicting injury on a police officer while resisting arrest. The district court did not exceed its broad discretion in sentencing and did not impose a substantively unreasonable sentence by sentencing Cutback to 292 months of imprisonment, which was within the guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
