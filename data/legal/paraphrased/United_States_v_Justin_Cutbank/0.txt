LOKEN, Circuit Judge.

A jury found Justin Cutbank—an individual with a significant criminal background—guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court imposed a prison sentence of 292 months, a term that fell within the range recommended by the advisory guidelines. On appeal, Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and erroneously applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby increasing the advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19-20, 2020, following the consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank roused his girlfriend, D.F., held a firearm to her face, and compelled her into a closet, expressing paranoid fears regarding “listening devices” within the residence and individuals outside poised to harm him. While holding D.F. in the closet at gunpoint—an ordeal that, according to D.F.’s testimony, lasted for hours—he issued threats against her life and struck her face with the gun. At one stage, Cutbank demanded that D.F. awaken others present in the household, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Cutbank ultimately absconded, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. Following these events, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, indicating that a male individual was present in the garage and refused to vacate the premises. Upon the officers' arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside and proceeded to issue unpredictable statements over several hours, also alleging that he was armed. When officers attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. A search of the route that Cutbank likely traversed from D.F.'s residence to the garage resulted in the discovery of D.F.’s mobile phone, a butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a shortened barrel and stock. Analysis revealed that Cutbank's DNA was present on the firearm. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. testified that the weapon matched one they had observed Cutbank possessing prior to the incident that evening. The jury subsequently found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge as well as being previously convicted of at least three violent felonies, which invoked the enhanced sentencing provisions under Minnesota law and § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court erred in a number of pretrial and trial determinations, and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A. Before trial, Cutbank filed a motion to prevent D.F. and C.F. from testifying

that they had previously seen him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such evidence would serve as impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court determined, aligning with the government’s position, that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating Cutbank’s continuous control over the firearm prior to the incident in question. We concur. “Evidence of prior possession of a firearm bears directly on later possession of that identical weapon, as it supports inferences regarding ownership or dominion over the firearm.” United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015). During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of occasions” preceding the incident.

B. Before trial, Cutbank also requested that the jury be instructed, to avoid

Due to concerns regarding hearsay and the requirements of the Confrontation Clause, the court determined that D.F.’s statements to the police could not serve as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct unless D.F. herself testified about the relevant events. The prosecution maintained that these statements were admissible for the limited purpose of illustrating the circumstances and understanding of the officers during the investigation, asserting that the officers’ recounting would not violate hearsay principles, and suggesting that, if necessary, a subsequent judicial instruction to the jury would be both routine and adequate. Appellate review of a district court's refusal to issue a limiting instruction is governed by an abuse of discretion standard. United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to Bennett, trial courts are not obligated to deliver an immediate instruction concerning every evidentiary item upon admission, provided a suitable instruction is given at the conclusion of the proceedings. In the present matter, the trial record reveals that no hearsay was improperly admitted. Moreover, D.F. provided in-court testimony. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met when a hearsay declarant appears and testifies in person. United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Thus, the court did not abuse its discretion.

C. At trial, D.F. testified she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a

Prior to the incident, the weapon had reportedly been seen “a handful of times.” The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection regarding this testimony. Similarly, when C.F. took the stand as a government witness and stated she had observed the firearm “once or twice” before the event, the court overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback does not provide any legal authority contesting the district court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge and was probative of his ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D. During cross examination regarding prior inconsistent statements regarding

D.F. testified that she could not recall seeing the gun on the night in question, and explained that some of her previous statements stemmed from her lack of familiarity with firearms and her aversion to them, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to exclude the testimony about D.F.’s sister’s death by firearm. This testimony was elicited in response to cross-examination focused on prior inconsistent statements. Cutback does not identify any resulting unfair prejudice or risk of confusion, nor is any apparent in the record. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., testifying for the prosecution, referenced the death of D.F.’s sister by gun without objection from the defense. Accordingly, the district court did not exceed its wide latitude in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E. The defense sought to undermine C.F.’s testimony that she had seen the gun

During cross-examination, defense counsel attempted to challenge C.F.'s claim that she had only encountered the item “once or twice” during the incident by questioning her about statements previously made to a police investigator. While the district court permitted inquiry into C.F.’s remarks to law enforcement, it upheld the government’s objections to defense counsel’s attempts to introduce segments of the police report into evidence, citing lack of proper foundation since C.F. was not its author. Although Cutback did not directly address this evidentiary ruling on appeal, the government asserted in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove of appellees who broaden appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless of whether this particular argument was preserved, it does not have substantive merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion in insisting on authentication of the evidence.

As recognized by Cutback, “this court will not overturn an incorrect evidentiary decision if the mistake was harmless. Put differently, reversal is warranted only if the jury’s decision could have been materially affected by evidence that was wrongly admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The prosecution presented robust testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the trial court’s evidentiary decisions “either did not affect or had only a minimal effect on the outcome.” Id. at 998. “We decline to vacate a conviction on the basis of the cumulative impact of trial errors unless the defendant suffered significant prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the charge of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended setting the base offense level at 26 on the grounds that the crime involved a sawed-off firearm as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and that Cutbank had at least two prior violent crime convictions. The report further recommended a three-level increase because the firearm's serial number was altered or obliterated, a two-level increase due to reckless endangerment during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the weapon in relation to another felony, producing a combined offense level of 35.\textsuperscript{2} The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, resulting in a recommended guidelines range of imprisonment from 292 to 365 months. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in imposing the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is applicable when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in connection with a separate federal, state, or local felony, regardless of whether formal charges have been filed or a conviction has been secured. See § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court adopted the Presentence Report’s factual account of Cutback’s earlier felony offenses without any challenge. Accordingly, the court did not commit any error—let alone a clear one—by determining that the Presentence Report established that the firearm was involved in another felony offense. Refer to United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that applying the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) yields a sentence within the guidelines range that is inconsistent with the fundamental principle in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), which requires that the punishment “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Furthermore, in cases involving qualifying convictions that trigger Armed Career Criminal Act sanctions, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would assign a total offense level of 34, with the Guidelines specifying application of the higher offense level. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court is required to initiate sentencing proceedings by accurately determining the relevant Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to evaluating whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, an appellate court must confirm that no major procedural mistakes occurred in the district court, including any error in calculating the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Subsequently, the court assesses if a departure or variance from the advisory guidelines is justified in light of the § 3553(a) factors.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the prescribed guidelines range, and such sentences are typically presumed reasonable on appellate review. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court referenced its consideration of the PSR, the parties’ sentencing submissions, and the relevant § 3553(a) factors that might justify a departure or variance. “Accordingly, the district court was apprised of [Cutbank’s] contentions, and we infer that the court considered and declined to adopt them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “A district court is not obliged to meticulously enumerate each factor it assessed to validate a sentence; the record need only make it apparent that the § 3553(a) factors were meaningfully evaluated in the sentencing decision.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record included almost twelve previous felony convictions, with several involving violence and firearms offenses. The present conviction comprised acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assault committed in the victim’s residence before her children, and causing injury to a law enforcement officer during his resistance to arrest. In imposing a 292-month prison sentence that remained within the guidelines range, the district court did not exceed its considerable discretion or render a substantively unreasonable judgment.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
