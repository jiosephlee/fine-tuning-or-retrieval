Case Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date of Decision: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal background, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act to 292 months in prison, a term that falls within the advisory guidelines range it calculated. On appeal, Cutbank challenges both his conviction and his sentence, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and erroneously imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby raising the advisory guidelines range. We uphold the district court’s judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions regarding "listening devices" in the residence and alleging that individuals outside intended him harm. He detained D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what she described in her testimony as feeling like several hours, during which he issued threats against her life and struck her face with the gun. Subsequently, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake the other occupants of the home, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the scene, taking with him D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. then contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, stating that a male individual was present in the garage and was refusing to leave the premises. Upon arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, where he proceeded, over several hours, to make incoherent remarks and assert that he possessed a weapon. During attempts by officers to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted, biting an officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. A search conducted along the route Cutbank was believed to have taken between D.F.'s residence and the garage yielded D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle featuring a shortened barrel and stock. Forensic analysis revealed DNA evidence on the firearm consistent with Cutbank. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the rifle as one they had seen Cutbank carrying earlier that evening. The jury subsequently found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of felon in possession of a firearm and further determined that he had previously been convicted of at least three violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court erred in various discretionary decisions made both before and during trial, and improperly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in enhancing his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents several arguments concerning evidentiary and procedural matters, yet offers virtually no legal authority or substantive analysis in their support. All of these arguments are unpersuasive.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their previous observations of him possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle. He contended that such testimony would constitute inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government's position, finding that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence that was pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as demonstrating Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm leading up to the relevant incident. We concur with that determination. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of times” prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank asked that the jury be specifically instructed not to treat D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. herself provided testimony about the events, aiming to prevent hearsay problems and Confrontation Clause violations. The government responded that these statements merely offered background for the officers’ actions and their understanding, that the officers’ testimony would comply with hearsay rules, and that, if required, a subsequent instruction from the court would be standard and sufficient. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not obligated “to instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this matter, the record shows that hearsay evidence was not introduced during trial. Further, D.F. testified in court. The Confrontation Clause requirements are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Therefore, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that prior to the incident, she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, asserted that she had previously seen the gun used during the incident on one or two occasions, the court again overruled a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback does not reference any legal authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as continuous possession of the firearm prior to the event. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

When questioned on cross-examination about previous inconsistent statements she had made about the firearm, including claims that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night, D.F. explained that some of her statements resulted from her lack of familiarity with firearms and a dislike of guns due to her sister having been killed by one. The court denied Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s reference to her sister’s death by gun. This evidence was elicited in the course of D.F. addressing inquiries into her earlier inconsistent statements. Cutback fails to identify any resulting undue prejudice or confusion arising from this testimony, nor does any such risk appear evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., while testifying as a witness for the government, referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun, and this occurred without objection from the defense. Under these circumstances, the district court did not exceed its substantial discretion over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s claim that she had observed the firearm in question “once or twice” before the event by questioning her during cross-examination about statements she previously made to a police investigator. The district court permitted the defense to inquire about C.F.'s communications with law enforcement, but sustained the prosecution's objections when defense counsel attempted to read selections from the related police report into evidence, citing lack of proper foundation since C.F. was not the author of that report. Although Cutback did not address this evidentiary ruling in his appeal, the government’s brief noted that his “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove of appellees who unnecessarily broaden the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, whether this contention was properly preserved or not, the argument fails. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), “‘Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.’” (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion by insisting on appropriate authentication.

In conclusion, as Cutbank concedes, "this court will not overturn an incorrect evidentiary decision if that error was harmless. . . . Put differently, we will only reverse if the jury might have been materially affected by evidence that was improperly admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The prosecution presented substantial testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions "either did not affect or had only a minimal impact on the verdict." Id. at 998. "We will not vacate a conviction due to the cumulative effect of trial mistakes unless the defendant experienced substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to enhanced penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) advised a base offense level of 26, citing involvement with a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and the existence of at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also recommended an additional three-level increase because the firearm’s serial number had been altered or removed, a two-level enhancement for recklessly causing a danger of death or serious injury during a police chase, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with an additional felony offense. These enhancements resulted in a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, leading to an advisory sentencing range under the guidelines of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by applying the four-level enhancement for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant either uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in connection with an additional federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During the sentencing phase, the district court accepted as accurate, without objection, the factual recitations in the PSR concerning Cutback’s earlier felony convictions. Consequently, there was no error, let alone clear error, in the court's determination that the information in the PSR supports the conclusion that the firearm was linked to another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank instead contends that applying the 4-level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which results in a sentence within the applicable range, “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)”—that the sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury found Cutback had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby invoking penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, application of the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines dictate that the higher offense level controls. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court must initially and accurately compute the relevant Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to reviewing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, an appellate court must confirm that the district court did not make any significant procedural mistakes, including a failure to calculate or a miscalculation of the Guidelines range. See Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Following this, the court evaluates whether the factors outlined in § 3553(a) justify any departures or variances from the recommended Guidelines sentence.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence falling within the applicable guideline range, which is presumed reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court indicated it had examined the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and the parties’ sentencing memoranda, as well as considered the factors under § 3553(a) that might support a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” Furthermore, in order to affirm the sentence, it is not necessary for a district court to give a rote listing of the factors it evaluated; rather, it needs only to be apparent from the record that the court actually took the § 3553(a) factors into account when determining the sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had close to twelve prior felony convictions, several of which included violent conduct and the use of firearms. The underlying offense included acts of domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault inside the victim’s residence while her children were present, and causing injury to a police officer during resistance to arrest. By imposing a sentence of 292 months' imprisonment, which falls within the guidelines range, the district court neither overstepped its broad discretion at sentencing nor imposed a sentence that was substantively unreasonable.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
