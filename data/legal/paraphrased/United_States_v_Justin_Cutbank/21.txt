Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a lengthy criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court imposed a sentence of 292 months’ imprisonment under the Armed Career Criminal Act, which fell within the calculated advisory guidelines range. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and that it erred by applying a sentencing enhancement pursuant to USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), resulting in a higher advisory guidelines range. We affirm the district court’s judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions about the presence of “listening devices” in the residence and individuals outside intending to harm him. D.F. testified that Cutbank held her at gunpoint in the closet for what seemed like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and hit her in the face with the firearm. At one juncture, Cutbank demanded that D.F. wake other people in the house, which included her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the premises, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. D.F. then contacted the police.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, stating that a man was present in the garage and would not leave. Upon arrival, police found Cutbank, who barricaded himself inside the garage and, over several hours, made unpredictable statements and claimed to possess a weapon. When officers attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting one officer on the face and neck and trying to take the officer’s firearm. A search conducted along the route that Cutbank was believed to have traveled between D.F.’s residence and the garage yielded D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA analysis revealed that Cutbank’s DNA was present on the firearm. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the same one they had previously observed in Cutbank’s possession that night. The jury subsequently found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge and determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the enhanced penalties set forth in § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made multiple discretionary errors in pretrial and trial rulings and improperly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in aggravating his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple challenges concerning evidentiary and procedural matters, yet offers neither relevant case law nor meaningful analysis to bolster his claims. These assertions are without merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted propensity evidence in violation of Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court determined, as urged by the government, that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and indicating continued possession of the firearm prior to the event in question. We concur. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” At trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the incident in question.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank asked that the jury be instructed—so as to prevent hearsay problems and possible Confrontation Clause violations—not to treat D.F.'s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself testified to those matters. The government contended that those statements would serve to clarify the background for the police officers’ actions and knowledge, that officers’ testimony would remain within the confines of the hearsay rules, and that, if necessary, a limiting instruction given later during the trial proceedings would be standard practice and sufficiently address the issue. As stated in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015): “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” According to Bennett, a district court is not required to instruct the jury on each piece of evidence when it is introduced, provided an adequate instruction is given at the end of the trial. In this matter, the record shows no hearsay was admitted at trial. Further, D.F. did take the stand and testified. The Confrontation Clause is fulfilled, as explained in United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted), when hearsay declarants “actually appear in court and testify in person.” Consequently, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident, describing it as a “handful of times.” The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this testimony. Additionally, when C.F., a government witness, indicated that she had seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” previously, the court similarly rejected a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback does not present any authority that contradicts the district court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence relevant to establishing the possession charge and to showing ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements she had made concerning the firearm, including her assertion that she did not recall seeing the gun on the night in question, D.F. explained that some of her statements were due to her lack of familiarity with guns and her aversion to them, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court refused to grant Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s reference to her sister’s death by gun. This particular testimony was given in direct response to cross-examination about inconsistencies in her prior statements. Cutback has not identified any resulting unfair prejudice or jury confusion stemming from this testimony, and none is evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., a government witness, referenced the killing of D.F.’s sister by a gun without objection from the defense. Thus, there was no error in the district court’s considerable discretion over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s claim that she had previously observed the firearm involved in the incident "once or twice" by questioning her, on cross-examination, about statements she had earlier made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into C.F.’s communications with police but upheld the government's objections to the defense counsel's attempt to read segments of the police report into evidence without first laying an appropriate foundation, since the report was not written by C.F. Even though Cutback’s appeal did not address this ruling, the government’s response maintained that Cutback’s "next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession." We do not look favorably upon appellees who broaden appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless, whether the argument was preserved is immaterial, as the "claim" is unfounded. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The court acted within its discretion in insisting on proper authentication.

Ultimately, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not overturn an incorrect evidentiary decision if the mistake was harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only if the jury’s verdict could have been significantly affected by evidence that was wrongfully admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank does not satisfy this standard. The prosecution presented considerable testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “either did not affect the verdict or had merely a minimal effect.” Id. at 998. “We will not set aside a conviction due to the cumulative impact of trial errors unless the defendant suffered substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm and further determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to enhanced sentencing provisions under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26, as the offense concerned a sawed-off firearm as identified in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and Cutbank had at minimum two past convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further recommended a three-level enhancement because the firearm’s serial number was altered or obliterated, a two-level increase for recklessly endangering others with risk of death or serious bodily harm while evading law enforcement, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These calculations yielded a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory guideline range for sentencing was determined to be between 292 and 365 months imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in imposing the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in relation to another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

The enhancement is triggered when a defendant either used or possessed a firearm or ammunition in relation to an additional federal, state, or local felony offense, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing hearing, the district court adopted, without any objection, the PSR’s account of Cutback’s previous felony convictions. Consequently, the court did not err—let alone clearly err—in concluding that the facts set out in the PSR show the firearm was connected to another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) leads to a Guidelines-range sentence that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that a sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had three or more prior violent felony convictions, thereby invoking the Armed Career Criminal Act sentencing enhancements, the corresponding Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines specify that, in such cases, the higher offense level should be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, "a district court must start every sentencing hearing by accurately determining the relevant Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "An appellate court cannot evaluate whether a sentence is substantively reasonable until it first confirms that the district court has not made a significant procedural mistake, such as neglecting to calculate, or incorrectly calculating, the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After that, the court evaluates whether the § 3553(a) factors justify departing from or varying the sentence from the suggested guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence falling within the correct guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable on appellate review. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court referenced its examination of the PSR and sentencing memoranda, as well as a review of the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had a record consisting of nearly twelve prior felony convictions, including multiple offenses involving violence and firearms. The current conviction included acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assault committed in the presence of the victim’s children at their residence, and the infliction of injuries upon a law enforcement officer during the course of resisting arrest. The district court acted within its broad discretion in sentencing and did not issue a substantively unreasonable sentence by imposing a term of 292 months’ imprisonment within the applicable guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
