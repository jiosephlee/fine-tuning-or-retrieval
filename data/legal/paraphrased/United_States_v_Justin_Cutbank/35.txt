Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant prior criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, he received a prison term of 292 months, a sentence falling within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the district court. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court committed several errors related to evidence and procedure, which he claims denied him a fair trial, and that it incorrectly imposed a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement that raised the advisory guideline range. We affirm the district court’s decision.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after having consumed alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while making paranoid statements regarding “listening devices” allegedly present in the residence and individuals supposedly waiting outside to cause him harm. While holding D.F. at gunpoint inside the closet for what D.F. described as feeling like several hours, Cutbank threatened to kill her and struck her face with the firearm. At one stage, Cutbank told D.F. to awaken the other individuals sleeping in the home, including her teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Subsequently, Cutbank left the premises, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the gun with him. D.F. then contacted the police.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home indicating that a man had entered the garage and was refusing to leave. Upon their arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, during which time he made incoherent remarks and asserted he possessed a weapon for several hours. When police sought to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting one officer’s face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. In a search along the likely route between D.F.’s residence and the garage, officers discovered D.F.’s cellular phone, a large butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. Testing revealed that DNA found on the firearm belonged to Cutbank. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had previously observed in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged felon in possession count and determined he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby subjecting him to the sentencing enhancement under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court committed reversible error through various pretrial and trial decisions and that it erroneously applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) as a basis for sentence enhancement.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents several objections regarding evidence and procedure but fails to cite relevant authority or offer substantive analysis for his positions. These arguments lack merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from testifying that they had previously witnessed him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute improper propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government's assertion that this testimony qualified as intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and to showing that Cutbank had continuously possessed the firearm leading up to the incident. We concur with this reasoning. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident at issue.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank moved for a jury instruction clarifying that, due to potential hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns, D.F.’s statements to law enforcement could not be treated as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct until such time as D.F. testified about those events herself. The government responded that these statements would merely serve to provide the background for the officers’ actions and understanding at the relevant moments, asserting that the officers' testimony would stay within the bounds of hearsay prohibitions, and that a subsequent curative instruction from the court, should it prove necessary, would be routine and adequate. "We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion." United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not required to issue jury instructions regarding specific pieces of evidence contemporaneously with their introduction, so long as a proper instruction is delivered at the conclusion of trial. Id. In this instance, the record indicates no hearsay was actually received in evidence during the proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. did, in fact, testify at trial. The Confrontation Clause is complied with "when the hearsay declarants ... actually appear in court and testify in person." United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). The district court did not abuse its discretion in this regard.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated under oath that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, when C.F., a witness for the government, indicated she had witnessed the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” before, the court also overruled a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback does not cite any legal authority challenging the trial court’s pretrial determination that this evidence constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge and to show ongoing possession of the firearm before the incident, as discussed in Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about inconsistent prior statements concerning the firearm—including her claim that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her statements were due to her lack of familiarity with firearms and her aversion to them, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court overruled Cutback’s motion to strike the testimony concerning the death of D.F.’s sister by gunfire. This statement was offered in response to the cross-examination about D.F.’s conflicting statements. Cutback does not identify any undue prejudice or jury confusion that might have resulted from the testimony, and none is evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, during her testimony for the government, C.F. referenced the death of D.F.’s sister by a firearm without any objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed its substantial discretion over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s account that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice,” by cross-examining her with her earlier statements to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. had told law enforcement but upheld the government’s objections to the defense’s attempt to read from the police report, citing lack of proper foundation since C.F. was not its author. On appeal, Cutback did not directly address this ruling, but the government’s brief asserted that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove of appellees who broaden appellate issues without necessity. Regardless, even if the issue was not preserved, the “claim” lacks merit. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court did not err in its discretion by demanding appropriate authentication.

Lastly, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not disturb an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the mistake was harmless. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only when the jury’s verdict may have been significantly influenced by the evidence that was admitted in error.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank does not meet this burden. The prosecution offered considerable testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We do not set aside a conviction based upon the combined effect of multiple trial errors unless the defendant suffers substantial prejudice as a result.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. They also determined he had three or more previous convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the Armed Career Criminal Act’s heightened penalties. According to the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), a base offense level of 26 was warranted because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and Cutbank had at least two earlier convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase due to the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase based on recklessly creating a risk of death or serious injury during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in relation to another felony offense, resulting in a total offense level of 35. With a calculated Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory guideline range for sentencing was set at 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. Cutbank now appeals, asserting that the district court erred by imposing the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is applicable when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted as true, without any objections, the PSR's factual assertions concerning Cutback’s earlier felony convictions. Therefore, there was no error, much less clear error, in the court’s determination that the PSR's facts demonstrated the firearm was used in connection with an additional felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (describing the standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank instead contends that imposing the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4-level enhancement leads to a sentence within the Guideline range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” namely that a sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” The jury’s finding that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions invoked the Armed Career Criminal Act sanctions; the Guideline for Armed Career Criminals would set the total offense level at 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level governs. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When determining a suitable sentence, "a district court should begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Additionally, "[b]efore a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this, the court evaluates whether application of the § 3553(a) factors justifies any deviation or variance from the advisory guidelines sentencing range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the correctly calculated guidelines range, which carries a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court acknowledged that it had examined the PSR and sentencing briefs, as well as considered the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a departure. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “We do not mandate that the district court enumerate every factor it evaluated to sustain the sentence; it must merely be evident from the record that the district court did take the § 3553(a) factors into account when determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history consisted of nearly twelve prior felonies, several of which were violent or involved firearms. The current conviction entailed domestic violence, kidnapping, assault in the victim’s residence in front of her children, and injuring a law enforcement officer during an attempt to evade arrest. By sentencing Cutback to 292 months’ imprisonment within the guidelines range, the district court exercised its considerable discretion properly and did not hand down a sentence that was substantively unreasonable.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
