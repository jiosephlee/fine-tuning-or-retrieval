Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of possessing a firearm as a felon, in breach of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). Cutbank, who has a significant criminal record, received a sentence of 292 months imprisonment under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term within the advisory guidelines range established by the district court. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, alleging the district court made various evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and erroneously applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby raising the advisory guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank roused his girlfriend, D.F., and aimed a firearm at her face. He then forced her into a closet, expressing paranoid fears regarding "listening devices" inside the house and individuals outside supposedly intending to harm him. Cutbank held D.F. at gunpoint in the closet for what D.F. described as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened her life and struck her in the face with the firearm. At one point, he instructed D.F. to wake the other individuals who were sleeping in the residence, namely her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Cutbank ultimately left, taking both D.F.'s cell phone and the gun with him. D.F. subsequently contacted the police.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and refused to leave when asked. Upon their arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage, made irrational statements over the next several hours, and asserted that he was armed. When the officers attempted to take Cutbank into custody, he resisted by biting one officer’s face and neck and attempted to seize the officer’s firearm. Officers who searched the likely route taken by Cutbank between D.F.’s residence and the garage discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a shortened barrel and stock. DNA analysis of the firearm revealed a match to Cutbank. At trial, D.F. and C.F. both identified the gun as the one they observed in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession as charged and determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, which subjected him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court erred in several pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied the enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from testifying that they had previously seen him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending such testimony would constitute propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government's position that this evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as to show Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. We concur with that conclusion. As noted in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be given an instruction precluding it from relying on D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct until such time as D.F. testified directly about the events at issue, in order to prevent hearsay problems and address concerns under the Confrontation Clause. In response, the government maintained that the statements in question would provide relevant context for the actions and understanding of the police officers involved, that the officers’ testimony would not breach the hearsay rules, and that any necessary cautionary instruction from the court could be appropriately issued later, in line with customary practice. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this instance, the record indicates that no hearsay was introduced during the trial proceedings. Additionally, D.F. took the stand and testified. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). The district court therefore did not abuse its discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court overruled Cutback's renewed objection regarding this testimony. Similarly, when C.F. appeared as a witness for the government and recounted seeing the firearm involved in the incident on “once or twice” before, the court likewise rejected a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback does not offer any authority to challenge the trial court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence, pertinent both to establishing the possession charge and to indicating ongoing possession of the firearm leading up to the event. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements about the firearm, such as stating she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant evening, D.F. explained that some of her remarks stemmed from being "not familiar with guns" and an aversion to firearms because "[her] sister was killed by a gun." The district court rejected Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s testimony concerning her sister’s death by gunfire. This information was offered as an explanation during cross-examination on her prior inconsistent remarks. Cutback has not identified, nor is there any clear evidence of, any undue prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., appearing as a government witness, also referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun, and defense counsel raised no objection. The district court thus did not exceed the bounds of its considerable discretion over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s statement that she had previously observed the gun “once or twice” by questioning her about earlier remarks she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. had conveyed to law enforcement but upheld the prosecution’s objections when defense counsel tried to read parts of the police report into evidence without proper foundation, as the report had not been written by C.F. While Cutback’s appeal did not address this ruling directly, the government’s brief contended that his “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disfavor appellees broadening the issues presented for appeal without cause. Regardless, whether or not the issue was preserved, the “claim” lacks merit. As explained in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court therefore did not abuse its discretion by insisting on proper authentication.

Ultimately, as Cutback concedes, “this court does not overturn a mistaken evidentiary decision if the mistake was harmless. . . . Put differently, we will grant a reversal only when there is a reasonable possibility that the erroneously admitted evidence substantially affected the jury’s verdict.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank is unable to meet this requirement. The government presented significant testimonial and physical evidence. Consequently, the evidentiary rulings made by the district court “either had no effect or only a minor effect on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We decline to vacate a conviction for the combined impact of trial errors unless the defendant has suffered substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. The jury further determined that he had three or more previous convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the increased penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) assigned a base offense level of 26, as the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and because Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also added a three-level enhancement due to the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement for recklessly placing others at risk of death or serious injury while evading police, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense, resulting in a combined offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category VI, the guidelines sentencing range was calculated to be 292 to 365 months in prison. On appeal, Cutbank challenges the district court’s application of the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is applicable if the defendant either used or possessed a firearm or ammunition in association with any other federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At sentencing, the district court, without any objection, accepted the Presentence Report’s factual findings regarding Cutback’s previous felony convictions. Consequently, the court did not err, much less commit clear error, in determining that the Presentence Report demonstrated the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that the imposition of the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) leads to a sentence within the advisory range that allegedly “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that a sentence should be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, which triggers penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines specify that the higher offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When deciding on a suitable sentence, “a district court should initiate the sentencing process by accurately computing the relevant Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “Prior to a court of appeals reviewing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first confirm that the district court did not make any significant procedural mistakes, such as neglecting to calculate, or calculating incorrectly, the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Following this, the court evaluates whether the factors outlined in § 3553(a) justify a sentence outside the advisory guidelines range, whether through a departure or variance.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence falling within the applicable guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable on appellate review. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court indicated it had reviewed the PSR, sentencing memoranda, and considered the § 3553(a) factors that could potentially justify a variance. As stated, “the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). We do not require that the district court recite each factor; it suffices that the record shows the court actually accounted for the § 3553(a) considerations in arriving at its sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had almost a dozen prior felony convictions, many relating to violent behavior and firearm offenses. The conviction at issue encompassed acts of domestic abuse, kidnapping, an assault that occurred within the victim’s home and in front of her children, as well as injuring a police officer while resisting arrest. Under these circumstances, the district court did not exceed its broad sentencing discretion or issue a substantively unreasonable sentence by imposing a 292-month imprisonment term within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
