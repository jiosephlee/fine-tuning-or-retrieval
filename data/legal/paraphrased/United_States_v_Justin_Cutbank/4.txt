Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant history of criminal offenses, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term falling within the advisory guidelines range it had calculated. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes which denied him a fair trial and that it erroneously applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), resulting in a higher advisory guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and compelled her into a closet, expressing paranoid suspicions about “listening devices” inside the residence and claiming individuals outside intended to harm him. He held D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described in her testimony as several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her face with the gun. At one point, Cutbank commanded D.F. to awaken other individuals asleep in the home, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Eventually, Cutbank left the scene, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement received another report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was inside the garage and refusing to leave. Upon their arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage and, over the span of several hours, made incoherent statements and asserted that he was armed. When officers attempted to take him into custody, Cutbank resisted arrest by biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. A search along the route that Cutbank was believed to have traveled between D.F.’s house and the garage resulted in the discovery of D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA testing revealed that the firearm contained Cutbank’s genetic material. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the same one they had previously observed in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of being a felon in possession and determined he had three or more prior violent felony convictions, subjecting him to the enhanced penalties under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court committed errors in several pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in aggravating his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their earlier observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, asserting that such testimony would constitute propensity evidence prohibited under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government's position that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge, as well as evidence indicating ongoing possession of the firearm preceding the incident. We concur with this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the event in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought an instruction directing the jury not to treat D.F.'s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. provided testimony about those incidents herself. Cutbank’s aim was to preclude hearsay issues and address concerns under the Confrontation Clause. The prosecution contended that these statements were admissible to illustrate the circumstances of police conduct and officers’ knowledge at relevant times, maintained that officers’ accounts would not contravene hearsay rules, and further asserted that, if necessary, an instruction from the court later in the proceedings would be conventional and adequate. As noted in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015): “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” The court in Bennett also observed, “The court need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this instance, the record shows no hearsay was admitted during trial. Moreover, D.F. did in fact testify. As stated in United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted), the requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Accordingly, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court rejected Cutbank’s renewed objection to this testimony. Similarly, the court overruled a defense objection when C.F., a witness for the government, testified that she had previously seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice.” On appeal, Cutbank does not reference any authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that this evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about her prior inconsistent statements concerning the gun—including statements indicating she did not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night—D.F. explained that her comments were partly because she was “not familiar with guns” and that she “did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court overruled Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s testimony regarding her sister’s death by gun. This testimony was provided in response to questions during cross-examination concerning inconsistencies in her prior statements. Cutback does not demonstrate any unfair prejudice or confusion arising from this testimony, nor is any evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., another government witness, referenced D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun, and the defense raised no objection. Therefore, the district court did not exceed its wide latitude in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s claim that she had previously seen the gun used in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her about earlier statements she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. had told police but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel attempted to read parts of the police report into evidence without proper foundation, as C.F. was not the author of the report. Although Cutback did not specifically address this evidentiary ruling on appeal, the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably upon appellees who unnecessarily broaden the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, whether the issue was preserved or not, the “claim” lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The court’s insistence on proper authentication did not constitute an abuse of discretion.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, "this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless... That is, reversal is appropriate only when the improperly admitted evidence could have had a significant impact on the jury's verdict." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government's case included strong testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. "We will not overturn a conviction on the basis of the cumulative effect of trial errors unless the defendant suffered substantial prejudice." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thus subjecting him to the heightened penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended setting the base offense level at 26 due to the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as specified in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and because Cutbank had a minimum of two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase based on the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement for recklessly endangering life while evading law enforcement, and a four-level increase on the grounds that the firearm was possessed in relation to another felony offense, culminating in a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, resulting in an advisory sentencing range under the guidelines of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by applying the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense as provided in § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings regarding Cutback’s previous felony convictions without any objection from the parties. Therefore, the district court did not commit error—let alone clear error—when it determined that the facts in the PSR demonstrated that the firearm was used in connection with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (setting forth the standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, however, contends that applying the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which yields a sentence within the guideline range, is at odds with the fundamental tenet of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence should be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury concluded that Cutback had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies—thus invoking penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have assigned a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level must be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court must initially and accurately determine the relevant Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). An appellate court may review the substantive reasonableness of a sentence only after confirming that the district court made no significant procedural mistakes, including errors in calculating or failing to calculate the Guidelines range. See Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this, the court evaluates whether application of the § 3553(a) factors justifies a departure from, or variance outside of, the advisory Guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the properly calculated guidelines range, and such a sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the district court indicated that it had considered the PSR, the sentencing memoranda, and evaluated the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. As stated, “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). It is not necessary for the district court to explicitly recite every factor it considered; the record only needs to reflect that the court took the § 3553(a) factors into account when imposing the sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record includes almost twelve prior felony convictions, several of which entailed violent conduct and the use of firearms. The instant offense consisted of domestic violence, kidnapping, assault committed in the victim’s home while her children were present, and causing harm to a police officer during an attempt to avoid arrest. The district court, therefore, did not exceed its broad discretion or impose a substantively unreasonable sentence by sentencing Cutback to 292 months’ imprisonment, which was within the guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
