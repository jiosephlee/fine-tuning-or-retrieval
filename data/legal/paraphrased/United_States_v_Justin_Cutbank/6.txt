Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, in contravention of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), noting his significant criminal background. The district court sentenced him to 292 months of imprisonment pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term within the advisory guideline range calculated by the court. On appeal, Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence, alleging that the district court committed several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and erroneously imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which raised the advisory guideline range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and compelled her to enter a closet while making paranoid allegations about the presence of "listening devices" inside the residence and individuals outside intending to cause him harm. He detained D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described in her testimony as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the gun. At a certain point, Cutbank commanded D.F. to wake others who were asleep in the house, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the premises, taking with him D.F.'s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and refused to leave the premises. Upon their arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, during which he made incoherent remarks over several hours and asserted that he was armed. When the police attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted arrest, biting an officer on the face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. Officers conducting a search along the likely route Cutbank would have used between D.F.’s house and the garage discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a shortened barrel and stock. DNA evidence found on the firearm corresponded to Cutbank. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged felon in possession count, and further determined he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, which invoked the enhanced sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now appeals his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court abused its discretion through several pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in enhancing his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle. He contended that such testimony would constitute inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court, however, concurred with the government’s position, determining that the evidence in question was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as to demonstrating Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm before the event in question. We concur with this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had witnessed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of times” preceding the incident at issue.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought an instruction directing the jury that, in order to avoid hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems, it should refrain from treating D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. herself provided testimony about those events. The government countered that these statements would only serve to supply context for the actions and knowledge of the officers at the relevant time, asserting that the officers’ testimony would remain within the bounds of hearsay restrictions, and further suggested that, if necessary, a limiting instruction issued later by the court would be both routine and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not obligated “to instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this case, the record shows that no hearsay was admitted during the proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. provided testimony in court. The Confrontation Clause is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Therefore, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, when C.F., a witness for the prosecution, testified that she had observed the firearm used during the incident “once or twice” before, the court overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback fails to present any legal authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge and relevant to establishing ongoing possession of the firearm before the incident, citing Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511, as an example.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier inconsistent statements she had made concerning the firearm—including a claim that she did not recall seeing the gun on the night in question—D.F. explained that some of her statements were attributable to her lack of familiarity with firearms and her general aversion to them, stating, “I did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court denied Cutback’s request to strike the testimony concerning D.F.’s sister’s death by firearm. This testimony was elicited in response to questions about her inconsistent prior statements during cross-examination. Cutback has failed to identify any unfair prejudice or confusion stemming from this testimony, nor is any evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., who also testified as a government witness, referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun, and the defense did not object. The district court did not exceed its wide latitude in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to cast doubt on C.F.'s assertion that she had previously observed the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her, during cross-examination, about statements she had previously given to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries concerning C.F.’s remarks to law enforcement, but sustained the government’s objections when defense counsel tried to read from the corresponding police report into the record without first establishing an appropriate foundation, since the report had not been written by C.F. Although Cutback did not address this particular ruling on appeal, the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably upon appellees who needlessly broaden the issues raised on appeal. Regardless, whether or not the issue was preserved, this “claim” lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The court’s insistence on proper authentication did not constitute an abuse of discretion.

At bottom, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not reverse an evidentiary error unless it affected the outcome. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only when the jury’s decision may have been meaningfully affected by the evidence that was wrongfully admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The prosecution introduced significant testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not vacate a conviction due to cumulative trial errors unless the defendant suffered substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of felon in possession of a firearm. It additionally determined that he had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the heightened penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended assigning a base offense level of 26 due to the firearm being a sawed-off weapon as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and because Cutbank had at least two previous convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further recommended a three-level increase for the firearm’s altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement for recklessly creating a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury while eluding law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement based on his possession of the firearm in relation to another felony offense. These adjustments brought the total offense level to 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, resulting in an advisory sentencing range under the guidelines of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by applying the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

The enhancement is triggered when a defendant either uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony under federal, state, or local law, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted as true, and without opposition, the PSR’s recitation of Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Accordingly, the court did not err—let alone commit clear error—in determining, based on the PSR, that the firearm was connected to another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, instead, contends that imposing the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) yields a sentence within the guideline range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” specifically that a sentence must “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thus triggering the penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the applicable Guideline for Armed Career Criminals would have established a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines specify that, when multiple offense levels apply, the higher offense level controls. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, “a district court must initially calculate the correct Guidelines range for the offense.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “Prior to assessing whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, a court of appeals must confirm that the district court did not commit any major procedural mistakes, such as omitting to compute, or incorrectly computing, the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this step, the court evaluates whether application of the § 3553(a) factors justifies a deviation or variance from the recommended Guidelines sentence.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the appropriate guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court indicated that it had considered the PSR, the parties’ sentencing memoranda, and the relevant § 3553(a) factors that could justify a variance. “Accordingly, the district court was cognizant of [Cutbank’s] contentions, leading us to presume that the district court took those arguments into account and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “We do not require the district court to methodically enumerate the factors it considered to affirm the sentence; it is sufficient that the record reflects the district court genuinely evaluated the § 3553(a) factors when deciding the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had nearly twelve previous felony convictions, several of which involved violence and firearms. The conviction at issue concerned domestic violence, kidnapping, assaulting the victim inside her home in the presence of her children, and causing injury to a law enforcement officer while resisting arrest. The district court did not exceed its considerable discretion in sentencing and did not impose a substantively unreasonable sentence by ordering a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
