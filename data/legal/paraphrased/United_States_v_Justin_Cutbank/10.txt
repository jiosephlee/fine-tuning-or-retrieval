Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant history of prior convictions, guilty of possessing a firearm as a felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison, a sentence that falls within the advisory guidelines range as calculated by the court. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and misapplied the sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby increasing the advisory range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night between November 19 and 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., and aimed a firearm at her face, compelling her to enter a closet. While holding her at gunpoint, he expressed paranoid suspicions regarding “listening devices” within the residence and individuals outside intending to cause him harm. D.F. testified that she remained in the closet under his threat for what felt like several hours, during which Cutbank threatened her life and struck her on the face with the firearm. At a certain point, Cutbank demanded that D.F. wake up other people in the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Eventually, Cutbank left, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. Following his departure, D.F. contacted the authorities.

Additionally, law enforcement received a report from a residence situated half a mile away from D.F.’s home, indicating that an individual was present in the garage and refused to leave. Upon their arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, where, over several hours, he exhibited erratic behavior and claimed possession of a weapon. When officers attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting one of the officers on the face and neck, and by attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. In the area officers believed Cutbank likely traversed between D.F.’s residence and the garage, they recovered D.F.’s cellphone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. Cutbank’s DNA was discovered on the firearm. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the same one they had previously seen with Cutbank earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge and determined he had previously been convicted of three or more violent felonies, resulting in the application of the enhanced penalties under § 924(e). Cutbank now appeals both his conviction and sentence, asserting that the district court committed errors in various pretrial and trial rulings, and also misapplied the sentence enhancement provided in USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously seen him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, asserting that such testimony would constitute propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court, however, sided with the government’s position that this evidence was intrinsic and relevant to establishing the possession charge, as well as to showing ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the alleged event. We concur with this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the incident at issue.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought an instruction for the jury aimed at preventing both hearsay issues and potential Confrontation Clause violations, requesting that the jury be told not to treat D.F.’s police statements as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. provided her own testimony about the incident. The prosecution countered that these statements were relevant to explain the background for the officers' actions and their contemporaneous knowledge, maintaining that the officers’ testimony would not violate hearsay prohibitions and that, if required, a subsequent limiting instruction from the court would be appropriate and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is “not required to instruct the jury about each individual piece of evidence at the time it is admitted as long as a proper instruction is delivered at the conclusion of trial.” Id. In this case, the evidence reveals that hearsay evidence was not received during trial. Moreover, D.F. did testify. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Consequently, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Similarly, the court rejected a defense objection when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” previously. On appeal, Cutback offers no case law contradicting the district court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge as well as showing ongoing possession of the firearm preceding the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about prior inconsistent statements concerning the gun—specifically, that she could not recall observing the gun on the night in question—D.F. explained that some of her previous remarks stemmed from her lack of familiarity with firearms and her general aversion to them, adding, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the testimony related to the death of D.F.’s sister. This testimony was provided in response to questions on cross-examination about inconsistent statements made earlier. Cutback does not identify, nor is there any clear indication of, any unfair prejudice or confusion that might arise from this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., who appeared as a government witness, referred to the fact that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun, without any objection raised by the defense. The district court did not exceed its considerable discretion over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s claim that she had previously seen the firearm “once or twice” by questioning her on statements she gave to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries concerning what C.F. told the police but upheld the prosecution’s objections when defense counsel tried to read segments of the resulting police report into evidence without an adequate foundation, noting that the report was not composed by C.F. While Cutback did not address this evidentiary ruling on appeal, the government's brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably upon appellees who unnecessarily broaden the appellate issues. Regardless, whether or not this matter is preserved, the “claim” lacks merit. As noted in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” (quotation omitted). The district court did not act outside its discretion in demanding proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court does not overturn an improper evidentiary decision unless the mistake was harmful. . . . Stated differently, reversal is warranted only if the inadmissible evidence could have meaningfully affected the jury’s decision.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank is unable to meet this requirement. The government presented considerable testimonial and physical evidence. Accordingly, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not impact or had only a minimal impact on the outcome.” Id. at 998. “A conviction will not be set aside on account of the cumulative impact of errors at trial unless the defendant experiences substantial prejudice as a result.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further determined that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (“PSR”) recommended assigning a base offense level of 26, citing the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), as well as Cutbank's record of at least two prior convictions for violent crimes. Additionally, the PSR suggested a three-level increase due to the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement based on reckless conduct resulting in the risk of death or serious bodily injury to others during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in furtherance of another felony offense, bringing the total offense level to 35. The PSR determined that Cutbank fell within Criminal History Category VI, yielding an advisory sentencing guideline range between 292 and 365 months’ imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court improperly imposed the four-level enhancement pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

The enhancement is applicable when a defendant uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony offense under federal, state, or local law, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted as true, without any objection, the PSR’s account of Cutback’s prior felony offenses. Therefore, there was no error, let alone clear error, when the court found that the PSR’s facts supported the conclusion that the firearm was used in relation to another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, however, contends that applying the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) leads to a sentence within the Guidelines range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” which requires that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby invoking penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, application of the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded a total offense level of 34. According to the Guidelines, the higher offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When selecting an appropriate sentence, the district court must initiate the sentencing process by accurately determining the applicable Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to assessing whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, an appellate court must confirm that the district court did not commit a significant procedural mistake, such as failing to compute—or incorrectly computing—the Guidelines range. See Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Following this, the court evaluates if the factors set out in § 3553(a) justify imposing a sentence that departs from or varies from the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court sentenced Cutback within the range set by the relevant sentencing guidelines, a sentence we presume to be reasonable when reviewed on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceedings, the court indicated it had examined the Presentence Investigation Report and the sentencing memoranda, as well as considered the § 3553(a) factors potentially supporting a variance. As noted, “the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “We do not require the district court to recite in a mechanical fashion each factor considered to uphold the sentence; rather, the record must simply establish that the district court did in fact evaluate the § 3553(a) factors when setting the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history comprised nearly twelve prior felony convictions, several of which involved violence and firearms. The crime for which he was convicted included acts of domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault that occurred in the victim’s residence and in the presence of her children, and also involved injuring a law enforcement officer during the course of resisting arrest. Based on the record, the district court acted within its broad discretion at sentencing and did not impose a substantively unreasonable sentence by committing Cutback to a 292-month term of imprisonment falling within the applicable guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
