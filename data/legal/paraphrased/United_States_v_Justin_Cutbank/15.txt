Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal background, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act to 292 months in prison, a term within the advisory guidelines range as calculated by the court. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and improperly applied a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, which increased the recommended guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., brandished a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while voicing paranoid suspicions about “listening devices” inside the residence and alleged threats from individuals outside intending him harm. He held D.F. at gunpoint within the closet—an ordeal D.F. described in her testimony as lasting hours—during which he threatened to kill her and struck her face with the firearm. At one juncture, Cutbank demanded that D.F. wake the others present in the home, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the scene, taking D.F.’s phone and the firearm with him. Following his departure, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and refused to leave. Upon their arrival, officers found Cutbank barricaded inside the garage, where, over a period of several hours, he made incoherent statements and asserted possession of a weapon. Cutbank resisted arrest when the officers tried to detain him, biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. Officers who later searched the route Cutbank was likely to have taken from D.F.’s home to the garage recovered D.F.’s cellphone, a butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA analysis of the firearm matched that of Cutbank. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the gun as the same one they had seen with Cutbank prior to the events of that night. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged felon in possession count and determined that he had three prior convictions for violent felonies, thus invoking the enhanced sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence, contending on appeal that the district court erred by abusing its discretion in various pretrial and trial rulings and by incorrectly applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle on earlier occasions, asserting that such testimony constituted impermissible propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court determined, consistent with the government’s position, that this constituted intrinsic evidence bearing on the possession charge and was relevant to show continuous possession of the firearm prior to the charged event. We concur with this reasoning. “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015). During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the relevant incident.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought a jury instruction directing that D.F.’s statements to law enforcement could not be treated as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself gave testimony regarding the events, seeking to preempt hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns. The government contended the statements were admissible to provide context for the police officers’ conduct and knowledge, that the officers' testimony would remain within hearsay restrictions, and that a subsequent instruction from the court, if necessary, would be usual and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. The record in this case shows no hearsay was admitted during trial. Additionally, D.F. did testify. The Confrontation Clause requirements are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Consequently, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this testimony. Additionally, when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had previously seen the firearm used during the incident on "once or twice" before, the court likewise rejected a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback does not reference any legal authority challenging the court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as indicating ongoing possession of the firearm before the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier contradictory statements she made concerning the firearm—specifically, that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant evening—D.F. explained that her unfamiliarity and aversion to guns stemmed from the fact that her sister had been killed with a gun. The district court declined Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister’s death by firearm. This statement served as a response to defense questions addressing her prior inconsistent statements. Cutback fails to identify, and the record does not reveal, any undue prejudice or jury confusion arising from this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, government witness C.F. also stated during his testimony that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun, without any objection from the defense. Accordingly, there is no indication that the district court exceeded its wide discretion in making evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s claim that she had previously seen the gun involved “once or twice” by questioning her about earlier remarks she made to a police investigator during cross-examination. The district court permitted questions addressing what C.F. communicated to law enforcement but upheld the government's objections to defense counsel’s attempt to read sections of the resulting police report into evidence, citing lack of proper foundation since C.F. was not the report’s author. On appeal, Cutback did not reference this specific ruling, but the government maintained in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disfavor when appellees unnecessarily broaden the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, even if the issue is properly preserved, this “claim” lacks merit. As noted in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The court acted within its discretion in insisting on proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, "this court will not overturn an incorrect evidentiary ruling unless the error affected the outcome. . . . That is, we reverse only if the jury's verdict may have been significantly affected by the evidence that was improperly admitted." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to satisfy this standard. The government presented ample testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court's evidentiary decisions "did not influence or had only a minimal impact on the verdict." Id. at 998. "We do not set aside a conviction based on the combined effect of errors at trial unless the defendant has suffered significant prejudice." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the increased sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) assigned a base offense level of 26, noting that the case involved a sawed-off firearm as specified under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and that Cutbank had a minimum of two earlier convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement based on his reckless creation of a risk of death or serious bodily injury during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense, resulting in a combined offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which produced an advisory sentencing guidelines range of 292 to 365 months in prison. Cutbank now contends on appeal that the district court improperly imposed the four-level increase for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is applicable when a defendant has used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony under federal, state, or local law, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the PSR’s factual account regarding Cutback’s past felony convictions without any objection from the parties. Consequently, the court did not commit error—and certainly not clear error—when it determined that the PSR established the firearm was used in connection with another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, however, maintains that imposing the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) creates a sentence within the Guidelines range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that punishment should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined Cutback had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, triggering the Armed Career Criminal Act, the applicable Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines dictate that the higher offense level should be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court is required to start by properly determining the applicable Guidelines range. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Additionally, “[b]efore a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates whether any of the § 3553(a) factors justify either a departure from or a variance to the advisory Guidelines sentence.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence that fell within the established guidelines range, which we regard as presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court indicated it had examined the PSR along with the sentencing memoranda, and considered the factors outlined in § 3553(a) that might support a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). A district court is not required “to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had almost twelve prior felony offenses, many of which included violence and the use of firearms. The instant conviction arose from acts involving domestic abuse, kidnapping, an assault carried out in the victim’s residence while her children were present, and inflicting injury on a police officer while evading arrest. The district court, therefore, did not misuse its considerable discretion in sentencing, nor did it impose a substantively unreasonable sentence, when it sentenced Cutback to a 292-month prison term within the advisory guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
