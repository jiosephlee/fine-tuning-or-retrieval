Case Name: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Decision Date: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) as a felon in possession of a firearm, noting his significant criminal background. The district court imposed a sentence of 292 months under the Armed Career Criminal Act, falling within the advisory guidelines as calculated by the court. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that resulted in an unfair trial and erroneously applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which raised the advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a firearm at her head, and forced her into a closet while making paranoid statements regarding supposed “listening devices” in the residence and alleging that individuals outside were intent on causing him harm. He detained D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her on the face with the weapon. At one stage, Cutbank commanded D.F. to wake the other occupants of the house, namely her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank absconded with D.F.’s mobile phone and the gun. D.F. then contacted law enforcement.

Police also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, stating that a man was inside the garage and refused to leave. Upon the officers’ arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside and, during several hours, made unpredictable statements and asserted that he possessed a weapon. When police attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted, biting one officer’s face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. A search along the likely route between D.F.’s residence and the garage resulted in the discovery of D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with both the barrel and stock sawed off. Testing of the firearm revealed DNA matching Cutbank. During the trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the same weapon they had seen with Cutbank prior to the incident. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge and determined he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, subjecting him to the increased penalties under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, alleging the district court abused its discretion in various pretrial and trial rulings, and that it erred in applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments concerning evidentiary and procedural matters, yet offers neither legal authority nor substantive analysis in their favor. All of these arguments lack merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, asserting that such testimony would constitute inadmissible propensity evidence in contravention of Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court sided with the government’s position, determining the testimony constituted intrinsic evidence material to establishing the possession charge and to show Cutbank’s ongoing control over the firearm before the incident in question. We concur with the district court. As explained in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. stated that she had witnessed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions preceding the event in dispute.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be instructed, in order to preclude hearsay concerns and potential Confrontation Clause violations, that it could not treat D.F.’s statements to the police as substantive proof regarding Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. provided her own testimony about the underlying events. The government responded that the statements in question were being used to clarify the officers’ actions and their knowledge at relevant times, that the officers’ recounting would remain within hearsay boundaries, and that if necessary, the court could issue a curative instruction from the bench, as is typical practice. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to the cited precedent, the district court is not obligated to deliver a limiting instruction on every item of evidence at the moment of admission so long as an adequate instruction is given at trial’s end. Id. The record here reflects that hearsay evidence was not introduced during trial proceedings. Additionally, D.F. did take the witness stand and provided testimony. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Accordingly, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this testimony. Similarly, when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had seen the weapon used in the incident “once or twice” beforehand, the court also denied a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback fails to present any authority contradicting the district court’s pretrial determination that this evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge as well as showing that Cutback had continuous possession of the firearm before the event in question. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about her previous inconsistent statements concerning the firearm—including her claim that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant evening—D.F. explained that some of her statements arose because she was “not familiar with guns” and “did not like guns,” further clarifying, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court denied Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister's death by gun violence. This information was given as a direct response to cross-examination on prior inconsistent statements. Cutback identifies no resulting unfair prejudice or confusion, nor does the record reveal any. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, C.F., called as a witness for the prosecution, likewise referred to D.F.’s sister having been killed by a gun without any objection from the defense. Therefore, there was no error in the district court’s wide latitude over evidentiary decisions. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s statement that she had observed the gun used in the incident “once or twice” before by cross-examining her regarding earlier remarks she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. told police, but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel tried to read parts of the police report into evidence without laying a sufficient foundation, as C.F. was not the report’s author. While Cutback did not address this particular evidentiary ruling on appeal, the government’s brief contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We discourage appellees from enlarging the scope of appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless of preservation, the “claim” lacks merit. As articulated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The court did not err in discretion by insisting on authentication.

Finally, as Cutback concedes, “this court does not overturn an evidentiary error unless the error is harmless. . . . That is, reversal occurs only where there is a possibility that the jury was materially affected by the wrongful admission of evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government introduced significant testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions either did not affect or had only a minimal effect on the jury’s verdict. Id. at 998. “We will not set aside a conviction based on the combined impact of trial errors unless the defendant suffered substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had at least three previous violent felony convictions, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced penalties prescribed by the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) advised a base offense level of 26 on the grounds that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and that Cutbank had two or more prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR also recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm having an altered or removed serial number, a two-level enhancement for recklessly endangering life or causing a risk of serious injury during flight from police, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with a separate felony, bringing the total offense level to 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory guideline range was calculated at 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank challenges the district court’s application of the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in relation to another felony under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered if the defendant either used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, and this is true "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During the sentencing phase, the district court accepted the PSR’s factual recitation of Cutback’s prior felony convictions without any objection. Therefore, the court did not make an error—and certainly not a clear error—when it determined based on the PSR that the firearm was involved in another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (addressing the standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Nevertheless, Cutbank contends that imposing the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) four-level enhancement produces a Guidelines sentence that is inconsistent with the fundamental goal of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), which mandates that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby activating the Armed Career Criminal Act’s sentencing provisions, the Guideline for Armed Career Criminals would have assigned a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the highest offense level must be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, the district court must initially and accurately determine the relevant Guidelines range. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). The appellate court may only review the substantive reasonableness of a sentence after verifying that the district court has not committed any significant procedural error, such as neglecting to calculate or incorrectly computing the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Following this, the court assesses whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a departure from, or variance from, the advisory Guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the relevant guideline range, which we regard as presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court indicated it had reviewed the PSR and the sentencing memoranda, as well as considered the § 3553(a) factors potentially supporting a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “A district court need not mechanically recite each factor considered to sustain a sentence; what matters is that the record shows the § 3553(a) factors were, in fact, considered in determining the appropriate sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had almost twelve prior felony convictions, many of which included violence and use of firearms. The instant offense involved domestic violence, kidnapping, an assault committed in the presence of the victim’s children in her home, and harm to a police officer while resisting arrest. The district court did not exceed its broad discretion at sentencing, nor did it issue a substantively unreasonable sentence, when imposing a within-guideline sentence of 292 months’ imprisonment on Cutback.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
