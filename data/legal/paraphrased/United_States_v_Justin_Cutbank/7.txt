Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal background, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. The district court imposed a sentence of 292 months’ imprisonment pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, falling within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, asserting that the district court made various evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and misapplied the USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, resulting in a higher guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and compelled her to enter a closet, while voicing paranoid suspicions about “listening devices” in the residence and alleging individuals outside intended to harm him. He held D.F. at gunpoint inside the closet for a period D.F. described as feeling like hours, during which he threatened her life and struck her in the face with the weapon. At one juncture, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake other occupants of the home, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Eventually, Cutbank left the scene, taking with him D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. Subsequently, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, indicating that a male subject was present in the garage and refused to leave the premises. Upon the officers’ arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage and, for several hours, made incoherent statements while asserting he possessed a weapon. When police tried to take him into custody, Cutbank physically resisted, biting an officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. Officers searching the route that Cutbank was likely to have taken between D.F.'s residence and the garage discovered D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher-type knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA analysis of the firearm matched Cutbank. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. identified this firearm as the one they had observed in Cutbank’s possession prior to that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged felon in possession offense and determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, resulting in the imposition of enhanced sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court abused its discretion through several pretrial and trial rulings, and that it erred in applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government’s position that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge and was probative of Cutbank’s continuous possession of the firearm prior to the incident in question. We concur with this reasoning. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the incident at issue.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested a jury instruction stating that, to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns, the jury should not use D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. provided her own testimony about the events. The government responded that the statements would merely supply context for the officers’ actions and state of knowledge at relevant times, that the officers’ testimony would not violate hearsay principles, and that, if necessary, a subsequent instruction from the court would be both typical and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to that precedent, the court is not required to instruct the jury about each individual piece of evidence as it is admitted, provided a proper instruction is issued at the conclusion of the trial. Id. In this matter, the record shows that the court did not allow hearsay evidence during trial. Moreover, D.F. did testify. The Confrontation Clause requirements are fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). There was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident, describing it as a “handful of times.” The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, the court overruled another defense objection when C.F., called as a government witness, indicated that she had seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” previously. On appeal, Cutback does not present any authority that challenges the district court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and to showing that Cutback continuously possessed the firearm before the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements concerning the firearm—including whether she recalled seeing it on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her prior statements were influenced by her lack of familiarity with firearms and her aversion to them, adding, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the testimony relating to the death of D.F.’s sister. This statement was elicited as a response to cross-examination on her inconsistent statements. Cutback has not identified any undue prejudice or jury confusion arising from this testimony, nor is any such prejudice evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., testifying for the government, referenced D.F.’s sister having been killed by a gun, and defense counsel did not object. The district court did not exceed its broad discretion over evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s claim that she had seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” previously by cross-examining her about statements she had made earlier to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into what C.F. told the police but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel attempted to read sections of the related police report into evidence without laying the proper foundation, noting that C.F. was not the author of the report. While Cutback did not specifically address this ruling on appeal, the government argued in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We are not receptive to appellees who unduly broaden the scope of appellate issues. Nevertheless, whether the issue was preserved or not, the “claim” lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion in insisting on proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, "this court does not overturn an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the mistake was harmless. . . . Stated differently, reversal is warranted only when the improperly admitted evidence may have had a significant impact on the jury's decision." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government presented ample testimonial and physical evidence. Accordingly, the district court’s evidentiary decisions "either did not affect or had only a minimal effect on the verdict." Id. at 998. "A conviction will not be reversed for the cumulative impact of trial errors unless those errors result in substantial prejudice against the defendant." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the charge of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby invoking the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) advised setting a base offense level at 26 because the offense included a sawed-off firearm as outlined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and because Cutbank had two or more prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR also recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement based on his reckless creation of a risk of death or serious injury during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in connection with a separate felony, leading to a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory guideline range for sentencing was calculated at 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level increase for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

The enhancement is triggered if the defendant either used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At sentencing, the district court accepted, without objection, the Presentence Investigation Report’s factual assertions about Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Therefore, the court did not err, let alone commit clear error, in concluding that the facts in the PSR demonstrate that the firearm was used in connection with another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that imposing the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) leads to a within-Guidelines sentence that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” requiring that the sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” While the jury determined that Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions, triggering penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the Guideline for Armed Career Criminals would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level is to be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When determining a suitable sentence, "a district court must initially and correctly compute the relevant Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "A court of appeals cannot address the substantive reasonableness of a sentence until it confirms that the district court did not make any significant procedural errors, such as failing to compute, or improperly computing, the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this, the court evaluates whether the factors listed in § 3553(a) justify either a departure from or a variance from the sentence recommended by the advisory guidelines.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence that fell within the applicable guidelines range, and such a sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). At the sentencing hearing, the court stated that it had reviewed the Presentence Investigation Report and the sentencing memoranda, as well as considered the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “A district court is not required to engage in a mechanical recitation of the factors it considered to uphold the sentence; it is enough if the record demonstrates the district court actually took the § 3553(a) factors into account when determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record included nearly twelve previous felony convictions, several of which entailed violence and firearm offenses. The conviction in this matter stemmed from acts of domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s residence in the presence of her children, and injuring a law enforcement officer while resisting arrest. The district court exercised its broad discretion in sentencing and did not impose a sentence that was substantively unreasonable when it sentenced Cutback to 292 months of imprisonment, which is within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
