Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of possessing a firearm as a felon, in contravention of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e); Cutbank has a significant criminal background. Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court imposed a 292-month prison sentence, which fell within the advisory guidelines range. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that resulted in an unfair trial and misapplied a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, which elevated the advisory guideline range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night between November 19 and 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and compelled her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions regarding “listening devices” in the residence and alleging that individuals outside intended to harm him. D.F. was held in the closet at gunpoint for what she described in testimony as several hours, during which Cutbank threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the firearm. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake up others sleeping in the home, specifically her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank departed, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. Subsequently, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home indicating that a man was present in the garage and refusing to leave. Upon the officers' arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage, during which he made a series of erratic comments over several hours and asserted he was armed. When police attempted to take him into custody, Cutbank resisted arrest, bit an officer on the face and neck, and grabbed for the officer’s firearm. Officers searching the likely route that Cutbank would have taken between D.F.’s residence and the garage discovered D.F.’s cellphone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a modified (sawed-off) barrel and stock. DNA analysis on the firearm identified Cutbank as a match. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. recognized and identified the weapon as the same one they had seen in Cutbank’s possession prior to that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession as charged and determined he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thus subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court abused its discretion through various pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in enhancing his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents several arguments regarding evidentiary and procedural matters, but offers little analysis and cites no authority to bolster these claims. All of these assertions lack merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from testifying that they had previously seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, on the grounds that such testimony would constitute propensity evidence in violation of Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government’s position, finding that the evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge as well as to demonstrating Cutbank’s continuous possession of the firearm prior to the charged event. We concur with this assessment. As noted in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had witnessed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of times” before the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank moved for a jury instruction clarifying, in order to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems, that D.F.’s statements made to police could not be treated as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself had testified regarding those occurrences. The prosecution contended that the statements were relevant to show the context behind the police officers’ actions and knowledge at the time, asserting that the officers' testimonies would not contravene hearsay restrictions, and further, that a bench instruction provided later—should it be necessary—was both conventional and adequate. As held in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), “[w]e review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” It is not required that a court provide a limiting instruction to the jury for each piece of evidence as it is admitted, provided that a suitable instruction is given at the end of trial. Id. The present record indicates that the district court did not permit the introduction of hearsay evidence during the proceedings. Additionally, D.F. gave live testimony. The Confrontation Clause is met where “the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person,” as stated in United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Accordingly, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on a “handful of times” prior to the incident in question. Cutback’s renewed objection to this testimony was denied by the district court. Additionally, the court overruled a defense objection when C.F., testifying for the government, indicated that she had observed the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” previously. On appeal, Cutback fails to cite any authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that such evidence was intrinsic and relevant both to establishing the possession charge and to showing continued possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier inconsistent statements related to the gun, including her prior assertion that she did not recall seeing the gun on the relevant evening, D.F. explained that some of her statements were influenced by the fact that she was “not familiar with guns” and “did not like guns,” adding, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The district court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the testimony referring to D.F.’s sister’s death by gun. This statement was given in response to questions during cross-examination concerning D.F.’s inconsistent statements. Cutback has not identified any particular prejudice or confusion caused by this testimony, nor is any evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, C.F., another government witness, referred to D.F.’s sister having been killed by a gun, and there was no objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed its considerable discretion concerning evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s statement that she had previously seen the gun involved in the incident "once or twice" by cross-examining her about earlier remarks she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted questioning about what C.F. told the police, but upheld the government's objections when defense counsel tried to have portions of the police report read into evidence without establishing an appropriate foundation, as C.F. was not the report's author. Although Cutback did not reference this ruling on appeal, the government’s response asserted that Cutback's "next claim is that the district court 'thwarted' his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession." We do not favor appellees who broaden the appeal’s issues unnecessarily. Regardless, preserved or not, this "claim" has no substance. As held in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), "Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated." Thus, the court was within its discretion in insisting on proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not overturn an erroneous evidentiary decision if the error was harmless. . . . That is, we will only grant reversal if the improperly admitted evidence may have had a significant impact on the jury’s decision.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to satisfy this standard. The government presented considerable testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the evidentiary decisions of the district court “either did not affect the verdict or affected it only minimally.” Id. at 998. “A conviction will not be set aside due to the cumulative effect of trial errors unless those errors result in substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. The jury further determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act. According to the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), the recommended base offense level was 26, citing the presence of a sawed-off firearm as described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank's record of at least two previous convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR added a three-level enhancement due to the firearm's altered or obliterated serial number, increased the offense level by two because Cutbank recklessly endangered others by creating a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury while evading law enforcement, and recommended an additional four-level enhancement based on his possession of the firearm in relation to another felony offense. This produced a total offense level of 35. The PSR also assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, leading to an advisory guidelines range of imprisonment between 292 and 365 months. On appeal, Cutbank challenges the district court’s application of the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing hearing, the district court accepted the PSR's description of Cutback’s prior felony convictions as accurate, and there was no objection to these facts. Accordingly, the court’s determination that the PSR established the firearm was connected to another felony offense was not erroneous, let alone clearly erroneous. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (review standard), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) four-level increase produces a sentence within the advisory range that allegedly “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” by resulting in a term greater than “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” While the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, which triggers the penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act, application of the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level must be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court must first accurately determine the correct Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to addressing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, an appellate court is required to confirm that the district court has not made a significant procedural mistake, such as neglecting to calculate or miscalculating the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates whether the factors outlined in § 3553(a) support a deviation or variance from the recommended Guidelines sentence.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the proper guidelines range, which we treat as presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceedings, the court acknowledged that it had reviewed the Presentence Report (PSR), the sentencing briefs, and examined the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a departure. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal record included nearly twelve previous felony convictions, with a number of those offenses involving violence and the use of firearms. The instant offense consisted of domestic violence, kidnapping, assaulting the victim in her residence in front of her children, and causing injury to a law enforcement officer while resisting arrest. The district court exercised its broad sentencing discretion appropriately and did not issue a substantively unreasonable sentence by sentencing Cutback to 292 months of imprisonment within the applicable range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
