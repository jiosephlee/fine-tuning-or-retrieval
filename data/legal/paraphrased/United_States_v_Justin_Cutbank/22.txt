Case Name: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Jurisdiction: United States Court of Appeals, Eighth Circuit  
Date of Decision: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant prior criminal record, guilty of possessing a firearm as a felon in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term falling within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made various evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and that it erroneously applied a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, resulting in a higher advisory guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., held a firearm to her face, and forced her into a closet, making paranoid allegations regarding “listening devices” within the residence and claiming that individuals outside intended to harm him. He detained D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what she described as seeming like several hours, threatened to kill her, and struck her in the face with the firearm. At one stage, Cutbank instructed D.F. to awaken the others in the house, which included her two teenage sons and her roommate, C.F. Subsequently, Cutbank escaped, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. D.F. then contacted the police.

Police were also alerted by a call from a residence situated about half a mile away from D.F.'s home, where the caller reported that a man was present in the garage and was refusing to leave. Upon arrival, officers encountered Cutbank, who had barricaded himself inside the garage, made disjointed statements over several hours, and asserted that he was armed. Cutbank actively resisted arrest efforts by the officers, biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer's firearm. A search along the route likely traversed by Cutbank between D.F.’s home and the garage resulted in the discovery of D.F.'s cellphone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with both its barrel and stock sawed off. Testing revealed that Cutbank’s DNA was present on the firearm. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession as charged, and further determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions outlined in § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, alleging that the district court committed abuse of discretion through various pretrial and trial rulings and erred in applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments concerning evidence and procedure but offers virtually no supporting case law or substantial analysis for these claims. Each of these arguments is without merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from offering testimony that they had previously seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government’s position, determining that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence relevant both to the possession charge and to establish continuous possession of the firearm prior to the charged event. We likewise concur. As explained in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” At trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of times” preceding the incident in question.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank sought an instruction to the jury that, in order to preclude hearsay issues and concerns related to the Confrontation Clause, D.F.’s statements to law enforcement could not be regarded as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. provided testimony regarding those events herself. The government countered that these statements were introduced to provide background for the officers’ actions and knowledge, asserted that officers’ testimony would not violate hearsay prohibitions, and further argued that, if necessary, it was both typical and adequate for any bench instruction to be delivered subsequently. As stated in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), “[w]e review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” The court is not required to provide jury instructions regarding individual items of evidence as they are presented, so long as an appropriate instruction is furnished at the conclusion of the trial. Id. In this instance, the record reflects that hearsay was not admitted during trial. Moreover, D.F. took the stand and testified. The Confrontation Clause is satisfied, as explained in United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted), “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Therefore, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. When Cutback renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court overruled it. Similarly, the court rejected a defense objection to testimony from C.F., a government witness, who indicated that she had seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” previously. On appeal, Cutback does not present any authority that contradicts the district court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence, pertinent both to establishing the possession charge and to showing that Cutback continuously possessed the firearm before the event. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent remarks about the firearm, specifically her claim that she did not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night, D.F. explained that some of her prior statements were due to her being “not familiar with guns” and her dislike of guns because “her sister was killed by a gun.” The court overruled Cutback’s request to strike the testimony that D.F.’s sister died from a gun. This information was provided in response to cross-examination regarding her inconsistent prior accounts. Cutback fails to identify any undue prejudice or confusion resulting from the testimony, nor is any evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., testifying for the government, also referenced the death of D.F.’s sister by firearm without any objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed its wide discretion in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had seen the firearm used during the incident “once or twice” before, by cross-examining her about earlier remarks she had made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into what C.F. communicated to law enforcement, but upheld the prosecution’s objections to defense counsel attempting to read specific sections of the related police report into evidence without proper foundation, on the grounds that C.F. was not the author of the report. While Cutback’s appellate brief did not mention this decision, the government maintained that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not encourage appellees to broaden the appeal’s scope without necessity. Regardless, whether or not the issue has been preserved, the “claim” lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The court acted within its discretion in insisting on proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutbank concedes, "this court does not overturn an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the mistake was harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only if the jury could have been significantly affected by the erroneously admitted evidence." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to overcome this standard. The government presented ample evidence, including both witness testimony and physical exhibits. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions "either did not affect or had only a minimal effect on the outcome of the verdict." Id. at 998. "We will only set aside a conviction due to the collective impact of trial mistakes if the defendant suffered substantial prejudice." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, it determined that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thus subjecting him to the increased penalties mandated by the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (“PSR”) proposed a base offense level of 26, based on the offense’s involvement of a sawed-off firearm as specified in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and because Cutbank had a minimum of two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement based on Cutbank’s reckless endangerment of life or serious injury while evading law enforcement, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense, bringing the total offense level to 35. The PSR assigned Cutbank a Criminal History Category of VI, resulting in a recommended sentencing range under the guidelines of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. Cutbank, on appeal, contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level increase for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense as set forth in § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant has used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony under federal, state, or local law, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court adopted the Presentence Investigation Report’s factual findings about Cutback’s past felony convictions, and did so without any objection. Therefore, the court did not make a mistake—let alone a clear one—in concluding that the information in the PSR demonstrates the firearm was utilized in connection with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Nevertheless, Cutbank contends that applying the 4-level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) yields a sentence within the advisory range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” requiring that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions, resulting in penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the guideline for an Armed Career Criminal would assign a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines stipulate that, in such cases, the higher offense level is to be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, “a district court must start by properly determining the relevant Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “A court of appeals cannot address the substantive reasonableness of a sentence until it has verified that the district court did not make a significant procedural error, such as neglecting to determine (or mistakenly determining) the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterwards, the court evaluates whether the factors set forth in § 3553(a) justify deviating or departing from the recommended guidelines sentence.

In this case, the district court sentenced the defendant to a term falling within the appropriate guidelines range, a sentence that we presume to be reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceedings, the court stated it had examined the PSR and the submitted sentencing memoranda, and it also considered the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. As such, “the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback possessed nearly twelve previous felony convictions, including several involving violence and the use of firearms. The underlying offense was comprised of domestic abuse, kidnapping, assaulting the victim in her home with her children present, and causing injury to a police officer during resistance to arrest. The district court’s imposition of a 292-month imprisonment, within the range, did not constitute an abuse of its wide sentencing discretion or result in a substantively unreasonable sentence.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
