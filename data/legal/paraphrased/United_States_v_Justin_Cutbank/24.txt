Caption: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date of Decision: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant prior criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term falling within the recommended guidelines range. On appeal, Cutbank challenges his conviction and sentence, alleging that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that deprived him of a fair trial, and that the court incorrectly applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which raised the applicable guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after having consumed alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while making paranoid claims regarding “listening devices” inside the residence and people outside intent on causing him harm. While confining D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described as feeling like several hours, he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the firearm. At some point during the incident, Cutbank instructed D.F. to awaken other individuals sleeping in the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Eventually, Cutbank left, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the gun with him. D.F. then contacted law enforcement.

Additionally, law enforcement received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile away from D.F.'s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and was refusing to leave. Upon arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage and, during several hours, made unpredictable statements and asserted that he possessed a weapon. Cutbank actively resisted officers' efforts to detain him, biting one officer on the face and neck and grabbing for the officer's firearm. When police searched the likely route Cutbank would have traveled between D.F.'s residence and the garage, they recovered D.F.'s cell phone, a large butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had been altered with a shortened barrel and stock. DNA analysis conducted on the firearm indicated a match to Cutbank. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. recognized the gun as one they had observed in Cutbank's possession prior to that night. Ultimately, the jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm, and determined that he had been previously convicted of no fewer than three violent felonies, resulting in the application of § 924(e)'s enhanced sentencing provisions. Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court erred in several discretionary rulings made both before and during the trial and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple challenges regarding evidence and procedure, but fails to cite relevant case law or offer meaningful analysis to substantiate his claims. These arguments lack merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from offering testimony that they had seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle on previous occasions, contending that such evidence would constitute improper propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court, siding with the government, found that this testimony was intrinsic to the case and pertinent to establishing the possession offense, as well as showing uninterrupted possession of the firearm leading up to the incident. We concur with this determination. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank asked that the jury be instructed—so as to prevent potential hearsay and Confrontation Clause complications—not to consider D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself had provided testimony regarding the incidents in question. The government responded that these statements would serve to clarify the context surrounding law enforcement actions and the officers' understanding at the relevant times, that the officers' accounts would not contravene hearsay prohibitions, and that, should it become necessary, a subsequent instruction from the court would ordinarily suffice. The standard of review for a district court’s refusal to give a limiting instruction is abuse of discretion. See United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not required to instruct the jury about each piece of evidence as it is received if it delivers suitable guidance at the conclusion of trial. Id. In this instance, the record shows that no hearsay evidence was admitted at trial. Moreover, D.F. took the stand and testified. The requirement of the Confrontation Clause is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Therefore, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, the court rejected a defense objection when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified she had seen the firearm used in the incident "once or twice" previously. On appeal, Cutback fails to provide any legal authority contesting the trial court’s earlier determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession offense and evidencing ongoing possession of the firearm before the event at issue. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier inconsistent statements concerning the firearm—including that she did not recall seeing the weapon on the night in question—D.F. explained that some of her remarks were due to her unfamiliarity with firearms and her aversion to them, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the testimony regarding the death of D.F.’s sister by gunshot. This evidence was given in response to cross-examination about contradictory previous statements. Cutback does not identify any unfair prejudice or potential for jury confusion arising from this testimony, nor is any such risk evident. Refer to Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, C.F., appearing as a government witness, also referenced D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun without objection from the defense. The district court’s broad discretion in matters of evidence was not abused in this instance. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to cast doubt on C.F.’s assertion that she had previously observed the firearm in question “once or twice,” by cross-examining her about earlier statements made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. had told police, but sustained the government's objections when defense counsel attempted to read sections of the police report into evidence without laying a sufficient foundation, since C.F. was not the author of that report. While Cutback did not mention this particular ruling on appeal, the government asserted in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We are not favorable towards appellees who broaden appellate issues without necessity. Regardless, whether or not this point is properly preserved, the argument lacks substance. As articulated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The court acted within its discretion in requiring that such evidence be properly authenticated.

Finally, as Cutback concedes, "this court will not overturn an incorrect evidentiary ruling unless the error affected the outcome. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only when there is a possibility the jury was significantly affected by the evidence admitted in error." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank is unable to satisfy this standard. The government's case included strong testimonial and physical evidence. Consequently, the district court’s evidentiary decisions "did not affect, or only minimally affected, the verdict." Id. at 998. "We will not set aside a conviction on account of the combined impact of trial errors in the absence of significant prejudice to the defendant." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm and determined that he had three or more previous violent felony convictions, thus subjecting him to the enhanced penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) set forth a base offense level of 26, citing that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and noted that Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR additionally recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm's altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement because Cutbank’s flight from police recklessly created a risk of death or serious injury, and a four-level enhancement on the grounds that he possessed the firearm in relation to another felony offense, producing a combined offense level of 35. The PSR also determined a Criminal History Category of VI, leading to an advisory guideline range of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank challenges the district court's application of the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant either used or possessed a firearm or ammunition in relation to a separate federal, state, or local felony offense, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted—without objection—the Presentence Investigation Report’s account of Cutback’s previous felony convictions as fact. Therefore, the court did not make an error, let alone commit clear error, in determining that the PSR’s facts established the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (setting forth standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutback, however, contends that imposing the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4-level enhancement results in a guidelines-range sentence that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” requiring that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury concluded that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions—leading to penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would yield a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level must be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, "the district court must start the sentencing process by accurately determining the relevant Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "An appellate court may address the substantive reasonableness of a sentence only after confirming that the district court has not made a significant procedural error, including a failure to calculate, or a miscalculation of, the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Subsequently, the court evaluates whether the § 3553(a) factors justify either departing from or varying the recommended range under the advisory guidelines.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence that fell within the applicable guideline range, which is presumed reasonable upon appellate review. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court indicated that it had examined the PSR and the sentencing memoranda, and had also considered the § 3553(a) factors that could support a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback's record included nearly twelve prior felony convictions, several of which involved violent conduct and the use of firearms. The crime underlying the conviction consisted of domestic violence, kidnapping, and assaulting the victim within her home and in the presence of her children, as well as causing injury to a police officer while attempting to evade arrest. By sentencing Cutback to a 292-month term of imprisonment, within the guideline range, the district court neither abused its considerable discretion nor imposed a sentence that was substantively unreasonable.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
