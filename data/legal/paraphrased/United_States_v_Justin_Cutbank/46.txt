Case Name: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date of Decision: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a lengthy criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term falling within the applicable advisory guidelines range it calculated. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and his sentence, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and erroneously imposed a sentencing enhancement pursuant to USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which raised the advisory guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following the consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., and aimed a firearm at her face. He forced her into a closet while making paranoid allegations concerning "listening devices" in the residence and individuals outside purportedly intending to cause him harm. D.F. testified that Cutbank held her in the closet under threat with the gun for what seemed to her to be several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the weapon. At a certain point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake others in the household, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the scene taking both D.F.'s cellphone and the firearm. Subsequently, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement received another report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, where an individual was found inside the garage and refused to exit. Upon arrival, officers encountered Cutbank, who barricaded himself in the garage and over several hours displayed erratic behavior, made various statements, and asserted that he possessed a weapon. When officers tried to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting an officer on the face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. A search along the probable route Cutbank used from D.F.’s residence to the garage uncovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with both its barrel and stock sawed off. The firearm bore DNA matching Cutbank. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. testified and recognized the rifle as one they had seen previously in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged felon in possession count and determined he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, invoking the heightened sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court erred in several evidentiary and procedural rulings before and during trial and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments regarding evidence and procedure, yet offers virtually no legal authority or substantive analysis to bolster these claims. All of these arguments are unpersuasive.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted propensity evidence prohibited by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government’s position, finding that the testimony was intrinsic to the offense and relevant for establishing the possession charge, as well as evidence of continuous possession of the firearm before the incident in question. We concur with this reasoning. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had witnessed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of times” preceding the incident at issue.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank asked that the jury be instructed, so as to prevent hearsay issues and potential violations of the Confrontation Clause, that it was not permitted to rely on D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as direct evidence of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. personally recounted the events on the stand. The prosecution maintained that D.F.’s statements would only serve to provide background for the police officers’ conduct and understanding at the relevant time, asserting that the officers’ testimonies would remain within the confines of the hearsay rules, and contended that any required limiting instruction delivered by the court at a later stage would be both standard practice and adequate. The standard of review for a district court’s refusal to issue a limiting instruction is abuse of discretion. See United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not compelled to instruct the jury about each individual piece of evidence at the moment of its introduction as long as it delivers suitable instructions to the jury at the conclusion of the proceedings. Id. In this case, the record indicates the district court did not admit any hearsay during the proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. did, in fact, testify at trial. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met when the declarant of the hearsay evidence “actually appear[s] in court and testif[ies] in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). No abuse of discretion occurred in this instance.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. When Cutback renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court rejected it. Additionally, the court overruled the defense's objection to testimony from C.F.—a witness for the government—who said she had seen the firearm used during the incident "once or twice" previously. On appeal, Cutback offers no legal authority to challenge the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence, pertinent to establishing the possession offense and to indicating that the firearm was in Cutback’s ongoing possession leading up to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier contradictory statements concerning the gun, specifically her claim of not recalling seeing the firearm on the relevant night, D.F. explained that certain remarks were due to her lack of “familiar[ity] with guns” and her dislike of firearms, explaining further, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The district court refused Cutback’s request to exclude from the record D.F.’s testimony that her sister had died due to gun violence. D.F.'s statements were elicited in direct response to questions raised during cross-examination regarding previous inconsistent statements. Cutback fails to identify, nor does the record demonstrate, any undue prejudice or confusion caused by this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., a witness for the government, also referred to the death of D.F.’s sister by gun without objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed the broad scope of its evidentiary authority. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s claim that she had previously seen the gun involved in the incident “once or twice” by cross-examining her concerning earlier statements she gave to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries regarding C.F.’s statements to police but upheld the government's objections to the defense's attempt to read parts of the related police report into evidence without an adequate foundation, noting that C.F. was not the author of the report. Though Cutback did not address this ruling directly on appeal, the government’s brief asserted that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove of appellees who broaden the scope of issues presented on appeal unnecessarily. Regardless, whether preserved or otherwise, this particular “claim” lacks merit. As articulated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (“quotation omitted”), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The court’s decision to require proper authentication did not constitute an abuse of discretion.

As Cutback concedes, "this court will not reverse a mistaken evidentiary decision unless the error affected the outcome. . . . Put another way, reversal is warranted only if the improperly admitted evidence might have had a significant impact on the jury’s decision." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank does not meet this standard. The government presented strong testimonial and physical evidence. Consequently, the evidentiary decisions made by the district court "did not affect or only minimally affected the verdict." Id. at 998. "We will not reverse a conviction due to the combined effect of errors at trial unless the defendant suffers substantial prejudice as a result." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26 based on the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as specified in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and because Cutbank had a minimum of two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR further recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm's altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase because he acted recklessly and endangered lives during a flight from law enforcement, and an additional four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These recommendations brought the total offense level to 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, which led to an advisory guideline sentencing range of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in imposing the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered if the defendant either used or had any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony under federal, state, or local law “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the factual details in the PSR concerning Cutback’s earlier felony convictions without any objection from the defense. Accordingly, the district court did not err, let alone commit clear error, in determining that the PSR’s facts support a finding that the firearm was involved in connection with another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (articulating the applicable standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank instead contends that imposing the 4-level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) leads to a sentence within the guideline range that is at odds with the “core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” which requires that sentences “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Even though the jury found that Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions, making Armed Career Criminal Act sanctions applicable, application of the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. According to the Guidelines, the highest offense level is to be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When determining the proper sentence, a district court must start the sentencing process by accurately computing the relevant Guidelines range. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). The appellate court, before reviewing whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, must confirm that the district court has avoided significant procedural errors, such as a failure to calculate or an incorrect calculation of the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court assesses if the considerations under § 3553(a) justify departing from or varying the sentence outside the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence falling within the properly calculated guidelines range, which is entitled to a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceedings, the district court confirmed that it had reviewed the PSR and the submitted sentencing memoranda, and it considered § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history included almost a dozen prior felony convictions, with many of those offenses involving violent conduct or firearms. The offense at issue entailed acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s home in the presence of her children, and the infliction of injury upon a police officer during an attempt to evade arrest. The district court’s exercise of its considerable discretion in sentencing did not result in a substantively unreasonable sentence by imposing a guidelines-range term of 292 months’ imprisonment.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
