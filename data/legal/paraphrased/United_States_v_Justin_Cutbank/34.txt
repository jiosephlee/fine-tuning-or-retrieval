Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant prior criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison, a term that fell within the advisory guidelines range. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, claiming that the district court made various evidentiary and procedural mistakes that deprived him of a fair trial and wrongly imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thus increasing his guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following the consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet. He leveled paranoid allegations regarding "listening devices" within the residence and purportedly dangerous individuals outside. While holding D.F. at gunpoint in the closet for what she described during testimony as hours, Cutbank threatened her with death and struck her face with the gun. At one stage, he demanded that D.F. wake the other occupants of the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Subsequently, Cutbank left the premises with both D.F.'s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. proceeded to contact law enforcement.

Additionally, law enforcement received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, indicating that an individual was present in the garage and refused to leave the premises. Upon police arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage and, over several hours, made unpredictable remarks and asserted that he possessed a weapon. When officers tried to take Cutbank into custody, he resisted by biting one officer's face and neck and attempting to seize the officer's firearm. Officers subsequently searched the likely route Cutbank took between D.F.'s house and the garage, and discovered D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA testing revealed Cutbank's genetic material on the firearm. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified this weapon as the same one they had observed Cutbank with earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge and determined that he had been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now appeals both his conviction and sentence, asserting that the district court abused its discretion through several pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in imposing an enhanced sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments concerning evidentiary and procedural matters, yet offers virtually no supporting legal authority or substantive analysis. These arguments are unfounded.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously seen him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute improper propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court sided with the government, determining that this evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as showing Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the charged incident. We concur. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the relevant incident.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought a jury instruction clarifying that, due to hearsay and Confrontation Clause considerations, the jury could not treat D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself testified about the matters. The prosecution maintained that these statements would only provide context for the actions and knowledge of the police officers at the relevant time, asserting that the officers’ accounts would not contravene hearsay rules and that any necessary instruction by the court could be appropriately given later, as is standard practice. The standard of review for a district court’s refusal to provide a limiting instruction is abuse of discretion. See United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The district court is not required to instruct the jury on every item of evidence at the time it is introduced so long as it issues an adequate instruction at the conclusion of the proceedings. Id. In this instance, the record reveals that hearsay evidence was not admitted at trial. Furthermore, D.F. did take the stand and gave testimony. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). The district court, therefore, did not abuse its discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident, describing it as a "handful of times." The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had previously seen the firearm used during the incident “once or twice” before, the court overruled a defense objection. In his appeal, Cutback fails to identify any authority challenging the court’s pretrial determination that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating continuous possession of the firearm preceding the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

When cross-examined about earlier inconsistent statements concerning the firearm, such as her statement that she did not recall seeing the gun on the relevant evening, D.F. explained that some of her statements were due to her lack of familiarity with guns and her dislike of them, stating, "[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun." The court refused to grant Cutback’s motion to strike D.F.’s remark about her sister’s death by firearm. This evidence was elicited in direct response to cross-examination about inconsistencies in her previous statements. Cutback does not identify any unfair prejudice or confusion arising from this testimony, nor is any apparent. Refer to Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., appearing as a government witness, also referenced D.F.’s sister’s death by gunshot without any objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed its considerable discretion over evidentiary rulings. See, for example, Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s testimony that she had seen the firearm involved in the event “once or twice” before, by questioning her about earlier statements she made to a police investigator during cross-examination. The district court permitted inquiries into C.F.’s statements to law enforcement but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel tried to read excerpts from the resulting police report into evidence without establishing the necessary foundation, since C.F. was not the report’s author. On appeal, Cutback did not directly contest this decision, but the government maintained in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not encourage appellees to broaden the issues presented on appeal without necessity. Regardless, whether this “claim” is preserved or not, it lacks merit. As explained in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (“quotation omitted”), “[a]lthough extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court did not exceed its discretionary authority by insisting on proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not overturn an incorrect evidentiary ruling unless the error was harmful. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only when the erroneously admitted evidence might have significantly influenced the jury’s decision.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government presented substantial testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s rulings on evidence “did not affect or had at most a minor effect on the outcome.” Id. at 998. “A conviction will not be set aside because of the combined impact of trial errors absent a showing of substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of felon in possession of a firearm. It further determined he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thus subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) assigned a base offense level of 26, due to the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and because Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also recommended a three-level increase based on the firearm’s altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily harm during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level increase for possession of the firearm in association with another felony offense, bringing the total offense level to 35. The PSR designated a Criminal History Category of VI, producing an advisory guideline range of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in imposing the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

The enhancement is applicable when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense, and this is true “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court incorporated the factual information from the PSR relating to Cutback’s earlier felony convictions, and there was no objection to those facts. As a result, the court did not commit error—clear or otherwise—when it determined that the PSR’s facts showed the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutback, instead, contends that applying the four-level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produces a guideline-range sentence that allegedly “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that a sentence must be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury found that Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions, subjecting him to Armed Career Criminal Act sanctions, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would yield a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the offense level that is higher will apply. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When determining a sentence, the district court must first accurately compute the relevant Guidelines range at the outset of any sentencing hearing. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to evaluating whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, a court of appeals must confirm that the district court has not committed a serious procedural mistake, such as neglecting to calculate or incorrectly determining the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court assesses whether the factors listed in § 3553(a) justify a deviation or variance from the advisory guideline sentence.

In this case, the district court sentenced Cutback within the guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court referenced its consideration of the PSR and the sentencing memoranda, as well as the § 3553(a) factors that could justify a variance. Therefore, “[t]he district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “A district court does not have to explicitly recite every factor it weighed to affirm a sentence; rather, the record must show that the court took account of the § 3553(a) factors when determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal record included nearly twelve prior felonies, several involving violence or firearms. The conviction stemmed from an incident involving domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault that took place inside the victim’s residence in front of her children, and injuring a law enforcement officer in the course of resisting arrest. The district court did not exceed its broad sentencing discretion nor deliver a substantively unreasonable sentence by imposing a within-guidelines term of 292 months of imprisonment.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
