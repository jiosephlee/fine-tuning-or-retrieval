Case Name: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a substantial criminal background, guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months of imprisonment, which fell within the advisory guidelines range it had calculated. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and incorrectly imposed a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement, thereby raising the applicable guidelines range. We affirm the district court's decision.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet. While doing so, he expressed paranoid suspicions about “listening devices” inside the residence and asserted that individuals outside intended to harm him. Cutbank held D.F. at gunpoint in the closet for a period that D.F. described as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her across the face with the firearm. At one point, he demanded that D.F. wake the other people in the home, among them her two teenage sons and her roommate, C.F. Cutbank ultimately left the premises with D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. Following his departure, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and refused to depart. Upon arrival, the police found that Cutbank had barricaded himself in the garage and, during several hours, made incoherent remarks and asserted possession of a weapon. When officers tried to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting an officer's face and neck and seizing the officer's firearm. In their search along the route Cutbank was presumed to have traveled from D.F.'s residence to the garage, officers discovered D.F.'s mobile phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with both the barrel and stock sawed off. DNA recovered from the rifle matched Cutbank's. During the trial, both D.F. and C.F. testified that they had previously seen Cutbank in possession of that weapon. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm, and further determined that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions set forth in § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several erroneous decisions before and throughout the trial and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in imposing a sentence enhancement.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments concerning evidentiary and procedural matters, but fails to cite authority or offer substantial analysis for these points. Each of these arguments lacks merit.

A

Prior to the trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their earlier observations of him possessing the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court, however, sided with the government's position that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge as well as ongoing possession of the firearm preceding the relevant event. We concur with this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. gave testimony that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank sought an instruction to the jury that, in order to avoid hearsay concerns and issues arising under the Confrontation Clause, they should refrain from considering D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct until such time as D.F. provided her own testimony concerning the events in question. The government responded that the statements were offered to establish the background for the actions and contemporaneous understanding of the police officers, and maintained that the testimony of the officers would comply with hearsay restrictions; moreover, the government stated that, should it be necessary, a subsequent instruction from the court would be both standard and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to provide a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to precedent, the court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In the present matter, the record reflects that the court did not admit hearsay evidence during trial proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. gave testimony. The Confrontation Clause is deemed satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Thus, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle several times prior to the event in question. When Cutback renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court denied it. Similarly, the court overruled a defense objection when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had seen the firearm used during the incident on one or two previous occasions. In his appeal, Cutback fails to present any authority challenging the court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and to showing Cutback’s ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier statements that conflicted regarding the firearm—including assertions that she did not recall observing the weapon on the relevant evening—D.F. explained that some of those statements stemmed from her lack of familiarity with firearms and her aversion to them, noting, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.'s testimony referencing her sister's death by gun. This evidence was offered in direct response to the cross-examination on her inconsistent statements. Cutback has not demonstrated that this testimony resulted in undue prejudice or confusion, nor is any such effect apparent from the record. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., testifying for the government, also referred to D.F.'s sister’s death by firearm without any objection from the defense. The district court thus acted within the wide latitude it possesses over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to cast doubt on C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident "once or twice" by questioning her about earlier statements she had made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries about what C.F. had communicated to law enforcement but sustained the government’s objections when defense counsel tried to read sections of the subsequent police report into evidence without first establishing a proper foundation, as C.F. was not the author of that report. On appeal, Cutback did not explicitly address this ruling; nonetheless, the government’s appellate brief contended that Cutback's "next claim is that the district court 'thwarted' his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession." We do not look favorably upon appellees who unnecessarily broaden the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, whether or not this particular issue was preserved, the “claim” is unsubstantiated. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion by requiring authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, "this court will not overturn an incorrect evidentiary decision if the mistake was harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only where the jury's decision could have been meaningfully affected by the inadmissible evidence." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government presented strong testimonial and physical evidence. Accordingly, the district court’s evidentiary decisions "either did not affect, or only minimally affected, the verdict." Id. at 998. "We decline to reverse a conviction on account of the combined impact of multiple trial errors absent a showing of significant prejudice to the defendant." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the charge of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that he had three or more previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) assigned a base offense level of 26 on the grounds that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and that Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also recommended a three-level increase because the firearm’s serial number had been altered or obliterated, an additional two-level enhancement based on the finding that Cutbank recklessly endangered life or caused a risk of serious bodily injury during flight from law enforcement, and a further four-level increase for possession of the firearm in connection with another felony under the provisions of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), resulting in a total offense level calculation of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the resulting advisory guidelines range was 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in imposing the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense as set forth in § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant employs or has possession of a firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing stage, the district court incorporated the Presentence Investigation Report’s factual accounts of Cutback’s earlier felony convictions without any objection. Accordingly, the court did not make any error—let alone a clear error—when it determined that the PSR’s facts established the firearm’s involvement with another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (discussing the standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutback contends instead that applying the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), even if within the guideline range, produces a sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)"—namely, that the sentence should "be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection." Although the jury determined that Cutback had three or more qualifying violent felony convictions, leading to penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would assign a total offense level of 34. According to the Guidelines, the higher offense level must be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, the district court must start by accurately computing the relevant Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). An appellate court cannot address whether a sentence is substantively reasonable without first determining that the district court did not make a material procedural mistake, such as neglecting to compute, or miscomputing, the Guidelines range. See Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court assesses whether the factors outlined in § 3553(a) justify a deviation or variance from the sentence indicated by the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the guideline range, and such a sentence is entitled to a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the district judge acknowledged having reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda, and specifically considered the § 3553(a) factors relevant to the possibility of a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” Furthermore, precedent holds that a district court is not obligated to itemize each factor considered, so long as the record reflects that the § 3553(a) factors were actually taken into account in crafting the sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback had almost twelve previous felony convictions, several of which were for offenses involving firearms and violence. The present conviction was for conduct that included domestic abuse, kidnapping, an assault that took place in the presence of the victim’s children at her home, and inflicting injury on a police officer while resisting arrest. The district court acted within its broad discretion at sentencing and did not impose a sentence that was substantively unreasonable in ordering a 292-month prison term within the guideline range for Cutback.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
