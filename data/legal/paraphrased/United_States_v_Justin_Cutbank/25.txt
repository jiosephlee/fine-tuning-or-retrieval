Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon with a significant prior criminal record. Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison, a term falling within the advisory guideline range calculated by the court. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, claiming the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and improperly imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), thereby increasing his guideline range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions regarding “listening devices” within the residence and claiming that individuals outside intended to harm him. Cutbank held D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. estimated to be several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the weapon. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake others in the home, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Cutbank eventually left the scene, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. Following his departure, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence situated approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, where an individual was found in the garage and refused to depart. Upon their arrival, officers encountered Cutbank, who barricaded himself inside the garage, during which he displayed erratic behavior for several hours and asserted he was armed. When officers attempted to take him into custody, Cutbank resisted by biting one of the officers on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. A search conducted along the route that Cutbank likely traveled from D.F.’s residence to the garage yielded D.F.’s cellphone, a large kitchen knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle modified with a shortened barrel and stock. Forensic testing determined that DNA found on the rifle belonged to Cutbank. During the proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. identified this rifle as the one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession prior to that evening. The jury ultimately found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm, as charged, and determined he had three or more prior violent felony convictions, thereby invoking the increased sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court committed several abuses of discretion in pretrial and trial decisions and improperly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to lengthen his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents several evidentiary and procedural arguments but offers virtually no supporting case law or substantive analysis. All of these assertions lack merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their previous observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted propensity evidence in violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government's position that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence relevant to establishing the possession charge and to illustrating a pattern of continuous possession of the firearm prior to the alleged incident. We concur with that assessment. “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015). During the trial, D.F. provided testimony that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions preceding the incident in question.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank moved for a jury instruction precluding the jury from treating D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct until such time as D.F. personally recounted the events during her testimony, raising concerns about hearsay and Confrontation Clause violations. In response, the government contended that such statements were admissible to provide context for the officers' actions and understanding, asserted that officers' testimony would comply with hearsay prohibitions, and maintained that a subsequent jury instruction by the court—should it become necessary—was standard and would suffice. The standard of review for a district court’s refusal to issue a limiting instruction is abuse of discretion. United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). Moreover, as stated in Bennett, it is not mandatory for the court to instruct the jury on each individual piece of evidence at the moment of its admission, so long as a proper instruction is delivered at the end of trial. Examination of the record reflects that hearsay was not introduced during trial, and, furthermore, D.F. did in fact testify. The Confrontation Clause is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Accordingly, the district court did not abuse its discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle several times prior to the incident, describing it as a "handful of times." The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Similarly, when C.F., a witness for the government, indicated that she had seen the firearm used in the incident on “once or twice” before, the court rejected a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback does not provide any authority that challenges the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constitutes intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showing the defendant’s ongoing possession of the firearm leading up to the event. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

When questioned on cross-examination about earlier inconsistent remarks concerning the firearm—including her claim of not recalling the gun on the night in question—D.F. explained that her responses stemmed from her lack of familiarity with guns and her aversion to them, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the testimony referencing the death of D.F.’s sister by firearm. This information was given in reply to cross-examination about inconsistencies in her previous statements. Cutback does not cite any resulting undue prejudice or confusion from this testimony, nor is any evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, during the government’s presentation, C.F., testifying for the prosecution, referenced D.F.’s sister’s death by gunfire without objection from the defense. The district court did not overstep its considerable discretion regarding evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to discredit C.F.'s testimony that she had previously observed the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” by cross-examining her about statements she had made earlier to a police investigator. The district court permitted questioning about C.F.’s statements to law enforcement but upheld the government's objections when defense counsel attempted to read sections of the police report into evidence without adequate foundation, since C.F. was not the author of that report. Although Cutback did not directly address this ruling on appeal, the government’s appellate brief responded by asserting that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably upon appellees who unnecessarily broaden the matters at issue on appeal. Regardless, whether the issue was preserved or not, this “claim” lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion in insisting upon proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, "this court does not reverse a mistaken evidentiary ruling if the error is harmless... That is, we reverse only where the improperly admitted evidence could have significantly affected the jury’s verdict." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The prosecution presented strong testimonial and physical evidence. Accordingly, the evidentiary determinations by the district court "either did not affect or only minimally affected the outcome." Id. at 998. "We do not set aside a conviction due to the aggregate effect of trial errors except where the defendant suffers substantial prejudice." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm and additionally determined that he had at least three previous violent felony convictions, making him subject to the enhanced penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) set forth a base offense level of 26 on account of the offense involving a sawed-off firearm as identified under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank's record of at least two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR recommended a three-level enhancement due to the firearm's serial number being altered or obliterated, a two-level enhancement for recklessly endangering others’ lives or causing serious bodily injury while fleeing law enforcement, and a further four-level increase for possessing the weapon in connection with another felony offense, bringing the total offense level to 35. According to the PSR, Cutbank’s Criminal History Category was assessed at VI, producing an advisory sentencing guidelines range of 292 to 365 months in prison. Cutbank appeals, claiming that the district court erred in applying the four-level sentencing increase for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense, as set forth in § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered if the defendant either used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with a separate federal, state, or local felony, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the PSR’s factual findings about Cutback’s prior felony convictions without any objection. Accordingly, the court did not err, let alone commit clear error, when it determined based on the PSR that the firearm was linked to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank instead maintains that the application of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) four-level increase produces a sentence within the guideline range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” that any sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Even though the jury determined that Cutback had three or more prior violent felony convictions, thus invoking penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would yield a total offense level of 34. According to the Guidelines, the higher offense level must be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court must initiate sentencing proceedings by accurately determining the applicable Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to reviewing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, a court of appeals must confirm that the district court did not make a significant procedural error, such as neglecting to compute the Guidelines range or calculating it incorrectly. See Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates whether application of the § 3553(a) factors justifies departing from or varying the sentence suggested by the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court handed down a sentence that fell within the appropriate guidelines range, a type of sentence we generally consider reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court referenced its review of the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and the sentencing memoranda, and addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. As stated, “[t]hus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). The court “does not need to mechanically recite the factors it considered; it is sufficient that the record demonstrates actual consideration of the § 3553(a) factors when determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal record included nearly twelve prior felony convictions, several involving violence and firearm offenses. In this case, his conviction stemmed from conduct involving domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s home in front of her children, and injuring a law enforcement officer during arrest resistance. Under these circumstances, the district court did not overstep its substantial discretion in sentencing or impose an unreasonably harsh sentence by ordering a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guideline range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
