Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant history of felony convictions, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him under the Armed Career Criminal Act to 292 months in prison, a sentence that fell within the advisory guideline range calculated by the court. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and that it erroneously applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which raised the advisory guideline range. We affirm the district court’s judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet. While doing so, he expressed paranoia, making accusations concerning the presence of “listening devices” in the residence and alleging that individuals were outside intending to do him harm. Cutbank held D.F. at gunpoint within the closet for what she testified seemed like several hours, issuing death threats and hitting her in the face with the gun. At a certain juncture, he commanded D.F. to wake the other occupants of the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank departed with both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and refusing to depart. Upon arrival, officers found Cutbank barricaded inside the garage. Over a span of several hours, he made unpredictable statements and asserted he was armed. Cutbank resisted officers' attempts to take him into custody, biting one officer’s face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. Police conducting a search along the route Cutbank was likely to have traveled between D.F.’s residence and the garage discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with both barrel and stock sawed off. The rifle’s DNA matched Cutbank’s profile. During the trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession earlier that night. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge, as well as of having at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, which subjected him to the heightened sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now contests his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court abused its discretion through several rulings before and during trial, and erred in applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) as a basis for sentence enhancement.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments regarding evidentiary and procedural matters, yet offers virtually no supporting case law or substantive analysis. These arguments are without merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their earlier observations of him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute improper propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court determined, in accordance with the government’s position, that this testimony would be intrinsic to the case, as it bore relevance to the possession charge and served to establish continued possession of the firearm leading up to the relevant event. We concur with this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident at issue.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank requested a jury instruction clarifying that, in order to avoid hearsay problems and concerns under the Confrontation Clause, the jury should not treat D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. provided her own testimony about the incidents. The government countered that these statements would serve to provide context for the officers’ actions and awareness at the relevant times, that the officers’ recounting of these statements would not violate the prohibition against hearsay, and that, if necessary, a subsequent instruction from the court would be both conventional and adequate. The standard of review for a district court’s refusal to issue a limiting instruction is abuse of discretion. See United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not obligated to direct the jury as to each piece of evidence at the moment it is admitted, provided a suitable instruction is given at the conclusion of the trial. Id. In this matter, the record reflects that the court did not introduce hearsay evidence during the proceedings. Furthermore, D.F. took the stand and testified. The Confrontation Clause requirement is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Accordingly, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court rejected Cutbank’s renewed objection to this portion of D.F.'s testimony. Similarly, when C.F. appeared as a government witness and testified that she had witnessed the same firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” previously, the court overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutbank fails to identify any authority challenging the district court’s pretrial decision that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and indicating ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about her previous inconsistent statements concerning the firearm—including her claim that she did not recall observing the gun on the relevant evening—D.F. explained that some of her earlier comments arose because she was “not familiar with guns” and “did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s statement about her sister being killed by a firearm. This statement was made in response to cross-examination about her conflicting prior remarks. Cutback fails to identify any unfair prejudice or confusion stemming from the testimony, and there is no apparent prejudice. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., appearing as a government witness, also referred to D.F.’s sister being killed with a gun, and no objection was raised by the defense. The district court did not exceed its broad discretion regarding evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the firearm “once or twice” during the incident by questioning her about statements she had made earlier to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. conveyed to law enforcement but upheld the government’s objections against defense counsel’s attempt to read segments of the police report into evidence without adequate foundation, as C.F. was not the report’s author. Cutback did not mention this specific ruling on appeal; however, the government contended in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not approve when appellees unnecessarily broaden the scope of issues on appeal. Regardless, irrespective of whether the issue was preserved, the “claim” lacks merit. As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The court acted within its discretion in requiring authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court does not reverse an improper evidentiary ruling if the mistake was harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only if the improperly admitted evidence could have significantly influenced the jury.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government presented ample testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the evidentiary determinations by the district court “either did not affect the verdict or had only minimal impact.” Id. at 998. “A conviction will not be set aside on the grounds of cumulative trial error unless the defendant suffered significant prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further determined that he had at least three previous violent felony convictions, subjecting him to the enhanced penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26, noting the involvement of a sawed-off firearm under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and at least two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR also included a three-level enhancement for the possession of a firearm with an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly endangering the lives or causing a risk of serious injury to others while fleeing police, and a four-level enhancement because he used the firearm in relation to another felony, culminating in a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory guidelines indicated a sentencing range between 292 and 365 months of imprisonment. Cutbank, on appeal, contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level increase for possessing a firearm in relation to another felony under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered if the defendant either used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony under federal, state, or local law, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court, without any objection, adopted as true the factual recitations in the PSR regarding Cutback’s earlier felony convictions. Consequently, the court did not err—let alone commit clear error—in concluding that the PSR’s facts demonstrate the firearm was used in connection with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that imposing the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) results in a sentence within the guideline range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” which mandates that a sentence must “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” While the jury concluded that Cutback had three or more previous convictions for violent felonies—triggering penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines specify that the higher offense level governs. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, “a district court must start each sentencing hearing by accurately determining the appropriate Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “An appellate court, prior to evaluating the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, must confirm that the district court did not commit a significant procedural error, including the failure to compute, or the incorrect computation of, the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Following this, the court assesses whether the § 3553(a) factors justify either a departure from or variance with the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the relevant guidelines range, which is presumed reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the district court acknowledged it had considered both the PSR and the sentencing memoranda, and addressed the § 3553(a) factors that could support a variance. As a result, “the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). We do not require the district court to recite the § 3553(a) factors in a rote manner to affirm the sentence; it suffices that the record demonstrates the court actually took those factors into account in reaching its sentencing decision. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal record includes nearly twelve prior felony convictions, many involving firearms and violent conduct. The crime at issue entailed domestic violence, kidnaping, and assaulting the victim in her residence with children present, as well as injuring a police officer while evading arrest. The district court’s imposition of a 292-month prison sentence, within the guidelines range, was not an abuse of its considerable discretion in sentencing nor was it substantively unreasonable.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
