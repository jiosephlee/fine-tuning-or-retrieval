Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) for possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term that falls within the recommended guidelines range calculated by the court. On appeal, he challenges both his conviction and sentence, claiming that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial, and further erred by applying a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement that raised his advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night between November 19 and 20, 2020, after consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet. He made paranoid allegations about supposed “listening devices” within the residence and individuals outside intending to harm him. While in the closet, Cutbank held D.F. at gunpoint for what she perceived as several hours, threatened to kill her, and hit her in the face with the gun. At one juncture, he commanded D.F. to wake up other occupants of the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank departed, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Police were also contacted by a resident living approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, who reported that a man was inside their garage and refused to leave. Upon law enforcement's arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself within the garage, during which he made incoherent statements over several hours and asserted he was armed. When officers tried to detain him, Cutbank resisted, biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. During a search of the likely route Cutbank would have used between D.F.'s residence and the garage, officers located D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both a shortened barrel and stock. DNA analysis revealed that Cutbank’s DNA was present on the rifle. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified this firearm as one they had seen in Cutbank’s possession prior to that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm and determined he had three or more prior convictions for violent felonies, which subjected him to enhanced penalties under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence, asserting on appeal that the district court abused its discretion in several pretrial and trial rulings and that it incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in enhancing his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents several arguments regarding evidence and procedure, but he offers little analysis and cites no authority to bolster his claims. These arguments are unpersuasive.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their earlier observations of him in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony would constitute impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court, siding with the government, determined that this evidence was intrinsic to the offense charged and pertinent to demonstrating Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm leading up to the relevant incident. We concur with this determination. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” At trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested a jury instruction to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns, seeking clarification that D.F.’s statements to police should not be taken as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct unless D.F. herself testified about those events. The government maintained that such statements would serve solely to establish context for the officers’ actions and their awareness at the relevant times, asserting that the officers’ accounts would comply with hearsay rules, and that any necessary cautionary instruction could be properly delivered later from the bench, following customary practice. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court is not obligated to instruct the jury on each evidentiary item as it is introduced, provided an adequate instruction is delivered at the conclusion of the trial. Id. In this case, the record reflects that no hearsay evidence was admitted during trial. Moreover, D.F. took the stand and gave testimony. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Thus, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court rejected Cutbank’s renewed objection to this testimony. Additionally, the court overruled a defense objection when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice.” On appeal, Cutbank does not reference any legal authority contradicting the district court’s pretrial determination that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showing ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier inconsistent remarks concerning the firearm, including her claim that she did not recall seeing the weapon on the relevant night, D.F. stated that certain comments resulted from her being "not familiar with guns" and that she "did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun." The court rejected Cutback's motion to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister's death by firearm. This statement was provided in direct response to cross-examination about her inconsistent statements. Cutback has not identified, nor is there any clear indication of, unfair prejudice or confusion arising from this testimony. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., a witness for the government, referred to D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun without objection from the defense. There was no misuse of the district court’s substantial latitude in making evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to cast doubt on C.F.'s claim that she had previously seen the gun used in the incident "once or twice" by questioning her during cross-examination about earlier statements she gave to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries concerning what C.F. told the police but sustained the government's objections when the defense tried to read sections of the associated police report into evidence without establishing proper foundation, since C.F. was not the author of that report. On appeal, Cutback did not address this evidentiary ruling directly, but the government contended in its brief that Cutback was asserting as his “next claim” that the district court had “‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not favor appellees who broaden the issues on appeal without necessity. Regardless, whether this issue was preserved or not, the “claim” lacks substance. As established in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), while extrinsic evidence may be introduced to impeach a witness with prior inconsistent statements, such evidence must be properly authenticated. The court’s insistence on proper authentication does not constitute an abuse of discretion.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not overturn an evidentiary decision if any error committed was harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only if the jury’s verdict could have been significantly affected by the evidence improperly admitted.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government's presentation, which included both witness testimony and physical exhibits, was compelling. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence the verdict, or at most had only a minimal impact.” Id. at 998. “We decline to set aside a conviction due to the combined effect of errors at trial unless those errors result in substantial prejudice against the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to enhanced sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26, noting that the offense included a sawed-off firearm as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and that Cutbank had no fewer than two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR also recommended a three-level increase based on the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, an additional two-level increase because Cutbank recklessly endangered others by creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury during flight from law enforcement, and another four-level enhancement for using the firearm in relation to another felony offense. These enhancements resulted in a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the PSR arrived at an advisory guidelines range of imprisonment between 292 and 365 months. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is applicable when a defendant uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony offense under federal, state, or local law, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At sentencing, the district court, with no objections from the parties, accepted the Presentence Report’s factual findings concerning Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Therefore, the court did not make an error—let alone a clear error—in determining based on the PSR that the firearm was involved with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that applying the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) yields a sentence within the Guidelines range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)” requiring that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury found that Cutback had three or more previous violent felony convictions—triggering the penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act—the Guideline for Armed Career Criminals would have set a total offense level at 34. Under the Guidelines, the offense level that is higher must be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When fashioning a suitable sentence, “a district court must first accurately determine the applicable Guidelines range at the outset of the sentencing process.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Furthermore, “an appellate court cannot address the substantive reasonableness of a sentence until it has confirmed that the district court did not commit a significant procedural error, such as neglecting to calculate or incorrectly calculating the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this, the court assesses whether the § 3553(a) factors justify a deviation or variance from the sentence recommended by the advisory guidelines.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the guideline range, and such a sentence is considered presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing proceedings, the court indicated that it had considered the Presentence Report as well as the parties’ sentencing memoranda, and it examined the § 3553(a) factors that could justify a departure from the guidelines. “Accordingly, the district court was made aware of [Cutbank’s] contentions, and, as a result, we presume it took them into account and declined them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To affirm the sentence, we do not mandate that a district judge recite each factor individually; what matters is that the record shows the judge gave actual consideration to the § 3553(a) factors in arriving at the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback possessed nearly twelve prior felony convictions, several of which involved violent conduct or the use of firearms. The instant offense included acts of domestic abuse, kidnapping, an assault that took place in the victim’s residence in the presence of her children, and an injury to a police officer during the course of resisting arrest. The district court, therefore, did not exceed its considerable discretion in sentencing nor issue a sentence that was substantively unreasonable in sentencing Cutback to 292 months of imprisonment within the applicable range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
