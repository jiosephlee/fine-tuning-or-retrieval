Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e); Cutbank has a significant criminal background. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, a term falling within the calculated advisory guidelines range. On appeal, Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and misapplied the sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which increased the advisory range. We affirm the district court’s judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following alcohol consumption and methamphetamine use, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., and held a firearm to her face, instructing her to move into a closet. While exhibiting paranoia about alleged “listening devices” inside the residence and individuals outside intending to harm him, he forced D.F. to remain in the closet at gunpoint for what she described as seeming like several hours. During this time, he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the gun. At a certain point, Cutbank commanded D.F. to wake up other occupants in the house, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Eventually, Cutbank departed the residence, taking both the firearm and D.F.’s cell phone. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

The police were also contacted by residents of a home located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s house, reporting that a man had entered their garage and refused to leave. Upon police arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage, where, over several hours, he made incoherent statements and asserted he was armed. Cutbank resisted arrest, biting an officer’s face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. Officers subsequently searched the area along the probable route between D.F.’s home and the garage, discovering D.F.’s cellphone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle modified with a shortened barrel and stock. The DNA found on the firearm was linked to Cutbank. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the rifle as the same weapon they observed in Cutbank’s possession prior to the incident that night. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm, as well as of having at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, subjecting him to the enhanced penalties outlined in § 924(e). Cutbank now appeals both his conviction and his sentence, contending that the district court made multiple erroneous pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied the enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple objections related to evidence and procedure but offers little analysis and cites no relevant authority to bolster his claims. Each of these arguments lacks merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank sought to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their prior observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending such statements would constitute improper propensity evidence in contravention of Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government's position that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and supporting evidence of Cutbank’s ongoing control over the firearm preceding the alleged event. We are in agreement. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle “a handful of times” prior to the relevant incident.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank moved for a jury instruction that would prevent the jury from treating D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. herself provided testimony about those incidents, aiming to avoid hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems. The government maintained that these statements would merely explain the circumstances leading to officers’ actions and their contemporaneous knowledge, asserting that the officers’ statements would comply with hearsay rules, and that any necessary judicial instruction delivered subsequently would be standard practice and would suffice. As stated in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), “[w]e review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” According to that opinion, the court is not required to deliver limiting instructions to the jury for every piece of evidence upon admission, so long as an appropriate instruction is provided at the conclusion of the trial. Id. In this case, the record reflects that the court did not admit any hearsay during the proceedings. Additionally, D.F. did take the witness stand and testify. The Confrontation Clause requirement is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Therefore, no abuse of discretion occurred.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection regarding this line of testimony. Similarly, the court overruled another defense objection when C.F., a government witness, testified that she had seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” before that day. On appeal, Cutback does not reference any legal authority challenging the court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and to showing that Cutbank had continual possession of the firearm leading up to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

When cross-examined about previous statements that conflicted regarding the gun, specifically her claim that she could not recall seeing the gun the night in question, D.F. stated that some of her remarks stemmed from her lack of familiarity with guns and her aversion to them, explaining, “as [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister’s death by gun. D.F.’s statement was elicited in response to questioning concerning inconsistencies in her earlier statements. Cutback does not identify any undue prejudice or confusion arising from this testimony, nor does the record reveal any. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., while testifying for the government, likewise referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun, and defense counsel did not object. The district court’s broad discretion over evidentiary matters was not abused. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s statement that she had previously observed the gun “once or twice” by questioning her about statements she previously made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries concerning C.F.’s statements to the police but upheld the prosecution’s objections when defense counsel tried to read portions of the police report into evidence without adequate foundation, as C.F. was not the author of the report. While Cutback did not specifically address this district court ruling on appeal, the government's brief asserted that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We are not favorable toward appellees who unnecessarily broaden the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, whether or not the issue was properly preserved, the “claim” lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court did not abuse its discretion in insisting on appropriate authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, "this court will not overturn a mistaken evidentiary ruling if the mistake was harmless. . . . Stated differently, reversal is warranted only when the improperly admitted evidence could have had a significant impact on the jury." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank is unable to meet this standard. The government presented extensive testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court's evidentiary decisions either did not affect or only minimally affected the verdict. Id. at 998. "We will not reverse a conviction due to the cumulative effect of trial errors except where the defendant has suffered substantial prejudice." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby invoking the enhanced penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26 on the basis that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm, as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and that Cutbank had at least two previous convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR recommended additional enhancements: three levels for possession of a firearm with an altered or obliterated serial number, two levels for recklessly endangering the lives or safety of others by creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury during flight from law enforcement, and four levels for having the firearm in connection with another felony offense, culminating in a total offense level of 35. The Criminal History Category was calculated as VI, which yielded an advisory guidelines range of imprisonment between 292 and 365 months. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred when it imposed the four-level enhancement pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony offense—federal, state, or local—“regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted as true, without objection, the Presentence Report’s factual account of Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Consequently, the court did not commit error, let alone clear error, when it determined, based on the PSR, that the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (discussing standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutback contends that imposing the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which leads to a sentence within the guideline range, undermines the essential aim of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)—specifically, that a sentence must “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions—invoking the Armed Career Criminal Act sanctions—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines dictate that the higher offense level must apply. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, a district court is required to initially and accurately determine the relevant Guidelines range. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Prior to reviewing the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, a court of appeals must first verify that the district court has not made any significant procedural mistakes, such as either neglecting to calculate or incorrectly determining the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this, the court must evaluate whether the factors set out in § 3553(a) justify either a departure from or a variance with respect to the advisory guideline sentence.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence falling within the appropriate guidelines range, a decision that is presumed to be reasonable when reviewed on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court indicated it had considered the Presentence Investigation Report as well as sentencing briefs, and specifically evaluated the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. As stated, “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history included almost twelve prior felony convictions, numerous of which were for violent acts or firearm offenses. The instant offense encompassed conduct such as domestic abuse, kidnaping, assaulting the victim in her residence with her children present, and causing injury to a police officer during resistance to arrest. By sentencing Cutback to a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guidelines, the district court properly exercised its significant discretion and did not hand down a sentence that was substantively unreasonable.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
