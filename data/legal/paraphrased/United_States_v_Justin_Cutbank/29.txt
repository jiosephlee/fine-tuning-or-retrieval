Case Name: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals, Eighth Circuit  
Date of Decision: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: No. 24-1225  
Opinion By: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, selecting a term within the advisory guideline range it calculated. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and his sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made several errors regarding evidence and procedure that deprived him of a fair trial, and further asserts that the court erroneously applied a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, which resulted in a higher advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following his consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., brandished a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while expressing paranoid concerns about “listening devices” in their residence and alleging there were individuals outside intending him harm. He held D.F. at gunpoint inside the closet for what D.F. later described in testimony as feeling like several hours, during which time he threatened to kill her and struck her face with the weapon. Subsequently, Cutbank instructed D.F. to awaken other occupants of the home, which included her two teenage sons as well as her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the premises, taking with him D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. Afterward, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man had entered the garage there and refused to leave. Upon arrival, officers encountered Cutbank, who barricaded himself inside the garage. Over several hours, Cutbank made incoherent remarks and asserted that he was armed. When officers sought to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting one officer on the face and neck and by attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. A search conducted along the likely route between D.F.’s residence and the garage turned up D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both a sawed-off barrel and stock. Testing revealed that Cutbank’s DNA was present on the firearm. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as one they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged felon in possession offense, and further determined that he had been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the enhanced penalty provisions of § 924(e). Cutbank now appeals both his conviction and sentence, asserting that the district court committed abuse of discretion in several pretrial and trial rulings, and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in enhancing his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments pertaining to evidentiary and procedural matters but offers virtually no supporting analysis or citation to authority. All of these arguments lack merit.

A

Prior to the trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from providing testimony that they had seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle on previous occasions, asserting that such testimony would constitute impermissible propensity evidence in contravention of Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government’s position that this testimony qualified as intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge and served to show a continued possession of the firearm leading up to the event in question. We concur with this analysis. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of times” prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank sought a jury instruction intended to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems, requesting that the jury not treat D.F.’s statements to the police as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself recounted the relevant events in court. The government, however, contended that these statements would merely serve to illuminate the circumstances of the police officers’ actions and their awareness at that time, asserting that the officers’ testimonies would stay within the confines of hearsay rules and that, if required, a subsequent instruction from the bench would be both typical and adequate. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this instance, the record confirms that no hearsay evidence was admitted at trial. Furthermore, D.F. did provide testimony. The Confrontation Clause is fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Thus, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the event in question. The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” before, the court overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback does not reference any authority that would undermine the district court’s earlier determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence, which was pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showing ongoing possession of the weapon before the event. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements concerning the firearm—including her claim that she could not recall seeing the gun on the night in question—D.F. explained that some of her earlier statements were due to her lack of familiarity with firearms and her aversion to them, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court denied Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s testimony about her sister's death by gun. This evidence was given in response to cross-examination about her prior inconsistent remarks. Cutback fails to identify any unfair prejudice or jury confusion stemming from this testimony, and none is evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, referenced D.F.’s sister having been killed by a firearm as well, and no objection was raised by the defense. The district court did not exceed its wide latitude in ruling on evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s account of seeing the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” by cross-examining her about earlier statements she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into C.F.’s statements to law enforcement but sustained the prosecution’s objections to defense counsel reading sections of the corresponding police report into evidence, finding a lack of proper foundation since C.F. was not the report’s author. On appeal, although Cutback did not specifically address this decision, the government’s brief asserted that Cutback “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove when appellees unnecessarily broaden the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, even assuming the issue were properly preserved, the “claim” fails on the merits. As stated, “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court did not err in insisting upon adequate authentication.

At the end, as Cutback concedes, "this court will not overturn a mistaken evidentiary decision if such an error is harmless. . . . Stated differently, reversal is appropriate only if the wrongly admitted evidence could have significantly affected the jury’s decision." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank falls short of meeting this requirement. The government offered considerable testimonial and physical proof. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions "either did not affect or affected only minimally the verdict." Id. at 998. "We decline to reverse a conviction due to the combined effect of trial mistakes unless the defendant experienced substantial prejudice." United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further determined that he had three or more previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) advised a base offense level of 26 due to the offense involving a sawed-off firearm as specified by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), in addition to Cutbank having at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also recommended an increase of three levels because the firearm’s serial number had been altered or obliterated, a two-level increase for recklessly endangering others’ lives or safety while evading law enforcement, and an additional four levels for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony, resulting in a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the resulting guidelines range was 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by imposing the four-level enhancement pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant utilizes or possesses a firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At sentencing, the district court accepted the Presentence Report’s (PSR) account of Cutback’s prior felony convictions, and there was no objection to those factual statements. Consequently, the court did not commit error, let alone clear error, in concluding based on the PSR that the firearm was used in connection with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank’s argument is instead that imposition of the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)—and the resulting within-Guidelines sentence—contradicts the central principle embodied in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), which requires that a sentence be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, subjecting him to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the offense level would be 34 under the Armed Career Criminal Guideline. Pursuant to the Guidelines, the higher offense level is applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, "a district court must initiate its sentencing process by accurately determining the relevant Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "Appellate courts, prior to addressing whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, are required to verify that the district court avoided any major procedural errors, such as neglecting to compute (or miscomputing) the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Afterward, the court evaluates if the factors set out in § 3553(a) justify either departing from or varying the sentence dictated by the advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence that fell within the relevant Guidelines range, which we consider presumptively reasonable on appellate review. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court indicated that it had examined the PSR along with the sentencing memoranda, and also addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. “Therefore, the district court was cognizant of [Cutbank’s] contentions, and as a result, we presume it considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “A district court is not required to explicitly enumerate all the factors it took into account in imposing sentence; what is necessary is that the record demonstrates the court did in fact take the § 3553(a) factors into consideration when determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutbank’s record included almost twelve previous felony convictions, a number of which involved firearms and violence. The instant offense comprised acts of domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault occurring in the victim’s residence in front of her children, and causing injury to a police officer while evading arrest. The district court acted within its broad sentencing discretion and did not issue a sentence that was substantively unreasonable in sentencing Cutbank to 292 months’ imprisonment within the prescribed range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
