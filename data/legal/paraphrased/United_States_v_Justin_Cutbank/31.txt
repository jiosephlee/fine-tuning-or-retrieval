Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-1225  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal background and is a convicted felon, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) for possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison under the Armed Career Criminal Act, selecting a sentence within the recommended advisory guidelines range. Cutbank has appealed both his conviction and sentence, alleging that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that resulted in an unfair trial and incorrectly imposed a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement, thereby increasing the advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following the consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., at gunpoint by aiming a firearm at her face and compelled her into a closet. While displaying paranoia about “listening devices” within the residence and individuals outside intent on harming him, he held D.F. in the closet under threat, repeatedly menacing her life and striking her face with the gun. D.F. testified that this ordeal seemed to last for hours. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake other people asleep in the home, which included her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank left the premises, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. Afterward, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement additionally received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and refused to leave. Upon the officers’ arrival, Cutbank barricaded himself inside the garage and, over a period of several hours, made unpredictable remarks and asserted he was armed. When officers attempted to take Cutbank into custody, he resisted, biting one officer on the face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. While searching the probable route Cutbank would have traveled between D.F.’s house and the garage, officers discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both a shortened barrel and stock. DNA analysis revealed that the firearm contained Cutbank’s DNA. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified this firearm as the one they had previously observed in Cutbank’s possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession as charged and determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, which resulted in the imposition of an enhanced sentence pursuant to § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, alleging that the district court abused its discretion in various pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple challenges regarding evidentiary and procedural matters, but offers neither relevant case authority nor substantial analysis in support of these claims. Each of these arguments lacks merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. regarding their previous observations of him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, asserting that such testimony constituted improper propensity evidence in contravention of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court, however, sided with the government's position that this testimony qualified as intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and was indicative of Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm preceding the incident. We concur with this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During the trial, D.F. stated that she had witnessed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question.

B

Prior to the trial, Cutbank asked that the jury receive an instruction clarifying that, to prevent hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems, D.F.’s statements to police could not serve as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct unless D.F. herself later testified about those events. The government responded that these statements would instead be introduced to provide background regarding the officers’ actions and state of knowledge, asserting that the officers’ testimony would not violate the hearsay rule, and suggesting that any necessary limiting instruction could appropriately be given by the court at a later stage, as is customary. As stated in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), “[w]e review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” According to that case, a court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In the present matter, the trial record indicates that hearsay evidence was not admitted. Moreover, D.F. did take the stand and testified. Under United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted), the Confrontation Clause is fulfilled “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Accordingly, there was no abuse of discretion here.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident, specifically “a handful of times.” The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, when C.F. testified as a witness for the prosecution and asserted that she had previously seen the gun used during the incident “once or twice,” the court also overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback does not reference any authority contradicting the district court’s pretrial determination that this evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as to showing Cutbank’s continuous possession of the firearm before the event in question. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier inconsistent remarks concerning the firearm, including her lack of recollection about seeing the gun on the relevant night, D.F. explained that certain of her statements stemmed from her unfamiliarity with firearms and her aversion to guns, stating, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The district court rejected Cutback's request to strike D.F.'s comment about her sister's death involving a firearm. This testimony was provided in response to questioning on prior inconsistent statements during cross-examination. Cutback fails to demonstrate, and none is evident, that the testimony caused any undue prejudice or confusion. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., called by the government, referred to D.F.'s sister being killed by a gun, again without objection from the defense. Thus, the district court did not exceed its wide latitude in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s statement that she had previously seen the gun involved in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her on cross-examination about earlier statements she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. had told the police but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel tried to read parts of the corresponding police report into evidence without laying an appropriate foundation, since C.F. was not the report’s author. Although Cutback did not address this ruling in his appellate brief, the government’s submission contended that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We do not look favorably on appellees who broaden the scope of appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless, preserved or otherwise, this “claim” fails. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court did not abuse its discretion by insisting upon proper authentication.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, "this court will not overturn a mistaken evidentiary ruling unless the error was not harmless. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only if the jury's decision could have been significantly affected by the erroneously admitted evidence." United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The government presented ample testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We do not set aside a conviction due to cumulative trial errors unless they resulted in substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that Cutbank had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to enhanced sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) proposed a base offense level of 26, as the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and Cutbank had two or more previous convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR recommended adding three levels due to the firearm’s serial number being altered or obliterated, two levels for recklessly causing a risk of death or serious bodily harm during flight from law enforcement, and four levels for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These enhancements resulted in a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the PSR calculated an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. Cutbank contends on appeal that the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant either used or possessed a firearm or ammunition in relation to a separate federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing hearing, the district court, without any objection from the parties, accepted as true the PSR’s factual accounts of Cutback’s earlier felony convictions. Consequently, the district court did not commit error—much less clear error—when determining that the facts set forth in the PSR demonstrate the firearm was associated with another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) 4-level enhancement creates a guidelines-compliant sentence that allegedly “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” which requires that the sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Even though the jury determined that Cutback possessed three or more earlier violent felony convictions—thereby making Armed Career Criminal Act penalties applicable—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded a total offense level of 34. Under the Guidelines, the higher offense level governs. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, the district court must first properly determine the relevant Guidelines range for the offense. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Additionally, an appellate court may only review a sentence’s substantive reasonableness once it is satisfied that the district court did not commit any material procedural errors, such as neglecting to compute or misapplying the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this, the court evaluates whether the factors enumerated in § 3553(a) justify deviating from or departing from the advisory guidelines range when determining the appropriate sentence.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence falling within the guideline range, a decision we treat as presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court acknowledged that it had examined the PSR as well as the sentencing memoranda, and it addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might support a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “[t]hus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” There is no requirement for the district court to recite the statutory factors in a formulaic manner to justify the sentence; it is sufficient that the record reflects actual consideration of the § 3553(a) factors in arriving at the sentence, as set forth in United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record reflects almost twelve prior felony convictions, several for violent or firearms-related crimes. The instant conviction concerned crimes of domestic violence, kidnapping, assault in the presence of the victim’s children in her residence, and harming a law enforcement officer during efforts to avoid apprehension. Accordingly, the district court’s decision to sentence Cutback to a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guideline range was not an abuse of its broad discretion, nor was the sentence substantively unreasonable.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
