Case Name: United States v. Justin Cutbank  
Tribunal: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date of Decision: October 7, 2025  
Case Number: No. 24-1225  
Opinion By: Circuit Judge Loken

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal background, guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison, a term that fell within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial, and erroneously applied a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which raised the advisory guidelines range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night between November 19 and 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her face, and forced her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions about “listening devices” in the residence and claiming that individuals were waiting outside intending to harm him. Holding D.F. at gunpoint in the closet for what she described in her testimony as feeling like hours, Cutbank threatened to kill her and hit her in the face with the weapon. At a later point, Cutbank commanded D.F. to wake up others asleep in the house, including her two teenage sons and her roommate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank escaped with both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, stating that a man was present in the garage and refused to depart. Upon arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage; during the ensuing several hours, he made unpredictable remarks and asserted that he possessed a weapon. When officers attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted arrest, biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. While retracing the route Cutbank was likely to have used between D.F.’s house and the garage, officers recovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle modified with a shortened barrel and stock. DNA evidence on the firearm matched Cutbank. During trial proceedings, both D.F. and C.F. identified the firearm as the one they had observed in Cutbank’s possession prior to that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon-in-possession charge, and further determined he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now appeals both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court abused its discretion through various pretrial and trial decisions, and improperly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in enhancing his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments regarding evidence and procedure, yet he cites no authority and offers minimal analysis in support of his claims. These arguments are without merit.

A

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously witnessed him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending such evidence would improperly suggest propensity in contravention of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government’s position that this evidence was intrinsic to the offense, as it bore directly on the possession charge and served to show Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the relevant incident. We concur with this reasoning. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. stated in her testimony that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of times” before the incident in question.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank moved for a jury instruction specifying, so as to prevent hearsay problems and avoid Confrontation Clause violations, that the jury could not use D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct unless and until D.F. herself took the stand and recounted the events. The government maintained that these statements would merely offer background for the officers’ actions and state of mind, asserted that the officers’ testimony would not violate hearsay rules, and further contended that any required instruction could appropriately be given by the court later in the trial, as is customary and sufficient. As stated in United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015), “[w]e review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” The court is not obligated “to instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this case, the record confirms no hearsay was admitted during trial. Moreover, D.F. took the stand and testified. The Confrontation Clause requirement is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Thus, the district court did not abuse its discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident, specifically noting “a handful of times.” The district court rejected Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Similarly, the court also dismissed a defense objection when C.F., testifying on behalf of the government, stated that she had previously seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice.” On appeal, Cutback does not provide any authority to challenge the court’s pretrial determination that this evidence was intrinsic, as it was pertinent to establishing the possession charge and illustrating that Cutbank possessed the firearm continuously before the incident occurred. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements concerning the firearm—including statements indicating she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night—D.F. explained that some of her prior statements resulted from her lack of “familiar[ity] with guns” and her dislike for firearms, adding, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The trial court denied Cutback’s request to strike D.F.’s remark that her sister was killed by a gun. D.F.’s testimony served as a response to inquiries during cross-examination about her earlier inconsistent statements. Cutback has not demonstrated that this testimony caused unfair prejudice or confusion, nor is any such effect evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, during her testimony as a witness for the government, C.F. also referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun, and the defense did not object. The district court did not exceed the wide latitude afforded it in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the firearm used in the incident “once or twice” by questioning her during cross-examination about earlier statements she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into C.F.’s comments to law enforcement, but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel attempted to read sections of the resulting police report into evidence without establishing a sufficient foundation, since C.F. was not the report’s author. While Cutback’s appeal did not specifically address this evidentiary ruling, the government’s response brief claimed that his “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove of appellees needlessly broadening the scope of appellate issues. Regardless of preservation, the supposed “claim” is unavailing. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court’s insistence on authentication did not constitute an abuse of discretion.

Lastly, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not reverse a mistaken evidentiary ruling unless the mistake affected the outcome. . . . That is, reversal is warranted only if the jury’s decision may have been significantly affected by the erroneously admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank is unable to meet this standard. The government offered considerable testimonial and physical evidence. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998. Furthermore, “we will not set aside a conviction due to the cumulative impact of trial errors unless they result in substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury determined that Cutbank was guilty of the charge of being a felon in possession of a firearm. It further found that he had at least three previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced sentencing provisions of the Armed Career Criminal Act. According to the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), a base offense level of 26 was recommended since the offense involved a sawed-off firearm as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and because Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also proposed a three-level enhancement because the firearm’s serial number was altered or obliterated, a two-level increase based on his reckless creation of a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense, arriving at a total offense level of 35. The PSR determined that Cutbank fell into Criminal History Category VI, which resulted in an advisory sentencing guidelines range of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred by applying the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense.

This enhancement is triggered when a defendant uses or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another felony offense—be it federal, state, or local—“regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the PSR’s account of Cutback’s past felony convictions without challenge. Therefore, the court’s determination that the PSR’s facts support that the firearm was used in connection with another felony was not erroneous, much less plainly erroneous. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). However, Cutbank maintains that applying the 4-level enhancement pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) produces a sentence within the guidelines range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” specifically that sentencing should be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined Cutback had at least three prior violent felony convictions, invoking penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have yielded a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines instruct that the higher offense level governs. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When determining a proper sentence, “a district court must start every sentencing hearing by accurately determining the relevant Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). “Prior to addressing whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, a court of appeals must verify that the district court made no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate, or miscalculating, the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After that, the court evaluates whether the factors outlined in § 3553(a) justify a deviation or variance from the advisory sentencing guidelines range.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence that fell within the guideline range, and we regard such sentences as presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During the sentencing hearing, the court indicated it had considered the PSR as well as the parties’ sentencing memoranda, and it addressed the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a departure. As stated, “the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). Further, “[t]o uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record included close to twelve prior felony convictions, many of which entailed acts of violence and firearms offenses. The instant offense encompassed domestic abuse, kidnapping, an assault that took place in the victim’s residence and in front of her children, and injury to a police officer during efforts to evade arrest. The district court properly exercised its significant sentencing discretion and did not issue a substantively unreasonable sentence in sentencing Cutback to a 292-month prison term, which was within the applicable range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
