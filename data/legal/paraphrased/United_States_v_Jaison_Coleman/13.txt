Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Addressing 911 reports involving domestic violence ranks among the most challenging duties for law enforcement. The facts here are illustrative. At around 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department responded to a report from an individual who stated her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained present in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and made contact with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned regarding the well-being of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and explained that her children were in one bedroom, her husband in another, and that their dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court concluded that this dialogue provided Officer Kizer with sufficient consent to enter and that the officers did not overstep the scope of Lisa’s permission once inside. Consequently, the district court refused to suppress the firearms discovered during their search. We concur and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for illegal possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are drawn from footage captured by body-worn cameras operated by Officer Kizer and other officers present at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon his arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and identified himself. In response, Lisa appeared on the porch and indicated that her son and daughter were inside. When questioned about the safety of her children, Lisa was initially noncommittal, merely stating they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer sought clarification by asking again if the children would be safe; however, Lisa remained ambiguous, replying that she “hope[d] so.” Changing tactics, Officer Kizer next inquired as to her husband's location within the home. Lisa replied that she did not know, leading Officer Kizer to request permission to enter. Lisa, who was facing the door, hesitated before replying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation then paused, during which Lisa informed Officer Kizer that she needed to retrieve her shoes from inside. After Lisa failed to return outside for some time, Officer Kizer, concerned for her welfare, partially opened the door and repeatedly called inside to check if she was alright. Shortly thereafter, Lisa came back to the doorway and exited onto the porch again, prompting Officer Kizer to ask for an update on the situation inside and, more specifically, to request the name of her husband, whom he understood had made threats against her. Lisa identified him as Jaison, whom this opinion will refer to as Coleman. At this point, a significant conversation took place. Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and if the children were safe, leading to the following exchange:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, moving her dogs aside to allow their entrance. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and identified the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not observe Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied that she was unaware of where he was. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman consented to a protective frisk by Officer Kizer. During their conversation with the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and hostile. The children further reported that their father pointed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When questioned further, the children stated they had seen the gun both on that occasion and previously, but they did not know its current location.

B

After obtaining information during their initial entry into the residence, the officers applied for and received a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman home. This search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which ultimately led to a federal indictment charging Coleman with being a felon in possession of a firearm in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that his wife never gave Officer Kizer and the other officers consent to enter the home initially. After holding a hearing, a magistrate judge found that Lisa did, in fact, consent to the officers’ entry and recommended denying Coleman’s motion. The district court concurred with the magistrate judge’s conclusion that Lisa granted consent for entry and further rejected Coleman’s argument that the officers' actions within the home exceeded the scope of his wife's consent. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea while preserving his right to appeal the denial of his suppression motion. The appeal of that ruling is now before this court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards in this area are not in dispute; both parties accept that under the Fourth Amendment, consent operates as an exception to the warrant requirement for residential searches. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties further agree that the defendant himself is not required to provide the consent: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). Supreme Court precedent makes clear that valid consent must be granted “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This requirement is reflected in our circuit's case law. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and applying the Bumper standard). Whether consent is voluntary is determined by examining the “totality of the circumstances.” See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations include, though are not confined to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is determined to be voluntary, any subsequent search must stay within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To assess the scope, the question is one of “objective reasonableness,” asking what “the typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the individual giving consent. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In this context, our review as an appellate court is confined: we consider a district court’s determination of voluntariness and any challenge to the scope of consent only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers to enter her residence. Two elements of the interaction are particularly noteworthy. First, throughout the encounter, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and demeanor that indicated concern rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her ability to refuse consent when she did so in response to Officer Kizer’s first request to enter. She subsequently agreed to allow him in after his second, non-coercive request. These facts are evident in the video evidence. From the moment Officer Kizer reached the Coleman home, his communication with Lisa was calm and respectful, while Lisa’s responses consistently indicated her understanding of the events. Although Officer Kizer did not explicitly inform her that consent was not required, his request for permission and her initial refusal both implied that her consent was necessary. Lisa was never detained during this interaction. Even though Officer Kizer sought permission twice, their entire dialogue was brief, lasting only several minutes. While Lisa may have experienced some pressure to admit the officers, there was no evidence of coercion by Officer Kizer. On these facts, the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented was not clearly erroneous. 

Coleman argues otherwise, highlighting Officer Kizer’s use of “need” in his second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this is a reasonable contention, Coleman's focus is too restrictive. Considering the context, the district court could properly determine that Officer Kizer’s wording did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer articulated his request with genuine concern for the welfare of Lisa’s children, conveying some urgency, but the district court did not clearly err in deciding this did not rise to the level of coercion. Lisa’s immediate acceptance—saying “Okay,” and explaining the children’s location while informing the officers about the dogs’ temperament—demonstrates she perceived the request as such.

Accordingly, we affirm the district court’s determination that Lisa granted the police permission to enter her home. We further concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers acted within the scope of that consent once inside. The officers’ stated objective was to ensure the well-being of the children, including ascertaining that Coleman posed no risk to them or anyone else. This purpose necessitated that they survey the premises, find each occupant, and ask preliminary questions. Their resulting findings led to obtaining a search warrant and seizing three firearms. We discern no flaw in the district court’s decision that the officers remained within the bounds of Lisa’s initial consent after entering the Coleman residence. These findings render it unnecessary to consider the government’s alternative arguments concerning exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as bases for the officers’ entry and search. For these reasons, we AFFIRM. Finally, we note that Officer Kizer and his team showed careful and thoughtful judgment in managing a sensitive situation.
