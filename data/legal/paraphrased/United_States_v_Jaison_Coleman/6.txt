Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers face significant challenges when responding to 911 calls involving domestic violence. This case illustrates such difficulties. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department arrived at a residence after receiving a report from a caller stating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the house. Officer Kizer knocked on the door and conversed with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the well-being of her children. Officer Kizer then responded, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” In response, Lisa said, “Okay” and informed Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and that their dogs were friendly and non-aggressive. The district court determined that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter the premises and that neither he nor the other officers exceeded the bounds of Lisa’s consent while inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms found in the house. We concur with the district court and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant details are derived from the body camera footage captured by Officer Kizer and other officers at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon his arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and identified himself. Lisa emerged onto the porch, informing Officer Kizer that both her son and daughter were inside. When questioned about her children’s safety, Lisa was initially reluctant, responding only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer pressed further, asking whether her children would be all right, to which Lisa replied ambiguously that she “hope[d] so.” Shifting tactics, Officer Kizer then inquired about her husband’s whereabouts inside the house. Lisa answered that she did not know. Officer Kizer then requested permission to enter, and after turning back toward the door, Lisa hesitated and replied, “um … I’d prefer you not.” There was a pause in the conversation at this point, during which Lisa informed Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to retrieve her shoes. After some time passed and Lisa did not reappear, Officer Kizer, now concerned, partially opened the door and called into the house several times, asking whether Lisa was all right. After approximately a minute, Lisa returned to the threshold and stepped outside again. This led Officer Kizer to once more inquire about the situation inside and specifically ask for her husband’s name, whom he understood to have threatened her. Lisa replied that his name was Jaison, whom we will refer to as Coleman from this point forward. A significant exchange then took place. Officer Kizer questioned whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and whether the children were safe, resulting in the following conversation:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, assisting their entry by moving her dogs aside. She escorted the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated where the children were located. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied that she was unaware of his location. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman consented to Officer Kizer performing a protective frisk. During interviews with the two children, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in an argument, during which Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and hostile. The children further recounted that their father had brandished a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. In response to further questions, the children stated they had seen the gun, both that night and on prior occasions, but did not know its current location.

B

After entering the residence, the officers obtained further information that led them to apply for and receive a warrant to search the Coleman residence. During the search, they discovered multiple firearms, which ultimately resulted in Coleman being federally charged for being a felon in possession of a firearm, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his home, arguing that his wife did not consent to Officer Kizer and the other officers initially entering the premises. After conducting a hearing, the magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa had in fact consented to the officers' entry and recommended denying Coleman’s motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s conclusion that Lisa consented, and also independently rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the residence surpassed the boundaries of his wife's consent. When the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea, but did so while expressly reserving his right to appeal the court’s suppression decision. That appeal is currently before this Court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are clear, and both parties agree that the consent exception permits a home search without a warrant under the Fourth Amendment. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both sides further concede that consent may be provided by someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that valid consent must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This Court’s precedent likewise follows that requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper’s standard). Whether consent is voluntary is determined by examining all circumstances surrounding the consent. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Courts typically look to factors such as: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of detention prior to consent; (4) whether consent was given immediately or only after repeated requests by authorities; (5) whether there was any physical coercion; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody at the time consent was provided.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is voluntary, any search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The relevant test for scope is one of “objective reasonableness,” asking, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenter? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate court, our function is confined: we review findings regarding voluntary consent and challenges to the scope for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we discern no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers into her residence. Two features of their interaction are particularly notable. First, throughout the encounter, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor and tone indicative of concern rather than one of coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse entry, as shown by her initial denial when Officer Kizer first sought permission. Ultimately, she permitted Officer Kizer to enter after he made a second, non-coercive request. These conclusions are plainly supported by the video evidence. From his arrival at the Coleman home, Officer Kizer addressed Lisa in a calm and respectful manner, and at all relevant moments, Lisa’s responses reflected her comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform Lisa that her consent was not required, his request for permission, combined with her initial refusal, inherently communicated that her consent was voluntary. At no stage was Lisa detained. Furthermore, although Officer Kizer requested entry twice, the interaction spanned only a brief period. While Lisa might have experienced some implicit pressure from the circumstances, Officer Kizer never crossed into coercive behavior. Given these facts, the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to entry does not constitute clear error.

Coleman challenges this, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s choice of the word “need” in his second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this point is arguable, Coleman’s analysis is too limited. The district court could reasonably conclude that Officer Kizer’s language, when viewed in the context of the entire exchange, did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s request to enter, motivated by concern for the children’s welfare and delivered with appropriate urgency, was not, in the district court’s view, unduly forceful or coercive. Lisa’s reply reinforces this perspective: after saying “Okay,” she proceeded to inform Officer Kizer where her children were and reassured him and the other officers about her dogs’ behavior. All these facts support the affirmation of the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to police entry.

We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers remained within the scope of Lisa’s consent after entering. Their explicit reason for entry was to ensure the wellbeing of her children, which required them to survey the residence, locate individuals inside, and make preliminary inquiries. The information they obtained during this check supported their later application for a search warrant and the seizure of three firearms. The record offers no basis to question the district court’s finding that the officers’ actions within the home adhered to the boundaries of the consent Lisa provided. These conclusions obviate the necessity of reaching the government’s alternative arguments regarding exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as justification for the officers’ entry and initial sweep.

Ultimately, we AFFIRM. We do so with the further observation that Officer Kizer and the other officers demonstrated prudent and well-balanced judgment when confronted with a sensitive and challenging situation.
