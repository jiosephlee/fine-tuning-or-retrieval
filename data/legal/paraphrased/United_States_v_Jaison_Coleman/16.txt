Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers often face significant challenges when answering 911 calls about domestic violence incidents. The present matter illustrates such difficulty. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department responded to a report from a caller who stated that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and conversed with Lisa, who hesitated when asked whether her children were safe. In response, Officer Kizer remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and subsequently informed Officer Kizer that her children were together in a bedroom, her husband was in a separate bedroom, and the dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court concluded that Lisa’s responses provided Officer Kizer with the consent needed to enter the home and further determined that neither Officer Kizer nor his fellow officers exceeded the limits of Lisa’s consent during their time inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered in the home. We concur with the district court’s analysis and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are drawn from footage recorded by body cameras worn by Officer Kizer and other officers at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and announced his presence. Lisa came onto the porch, informing Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. When initially asked if her children were safe, Lisa hesitated, responding only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer pressed again, asking if her children would be all right; Lisa again answered uncertainly, stating she “hope[d] so.” Shifting his approach, Officer Kizer inquired about the whereabouts of her husband inside the house. Lisa replied that she did not know. When Officer Kizer then asked for permission to enter, Lisa, who had just turned back toward the entrance, paused and answered, “um … I’d prefer you not.” After this, there was a pause in the conversation, and Lisa informed Officer Kizer that she needed to go back inside to retrieve her shoes. Time passed without Lisa reappearing, prompting Officer Kizer, out of concern, to slightly open the door and repeatedly call into the home to check on her wellbeing. After about a minute, Lisa reemerged at the door and stepped outside again. At this point, Officer Kizer resumed questioning her about the situation inside and specifically asked for the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had previously threatened her. Lisa responded that his name was Jaison, who will hereafter be referred to as Coleman. A significant exchange then ensued. When Officer Kizer inquired whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and if the children were safe, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers to a rear bedroom, indicating where the children could be found. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware of his location. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to conduct a protective pat down. During conversations with the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had engaged in a fight, with the children describing Coleman as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further explained that their father had brandished a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When asked, the children confirmed they had seen the firearm, both on that night and previously, but were unsure of its current location.

B

After obtaining information during their initial entry into the residence, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a search warrant for the Coleman residence. Execution of this search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which subsequently formed the basis for a federal indictment accusing Coleman of violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. Coleman sought to exclude the firearms evidence, arguing that his wife never gave Officer Kizer and his fellow officers consent to enter the home in the first place. After conducting a hearing, a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had, in fact, given consent for the officers to come into the residence, and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court adopted the magistrate judge’s conclusion regarding Lisa’s consent and also independently rejected Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct within the home exceeded the boundaries of the consent given by his wife. Upon the district court's refusal to grant his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea while preserving his ability to challenge the denial of suppression on appeal. That appeal is presently before this court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are well-settled, and both parties acknowledge that consent is an established exception to the warrant requirement for home searches under the Fourth Amendment. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). It is also undisputed that such consent does not have to be obtained from the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). According to the Supreme Court, valid consent requires that it be “freely and voluntarily” given. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our circuit has consistently reiterated this principle. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (applying the Bumper standard). In assessing whether consent was voluntary, courts examine the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). The analysis typically includes considerations such as “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of detention prior to giving consent; (4) whether consent was given immediately or after repeated police requests; (5) the presence of any physical coercion; and (6) whether the person was in police custody when providing consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even if consent is voluntary, any search must still remain within the “scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To determine the extent of consent, courts apply an “objective reasonableness” standard—specifically, courts consider what a reasonable person would have understood from the interaction between law enforcement and the individual giving consent. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate court, our function is circumscribed; we review a district court’s findings on the voluntariness of consent and challenges to its scope only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

Upon review of the entirety of the interaction between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we discern no manifest error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two particular elements of their exchange are noteworthy. First, throughout the interaction, Officer Kizer displayed a demeanor and manner of speaking indicative of concern rather than of coercion. Second, Lisa’s conduct revealed her capacity to refuse consent to entry, as evidenced by her initial denial of Officer Kizer’s request. After Officer Kizer repeated his request, again without applying any coercive tactics, Lisa opted to permit his entry. The video recording clearly demonstrates these facts. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman home, he addressed Lisa in a composed and respectful fashion, and Lisa, at all times, responded in ways that reflected her understanding of the ongoing events. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform Lisa that she could refuse consent, his request for permission implied as much, as did her original refusal. Lisa was never detained. While Officer Kizer twice sought entry, the total duration of their conversation spanned only several minutes. Although Lisa may have experienced some pressure to acquiesce, there was no point at which Officer Kizer applied undue pressure or coercion. On this basis, we find no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa’s consent was voluntary.

Coleman offers a different interpretation, arguing that Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” in his second request—“Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay]”—rendered the request coercive. While this argument is not unreasonable, it views the matter too narrowly. Given the surrounding context, the district court reasonably concluded that Officer Kizer’s phrasing did not transform his request into coercion. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer presented his request out of concern for the children’s welfare and did so with appropriate urgency and seriousness, but the district court correctly determined this approach remained free from improper compulsion. Lisa’s subsequent actions confirm she understood the officer’s words as a request rather than an order; in response, she said “Okay,” directed Officer Kizer to the children’s location within the house, and reassured the officers about the behavior of her dogs. All of this supports affirming the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry.

Furthermore, we concur with the district court’s determination that once admitted, the officers acted within the bounds of the consent provided by Lisa. Their stated objective was to ensure the safety of Lisa’s children, including verifying Coleman’s threat level. Fulfilling that objective inherently required the officers to look throughout the house, account for those present, and pose a few straightforward questions. The resulting information enabled law enforcement to procure a search warrant for the residence, leading to the recovery of three firearms. We discern no flaw in the district court’s assessment that the officers’ conduct after entering the Coleman home honored the consent Lisa had granted. Because of these findings, it is unnecessary to address the government’s alternative theories of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery as bases for entry or initial sweep. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. We also note in closing that Officer Kizer and his colleagues demonstrated prudent and balanced judgment under challenging circumstances.
