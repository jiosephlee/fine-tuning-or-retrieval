Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa subsequently allowed Officer Kizer and his fellow officers inside by opening the door and moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers toward a rear bedroom, indicating the children’s location. When Officer Kizer did not observe Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied that she was unaware of his location. Officer Kizer quickly searched the house and ultimately located Coleman in a different bedroom. A short exchange ensued, during which Coleman consented to a protective frisk by Officer Kizer. Upon speaking with the two children, the officers were informed that an altercation had occurred between Lisa and Coleman, characterized by Coleman’s reported loudness, aggression, and harsh demeanor. The children further stated that their father brandished a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and said he would set the home on fire. In response to questions, the children affirmed witnessing the firearm on that night and previously, though they did not know its current location.

B

After entering the home, the officers obtained additional information which they used to apply for and receive a warrant to search the Coleman residence. During the subsequent search, they discovered multiple firearms, ultimately resulting in a federal indictment accusing Coleman of violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) by possessing firearms as a convicted felon. Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his residence, arguing that his wife did not initially provide consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa had indeed permitted the officers to enter and recommending denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s conclusion that Lisa gave consent for the entry and further rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ actions inside the home exceeded the scope of his wife's permission. Once his motion to suppress was denied by the district court, Coleman entered a guilty plea while preserving his ability to contest the suppression decision on appeal. That appeal is currently before this court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are settled, and both parties acknowledge that consent constitutes an exception to the Fourth Amendment's requirement of a warrant for a search of a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties also concur that the defendant himself does not need to provide this consent: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has held that valid consent must be “freely and voluntarily” provided. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This principle is echoed in circuit precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and utilizing the Bumper standard). Courts determine whether consent was voluntary by evaluating all relevant circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Important considerations generally include—but are not limited to—“(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before giving consent; (4) whether consent was granted immediately or after multiple police requests; (5) whether there was any physical coercion; and (6) whether the individual was already in police custody when he consented.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even if consent is voluntary, any ensuing search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Whether that requirement is satisfied depends on “objective reasonableness,” requiring the court to assess “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenter. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our review is constrained. We consider the district court’s determinations regarding voluntariness of consent and the scope of that consent under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we discern no plain error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers into her residence. Two components of the interaction are particularly noteworthy. First, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and manner throughout which reflected concern rather than coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse entry—she did so when Officer Kizer first requested access. Upon his subsequent, non-coercive request, she opted to permit his entry. These findings are corroborated by the video recording. From Officer Kizer’s arrival at the Coleman household, his communication with Lisa was consistently calm and respectful, and at all relevant times, Lisa’s responses indicated her awareness and understanding of the situation. While Officer Kizer did not directly state that her consent was optional, both his act of asking and her initial refusal carried that implication. Lisa was never in custody. Although Officer Kizer requested entry twice, their entire exchange lasted only a few minutes. Lisa may have experienced some level of psychological pressure as a result of police presence, but at no time did Officer Kizer engage in coercion. Based on this record, we find no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry.

Coleman contests this assessment, highlighting Officer Kizer’s statement during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Though this argument has merit, it narrowly focuses on particular language. The district court could justifiably conclude that Officer Kizer’s choice of the word “need” did not render his request coercive when considering the entire context. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought entry out of expressed concern for the children’s welfare, conveying urgency and seriousness, but the district court reasonably determined he remained within appropriate bounds and did not coerce consent. Lisa’s reaction substantiates this view: following her response of “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer of the children’s location and assured the officers regarding her dogs’ non-aggressive behavior. All of these facts support our decision to uphold the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the officers entering her home.

Furthermore, we concur with the district court’s determination that the officers’ actions upon entry did not exceed the scope of Lisa’s consent. Their explicit aim was to ensure the wellbeing of the children and confirm that Coleman posed no threat to them or to anyone else. Accomplishing this required the officers to visually inspect the residence, ascertain occupants’ locations, and ask straightforward questions. The resulting information led the officers to obtain a warrant and seize three firearms from the residence. The record contains no basis to disturb the district court’s finding that the officers stayed within the bounds of Lisa’s original consent during their presence in the Coleman home.

These conclusions make it unnecessary to address the government’s alternate argument that the exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery doctrines also justified the officers’ initial entry and search. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. We conclude with an overarching observation: Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and even-handed judgment amid sensitive and challenging circumstances.
