Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls involving domestic violence is among the most challenging tasks assigned to law enforcement officers. The facts of this case illustrate that difficulty. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department responded to a report from a caller stating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained in the residence. Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked and conversed with Lisa, who hesitated when asked whether her children were safe. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” then explained that her children were in one bedroom, her husband in another, and their dogs were harmless. The district court determined that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with the consent needed to enter and concluded that the officers did not overstep the boundaries of Lisa’s consent once inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms found in the residence. We concur with this reasoning and affirm Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant details are derived from footage captured by body cameras worn by Officer Kizer and other officers present at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon their arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and announced his presence. Lisa came onto the porch and informed Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside the house. When asked about the safety of her children, Lisa was initially reluctant to respond, stating only that the children were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer repeated his inquiry about whether her children were alright, to which Lisa responded somewhat ambiguously that she “hope[d] so.” Shifting his questioning, Officer Kizer then asked where her husband was in the residence. Lisa replied that she was unsure. Officer Kizer requested permission to enter, and Lisa—having just turned toward the door—hesitated before replying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” There was a pause in their interaction, after which Lisa indicated to Officer Kizer that she needed to reenter the house to retrieve her shoes. After a period with no sign of Lisa returning, Officer Kizer, growing concerned, partially opened the door and repeatedly called out into the home, asking if she was alright. Approximately a minute later, Lisa reappeared at the doorway and stepped outside once more. This led Officer Kizer to inquire again about the situation inside the house, specifically requesting the name of her husband—who, according to Officer Kizer’s understanding, had made threats against her. Lisa identified her husband as Jaison, whom, for purposes of this opinion, will be referred to as Coleman. A significant interaction then ensued. When Officer Kizer questioned whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and, subsequently, whether the children were safe, the following exchange occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not observe Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she was unaware. After a brief search of the residence, Officer Kizer located Coleman in a different bedroom. They engaged in a short conversation, during which Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective frisk. Upon speaking with the two children, the officers discovered that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father had pointed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When asked, the children stated they had seen the firearm, both that night and previously, but did not currently know its location.

B

After entering the house, the officers obtained additional information that prompted them to apply for and obtain a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman residence. The ensuing search uncovered multiple firearms, which subsequently resulted in Coleman’s federal indictment for possession of a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion seeking suppression of the firearms recovered from his home, arguing that his wife did not consent to Officer Kizer and the other officers entering the residence in the first place. At a hearing, a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had given the officers permission to enter and recommended denial of Coleman’s motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s finding that Lisa had provided consent, and independently determined that the officers did not exceed the boundaries of the consent Lisa had given once inside the home. After the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea while explicitly preserving his right to challenge the suppression decision on appeal. The matter is now before this court on that appeal.

II

A

The controlling legal standards in this area are well-settled. Both parties agree that consent serves as an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s general requirement of a warrant to conduct a home search. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Furthermore, both sides concur that consent may be obtained from an individual other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that in order for consent to be valid, it must be provided “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This same requirement is reflected in our precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper). Whether consent was voluntary must be determined by examining the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Courts typically consider several relevant factors, such as: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate or resulted from persistent requests by authorities; (5) whether any physical force was applied; and (6) whether the person was in police custody when consent was provided.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is voluntary, officers may not exceed “the scope of consent” during the subsequent search. United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To evaluate scope, courts employ an “objective reasonableness” test, considering what a reasonable person would understand was authorized by the exchange between the officer and the consenting individual. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate court, our function is circumscribed: we only review a district court’s conclusions as to voluntary consent and its scope under a clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

Upon reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we identify no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two components of their exchange merit emphasis. First, throughout the interaction, Officer Kizer’s voice and manner projected concern rather than intimidation. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse consent by initially declining Officer Kizer’s request to enter. However, upon a renewed request from Kizer—made without any hint of coercion—Lisa opted to permit his entry. The video evidence makes these points apparent. Throughout Officer Kizer’s presence at the Coleman residence, his communication with Lisa remained composed and courteous, and Lisa consistently replied in ways that reflected her awareness of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not specifically advise her that she was not obliged to grant entry, his act of requesting entry implicitly suggested as much, as did her initial refusal. Lisa was never detained. Even though Kizer asked for entry twice, the entire interaction was brief, lasting only several minutes. Although Lisa may have felt some pressure to allow the officers inside, the record contains no evidence of coercive conduct by Officer Kizer. Under these circumstances, there was no clear error in the district court's finding that Lisa’s consent was voluntary.

Coleman disputes this, focusing particularly on Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this is a plausible point, Coleman’s emphasis is overly narrow. The district court could properly conclude, after evaluating all relevant circumstances, that the term “need” did not render the request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Kizer’s request to enter stemmed from concern for the wellbeing of Lisa’s children and was conveyed with a sense of urgency and concern, but the district court permissibly found that his actions did not cross the threshold into coercion. Lisa’s subsequent actions reinforce this interpretation: after saying “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer where her children were inside the house and notified the officers that her dogs might jump but would not bite. 

Taken together, these facts support upholding the district court’s conclusion that Lisa granted lawful consent for the officers’ entry. We likewise concur with the district court's finding that, upon entering, the officers acted in accordance with the scope of that consent. Their purpose—to verify the safety of Lisa’s children and determine whether Coleman posed any threat—necessarily entailed a scan of the residence, locating those present, and asking basic questions. The knowledge gathered prompted the officers to secure a warrant and ultimately recover three firearms. We identify no error in the lower court’s finding that the officers stayed within the bounds of Lisa’s original consent. As these conclusions dispose of the question, there is no need to address the government's secondary arguments regarding exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery supporting the initial entry and sweep. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. As a final observation, Officer Kizer and his fellow officers exercised prudent and thoughtful judgment amid sensitive and complex circumstances.
