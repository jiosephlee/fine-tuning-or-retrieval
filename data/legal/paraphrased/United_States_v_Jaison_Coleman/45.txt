Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-3051  
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 reports of domestic violence ranks among the most challenging duties assigned to law enforcement personnel. The present case illustrates this point. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a 911 call where a woman stated that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened her mother, Lisa Coleman, with death and remained in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and initiated conversation with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the wellbeing of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer that her children were located in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and her dogs posed no threat. The district court concluded that this dialogue provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter the house and further determined that neither he nor other officers went beyond the boundaries of the consent granted by Lisa after entry. Accordingly, the district court declined to suppress the firearms later discovered in the residence. We concur with these findings and affirm Jaison Coleman’s conviction for being a felon in possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa subsequently allowed Officer Kizer and his fellow officers to enter by opening the door and moving her dogs aside. She directed them to a rear bedroom, indicating where the children were located. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. A brief search of the house led Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in another bedroom. Following a short exchange, Coleman consented to a protective pat-down by Officer Kizer. During interviews with the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further stated that their father had threatened their mother by pointing a gun at her, threatening to shoot her and to set the house on fire. Upon further questioning, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun both on that night and on previous occasions, but were unsure of its current location.

B

After obtaining information subsequent to their initial entry into the residence, the officers applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman home. During this search, multiple firearms were discovered, which ultimately resulted in a federal indictment against Coleman for being a felon in possession of a firearm, in contravention of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms recovered from his residence, asserting that his wife did not give Officer Kizer and his fellow officers consent to enter in the first place. After conducting a hearing, a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa had indeed given the officers permission to enter her home, and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry and, additionally, rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the residence exceeded the limits of the consent given by his wife. After the district court’s denial of his suppression motion, Coleman opted to plead guilty, expressly preserving his right to contest the suppression decision on appeal. That issue is now before this court.

II

A

The relevant legal standards are clearly established, and both sides acknowledge that consent operates as an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s warrant mandate for searches of residences. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties also concur that it is not necessary for the consent to originate from the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has made clear over time that valid consent requires it be “freely and voluntarily” provided. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This same principle is found consistently in our circuit precedents. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and applying Bumper’s standard). Courts analyze the voluntariness of consent based upon the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Typical factors assessed may encompass, among others, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Furthermore, when consent is voluntarily given, the search that follows must still adhere to “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (referencing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To determine scope, the test is one of “objective reasonableness”: courts look to what an average reasonable person would have construed from the communication between the officer and the consenting individual. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appellate review, our function is constrained; we evaluate a district court’s determinations as to voluntariness of consent and the breadth of its scope under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the full sequence of events involving Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two factors in their encounter are particularly significant. First, Officer Kizer consistently displayed a tone and manner suggestive of concern rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated she was capable of refusing entry, as evidenced by her initial denial of Officer Kizer’s request. When Officer Kizer asked a second time—without any coercive behavior—Lisa elected to grant him permission to come inside. The video record supports these observations. Throughout his presence at the Coleman home, Officer Kizer engaged Lisa calmly and with respect, and Lisa responded in ways that indicated her comprehension of the ongoing situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform her that consent was not obligatory, the nature of his request and her first refusal made clear that she had the option to deny entry. Lisa was never detained. Although Officer Kizer requested entry twice, their exchange was brief, lasting only a few minutes. Even if Lisa experienced some internal pressure, there is no indication that Officer Kizer imposed coercion during their interaction. Given this, the district court did not plainly err by determining Lisa’s consent was voluntary.

Coleman disagrees, highlighting Officer Kizer’s choice of the word “need” during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this point is reasonable, Coleman’s focus is too restrictive. The district court was entitled to find that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” did not render the request coercive when evaluated against the overall context. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer made his request based on stated concerns for the safety of Lisa’s children, presenting that worry with a measure of urgency and seriousness, yet, as the district court found, he refrained from using undue pressure. Lisa’s reaction further confirms she understood his words as a request; upon responding “Okay,” she specified her children’s location and notified the officers about her dogs. Thus, we uphold the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the officers entering her residence.

We also concur with the district court’s determination that the officers acted within the parameters of Lisa’s consent once inside the home. Their express goal was to assess her children’s welfare, which required locating the children, confirming Coleman posed no threat, and asking basic questions. The information they subsequently obtained enabled them to secure a search warrant and seize three firearms. We find no flaw in the district court’s determination that, upon entry, the officers operated within the bounds of Lisa’s original consent. Accordingly, we need not reach the government’s alternative arguments concerning exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as justifications for entering and initially sweeping the Coleman home. In conclusion, we AFFIRM. Finally, we note that Officer Kizer and his colleagues demonstrated prudent and measured judgment under challenging circumstances.
