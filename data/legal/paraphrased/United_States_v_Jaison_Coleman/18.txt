Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls involving domestic violence ranks among the most challenging duties for law enforcement. The present matter illustrates this point. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department responded to a report from a caller indicating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened her mother, Lisa Coleman, with death and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and engaged Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the safety of her children. Officer Kizer then remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded, “Okay,” and subsequently informed Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and that her dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court concluded that this interaction constituted valid consent for Officer Kizer to enter the premises and determined that he and his fellow officers remained within the bounds of Lisa’s consent during their presence inside. As a result, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered in the residence. We concur and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant events are derived from body-worn camera footage obtained by Officer Kizer and other officers present at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon his arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and identified himself. Lisa came onto the porch, informing Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. When questioned regarding her children’s safety, she initially avoided a direct answer, stating only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer repeated his inquiry, asking if the children would be alright. Lisa again responded ambiguously, expressing, “hope[d] so.” Shifting tactics, Officer Kizer then inquired as to her husband’s whereabouts within the house. Lisa replied she was unsure of his location. Officer Kizer requested permission to enter the residence; Lisa, turning back toward the door, hesitated before stating, “um … I’d prefer you not.” Conversation ceased briefly, after which Lisa told Officer Kizer she needed to retrieve her shoes from inside. When Lisa did not promptly return, Officer Kizer, concerned for her welfare, slightly opened the door and called into the home several times to check if she was alright. After about a minute, Lisa came back to the doorway and stepped outside again, leading Officer Kizer to ask once more about the situation inside, including for her husband’s name—whom Officer Kizer believed had previously threatened her. Lisa identified him as Jaison, hereafter referred to as Coleman. At that point, a significant conversation took place. Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now,” followed by an inquiry about the children’s well-being; the subsequent exchange proceeded as follows:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, moving her dogs aside to allow them entry. She directed the officers to a back bedroom, indicating where the children could be found. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she did not know. Officer Kizer quickly searched the home and located Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman agreed to let Officer Kizer perform a protective pat down. When the officers spoke with the two children, they learned that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with the children describing Coleman as being loud, aggressive, and mean. The children also stated that their father had pointed a gun at their mother and threatened both to shoot her and to set the house on fire. In response to further questions, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun, that night as well as on previous occasions, though they were unaware of its current location.

B

After gathering information subsequent to their initial entry into the residence, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a warrant to search the Coleman household. During the execution of this search, they discovered multiple firearms, which ultimately led to a federal indictment charging Coleman with being a felon in possession of a firearm in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion seeking to suppress the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that his wife never provided consent for Officer Kizer and other officers to enter the home initially. Following a hearing on the matter, a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers' entry and recommended denying Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred with these findings, adopting the magistrate judge's determination regarding Lisa’s consent, and further holding that the officers’ conduct inside the residence did not exceed the scope of any consent given by Coleman’s wife. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea, while explicitly preserving his ability to contest the suppression decision on appeal. That challenge is now before this court.

II

A

The fundamental legal doctrines are firmly settled, and both parties acknowledge that consent serves as an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s requirement for a warrant to search a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties likewise concede that such consent does not have to originate from the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). It has long been established by the Supreme Court that, to be legitimate, consent must be provided “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This court’s precedent mirrors that same requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and utilizing the standard set forth in Bumper). Courts evaluate whether consent was voluntary by looking at the entirety of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations frequently include—but are not confined to—“(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of detention before consent was provided; (4) whether consent was granted promptly or after repeated requests from the authorities; (5) the presence or absence of physical coercion; and (6) whether the person was already in custody at the time consent was obtained.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even with consent that is voluntary, the following search must stay within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The correct standard for determining scope is “objective reasonableness,” meaning courts assess what “the typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the individual providing consent. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our function is constrained. We review district courts’ determinations regarding the voluntariness of consent and any contest over its scope only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers to enter her residence. Two notable aspects of the encounter support this conclusion. First, Officer Kizer consistently conducted himself with concern rather than with coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her ability to refuse consent—she did so when Officer Kizer initially asked to enter. Ultimately, she permitted Officer Kizer to enter following his second request, which was presented without any coercive conduct. These points are evident from the video evidence. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman residence, he interacted with Lisa in a calm and courteous manner; throughout the interaction, Lisa responded in ways that reflected her understanding of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not directly advise Lisa of her right to refuse his entry, both his request for consent and Lisa’s original refusal made this clear by implication. Lisa was never placed in custody. Despite the fact that Officer Kizer sought permission a second time, their entire exchange was brief. Although Lisa may have experienced some pressure in the circumstances to consent to the entry, Officer Kizer at no point applied undue influence. On this record, we discern no clear error in the district court’s finding that Lisa consented.

Coleman disagrees, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s language during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this argument is reasonable, it adopts too limited a perspective. Considering the totality of the circumstances, as required by United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008), the district court was entitled to find that the mere use of the word “need” did not render the request coercive. Officer Kizer couched his request within an expression of genuine concern for the children’s safety, conveying a sense of urgency but not crossing the line into coercion; the district court’s determination on this point was not clearly erroneous. Lisa’s response strongly supports this reading: after she answered, “Okay,” she directed Officer Kizer to the children’s location inside and reassured the officers that while her dogs might jump, they would not bite. These considerations lead us to uphold the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry.

We also concur with the district court’s finding that the officers remained within the limits of Lisa’s consent when inside. Their expressed objective was to check on the wellbeing of her children, including confirming whether Coleman posed any risk to anyone inside. That task required the police to visually sweep the residence, identify the occupants, and pose some preliminary questions. This investigation then supported their procurement of a search warrant and the subsequent discovery of three firearms. We perceive no flaw in the district court’s determination that the officers did not exceed the scope of the consent granted by Lisa. As a result, it is unnecessary to address the government’s alternative arguments based on exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. For these reasons, we AFFIRM. In closing, we emphasize that Officer Kizer and his colleagues exercised prudent and measured judgment under challenging conditions.
