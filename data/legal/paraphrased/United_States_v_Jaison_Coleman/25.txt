Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-3051  
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Addressing emergency calls related to domestic violence represents one of the more challenging aspects of law enforcement work. The present matter illustrates such difficulties. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a 911 report stating that Jaison Coleman, the caller’s stepfather, had made threats to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated in responding to his inquiry about her children’s welfare. In response, Officer Kizer remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and subsequently informed Officer Kizer that her children were together in a bedroom, her husband was in a separate room, and their dogs were not dangerous. The district court determined that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter the residence, and further concluded that the officers stayed within the bounds of Lisa’s consent during their entry. Accordingly, the district court declined to suppress the firearms that were later located in the house. We concur with this assessment and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated where the children could be found. Since Coleman was not visible, Officer Kizer inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she was unaware. A brief walkthrough of the residence allowed Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in another bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman consented to a protective pat down by Officer Kizer. When officers spoke with the two children, they learned that Lisa and Coleman had been having an argument, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father had aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. Responding to questioning, the children affirmed they had seen the firearm, both that night and previously, although they could not say where it was at that time.

B

After obtaining information upon entering the residence, the officers applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman residence. This search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which ultimately led to Coleman being federally indicted for possession of a firearm by a convicted felon, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms discovered in his home, arguing that Officer Kizer and the other officers lacked valid consent from his wife for their initial entry. At a subsequent hearing, a magistrate judge concluded in a report that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers entering her home and recommended denying Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred with this conclusion, adopting the magistrate judge’s finding regarding Lisa’s consent and further dismissing Coleman’s separate argument that the officers’ actions within the residence went beyond the boundaries of the consent given. Once his motion to suppress was denied by the district court, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly reserving his right to challenge the suppression decision on appeal. The present appeal concerns that issue.

II

A

The relevant legal standards are clear and uncontested. Both parties acknowledge that consent constitutes an exception to the requirement under the Fourth Amendment for obtaining a warrant prior to a search of a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Likewise, both agree that the authority to grant such consent is not restricted to the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that, for consent to validate a search, it must be “freely and voluntarily” given. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This requirement is reiterated in our precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and following the standard in Bumper). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts look to the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Among the considerations courts typically weigh are: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was informed of his constitutional rights; (3) how much time he was detained before providing consent; (4) whether consent was offered promptly or after repeated requests from authorities; (5) the presence or absence of physical coercion; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he consented.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Additionally, even where consent is given voluntarily, the resulting search must not exceed the “scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). In assessing whether the search stayed within these boundaries, courts apply an “objective reasonableness” standard—namely, what would an average reasonable person have understood from the interaction between the officer and the consenting individual? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On review, our examination is deferential; we overturn a district court’s findings regarding the voluntariness of consent and the scope of consent only if there is clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two particular elements of the interaction are noteworthy. First, throughout the encounter, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and manner consistent with concern rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her ability to withhold consent for entry, as evidenced by her initial refusal in response to Officer Kizer’s first request. It was only after a subsequent, non-coercive request that she elected to allow entry. The video footage makes these points apparent. From Officer Kizer’s arrival at the Coleman home, he communicated with Lisa in a calm and respectful fashion, and Lisa’s responses throughout reflected her understanding of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not specifically inform her that she could refuse his entry, his act of requesting permission, along with her initial denial, made that right implicit. At no stage was Lisa detained. Furthermore, while Officer Kizer sought consent on two occasions, the entire interaction spanned only a few minutes. Lisa might have experienced some pressure to permit police entry, but Officer Kizer never acted coercively. Given this record, we see no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa provided consent for the officers to enter. 

Coleman disputes this point, highlighting Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” in his second request—“Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this is a reasonable argument, Coleman’s view is too limited. The district court could reasonably decide that Officer Kizer’s language did not transform his request into coercion when viewed in the overall context. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer, motivated by concern for the children’s safety and conveying an appropriate sense of urgency, asked to enter, but the district court did not err in finding that this request did not override Lisa’s free will. Lisa’s subsequent actions strongly suggest she perceived his request in this exact manner. Upon replying, “Okay,” she immediately explained the children’s locations within the home and reassured the officers about her dogs’ demeanor. These facts support affirming the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to entry.

We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers remained within the boundaries of Lisa’s consent once they were inside. Their express reason for entry was to assess the welfare of her children, including confirming that Coleman did not pose a threat to them or anyone present. Achieving this required them to visually check the residence, find its occupants, and pose relevant questions. The subsequent information permitted officers to obtain a search warrant, resulting in the discovery of three firearms. On these facts, we identify no error in the district court’s finding that the officers properly adhered to the scope of Lisa’s initial consent. Accordingly, there is no necessity for us to examine the government’s secondary arguments regarding exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as justifications for the officers’ entry and initial protective sweep.

In conclusion, we AFFIRM the district court’s decision. We conclude with a general observation that Officer Kizer and the responding officers exercised prudent and thoughtful judgment when confronted with a sensitive and challenging situation.
