Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-3051  
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 domestic violence calls represents a particularly challenging duty for law enforcement. The circumstances of this case illustrate that reality. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a report from a caller who said her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and that he remained in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and engaged with Lisa at the doorway; when asked about the safety of her children, Lisa hesitated. Officer Kizer responded by stating, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa then replied, “Okay,” and informed Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband occupied another room, and their dogs were inoffensive. The district court concluded that this conversation gave Officer Kizer valid consent to enter and also found that neither he nor other officers went beyond the boundaries of Lisa’s consent while inside the house. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered during their presence. We concur with that determination and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, making it easier for them to come in by moving her dogs aside. She escorted the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. Officer Kizer conducted a swift check of the house and located Coleman in a different bedroom. After a brief conversation, Coleman consented to Officer Kizer performing a protective pat down. Upon speaking with the two children, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in an argument during which Coleman was characterized as being loud, aggressive, and mean. The children reported that their father had brandished a gun at their mother and threatened both to shoot her and to set the house on fire. When questioned further, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun, both on that evening and previously, though they did not know its present location.

B

After the officers entered the residence and gathered additional information, they obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman household. During the search, multiple firearms were discovered, which ultimately resulted in a federal indictment charging Coleman with being a felon in possession of a firearm, in contravention of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that his wife had not given permission for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers to enter the home initially. A hearing was conducted, after which a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa did, in fact, consent to the officers’ entry, and recommended denying Coleman’s motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s finding regarding Lisa’s consent and further rejecting Coleman’s separate argument that the officers had acted beyond the boundaries of her consent while inside the home. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea, expressly reserving his right to appeal the suppression decision. That appeal is presently before this Court.

II

A

The foundational legal standards are not in dispute; both parties concur that the Fourth Amendment’s requirement for a warrant to search a residence does not apply where valid consent has been provided. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Additionally, both sides acknowledge that someone other than the defendant may validly consent to a search: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has firmly held that, to constitute valid consent, it must be “freely and voluntarily” given. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This principle is also reflected in our precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (following the Bumper standard). Courts determine the voluntariness of consent by looking to the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Considerations relevant to voluntariness commonly include, but are not exclusive to, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of his detention before consenting; (4) whether consent was spontaneous or came after repeated police requests; (5) any use of physical force; and (6) whether the person was in police custody at the time consent was given.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is voluntary, officers must still conduct the ensuing search within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Evaluating the extent of granted consent involves an assessment of “objective reasonableness,” where the inquiry is whether a typical reasonable person would interpret the exchange between the officer and the individual giving consent in a particular way. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As a reviewing court, our scope is constrained; we assess a district court’s determinations regarding voluntary consent and any challenge to its breadth under a clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the full encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two notable elements are present in their exchange. First, Officer Kizer consistently maintained a tone and manner reflective of genuine concern rather than any attempt at coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her ability to refuse consent by declining Officer Kizer’s first request to enter. When he asked a second time—without any force or pressure—Lisa elected to permit entry. These facts are apparent on the video record. From Officer Kizer’s arrival at the Coleman household, he communicated in a calm and courteous way to Lisa, who—throughout—indicated an awareness of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform Lisa that she was not obligated to grant consent, his asking permission inherently conveyed such, as did her initial refusal. At no time was Lisa detained. Despite Officer Kizer making a second request, the overall conversation was brief, spanning only a few minutes. Even if Lisa experienced some implicit pressure from the presence of law enforcement, there is no indication that Officer Kizer applied coercion. On this evidentiary record, the district court’s finding that Lisa voluntarily consented was not clearly erroneous.

Coleman disputes this conclusion, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s statement during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this point merits consideration, Coleman’s argument fails to account for the broader context. The district court reasonably concluded that Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” did not transform his request into a coercive one when viewed alongside all circumstances, see United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer expressed concern about Lisa’s children’s safety and conveyed his request with urgency, but without exerting undue influence; the district court’s lack of clear error in this finding stands. Lisa’s subsequent reactions further support that she interpreted Officer Kizer’s conduct as a genuine request: following her “Okay,” she specified where the children were located and advised the officers that while her dogs might jump on them, they would not bite.

Based on these considerations, we uphold the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry. We similarly endorse the finding that the officers’ actions upon entry remained within the boundaries of the consent Lisa provided. Their entry was for the purpose of verifying the children’s welfare and ensuring Coleman did not pose a threat to anyone. Accomplishing this required the officers to survey the residence, ascertain occupants’ locations, and conduct some basic inquiry. The resulting information enabled them to obtain a search warrant and subsequently seize three firearms. There is no deficiency in the district court’s conclusion that the officers honored the scope of Lisa’s initial consent upon entry.

Given these findings, we do not address the government’s alternative argument that exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery principles independently justified the officers’ entry and the initial protective sweep. In conclusion, we AFFIRM. In closing, it is worth noting that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers acted with prudence and balance amid sensitive and challenging circumstances.
