Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers often confront substantial challenges when answering 911 calls related to domestic violence, as this matter illustrates. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a report that Jaison Coleman, the caller's stepfather, had issued threats to kill the caller’s mother, Lisa Coleman, and was reportedly still present in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa. When Officer Kizer inquired whether her children were safe and Lisa hesitated, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and continued by informing Officer Kizer that her children were together in one bedroom, her husband was located in another, and her dogs were friendly and not aggressive. According to the district court, this interaction amounted to Officer Kizer obtaining consent to enter the house; furthermore, the court determined that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers did not go beyond the parameters of Lisa’s consent after entry. As a result, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered in the residence. We concur with the district court’s conclusions and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful firearm possession.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to allow Officer Kizer and the other officers entry by opening the door and moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers toward a back bedroom and indicated the children's location. When Officer Kizer could not locate Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied she was unaware. Upon conducting a quick search of the residence, Officer Kizer discovered Coleman in a different bedroom. The officers engaged in a short conversation during which Coleman consented to a protective frisk. While speaking with the children, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had an altercation, during which Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children reported that their father aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When questioned further, the children affirmed that they had seen the firearm both that night and previously, but were unable to identify its present location.

B

After entering the residence, the officers obtained further information and consequently applied for and received a warrant to search the Coleman household. During the execution of this search, multiple firearms were discovered. As a result, Coleman was later federally indicted for possession of a firearm by a felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his residence, arguing that his wife did not consent to Officer Kizer and the other officers entering the home initially. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa had indeed consented to the officers’ entrance and recommending that Coleman’s suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred with this conclusion, adopting the magistrate judge’s finding of Lisa’s consent, and additionally, it dismissed Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the house went beyond the permission granted by his wife. Once the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea, but specifically reserved his right to contest the suppression decision on appeal. That appellate challenge is now before this Court.

II

A

The foundational legal rules in this area are clear. Both parties accept that consent operates as an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s requirement that police obtain a warrant before searching a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). It is likewise undisputed that someone other than the defendant can consent to a search: “A third party may provide valid consent to search premises where both the third party and the defendant possess legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant is entitled to challenge the legitimacy of that consent.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has made it clear that, to be effective, consent must be provided “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Circuit precedent restates this requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and following Bumper’s holding). Whether consent is voluntary is determined by examining all circumstances surrounding the case. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations typically involve, but are not confined to, “(1) the individual’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was notified of his constitutional rights; (3) the length of detention prior to consenting; (4) if consent was offered promptly or only after multiple requests by law enforcement; (5) if any physical force was involved; and (6) the individual’s custodial status at the time consent was given.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is found to be voluntary, officers are confined to the “scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The proper test for scope considers “objective reasonableness”—specifically, courts must determine, “what would an ordinary reasonable person have understood from the interaction between the officer and the individual granting consent?” Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our review is confined. We examine the district court’s findings on whether consent was voluntary, as well as disputes concerning the scope of that consent, under a clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we identify no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers to enter her residence. There are two notable elements in their encounter. First, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and demeanor indicative of concern, rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated the capacity to refuse entry—she initially denied Officer Kizer’s first request. However, after a second, non-coercive request from Officer Kizer, she allowed him to come inside. These details are apparent from the video evidence. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman home, he addressed Lisa in a calm and respectful manner, and Lisa consistently responded in a way that indicated her comprehension of the circumstances. Although Officer Kizer did not state outright that Lisa was not required to consent, his act of seeking permission and her initial refusal strongly suggested as much. At no time during their interaction was Lisa detained. Despite Officer Kizer making a second request to enter, the whole dialogue transpired within a brief span of minutes. Although Lisa may have perceived some pressure to consent, there was never any coercion on Officer Kizer’s part. The record therefore supports the district court’s finding that Lisa’s consent to entry was valid. Coleman, however, contests this conclusion, highlighting Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” in his follow-up request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this argument has merit, it misses the broader context. Considering the situation in its entirety, the district court reasonably concluded that Officer Kizer’s reference to “need” did not transform the request into an act of coercion. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). When Officer Kizer sought permission to enter, he expressed urgency and concern for Lisa’s children’s safety, but did not exert undue influence. The district court’s finding that no coercion occurred is therefore supported by the evidence. Lisa’s response supports this; after she agreed (“Okay”), she disclosed the children’s location inside and informed the officers that, although her dogs might jump, they would not bite. All of these considerations affirm the district court’s finding of valid consent to entry. We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that, once admitted, the officers operated within the boundaries of Lisa’s consent. Their articulated purpose was to ensure the welfare of the children and confirm that Coleman was not a threat. This legitimate purpose necessitated briefly looking through the premises, locating each occupant, and posing some straightforward questions. The information thus obtained led to a search warrant and the seizure of three firearms. Nothing in the record contradicts the district court’s determination that the officers acted within the scope of the consent Lisa gave. These findings obviate the need to assess the government’s alternative arguments that entry and the initial sweep would also be justified by exigent circumstances or the doctrine of inevitable discovery. Ultimately, therefore, we AFFIRM. Finally, we note that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers displayed prudent and judicious conduct in a sensitive and challenging situation.
