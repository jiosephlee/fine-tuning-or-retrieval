Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls related to domestic violence ranks among the most challenging tasks for police personnel. The present matter aptly illustrates this point. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department responded to a report from a caller stating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained present in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and engaged in conversation with Lisa. When Officer Kizer inquired about the well-being of Lisa’s children, Lisa hesitated. Officer Kizer then stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer of the children’s location in one bedroom, her husband’s presence in a separate bedroom, and that the family dogs were friendly and not prone to biting. The district court determined that this interaction granted Officer Kizer the consent required to enter the premises and further found that the officers remained within the limits of Lisa’s consent while inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered in the residence. We concur with this reasoning and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant events are reflected in the body camera recordings taken by Officer Kizer and other officers present at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and identified himself. In response, Lisa came out onto the porch, stating that her son and daughter were inside the house. When questioned regarding her children's safety, Lisa initially only indicated that they were in her bedroom and did not give a clear answer. Officer Kizer repeated his question, asking whether her children would be alright; Lisa again responded uncertainly, stating she “hope[d] so.” Officer Kizer then changed tactics by inquiring about her husband’s whereabouts inside. Lisa replied she did not know, leading Officer Kizer to request permission to enter the home. Lisa, having turned towards the door, hesitated before answering, “um … I’d prefer you not.” Conversation temporarily ceased, at which point Lisa informed Officer Kizer she had to go inside for her shoes. After a period during which Lisa did not reappear, Officer Kizer, now concerned, opened the door slightly and called out several times, asking if Lisa was all right. Roughly a minute later, Lisa came back to the doorway and stepped outside again. Officer Kizer again asked about conditions inside and specifically asked for the name of her husband—whom he believed had threatened her. Lisa answered that it was Jaison, whom we will refer to as Coleman from this point on. What followed was a significant interaction. When Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and whether the children were safe, the following conversation ensued:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She escorted the officers to a rear bedroom, indicating the location of the children. Officer Kizer, not seeing Coleman present, inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she was unaware. After briefly surveying the premises, Officer Kizer discovered Coleman in another bedroom. They engaged in a short conversation, during which Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective pat-down. In discussions with the two children, officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with reports of Coleman being “loud, aggressive, and mean.” The children further reported that their father aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When asked further, both children stated they had seen the firearm, both that evening and previously, though they could not say where it was currently.

B

After entering the home and obtaining additional information, the officers applied for and were granted a search warrant for the Coleman residence. Their search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which subsequently led to a federal indictment accusing Coleman of violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) by being a convicted felon in possession of firearms. Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms evidence, asserting that his wife had not granted consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter the home initially. At a hearing, a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had indeed provided consent for the officers’ entry and thus recommended that Coleman’s suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred with the magistrate judge's determination that Lisa consented, and also rejected Coleman’s separate argument that the officers exceeded the bounds of her consent while inside the house. After the district court refused to suppress the evidence, Coleman entered a guilty plea but expressly reserved his right to appeal the suppression determination. The matter is now before this court on appeal.

II

A

The relevant legal doctrines are clearly established, and both parties concur that consent operates as an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s general warrant requirement for home searches. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties further agree that the person consenting need not be the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that valid consent must be provided “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our precedent similarly requires voluntary consent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (applying the standard from Bumper). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts analyze the totality of circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations generally include—but are not restricted to—“(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is deemed voluntary, any resulting search must be confined to “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The benchmark for determining scope is “objective reasonableness”—that is, the analysis turns on “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting individual? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As a reviewing court, our function is narrow; we assess the district court’s determinations regarding voluntary consent and challenges to scope under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After thoroughly reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we identify no clear mistake in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers entry into her home. Two features of their interaction are particularly notable. First, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor and tone consistent with someone acting out of genuine concern rather than using force or intimidation. Second, Lisa demonstrated she was capable of refusing entry—she in fact declined Officer Kizer’s initial request. She subsequently opted to allow Officer Kizer inside upon his second request, a request which, notably, was not accompanied by coercive conduct. These facts are evident on the video record. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman house, his communications with Lisa were calm and respectful, and Lisa consistently showed awareness and comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly state that she could withhold consent, his act of asking for her permission—and her initial refusal—effectively conveyed that choice. Lisa was never detained at any point. While Officer Kizer made a second request to enter, their entire dialogue lasted only a few minutes. Although it is possible Lisa experienced some pressure about allowing the officers inside, Officer Kizer did not employ coercive tactics at any stage. Based on the record, the district court was not clearly erroneous in its determination that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry.

Coleman disputes this point by emphasizing Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this argument has merit, it adopts too narrow a focus. Considering the totality of the circumstances, the district court could sensibly determine that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer expressed a desire to enter the home based on concerns for the children’s welfare, communicating those concerns with urgency, but the district court reasonably found this was not coercive. Lisa’s response further supports this view: after replying “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer of her children’s whereabouts in the residence and also advised him that while her dogs might jump on visitors, they would not bite.

All these factors support affirming the district court’s conclusion that Lisa gave her voluntary consent for police to enter her home. We also concur with the district court’s finding that, after entry, the officers stayed within the parameters of the consent Lisa granted. Their primary objective was to ensure her children’s safety, including making sure Coleman posed no threat to them or others in the house. Accomplishing this necessarily required the officers to survey the premises, locate occupants, and ask some basic questions. The details they gathered led to obtaining a search warrant for the home and ultimately recovering three firearms. We find no error in the district court’s finding that the officers complied with Lisa’s original consent throughout their actions inside the residence.

Given these determinations, we need not address the government’s alternative argument that exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery justified the officers’ entry and preliminary sweep of the Coleman home. Ultimately, we AFFIRM. We add, in closing, a general observation: Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and measured judgment under sensitive and challenging circumstances.
