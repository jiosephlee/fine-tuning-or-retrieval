Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 reports of domestic violence ranks among the most challenging duties police officers face, as this matter aptly illustrates. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police responded to a 911 call in which a woman reported her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained in the residence. Officer Kizer knocked on the door and engaged Lisa in conversation. When Officer Kizer inquired about the children’s welfare, Lisa hesitated. Officer Kizer then stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” In response, Lisa replied, “Okay,” subsequently informing Officer Kizer that the children were together in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and that their dogs were harmless and would not bite. The district court determined that this conversation provided Officer Kizer with the requisite consent to enter the residence and, additionally, that neither he nor the other officers overstepped the scope of Lisa’s consent once inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms that were found in the home. We concur and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for possessing a firearm unlawfully.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, aiding their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied that she was unaware of his location. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in a different bedroom. They then had a short exchange, during which Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective pat-down. When the officers spoke with the two children, they learned that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, and Coleman was described as being loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further stated that their father had pointed a firearm at their mother and threatened to shoot her and set the house on fire. In response to the officers' questions, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun, both that evening and previously, but did not know its current location.

B

After entering the house and obtaining additional information, law enforcement officers applied for and received a warrant to search the Coleman residence. During the search, officers discovered multiple firearms, ultimately resulting in a federal indictment against Coleman for violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), which prohibits firearm possession by convicted felons. Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms evidence, arguing that his wife never provided consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter the home initially. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge concluded in a report that Lisa did, in fact, consent to the officers’ entry and recommended denying the suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa consented and, in addition, dismissing Coleman’s argument that the officers exceeded the scope of consent while inside the house. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea but preserved his right to contest the suppression issue on appeal. The matter is now before this Court for review.

II

A

The applicable legal standards are firmly established, and both parties concede that consent constitutes an exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement for home searches. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties likewise agree that consent to search is not limited to the defendant; “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (referencing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has maintained that valid consent must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Precedents in this Circuit align with that requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper). When considering whether consent was voluntary, courts evaluate the circumstances as a whole. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations often involve, but are not restricted to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Furthermore, even when consent is found to be voluntary, the authorities’ search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The critical issue in determining the scope is the standard of “objective reasonableness”—specifically, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting party? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appellate review, our function is circumscribed: we assess the district court’s determinations of voluntary consent and any objections to the scope of consent solely for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After thoroughly reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court's conclusion that Lisa voluntarily agreed to allow the officers into her home. Two particular factors merit attention. To begin with, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and manner that conveyed genuine concern rather than any coercive intent. Additionally, Lisa demonstrated that she was capable of withholding consent to entry, which she initially did when Officer Kizer first asked. However, after a subsequent, uncoerced request from Officer Kizer, she decided to permit him entry. The video footage makes these details readily apparent. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived, he addressed Lisa in a calm and respectful way, and throughout their interaction, Lisa responded in a manner showing clear comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not state outright that Lisa could refuse consent, his request for permission implied as much, as did her initial refusal. Lisa was not detained at any time. Even though Officer Kizer repeated his request to come inside, their entire conversation spanned only a few minutes. Lisa may have perceived some degree of pressure to admit the officers, but at no point did Officer Kizer use coercive tactics. On this basis, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa provided voluntary consent.

Coleman disputes this outcome, placing particular weight on Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” during the pivotal second exchange: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Coleman’s argument is certainly plausible, but ultimately too constrained in focus. Considering the entire context, the district court was justified in finding that the officer’s use of the word “need” did not render the request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought permission to enter out of apparent concern for Lisa’s children's safety, conveying a sense of urgency but not stepping over the line into coercion. Lisa’s reply corroborates that she understood the request in these terms: after saying “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer about the children’s whereabouts and assured the officers that her dogs might jump on them but would not bite.

Accordingly, we uphold the district court’s decision that Lisa consented to the entry of the police officers. We further concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers stayed within the scope of the consent given by Lisa when entering the residence. The officers’ declared intent was to ensure the safety of Lisa's children, which involved confirming that Coleman posed no threat. This necessitated visually scanning the premises, locating all present, and asking routine questions. The facts learned by the officers prompted them to obtain a search warrant and to seize three firearms. We detect no flaw in the district court’s finding that the officers acted within the original boundaries of Lisa’s consent once inside the home.

Given these determinations, we need not address the government’s alternative arguments regarding exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery justifying the entry and initial search of the Coleman home. For these reasons, we AFFIRM. To conclude, we observe that Officer Kizer and the other officers acted prudently and with balanced judgment amid sensitive and challenging circumstances.
