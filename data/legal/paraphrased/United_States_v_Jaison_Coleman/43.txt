Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Responding to 911 reports of domestic disturbances ranks among the most challenging tasks faced by law enforcement. The facts before us exemplify such circumstances. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield Police Department in Wisconsin answered a 911 call from an individual alleging that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had made threats to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. After arriving at the home, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and engaged with Lisa, who hesitated when Officer Kizer inquired about the welfare of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded, “Okay,” subsequently informing Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was located in a different room, and that their dogs were not aggressive. The district court concluded this interaction provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter the premises, and further determined that neither he nor his fellow officers exceeded the boundaries of that consent once inside. Accordingly, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered in the house. We concur and affirm Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, helping them gain access by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers to a rear bedroom, indicating the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not observe Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware of his location. A swift search of the premises led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in another bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman consented to a protective pat down by Officer Kizer. Upon speaking with the two children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had engaged in a dispute, during which Coleman had been loud, aggressive, and unkind. The children further stated that their father had threatened their mother with a gun and made threats to shoot her and to set the house on fire. When the officers questioned the children, they affirmed they had seen the firearm, not only that night but on prior occasions as well, though they were currently unaware of its location.

B

After obtaining information as a result of their initial entry into the residence, the officers applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman household. The execution of this search uncovered multiple firearms, eventually resulting in a federal indictment against Coleman for being a felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that his wife never provided consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter the house. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had indeed consented to the entry and recommended the denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s finding that Lisa consented to the entry, and also independently rejecting Coleman’s claim that the officers’ conduct inside the home went beyond the permission his wife had given. Following the district court’s ruling denying his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea while maintaining his right to appeal the suppression decision. The present appeal concerns that suppression ruling.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are clear and uncontested, as both parties concede that consent constitutes an established exception to the Fourth Amendment’s general requirement for a warrant to search a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties also do not dispute that consent to search need not be given by the defendant himself: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has held that valid consent must be provided “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our precedent aligns with this rule. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (applying and citing Bumper). In evaluating whether consent was voluntary, courts look to the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations commonly include, but are not limited to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even if consent is voluntarily given, the ensuing search must not exceed “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (referencing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Determining the scope of the consent turns on “objective reasonableness,” specifically focusing on “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person authorizing the search? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our appellate capacity, our function is narrower. We review findings by the district court concerning the voluntariness of consent, as well as issues relating to its scope, for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After considering the entirety of the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no manifest error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her home. Two features of their interaction are particularly notable. First, throughout their interaction, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and demeanor reflective of genuine concern rather than one designed to compel or pressure Lisa. Second, Lisa demonstrated her ability to refuse permission for entry, as evidenced by her initial denial of Officer Kizer’s request. However, she subsequently consented to his entry when he made a second, non-coercive request. These conclusions are amply supported by the video record. From the moment he arrived at the Coleman home, Officer Kizer communicated with Lisa in a calm and respectful manner, and Lisa’s responses throughout reflected her understanding of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly tell Lisa that she was not required to allow him inside, his requests for permission implied as much, further underscored by her initial refusal. Lisa was never placed in custody during the encounter. While Officer Kizer repeated his request for entry, the entire discussion spanned only several minutes. Although Lisa may have felt some pressure inherent in the circumstances, there was no coercive conduct on Officer Kizer’s part at any time. On this record, the district court did not clearly err in concluding that Lisa provided valid consent for the officers’ entry. Coleman contends otherwise, arguing that Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” in the pivotal second exchange—“Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay]”—rendered his request coercive. While Coleman’s position has some merit, it focuses too narrowly on isolated language. Considering the totality of the circumstances, see United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008), the district court could reasonably conclude that the use of the word “need” did not transform Officer Kizer’s request into coercion. Officer Kizer’s request stemmed from articulated concern for the safety of Lisa’s children and conveyed a sense of urgency, but there is no clear error in the district court’s determination that he remained within appropriate bounds and did not compel her consent. Lisa’s reply reinforces this view: she responded “Okay” to his request, instructed Officer Kizer regarding the children’s location, and alerted him and the other officers that her dogs may approach but were not dangerous. All these facts support the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to police entry. We further concur with the district court’s finding that, after entry, the officers remained within the boundaries of Lisa’s original consent. Their expressed purpose in entering was to ascertain the well-being of Lisa’s children, including confirming that Coleman did not pose a threat to anyone present. Fulfilling this objective necessitated visually assessing the premises, locating all individuals, and posing basic questions. Information obtained during this process enabled the officers to obtain a search warrant and recover three firearms. We detect no flaw in the district court’s assessment that the officers abided by the scope of Lisa’s consent throughout their time inside the Coleman residence. As these findings resolve the issue, we need not address the government’s alternative arguments based on exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. Ultimately, we AFFIRM, and we emphasize our overall view: Officer Kizer and his colleagues demonstrated prudence and sound judgment under challenging circumstances.
