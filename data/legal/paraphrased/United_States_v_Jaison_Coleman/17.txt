Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls involving domestic violence represents one of law enforcement’s most challenging tasks. The present case illustrates this difficulty. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a 911 report from a woman stating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the house. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and made contact with Lisa, who hesitated when asked about the safety of her children. Officer Kizer responded by saying, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” At that point, Lisa replied, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer that her children were together in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and that her dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court determined that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with the required consent to enter and that he and other officers did not act outside the boundaries of Lisa’s consent after entry. As a result, the district court refused to suppress the firearms found during the entry. We concur in this reasoning and affirm Jaison Coleman's conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant events are documented in body camera footage obtained by Officer Kizer and other officers at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon their arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and identified himself. Lisa appeared on the porch, informing Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside the house. When first asked whether her children were safe, Lisa hesitated, merely indicating at that time that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer pressed the issue, inquiring again if her children would be alright. Lisa responded ambiguously, stating that she “hope[d] so.” Changing tactics, Officer Kizer next questioned her about her husband's whereabouts in the home. Lisa replied that she did not know, prompting Officer Kizer to request permission to enter the house. Turning back toward the door, Lisa stopped and answered, “um … I’d prefer you not.” After a pause, Lisa mentioned to Officer Kizer that she needed to re-enter to retrieve her shoes. Some time went by without Lisa reemerging, which caused Officer Kizer concern; he slightly opened the door and repeatedly called into the home to check on Lisa's wellbeing. Approximately a minute later, Lisa reappeared at the doorway and stepped outside again. Officer Kizer used this opportunity to once more ask about conditions inside the residence and specifically requested the name of her husband, believing he had threatened her. Lisa provided the name Jaison, whom we will hereafter refer to as Coleman. A critical exchange then occurred. In response to Officer Kizer’s questions regarding whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and whether the children were safe, the following conversation took place:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, aiding their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She directed them to a rear bedroom, indicating where the children were located. When Officer Kizer failed to see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied that she was unaware. After briefly surveying the premises, Officer Kizer located Coleman in a different bedroom. They engaged in a short conversation during which Coleman consented to a protective frisk. Upon speaking with the two children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in a dispute, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further stated that their father had aimed a firearm at their mother, threatening to shoot her and set the house on fire. When questioned, the children affirmed that they had seen the firearm, both on that evening and on previous occasions, but were uncertain of its current location.

B

After entering the residence and obtaining additional information, the officers applied for and received a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman residence. This search uncovered multiple firearms, which eventually resulted in a federal indictment charging Coleman with being a felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman sought to have the firearms evidence excluded, arguing that his wife never authorized Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter the home initially. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa had given consent for the officers to enter and recommending that Coleman’s suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s conclusion regarding Lisa’s consent and further dismissing Coleman’s argument that the officers’ actions within the residence went beyond the scope of her consent. When the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly preserving his ability to appeal the suppression decision. The current appeal concerns that suppression ruling.

II

A

The foundational legal standards in this area are firmly established, and both parties acknowledge that consent represents a recognized exception to the Fourth Amendment’s warrant requirement for searches of residences. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties also concur that the source of consent does not have to be the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has made clear that, for consent to qualify as valid, it must be offered “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This standard is reiterated throughout this circuit’s decisions. See, for example, United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and implementing the standard articulated in Bumper). Courts evaluate whether consent was voluntary by looking at the “totality of the circumstances.” See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations generally include, though are not strictly limited to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before providing his consent; (4) whether his consent was given promptly or after repeated prompting by authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion occurred; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody at the time he gave consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is determined to be voluntary, the scope of the search must not exceed the boundaries of that consent. United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To assess the appropriate scope, courts apply an “objective reasonableness” test, considering “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person consenting? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate court, our review is narrowly circumscribed; we examine the district court’s findings on the voluntariness of consent and any questions about scope for clear error only. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court's determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers into her residence. Two elements of their exchange are particularly notable. First, throughout the interaction, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor and tone that reflected genuine concern, rather than any attempt at coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse entry; she, in fact, initially declined Officer Kizer’s request. However, upon his second, non-coercive request, she opted to allow him inside. The video record supports these conclusions. From his arrival at the Coleman house, Officer Kizer addressed Lisa in a calm and courteous manner, and Lisa consistently responded in ways showing comprehension of the ongoing situation. Though Officer Kizer did not state explicitly that she was free to refuse entry, his requests for permission inherently carried that implication, reinforced by Lisa's initial denial. Lisa was never placed in custody. While Officer Kizer made two requests to enter, their conversation lasted only a brief period. Although Lisa may have experienced some implicit pressure to permit entry, Officer Kizer did not exert undue influence at any time. Given these facts, we cannot find that the district court erred in concluding Lisa gave valid consent. 

Coleman offers a contrary argument, focusing on Officer Kizer’s statement during the second request, in which he said, “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While Coleman’s point is arguable, her focus is too limited. The district court could reasonably conclude that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” was not coercive when assessed under the circumstances as a whole. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer requested entry out of concern for the children’s safety, relaying that concern with some urgency, but the court did not err in finding that he refrained from coercing Lisa's consent. Lisa's reaction indicates she interpreted Officer Kizer's words as a genuine request; she replied “Okay,” then informed the officers of her children’s whereabouts and reassured them about her dogs. Taken together, these considerations support affirming the district court's finding that Lisa consented to the officers entering her home.

Similarly, we concur with the district court that the officers acted within the boundaries of Lisa’s consent once inside. Their express purpose was to check on the children’s welfare, including verifying that Coleman did not pose a risk to them or anyone else. To accomplish this, the officers reasonably needed to look through the home, find all occupants, and ask essential questions. The information they obtained provided justification for a search warrant, which resulted in the recovery of three firearms. We detect no error in the court's finding that the officers honored the scope of Lisa’s original consent. Given these determinations, we need not address the government’s alternative arguments regarding exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as bases for entry and the protective sweep of the Coleman residence. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. In closing, we observe that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and careful judgment under complex and sensitive conditions.
