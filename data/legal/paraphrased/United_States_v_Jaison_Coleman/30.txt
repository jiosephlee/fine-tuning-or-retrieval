Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers face significant challenges when responding to reports of domestic violence, and the present matter illustrates such difficulties. Around 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a report from an individual stating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened her mother's (Lisa Coleman) life and remained in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the well-being of her children. In response, Officer Kizer remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer of the locations of her children and husband within the house, in addition to assuring him that the family dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court determined that this dialogue constituted the consent necessary for Officer Kizer to lawfully enter the residence, and further held that neither he nor his fellow officers went beyond the bounds of the consent granted by Lisa once inside. Consequently, the district court denied the motion to suppress the firearms found in the home. We concur with that reasoning and therefore uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful firearm possession.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to allow Officer Kizer and his fellow officers inside by opening the door and moving her dogs aside to assist their entrance. She guided them to a rear bedroom and identified the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not locate Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied that she was unaware of his location. After a brief sweep of the residence, Officer Kizer discovered Coleman in a different bedroom. There was a short exchange, during which Coleman consented to a protective pat down by Officer Kizer. Upon speaking with the two children, the officers were informed that there had been an argument between Lisa and Coleman, during which Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further recounted that their father aimed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. Responding to questions from the officers, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun, both on that night and on previous occasions, but stated they did not know its current location.

B

After entering the residence and obtaining additional information, the officers applied for and received a warrant to search the Coleman residence. This search uncovered multiple firearms, eventually resulting in a federal indictment against Coleman for possession of a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his home, arguing that his wife did not give consent for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers to enter initially. After conducting a hearing, a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa did grant consent for the officers’ entry and recommended denying Coleman’s motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa had consented to the officers’ entry, and also separately rejected Coleman’s argument that the officers acted beyond the boundaries of his wife’s consent once inside the house. Upon the district court’s denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly preserving his right to appeal the court’s suppression decision. That appeal is now before this court.

II

A

The relevant legal standards are clearly settled, and both parties acknowledge that, under the Fourth Amendment, consent serves as a recognized exception to the requirement of a search warrant for entry into a home. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties further concede that the individual providing consent does not have to be the defendant: “A third party may give consent to search premises where both she and the defendant maintain legitimate privacy interests, and the defendant is permitted to dispute the validity of third-party consent.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has long made clear that for consent to be effective, it must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Seventh Circuit precedent reiterates this rule. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing the standard set in Bumper). When courts decide whether consent was voluntary, they evaluate all surrounding circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations typically include, though are not limited to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether constitutional rights were explained; (3) the duration of detention before consent was given; (4) whether consent was given promptly or after repeated law enforcement requests; (5) if physical coercion occurred; and (6) whether the person was in police custody at the time consent was provided.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Additionally, the resulting search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The key question regarding the extent of the consent is “objective reasonableness,” specifically: “what would the typical reasonable person have understood from the exchange between the officer and the consenting individual?” Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate body, our review is narrow; we overturn district court findings on voluntary consent and challenges to its scope only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to the officers' entry into her home. Two factors are particularly noteworthy. First, Officer Kizer consistently maintained a tone and manner that was attentive and concerned, rather than intimidating or forceful. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse consent, as she actually did so when Officer Kizer first requested to enter. It was only after a second, non-coercive request that she decided to allow Officer Kizer inside. These facts are evident in the video evidence. Throughout Officer Kizer’s presence at the Coleman residence, he addressed Lisa in a composed and respectful way, and Lisa consistently reacted in ways that reflected her understanding of the events. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform her that she could refuse entry, his act of seeking her permission effectively conveyed that, as did her initial refusal. Lisa was never detained at any time. Even though Officer Kizer sought consent twice, the interaction lasted only a few minutes. While Lisa might have felt some level of pressure to permit entry, Officer Kizer’s conduct never amounted to coercion. Given these circumstances, the district court did not clearly err in finding Lisa’s consent to the police entry.

Coleman disagrees, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the second, pivotal request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” This argument is reasonable, yet overly restrictive. The district court was within reason to determine that, considering all factors, Officer Kizer’s mention of “need” did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer requested entry out of concern for the well-being of Lisa’s children, communicating that concern with some urgency, but the district court appropriately found that he did so without coercing her into compliance. Lisa’s reply largely confirms her understanding of his request; after replying “Okay,” she directed Officer Kizer to the children's location inside the home and notified him and the other officers about her dogs’ friendly nature. All of these circumstances support the district court’s conclusion that Lisa granted her consent for the officers to enter.

We likewise concur with the district court’s conclusion that, upon entering, the officers acted within the scope of the permission Lisa granted. Their explicit purpose was to verify the welfare of her children, and that required them to look throughout the home, ensure everyone’s location, and pose basic inquiries. The police gathered information that resulted in a search warrant and the seizure of three firearms. We find no error in the district court’s conclusion that the officers complied with Lisa’s initial consent once inside. These findings make it unnecessary to address the government's alternative arguments involving exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery to justify the entry and preliminary search of the Coleman residence.

Accordingly, we AFFIRM. In conclusion, we note that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers exercised prudent and well-reasoned judgment in the face of challenging and sensitive circumstances.
