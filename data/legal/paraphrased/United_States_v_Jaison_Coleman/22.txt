Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers frequently face immense challenges when responding to 911 calls involving domestic violence, as illustrated in this matter. Around 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department was dispatched following a report from a caller stating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and was still present in their residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa Coleman, who hesitated when questioned about the wellbeing of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and continued by indicating that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and their dogs would not bite. The district court concluded that this conversation provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter, and further determined that neither he nor the accompanying officers exceeded the boundaries of Lisa’s consent while inside. Accordingly, the district court declined to suppress the firearms later discovered within the home. We concur with this reasoning and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to allow Officer Kizer and his fellow officers inside by opening the door and ushering her dogs out of the way. She directed the officers toward a rear bedroom and indicated the location of the children. When Officer Kizer could not locate Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. Upon a brief survey of the residence, Officer Kizer discovered Coleman in another bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman agreed to a protective pat down by Officer Kizer. While speaking with the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with the latter described as being loud, hostile, and unkind. The children reported that their father aimed a firearm at their mother and made threats to shoot her and to set the house ablaze. When questioned further, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun, both that evening and on prior occasions, but stated they did not presently know its location.

B

After gathering information during their initial entry into the residence, law enforcement officers obtained a warrant authorizing the search of the Coleman household. The execution of this warrant uncovered multiple firearms, eventually resulting in Coleman being federally charged under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) for firearm possession as a previously convicted felon. Coleman sought to exclude the firearms as evidence, arguing that his wife had never granted Officer Kizer and the accompanying officers permission to enter the home. Upon conducting a hearing, the magistrate judge determined that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the entry and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, formally adopting the magistrate’s conclusion regarding Lisa’s consent and, in addition, dismissing Coleman’s argument that the officers exceeded the boundaries of his wife’s consent while in the home. After his motion to suppress was denied by the district court, Coleman entered a guilty plea but expressly preserved his right to appeal the suppression decision. That appeal is now before this Court.

II

A

The applicable legal standards are settled, as both parties acknowledge that consent constitutes an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s general rule requiring a warrant to search a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both sides further concede that the authority to consent need not reside with the defendant alone: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). According to longstanding Supreme Court precedent, valid consent must be “freely and voluntarily” given. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This circuit has reaffirmed that same principle. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and applying Bumper’s standard). The evaluation of whether consent was voluntary requires consideration of all surrounding circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations frequently include, among other factors: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is voluntarily provided, any subsequent search must remain within the “scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Determining whether the scope was exceeded requires an analysis of “objective reasonableness”: courts ask, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting party? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our capacity as a reviewing court, our function is narrowly defined. We assess the district court’s conclusions on voluntary consent and the breadth of that consent for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entirety of the encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no manifest error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the police officers to enter her residence. There are two notable features of their exchange. Initially, Officer Kizer maintained a manner and tone indicative of concern rather than coercion. Additionally, Lisa demonstrated the capacity to withhold consent—she refused Officer Kizer’s first request to enter. Only after a second request, this time without any element of coercion, did she agree to permit entry. The video evidence supports these points. From his arrival at the Coleman home, Officer Kizer addressed Lisa in a composed and courteous fashion; Lisa consistently replied in a way that showed she comprehended the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not specifically inform Lisa that her consent was not obligatory, his act of requesting permission implicitly suggested such, as did her initial refusal. Lisa was never subjected to custodial restraint at any moment. While Officer Kizer made two requests to enter, their dialogue lasted only a few minutes. Even though Lisa may have experienced some implicit pressure to allow the police inside, Officer Kizer did not, at any time, act coercively. The evidence thus does not show clear error in the district court’s finding that Lisa gave consent for entry.

Coleman contests this conclusion, particularly highlighting Officer Kizer’s statement, “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay],” during the second interaction. Though this argument carries weight, it places undue emphasis on one aspect. The district court was well within reason in determining that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” did not render his request coercive, when examined in context. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer articulated his wish to enter out of concern for the children’s well-being, expressing a sense of urgency and concern, but the district court’s finding that this was not coercion is not clearly erroneous. Lisa’s immediate response underscores her understanding: after replying “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer where the children were located and reassured the officers about the dogs inside. Based on these circumstances, we uphold the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to entry by the police.

Similarly, we agree with the district court’s assessment that the officers’ actions after entering fell within the bounds of Lisa’s consent. The officers entered for the declared purpose of ensuring the safety of Lisa’s children and verifying whether Coleman posed any threat, which necessarily entailed looking throughout the home, identifying those present, and making routine inquiries. The knowledge gained during this process prompted them to seek a search warrant and ultimately recover three firearms. We detect no error in the lower court’s conclusion that the officers respected the scope of consent Lisa initially granted. Because of these determinations, we need not consider the government’s argument based on exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery justifying entry or the subsequent sweep of the residence.

In sum, we AFFIRM. We further note, as a closing observation, that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers exhibited commendable judgment and equilibrium under sensitive and challenging circumstances.
