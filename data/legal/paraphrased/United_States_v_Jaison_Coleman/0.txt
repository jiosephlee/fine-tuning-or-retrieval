Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls concerning domestic violence ranks among the most challenging duties faced by law enforcement. This matter exemplifies that reality. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a report from a caller who stated that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked at the door and engaged Lisa in conversation. When Officer Kizer inquired about the welfare of Lisa’s children, Lisa hesitated. Officer Kizer responded by stating, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and went on to inform Officer Kizer that her children were together in one bedroom, her husband was in a different room, and that her dogs were friendly and non-aggressive. The district court determined that this interaction gave Officer Kizer the necessary consent to enter the home, and additionally concluded that neither he nor his fellow officers exceeded the bounds of the consent provided by Lisa once inside. Accordingly, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered in the residence. We concur and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are derived from the body camera recordings made by Officer Kizer and other officers present at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). When Officer Kizer arrived, he knocked on the front door and identified himself. In response, Lisa came out onto the porch and informed Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside the house. When asked if her children were safe, Lisa hesitated, initially saying only that the children were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer repeated his question about the children’s well-being, to which Lisa replied that she “hope[d] so.” Officer Kizer then shifted his inquiry, asking where her husband was in the home. Lisa answered that she did not know, and when Officer Kizer asked for permission to enter, Lisa, having just turned toward the door, hesitated before stating, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation paused, after which Lisa told Officer Kizer she needed to re-enter the house to retrieve her shoes. After some time passed and Lisa did not reappear, Officer Kizer, concerned, opened the door slightly and called into the residence multiple times to check on her welfare. Roughly a minute later, Lisa returned to the door and stepped outside again, prompting Officer Kizer to repeat his questions regarding the situation inside and specifically ask for the name of her husband, whom he understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that his name was Jaison, whom the opinion will refer to as Coleman going forward. A significant interaction then took place. Officer Kizer inquired whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and if the children were all right, which led to the following conversation:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door to allow Officer Kizer and the other officers inside, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs out of the way. She escorted the officers to a rear bedroom, indicating the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware of his location. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in another bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman agreed to let Officer Kizer perform a protective frisk. While speaking with the two children, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in an argument, during which Coleman had allegedly been loud, aggressive, and hostile. The children further reported that their father aimed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and also threatened to set the house on fire. When asked follow-up questions, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun both that evening and on previous occasions, although they did not know its current location.

B

After entering the residence and gathering additional information, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a search warrant for the Coleman residence. The execution of this warrant resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which ultimately led to a federal indictment accusing Coleman of violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) by possessing a firearm as a previously convicted felon. Coleman filed a motion to suppress the evidence of the firearms found in his home, arguing that Officer Kizer and the other officers lacked initial consent from his wife for entry. After conducting a hearing, the magistrate judge determined that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers’ entry and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, formally adopting the magistrate judge’s finding regarding Lisa’s consent and separately dismissing Coleman’s argument that the officers’ actions inside the home surpassed the limits of his wife’s consent. Once the district court denied the suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly reserving his right to appeal the suppression decision. The present appeal concerns that ruling.

II

A

The controlling legal standards in this area are firmly established, and both parties acknowledge that under the Fourth Amendment, consent serves as an exception to the general requirement for a warrant to search a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both sides also concur that such consent does not necessarily need to originate from the defendant; “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (referencing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has long held that consent must be “freely and voluntarily” given in order to be valid. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our precedents are consistent with this rule. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and employing the Bumper standard). In determining whether consent is voluntary, courts evaluate the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations may encompass, among others: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is voluntarily provided, the search conducted pursuant to that consent must stay within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To determine the boundaries of consent, courts apply an “objective reasonableness” test, considering “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person granting consent. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our function is circumscribed; we examine a district court’s determinations regarding voluntariness of consent and challenges to its scope under a clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the full encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers’ entry into her home. Two key elements of their interaction are notable. First, Officer Kizer’s manner was consistently concerned rather than coercive. Second, Lisa demonstrated her ability to refuse entry, as evidenced by her initial denial of Officer Kizer’s request. She subsequently permitted Officer Kizer inside after a second, non-coercive request. This is plainly observable in the video record. From Officer Kizer’s arrival at the Coleman home, his communication with Lisa was measured and courteous, and Lisa at all times reacted in a way that showed she comprehended the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not specifically inform her that she was not obliged to consent, his act of asking for permission conveyed this implicitly, as did Lisa’s initial refusal. Lisa was never detained during the exchange. While Officer Kizer made two requests to enter, their discussion spanned only a brief period. Although Lisa may have felt some pressure from the circumstances, there is no indication that Officer Kizer applied any improper force or coercion. Accordingly, the district court’s finding that Lisa gave consent for entry is not clearly erroneous.

Coleman challenges this, highlighting Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this argument is not without merit, it is overly focused on a single word. The district court was entitled to conclude, under the totality of the circumstances, that Officer Kizer’s phrasing did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought consent to enter on the basis of concern for the children’s welfare and communicated his request with urgency, but the district court reasonably found he did not overreach or compel consent. Lisa’s reaction supports this view: she responded “Okay,” directed Officer Kizer to where the children were located, and reassured the officers regarding her dogs’ behavior. All of this substantiates the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to police entry.

We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers remained within the bounds of Lisa’s consent during their time inside the home. The officers’ expressly stated objective was to check on the well-being of Lisa’s children and to verify that Coleman did not pose a threat. Fulfilling this purpose required the officers to look through the home, find all occupants, and ask necessary questions. Information they gathered then formed the basis for a search warrant and the subsequent recovery of three firearms. We detect no error in the district court’s determination that the officers respected the scope of Lisa’s initial consent once inside. These findings make it unnecessary to address the government’s alternative arguments based on exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery with respect to the entry and initial sweep. Ultimately, we AFFIRM. In closing, we note that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and measured judgment under sensitive and challenging conditions.
