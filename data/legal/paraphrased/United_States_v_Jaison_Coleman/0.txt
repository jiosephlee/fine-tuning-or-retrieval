Before ROVNER, HAMILTON, and SCUDDER, Circuit Judges. SCUDDER, Circuit Judge. Police officers often face substantial challenges when addressing 911 calls involving domestic violence, and this case exemplifies those complexities. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a report from a woman who alleged that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and engaged in conversation with Lisa. When questioned about the welfare of her children, Lisa hesitated, prompting Officer Kizer to respond, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” subsequently informing Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband occupied a separate bedroom, and the dogs present in the house were friendly and non-aggressive. The district court determined that this interaction constituted valid consent for Officer Kizer’s entry and that neither he nor his fellow officers exceeded the limitations of Lisa’s consent while inside the premises. Consequently, the district court declined to exclude the firearms discovered within the home. We concur with this assessment and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for the unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant information is derived from body camera videos captured by Officer Kizer and his fellow officers during their time at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and identified himself at the front door. Lisa emerged onto the porch and informed Officer Kizer that her son and daughter remained inside the house. When questioned as to the children’s wellbeing, Lisa at first offered only that they were in her bedroom, hesitating to give a clear answer. Officer Kizer repeated his inquiry about the children’s safety, prompting Lisa to reply ambiguously, expressing that she “hope[d] so.” Shifting tactics, Officer Kizer asked about her husband’s whereabouts in the residence. Lisa replied that she was unsure. When Officer Kizer requested entry, Lisa—having just faced the door again—paused before declining: “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation then paused briefly, at which point Lisa told Officer Kizer she needed to retrieve her shoes from inside. After a noticeable interval during which Lisa did not return, Officer Kizer, concerned, opened the door slightly and repeatedly called out to check on her wellbeing. Roughly a minute later, Lisa reappeared at the entrance and came back outside. Officer Kizer then reiterated his questions about the current situation inside, and specifically asked for the name of her husband, whom Officer Kizer believed had made threats against her. Lisa identified him as Jaison, whom we will refer to as Coleman from this point forward. A crucial dialogue then ensued: when Officer Kizer inquired whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and if the children were all right, the following exchange unfolded:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay. No. 24-3051 Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to admit Officer Kizer and his associates, ushering them in by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers to a rear bedroom, indicating the location of the children. Since Coleman was not immediately visible, Officer Kizer inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she was unaware. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective frisk. During interviews with the two children, officers were told that an altercation had taken place between Lisa and Coleman, characterized by Coleman’s loud, aggressive, and hostile behavior. The children further stated that their father had threatened their mother with a firearm, saying he would shoot her and set the house ablaze. When questioned further, the children confirmed witnessing the firearm, both that night and on prior occasions, though they could not say where it was at present.

B

After obtaining information during their initial entry into the residence, law enforcement officers applied for and received a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman household. This search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms and ultimately led to Coleman’s indictment on federal charges for violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), which prohibits convicted felons from possessing firearms. Coleman subsequently filed a motion to exclude the firearms evidence, asserting that Officer Kizer and his team lacked valid consent from his wife for their initial entry into the home. At a hearing, a magistrate judge determined that Lisa had indeed permitted the officers to enter and recommended denying Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s conclusion regarding Lisa’s consent and independently concluding that the officers did not exceed the bounds of the consent provided. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea but preserved his right to appeal the denial of suppression. The present appeal arises from that ruling.

II

A

The foundational legal doctrines relevant to this case are firmly rooted in precedent. Both parties concede that the Fourth Amendment's general requirement of a warrant for home searches admits of an exception when there is consent. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Furthermore, there is consensus that the defendant need not personally give consent for the exception to apply: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (referencing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). Longstanding Supreme Court jurisprudence makes clear that valid consent must be provided “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Circuit precedent maintains this same requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (reaffirming Bumper). To evaluate whether consent was voluntary, courts look to the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Courts commonly consider a range of factors, including, but not limited to, “(1) the individual’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was informed of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of detention prior to consent; (4) whether consent was immediately given or followed repeated police requests; (5) the presence of physical coercion; and (6) whether the person was in custody when consenting.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Importantly, even where consent is voluntary, any search conducted must stay within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (quoting United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Assessing the scope centers on “objective reasonableness”: courts consider what a reasonable person would interpret from the interaction between law enforcement and the consenting party. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (internal citations omitted). On appeal, our responsibility is narrow—we review the district court’s determinations regarding both the voluntariness and the extent of consent under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

Upon reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no reason to overturn the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers to enter her residence. Two elements of their exchange are particularly significant. First, Officer Kizer’s approach—marked by concern rather than compulsion—pervaded the interaction. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to withhold permission, which she exercised in response to Officer Kizer’s first request to enter. It was only after a subsequent, non-coercive inquiry that Lisa opted to allow entry. These aspects are plainly visible in the video evidence. Throughout Officer Kizer’s presence at the Coleman household, he engaged with Lisa in a composed and courteous manner. At every stage, Lisa’s reactions reflected an understanding of the circumstances. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform Lisa that consent was not obligatory, his act of seeking permission implied this, as did her initial refusal. At no time was Lisa detained or in custody. The officer did make two requests to enter, yet the entire interaction spanned only several minutes. While some psychological pressure may have been present, nothing in Officer Kizer’s conduct constituted actual coercion. Thus, the district court’s conclusion that Lisa’s consent was valid is supported by the record.

Coleman argues to the contrary, concentrating on Officer Kizer’s phraseology during the crucial second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this point is not without merit, Coleman’s critique is too restrictive. The district court could reasonably conclude, based on the totality of circumstances, that the use of “need” did not convert the request into a coercive demand. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer conveyed his desire to enter out of concern for the safety of Lisa’s children, and though he articulated a sense of urgency, the district court did not err in determining that his behavior remained within the bounds of non-coercive persuasion. Lisa’s reply—an unequivocal “Okay,” followed by instructions about her children’s location and a warning about her dogs—underscores her perception of the officer’s intent as a request rather than a demand.

On this basis, we affirm the district court’s holding that Lisa gave her consent for the police to enter her home. Additionally, we concur with the district court that the officers stayed within the limits of Lisa’s authorization once inside. Their express goal was to ensure the children’s well-being, which entailed surveying the premises, locating all occupants, and posing essential questions. The evidence thereby gathered provided the basis for a search warrant, resulting in the discovery of three firearms. We detect no flaw in the district court’s assessment that the officers honored the scope of the original consent after entering the home. These findings make it unnecessary to evaluate the government’s alternative arguments grounded in exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery as justification for the initial entry and search. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. As a final and general remark, Officer Kizer and his team demonstrated prudent and judicious action under challenging circumstances.
