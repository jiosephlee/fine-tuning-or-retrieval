Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers face particularly challenging situations when answering 911 calls related to domestic violence. This case illustrates that point. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department was dispatched to a residence following a call from a woman who reported that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and was still present in the house. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated when asked about the well-being of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded, “Okay,” then informed Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband occupied another room, and their dogs were not aggressive. The district court determined that this conversation provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter the residence, and that he and the other officers did not go beyond the boundaries of Lisa’s consent while inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms that were discovered in the residence. We concur with this assessment and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant events are depicted in body camera videos recorded by Officer Kizer and other officers at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). When he arrived, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and identified himself. Lisa came onto the porch and informed Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside the house. When asked whether her children were safe, she at first only said they were in her bedroom and avoided giving a direct answer. Officer Kizer repeated his question about the children's safety, to which Lisa replied uncertainly that she “hope[d] so.” Officer Kizer then shifted his questioning and inquired about her husband’s whereabouts within the house. Lisa answered that she was unsure. He proceeded to ask if he could come inside, and as Lisa turned to the door, she hesitated before replying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation paused, after which Lisa stated she needed to go inside for her shoes. When Lisa did not return for some time, Officer Kizer, becoming concerned, slightly opened the door and called out several times to check if she was alright. Approximately a minute later, Lisa reappeared at the entrance and again stepped outside. This prompted Officer Kizer to ask once more about the situation inside the house and, more specifically, for her husband’s name, since he understood that the husband had made threats against her. Lisa identified her husband as Jaison, hereinafter referred to as Coleman. This was followed by a significant interaction. Officer Kizer inquired if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, further, whether the children were safe, leading to the following exchange:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, ushering them inside by moving her dogs out of their path. She directed the officers to a rear bedroom and identified the location of the children. When Officer Kizer inquired about Coleman's whereabouts, Lisa indicated she did not know. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in another bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective frisk. Upon interviewing the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in a dispute, during which Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and unkind. The children further recounted that their father had aimed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. In response to officer questioning, the children affirmed having seen the gun both that evening and on earlier occasions, but stated they did not know its present location.

B

After entering the residence and obtaining additional information, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman home. This search uncovered multiple firearms, which eventually resulted in Coleman being federally charged with possession of a firearm by a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman subsequently filed a motion to suppress the firearms seized from his home, arguing that Officer Kizer and other officers entered without valid consent, as his wife had never authorized their initial entry. At a subsequent hearing, a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa did provide consent for the officers to enter and recommended that Coleman’s motion be denied. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa had consented to the officers’ entry, and also separately rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the home surpassed the bounds of her consent. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea but preserved his right to challenge the suppression decision on appeal. The appeal regarding that issue is now before this court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards in this area are well-settled, with both parties agreeing that consent constitutes an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s requirement of a warrant to search a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties further concur that consent is not restricted to the defendant; indeed, “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has long held that, for consent to be lawful, it must be granted “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This principle is reflected in our precedent as well. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and applying the Bumper standard). In evaluating whether consent was voluntary, courts consider the “totality of the circumstances.” See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Factors that are commonly considered include—but are not limited to—“(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the length of detention before consent was provided; (4) whether consent was immediate or followed multiple law enforcement requests; (5) the presence of any physical coercion; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when providing consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is found to be voluntary, the resulting search must adhere to the “scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The evaluation of the scope hinges on “objective reasonableness,” meaning the court asks “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenter? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate body, our function is circumscribed; we review findings of voluntary consent and challenges to scope under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we conclude that the district court did not clearly err when determining that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers to enter her residence. Two elements of their encounter are particularly notable. First, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor and tone characterized by concern, as opposed to coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated that she was capable of refusing entry, as evidenced by her initial denial of Officer Kizer’s request. It was only after he made a second, non-coercive request that Lisa agreed to allow him inside. These findings are borne out by the video evidence. From Officer Kizer’s arrival at the Coleman home, he interacted with Lisa calmly and courteously, and her consistent responses revealed her understanding of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not affirmatively state that she was not obliged to consent, his explicit request for permission, coupled with her initial refusal, effectively communicated this understanding. Lisa was not detained at any point, and, while Officer Kizer made two requests, the entirety of their exchange lasted only a few minutes. While Lisa may have felt some pressure from the circumstances themselves, there is no indication that Officer Kizer actually coerced her. Therefore, the record supports the district court’s conclusion that Lisa gave voluntary consent to entry.

Coleman disputes this assessment, drawing particular attention to Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the crucial second exchange—“Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this objection is a plausible one, it is ultimately too narrow. The district court could reasonably find, given the whole context, that Officer Kizer’s choice of words did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought entry on the grounds of concern for the children's welfare, expressing some urgency and intensity, but the record does not show that he crossed the line into improper pressure or coercion. Lisa’s own actions reinforce this; after replying “Okay,” she identified the children’s location in the home and notified the officers about her dogs, clarifying they were not a threat. Consequently, we uphold the district court’s finding that Lisa voluntarily consented to the entry.

We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers did not exceed the boundaries of Lisa’s consent once inside the residence. The officers entered to check on the wellbeing of the children and to ensure Coleman posed no risk to anyone present, a task that naturally involved surveying the premises, identifying the occupants, and asking pertinent questions. Information acquired during this process supported a subsequent search warrant application, culminating in the seizure of three firearms. There is no flaw in the district court’s determination that the officers honored Lisa’s original consent during their time inside the home.

Given these conclusions, we need not address the government’s alternative theories involving exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery as justifications for entry and initial search. In summary, we AFFIRM the judgment below and emphasize, in closing, that Officer Kizer and the other responding officers handled a sensitive and complex situation with judiciousness and balance.
