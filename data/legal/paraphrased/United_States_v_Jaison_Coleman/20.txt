Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls involving domestic violence stands among the most challenging duties for law enforcement. This matter provides a telling illustration. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department responded to a report from an individual stating her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened the life of her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained present in the residence. Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked at the door and engaged Lisa in conversation. When questioned regarding the welfare of her children, Lisa hesitated. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” At this, Lisa replied, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband occupied another, and that their dogs were friendly and would not pose a threat. The district court determined that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with sufficient consent to enter the premises, and also concluded that neither he nor the other officers went beyond the limits of Lisa’s consent during their entry. As a result, the district court declined to suppress the firearms subsequently discovered within the home. We concur with this reasoning and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to admit Officer Kizer and the other officers into the residence by opening the door and clearing her dogs from their path. She guided the officers toward a rear bedroom and indicated where the children were located. Upon failing to observe Coleman, Officer Kizer inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. Officer Kizer located Coleman in a different bedroom after conducting a quick search of the premises. Following a short conversation, Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective pat down. When officers spoke with the two children, they were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been involved in an argument during which Coleman acted in a loud, aggressive, and unkind manner. According to the children, their father had pointed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When questioned, the children stated they had seen the gun, both that night and previously, but could not identify its current location.

B

After entering the residence, officers obtained additional information that led them to apply for and receive a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman home. During the execution of that search, officers discovered multiple firearms, which ultimately resulted in Coleman being federally charged with possession of a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found at his residence, arguing that Officer Kizer and the other officers lacked initial consent from his wife to enter. After conducting a hearing, the magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa Coleman had, in fact, given consent for the officers to enter the home and recommended that the suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination regarding consent and, in addition, rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers had exceeded the parameters of his wife’s consent during their time inside the residence. Once the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea, preserving his right to appeal the suppression determination. That appeal is presently before this Court.

II

A

The foundational legal standards are settled and uncontested here: both parties acknowledge that consent serves as an established exception to the Fourth Amendment’s requirement of a warrant before entering and searching a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties also do not dispute that consent can be provided by someone other than the defendant, specifically, “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (referencing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that valid consent requires it be granted “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This approach is also reflected in circuit precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (relying on and implementing Bumper’s standard). To determine voluntariness, courts consider the total circumstances surrounding the consent. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations may include “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even with valid, voluntary consent, the resulting search must stay within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The relevant test for defining the boundaries of consent is “objective reasonableness,” assessed by asking, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the individual providing consent? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate tribunal, our review function is constrained—we evaluate the district court’s determinations concerning voluntariness of consent and challenges regarding the scope of consent for clear error only. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After examining the full sequence between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry into her residence. Two key factors are particularly noteworthy. First, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor and tone consistent with that of a concerned individual rather than one exerting pressure. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse consent by in fact declining Officer Kizer’s first request for entry. Following a second, non-coercive request by Officer Kizer, Lisa elected to grant permission. These points are clearly reflected in the video evidence. Throughout Officer Kizer’s interactions at the Coleman house, he addressed Lisa in a calm and respectful manner, while Lisa consistently displayed an understanding of the events as they unfolded. Although Officer Kizer did not specifically advise her that she was under no obligation to allow his entry, the nature of his request for permission and her initial refusal indicated as much. Lisa was never detained during the interaction. Even though Officer Kizer requested entry twice, their exchange lasted only several minutes. Lisa may have experienced some level of compulsion to allow entry, but at no juncture did Officer Kizer engage in coercive conduct. On this record, the district court’s conclusion that Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers’ entrance was not clear error.

Coleman challenges this conclusion, placing particular emphasis on Officer Kizer’s phrasing during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this argument has some merit, it takes too limited a view. Considering the totality of the circumstances, see United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008), the district court could reasonably conclude that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” did not convert his request into a coercive demand. Officer Kizer requested entry out of concern for Lisa’s children’s well-being, expressing urgency and seriousness, yet the district court did not clearly err in determining he did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s reaction supports this reading. She answered “Okay,” informed Officer Kizer where the children were, and advised the officers that the dogs might jump but would not bite. Taken as a whole, the record supports the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry into her home.

We further agree with the district court’s conclusion that the officers did not exceed the bounds of Lisa’s consent once inside. Their express reason for entering was to verify the children’s safety, including confirming that Coleman did not pose a threat to anyone in the residence. Accomplishing this necessarily involved inspecting the house, identifying everyone present, and posing some basic inquiries. The information gathered led the officers to seek and obtain a search warrant, resulting in the recovery of three firearms. The record does not support any deficiency in the district court’s determination that the officers respected the scope of Lisa’s initial consent throughout their actions inside the home.

Accordingly, we need not address the government’s additional arguments that exigent circumstances or the inevitable discovery doctrine justified the officers’ entry and initial sweep of the Coleman home. Ultimately, we AFFIRM. We close with an overarching observation: Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and balanced judgment when faced with a sensitive and challenging situation.
