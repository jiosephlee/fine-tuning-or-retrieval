Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-3051  
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge  

Handling 911 reports of domestic violence is among the most challenging tasks law enforcement officers face. This case exemplifies that difficulty. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a call reporting that Jaison Coleman, the stepfather, had threatened to kill Lisa Coleman, the caller's mother, and remained inside the residence. Officer Kizer knocked and engaged with Lisa at the door. When asked about her children's wellbeing, Lisa hesitated, prompting Officer Kizer to state, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” In response, Lisa replied, “Okay,” then informed Officer Kizer that her children were together in one bedroom, her husband was in another room, and that their dogs were gentle and would not bite. The district court concluded this conversation gave Officer Kizer the consent required to enter the home, and also determined that neither Kizer nor the other officers exceeded the scope of that consent during their time inside. As a result, the district court denied suppression of the firearms ultimately discovered in the residence. We concur with this assessment and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful firearm possession.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door, allowing Officer Kizer and the other officers to come inside, and made their entry easier by pushing her dogs aside. She took the officers to a rear bedroom, indicating where the children could be found. As Officer Kizer did not see Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she was unsure. Officer Kizer subsequently located Coleman in another bedroom during a brief search of the residence. After a short conversation, Coleman agreed to let Officer Kizer perform a protective pat down. Upon speaking with the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children also reported that their father aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and said he would set the house on fire. When asked further, the children confirmed that they had seen the firearm, both that night and previously, but did not currently know its location.

B

After the officers entered the residence and gathered additional information, they applied for and obtained a warrant to search the Coleman household. The subsequent search revealed multiple firearms, which eventually resulted in a federal indictment against Coleman for possessing a firearm as a previously convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion seeking to suppress the firearms found in his home, arguing that his wife had never provided consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter initially. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa did in fact consent to the officers’ entry and recommending that Coleman’s suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa had consented, and also separately rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct within the residence went beyond the parameters of his wife’s consent. Once the district court denied the suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea, reserving the right to appeal the court’s decision regarding suppression. That appeal is presently before this Court.

II

A

The relevant legal standards are firmly established, with both parties acknowledging that consent constitutes an exception to the requirement of a warrant under the Fourth Amendment for residential searches. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties also concede that the defendant himself need not provide the consent: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has held for many years that, to be effective, consent must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our circuit follows this same principle. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (applying the rule as stated in Bumper). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts examine all circumstances involved. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations commonly encompass: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before providing consent; (4) whether consent was given immediately or was elicited by repeated requests from authorities; (5) whether any force or physical pressure was applied; and (6) whether the person was in police custody at the time consent was given.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is voluntary, the resultant search must not exceed “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The central question in defining the scope of consent is whether it is “objectively reasonable,” specifically, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person granting consent? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our capacity as an appellate court, our review is deferential; we only overturn district court determinations regarding the voluntariness or the scope of consent if there is clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

Upon review of the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we identify no manifest error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers to enter her residence. Two features of their encounter are notable. To begin, Officer Kizer consistently spoke and behaved with a demeanor reflecting concern rather than compulsion. Additionally, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to withhold consent, evidenced by her initial refusal to allow entry following Officer Kizer’s first request. Only after a second, non-coercive request did Lisa agree to permit him inside. The video footage substantiates these points. From his arrival at the Coleman residence, Officer Kizer communicated with Lisa in a calm and respectful manner, and Lisa responded throughout in ways indicating her comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform Lisa that she had the right to refuse his entry, the fact that he sought her permission and that she initially declined conveyed that understanding. Lisa was never detained during the encounter. Although Officer Kizer twice requested entry, their discussion was brief, lasting only a few minutes. Lisa may have experienced some pressure to admit the officers, but there is no indication that Officer Kizer engaged in coercion. On this record, we find no clear error in the district court's conclusion that Lisa consented to entry by the police. Coleman argues to the contrary, highlighting Officer Kizer’s use of “need” during the second, pivotal exchange: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” This argument, though not unfounded, is ultimately too narrow in focus. The district court was justified in determining that use of the term “need” did not render Officer Kizer’s request coercive when considering the overall context. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s inquiry to enter, motivated by concern for the safety of Lisa’s children, was delivered with urgency and seriousness, but the district court reasonably concluded this did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s subsequent conduct indicates that she perceived his request in this light. After saying “Okay,” Lisa directed Officer Kizer to the children’s location within the home and reassured him and the other officers that while her dogs might jump, they would not bite. All these circumstances support affirming the district court's finding that Lisa granted consent for entry. We also concur with the district court’s assessment that the officers remained within the bounds of Lisa’s consent once inside the residence. Their expressed intent was to ensure the well-being of Lisa's children, which included determining whether Coleman posed a threat to them or anyone else. This purpose inevitably required officers to look through the home, find everyone present, and ask basic questions. The facts the officers obtained led them to seek and secure a warrant, ultimately resulting in the recovery of three firearms. We discern no flaw in the district court’s conclusion that, after entering, the officers adhered to the scope of consent given by Lisa. Consequently, there is no necessity to address the government's alternative arguments regarding the applicability of exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery justifying the officers’ entry and initial sweep. In conclusion, we AFFIRM. We do so with a final observation: Officer Kizer and his colleagues demonstrated prudent and thoughtful judgment in a sensitive and challenging situation.
