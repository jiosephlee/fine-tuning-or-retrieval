Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers frequently face their greatest challenges when answering 911 reports of domestic violence. This matter serves as a notable illustration. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a 911 call made by a woman who reported that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained present in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and engaged in conversation with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the safety of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” then explained to Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband occupied a separate bedroom, and that her dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court determined that this dialogue provided Officer Kizer with the consent necessary to enter the home and that he, along with his fellow officers, remained within the bounds of the consent granted by Lisa once they were inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered during the search of the home. We concur with this determination and uphold Jaison Coleman's conviction for unlawful firearm possession.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his team, allowing them entry and moving her dogs aside to clear their path. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated the location of the children. When Officer Kizer inquired about Coleman's whereabouts after not observing him, Lisa replied that she was unaware of his location. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in another bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman consented to a protective frisk by Officer Kizer. While speaking with the two children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with the children describing Coleman's behavior as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father had aimed a gun at their mother, threatening to shoot her and set the house on fire. Upon questioning, the children stated they had seen the firearm that night and on previous occasions, but did not know its current location.

B

After obtaining information subsequent to their initial entrance into the residence, the officers applied for and were granted a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman household. This search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, ultimately culminating in a federal indictment that charged Coleman with firearm possession as a convicted felon, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion seeking to suppress the firearms recovered from his home, arguing that his wife had not consented to the entry of Officer Kizer and the other officers at the outset. After conducting a hearing, a magistrate judge determined in a report that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers' entry and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred with this conclusion, formally adopting the magistrate judge’s finding regarding Lisa’s consent and, independently, rejecting Coleman’s assertion that the officers acted beyond the boundaries of his wife’s consent once inside. When the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea but expressly preserved the right to appeal the denial of suppression. The matter is now before this Court on appeal.

II

A

The controlling legal standards in this area are clear, and both parties acknowledge that under the Fourth Amendment, searches of a residence generally require a warrant, but an exception exists where consent is given. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Similarly, the parties agree that the consent to search does not have to come directly from the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). For consent to be effective, it must be provided “freely and voluntarily,” a principle the Supreme Court has consistently endorsed. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our court’s jurisprudence has reaffirmed this requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (adopting Bumper’s standard). Courts determine whether consent was voluntary by examining the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Factors relevant to this analysis commonly include, though are not restricted to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of any detention prior to consenting; (4) whether the consent was given immediately or after persistent police requests; (5) any use of physical force; and (6) whether the consenting individual was in custody at the time.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). The authority granted by voluntary consent also limits the bounds of any search to “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). In determining this scope, courts apply an “objective reasonableness” test, asking what “a typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person who gave consent. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appellate review, our examination is limited, as we review district courts’ findings as to voluntariness of consent and scope challenges only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that she voluntarily allowed the officers into her residence. There are two notable features of their interaction. First, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and conduct marked by concern, not coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated that she could refuse consent to entry—indeed, she did so in response to Officer Kizer’s initial request. Upon being asked a second time—without any undue pressure—she opted to permit Officer Kizer inside. These facts are evident in the video record. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman home, he addressed Lisa in a calm, respectful manner, and Lisa’s responses throughout signaled her comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not specifically inform Lisa that she was under no obligation to grant entry, his act of asking for permission, together with her initial denial, carried that implication. Lisa was never in custody during the interaction. And, while Officer Kizer did seek consent twice, the entirety of their exchange lasted only a few minutes. While Lisa may have experienced some level of pressure inherent in a police request, there was no coercion exerted by Officer Kizer at any stage. Given this record, the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to the police entry is not clearly erroneous.

Coleman challenges this, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Though this is a plausible point, Coleman’s emphasis is too limited. In assessing the entire situation, the district court could reasonably determine that Officer Kizer’s reference to a “need” to enter was not coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought permission for entry by expressing concern about the well-being of Lisa’s children and articulated that concern with a certain degree of urgency, but the district court was within reason to find his conduct did not cross into coercion. Lisa’s reaction nearly confirms this: after stating “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer where her children were inside the house, while assuring the officers that although her dogs might jump on them, the dogs would not bite. On this basis, we uphold the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry.

We also concur with the district court’s determination that the officers remained within the scope of Lisa’s consent once inside. Their stated aim was to verify the safety of her children and ensure Coleman posed no threat. Accomplishing this necessarily involved surveying the home, locating occupants, and making basic inquiries. The evidence gathered during this process formed the basis for obtaining a search warrant and led to the seizure of three firearms. We discern no error in the district court’s finding that the officers respected the boundaries of Lisa’s initial consent after entering the Coleman home. These findings make it unnecessary for us to address the government’s alternative arguments based on exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery for supporting the entry and initial sweep of the residence. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. Finally, we note that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers exhibited prudence and careful judgment under challenging circumstances.
