Responding to emergency domestic violence calls is among the most challenging duties assigned to law enforcement officers. This matter aptly illustrates that point. Around 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department answered a report from a caller stating her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked at the door and interacted with Lisa, who hesitated when asked about the safety of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” then went on to inform Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in another room, and her dogs were harmless. The district court determined that this dialogue provided Officer Kizer with sufficient consent to enter the house and concluded that neither he nor the other officers surpassed the boundaries of Lisa’s consent once inside. Accordingly, the district court declined to suppress the firearms later discovered on the premises. We concur and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, assisting them in entering the house by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers to a back bedroom, indicating where the children were located. Upon not finding Coleman, Officer Kizer inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she was unaware. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in a different bedroom. Following a short conversation, Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective frisk. When speaking with the two children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had engaged in a fight, during which Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father had aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. In response to further inquiries, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun, both that evening and on previous occasions, although they did not know its current location.

B

After entering the residence, the officers obtained further information that prompted them to apply for and receive a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman household. During the execution of this warrant, authorities discovered multiple firearms, which ultimately resulted in a federal indictment against Coleman for being a felon in possession of firearms, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman subsequently filed a motion to suppress the evidence of the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that his wife never granted consent for Officer Kizer and his associates to enter initially. At a subsequent hearing, a magistrate judge determined that Lisa had indeed consented to the officers’ entry and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s factual finding that Lisa gave consent, and separately held that the officers did not exceed the bounds of that consent while inside the home. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea but specifically reserved his right to contest the suppression ruling on appeal. That appeal is now before this Court.

II

A

The relevant legal standards are firmly established, and both parties acknowledge that consent serves as an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s requirement of a warrant for searches of a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). There is also consensus that the defendant need not personally provide such consent: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently maintained that valid consent must be provided “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This requirement is likewise reiterated in our circuit’s precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (relying on the standard from Bumper). To determine if consent was voluntary, courts examine the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Courts typically consider factors such as: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was informed of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of any detention before consent was obtained; (4) whether consent was given immediately or after multiple requests from authorities; (5) the existence of any physical coercion; and (6) whether the individual was in custody when providing consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is found to be voluntary, a subsequent search must not exceed “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). In assessing the permissible scope, the analysis turns on “objective reasonableness”—specifically, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting party? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate tribunal, our review is constrained; we examine district court determinations regarding voluntary consent and scope of that consent for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

Upon review of the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we detect no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers’ entry into her residence. Two elements of their interaction are particularly notable. First, Officer Kizer consistently maintained a tone and demeanor indicative of concern rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated the capacity to refuse entry—evident when she initially declined Officer Kizer’s first request. Following a second, non-coercive request from Officer Kizer, Lisa decided to permit him entry. These circumstances are apparent from the video evidence. Throughout the encounter, Officer Kizer addressed Lisa calmly and courteously, and her reactions throughout indicated she comprehended the situation at hand. Although Officer Kizer did not affirmatively state that Lisa had the right to refuse consent, his manner of seeking permission suggested as much, reinforced by her initial denial. Lisa was never in custody. Despite Officer Kizer’s two requests to enter, the conversation was brief, spanning only a few minutes. While some inherent pressure may have been felt by Lisa in agreeing to the officers’ entry, Officer Kizer’s conduct never crossed into coercion. The record thus supports the district court’s absence of clear error in its consent finding.

Coleman contends otherwise, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the second, crucial request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this is a fair argument, it focuses too narrowly on a single aspect. The district court could reasonably determine that the use of the word “need” did not render Officer Kizer’s request coercive, especially when considering all circumstances as required by United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer couched his request for entry in genuine concern for the children’s safety, communicating that concern with appropriate urgency but without crossing into improper compulsion as the district court found. Lisa’s reply reinforces this conclusion—her agreement, followed by instructions concerning the children’s whereabouts and reassurances regarding her dogs, demonstrate that she understood and responded voluntarily to the request.

Based on these facts, we uphold the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the officers entering her residence. We also concur with the district court’s finding that, once inside, the officers’ actions remained within the boundaries of the consent given. Their stated objective was to check the welfare of Lisa’s children, which required a brief scan of the premises, locating individuals present, and posing basic inquiries. Information gained during this consensual entry supported their later decision to secure a search warrant and ultimately recover three firearms. The district court correctly found that the officers did not exceed the scope of Lisa’s initial consent once inside the home.

In view of these determinations, there is no need to address the government’s alternative argument based on the exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery doctrines with respect to the entry and initial search of the Coleman residence. Therefore, we AFFIRM. We conclude with the broad observation that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers exercised prudent and balanced judgment in an inherently sensitive and challenging context.
