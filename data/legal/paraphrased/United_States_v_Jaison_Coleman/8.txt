Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers often face significant challenges when responding to 911 calls involving domestic violence, as illustrated by the circumstances of this case. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a report by a caller who alleged that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and engaged Lisa in conversation. When Officer Kizer inquired about the welfare of Lisa's children, Lisa hesitated. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and informed Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in a different room, and that their dogs were friendly and not a threat. The district court determined that Lisa’s responses amounted to consent for Officer Kizer to enter the residence, and further concluded that Officer Kizer and other officers did not act beyond the limits of Lisa’s consent during their entry. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered in the home. We concur with this reasoning and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant events are documented in body camera recordings captured by Officer Kizer and his fellow officers at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and identified himself. Lisa responded by coming out onto the porch and informing Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside the home. When asked whether her children were safe, she hesitated, initially stating only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer inquired again, questioning if her children would be all right. Lisa once more expressed uncertainty, this time replying that she “hope[d] so.” Shifting his line of questioning, Officer Kizer then asked for the whereabouts of her husband inside the residence. Lisa answered that she was unaware of his location, at which point Officer Kizer requested permission to enter. As Lisa, who had just turned back towards the door, paused, she eventually responded, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation then paused, and Lisa stated that she needed to go back inside to retrieve her shoes. After some time passed without Lisa reappearing, Officer Kizer, becoming concerned, partially opened the door and repeatedly called into the home to ask if she was all right. Approximately a minute later, Lisa returned to the entry and stepped back outside, leading Officer Kizer to ask again about the situation inside and, specifically, for the name of her husband—whom Officer Kizer believed had threatened her. Lisa identified her husband as Jaison, whom we will refer to as Coleman for the remainder of the discussion. At this point, a significant exchange took place. Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and, subsequently, whether the children were safe, resulting in the following conversation:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in another bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman agreed to allow Officer Kizer to perform a protective frisk. In their conversations with the two children, officers discovered that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in an altercation, during which Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further recounted that their father had pointed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When asked, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun that evening as well as on previous occasions, but stated they did not know its present location.

B

After gathering information during their initial entry into the residence, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman home. The subsequent search uncovered multiple firearms, which ultimately resulted in Coleman being federally charged with possession of a firearm as a convicted felon, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion seeking to exclude the firearms evidence, arguing that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers entered without valid consent from his wife. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa did provide consent for the officers to enter and recommending that Coleman’s motion be denied. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa had consented to the officers’ entry and, in addition, dismissed Coleman’s separate claim that the officers’ conduct inside the residence went beyond the limits of his wife’s consent. Once the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea, expressly preserving his right to appeal the suppression decision. That appeal is now before this Court.

II

A

The foundational legal standards at issue are firmly settled, and both parties acknowledge that, under the Fourth Amendment, consent serves as an exception to the general rule that a warrant is required for a home search. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). There is likewise agreement between the parties that such consent does not have to originate from the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that valid consent must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This principle is mirrored in our own precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper). The voluntariness of consent is evaluated by courts by looking to all circumstances surrounding the consent. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Factors commonly considered include, though are not limited to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Furthermore, even if consent is voluntary, any subsequent search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The assessment of consent’s scope turns on “objective reasonableness”—specifically, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person giving consent? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our appellate capacity, our review is constrained; we evaluate findings of voluntary consent and challenges to the scope of consent only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

Upon reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her home. Two features of their interaction are particularly noteworthy. First, throughout the encounter, Officer Kizer’s attitude and approach were characterized by concern rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse entry, as evidenced by her initial rejection of Officer Kizer’s first request. She subsequently chose to grant entry after he repeated his request, with no signs of coercion accompanying this second solicitation. The video record substantiates these conclusions. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman residence, his communication with Lisa was measured and courteous, and Lisa consistently responded in a manner that indicated she was aware of the circumstances. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly advise Lisa that she was free to withhold consent, his requests for permission, as well as her initial denial, implicitly conveyed her right to refuse. At no time was Lisa detained. Even though Officer Kizer made two requests for entry, their entire interaction lasted only a brief period. While it is possible that Lisa felt some degree of implicit pressure, Officer Kizer did not employ coercive tactics at any point. Based on this record, the district court did not clearly err in concluding that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry.

Coleman disputes this conclusion, placing particular emphasis on Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this argument is reasonable, Coleman’s perspective is too restrictive. The district court was justified in finding that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” did not render his request coercive when evaluated in light of all circumstances. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer framed his request as an expression of concern for the welfare of Lisa’s children and communicated that concern with a sense of urgency, but the district court did not err in concluding he did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s response reinforces this view; after replying, “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer of the children’s location in the home and reassured him and the other officers regarding her dogs, stating that while they might jump, they would not bite. Collectively, these facts support the district court’s finding that Lisa gave her consent to the officers’ entry.

We further concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers remained within the bounds of Lisa’s consent after entering the home. The officers’ articulated objective was to ensure the safety of the children and confirm that Coleman posed no threat to them or others. This purpose necessarily involved inspecting the premises, accounting for all individuals present, and asking preliminary questions. The resulting information led to obtaining a search warrant and the recovery of three firearms. We identify no flaw in the district court’s finding that the officers, once admitted, acted within the scope of the consent Lisa provided. Accordingly, we need not address the government’s alternative arguments relying on the doctrines of exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as justifications for the initial entry and sweep of the Coleman residence.

Ultimately, we AFFIRM. We close by noting that Officer Kizer and the other officers displayed prudent and balanced judgment under challenging and sensitive conditions.
