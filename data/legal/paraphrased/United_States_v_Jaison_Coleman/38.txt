Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-3051  
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Answering emergency calls involving domestic violence ranks among the most challenging duties law enforcement officers face. This matter serves as a telling illustration. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department responded to a report from a caller stating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and that he remained within the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked at the door and engaged with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the well-being of her children. In response, Officer Kizer remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer that her children occupied one bedroom, her husband was in a separate room, and her dogs were harmless. The district court determined that this dialogue provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter the premises and further concluded that neither he nor his fellow officers exceeded the boundaries of Lisa’s consent once inside. As a result, the district court declined to suppress the firearms subsequently discovered during the search. We concur with this assessment and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and the accompanying officers, helping them enter by moving her dogs aside. She guided them to a back bedroom and indicated the children's location. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unsure. Officer Kizer quickly searched the residence and located Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective pat down. In interviewing the two children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had engaged in an argument, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children also stated that their father had pointed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When asked, the children confirmed having seen the gun both that night and previously, but stated they did not know its current location.

B

After entering the house and obtaining additional information, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman residence. This search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which eventually led to Coleman being federally charged with possession of a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress evidence of the firearms found in his home, arguing that his wife did not consent to Officer Kizer and the other officers entering the residence initially. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge determined that Lisa had indeed consented to the officers’ entry and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s conclusion that Lisa gave consent, and additionally dismissed Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the house surpassed the boundaries of his wife’s consent. After the district court denied the motion to suppress, Coleman pleaded guilty, specifically reserving the right to appeal the denial of suppression. Coleman’s appeal on this issue is now pending before this Court.

II

A

The foundational legal standards are clearly settled, and both parties acknowledge that under the Fourth Amendment, consent acts as an exception to the usual requirement of a warrant for searching a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties are also in agreement that consent does not necessarily have to be provided by the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). For such consent to be considered valid, the Supreme Court has consistently held it must be provided “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This criterion is reiterated in our own precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and relying on Bumper). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts examine all surrounding circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant factors that may be considered include, but are not restricted to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is found to be voluntary, the ensuing search must stay within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Whether the consent’s scope was exceeded is judged by “objective reasonableness”—that is, what would an average reasonable person understand based on the exchange between law enforcement and the consenting party? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As a reviewing court, our responsibility is circumscribed; we assess a district court’s determination that consent was voluntary, as well as any related dispute regarding its scope, for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entirety of the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we discern no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two particular aspects of their interaction are especially noteworthy. First, throughout, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and conduct that expressed concern rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated she could refuse consent, which she did when initially declining Officer Kizer’s request. Following a second, non-coercive request, Lisa permitted Officer Kizer to enter. These facts are apparent from the video recording. From his arrival at the Coleman home, Officer Kizer addressed Lisa calmly and with respect, and Lisa consistently reacted in ways that indicated her awareness of what was happening. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly state Lisa could refuse him entry, his manner of seeking her permission, coupled with her initial refusal, clearly implied such a choice. Lisa was never detained during this period. Additionally, while Officer Kizer made two requests to enter, the entirety of the dialogue lasted only a few minutes. Though Lisa may have experienced some pressure in deciding to admit the officers, at no time did Officer Kizer exert undue influence or force. Based on these circumstances, the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented cannot be deemed clearly erroneous.

Coleman disputes this, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s reference to a "need" during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this argument is not without merit, it takes too narrow a view. The district court was justified in concluding, based on the overall context, that Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” did not make his request unduly coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer, motivated by concern for the welfare of Lisa’s children, sought permission to enter, conveying his worries with a certain level of urgency, but the district court reasonably found he did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s own reaction—her affirmative response and her instructions regarding the location of her children and the temperament of her dogs—supports the finding that she perceived his approach as a request, not a directive. All these considerations support upholding the district court’s conclusion that Lisa gave valid consent to the police’s entry.

Similarly, we concur with the district court that the officers did not exceed the boundaries of Lisa’s consent upon entering her home. The officers’ objective was to check on the welfare of the children and ensure that Coleman did not pose a threat. This task necessitated surveying the premises, locating all individuals inside, and conducting basic inquiries. The evidence obtained during this process justified obtaining a warrant, which ultimately led to the seizure of three firearms. We detect no deficiency in the district court’s finding that the police acted within the consent initially given by Lisa. Consequently, we need not address the government’s alternative argument regarding the doctrines of exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as additional justifications for the officers’ actions.

Accordingly, we AFFIRM. In closing, we note that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and balanced judgment while handling a sensitive and challenging situation.
