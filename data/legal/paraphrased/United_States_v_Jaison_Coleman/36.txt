Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers often face considerable challenges when responding to 911 reports of domestic violence. The present matter exemplifies such difficulties. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department answered a report from a caller who indicated her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had made death threats against her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Officer Kizer arrived, knocked on the door, and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated to answer a question regarding the welfare of her children. In response, Officer Kizer remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer that her children occupied one bedroom, her husband another, and that her dogs were nonaggressive and would not bite. The district court determined that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with the requisite consent to enter the house, and additionally concluded that he and his fellow officers remained within the limits of Lisa’s consent during their presence inside. As a result, the district court declined to exclude the firearms discovered within the home. We concur with the district court's reasoning and affirm Jaison Coleman's conviction for unlawful firearm possession.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door, allowing Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter, and aided their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers to a back bedroom and indicated the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware of his location. Officer Kizer and his colleagues quickly searched the residence, eventually locating Coleman in another bedroom. Following a short conversation, Coleman agreed to let Officer Kizer conduct a protective pat-down. During interviews with the children, officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in a dispute, with Coleman allegedly behaving in a loud, aggressive, and hostile manner. The children further reported that their father had brandished a firearm at their mother, made threats to shoot her, and threatened to set the house ablaze. Upon further questioning, the children confirmed they had seen the firearm, both on that evening and previously, but were unable to specify its current location.

B

After entering the house, the officers obtained additional information and subsequently applied for and received a warrant to search the Coleman residence. During the ensuing search, authorities discovered multiple firearms. This discovery ultimately resulted in Coleman being federally charged with possession of a firearm by a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the evidence of the firearms found in his residence, asserting that his wife had not provided valid consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to initially enter the home. After holding a hearing, the magistrate judge determined that Lisa did, in fact, consent to the officers’ entry and recommended that Coleman’s motion be denied. The district court concurred with this conclusion, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination regarding Lisa’s consent and, in addition, rejecting Coleman’s separate argument that the officers’ conduct within the home exceeded the bounds of the consent granted by his wife. When the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea but expressly preserved his right to contest the suppression decision on appeal. The present appeal arises from that issue.

II

A

The applicable legal standards are clearly established, and both parties concur that under the Fourth Amendment, consent serves as an exception to the requirement of a warrant for searching a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties likewise do not dispute that someone other than the defendant can provide such consent: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that, in order to be valid, consent must be “freely and voluntarily” given. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our precedents have adopted and reiterated this requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper). When evaluating whether consent was voluntary, courts look at the total circumstances surrounding the situation. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations often comprise, though are not confined to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of any detention prior to giving consent; (4) the immediacy of the consent versus whether officials made multiple requests; (5) the presence of physical coercion, if any; and (6) whether the person was in police custody when providing consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is deemed voluntary, any ensuing search is subject to “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The determining standard for scope is one of “objective reasonableness,” with courts considering “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person consenting. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our capacity as a reviewing court, our examination is circumscribed. We evaluate district court determinations on whether consent was voluntary or within its proper scope for clear error only. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the full encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we do not discern any clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily agreed to allow the police officers into her residence. Two main features of the interaction are especially notable. To start, Officer Kizer consistently adopted a tone and demeanor suggesting genuine concern rather than compulsion. Additionally, Lisa’s conduct evidenced her ability to refuse consent to entry, as demonstrated by her initial denial when Officer Kizer first made his request. Following Officer Kizer’s renewed request—delivered without any coercive overtones—Lisa then elected to allow him entry. The video evidence makes these points apparent. Throughout Officer Kizer’s presence at the Coleman home, he communicated with Lisa in a calm and respectful manner, and at all times, Lisa’s responses indicated she understood the situation. While Officer Kizer did not specifically inform her she was under no obligation to consent to his entry, his request for permission implicitly conveyed as much, as did Lisa’s initial refusal. Lisa was never detained during this encounter. Though Officer Kizer requested entry twice, the entire discussion lasted only a short time. Lisa might have experienced some pressure from the situation, but nothing in Officer Kizer’s behavior amounted to coercion. Given the record before us, we find no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the entry.

Coleman challenges this finding by focusing on Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” This is a reasonable point for argument; however, Coleman’s reading is unduly narrow. In the context of the overall circumstances, the district court was entitled to determine that Officer Kizer’s choice of the word “need” did not transform his request into an act of coercion. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s permission request centered on his expressed concern for the safety of Lisa’s children and conveyed some urgency, yet the district court reasonably concluded that he did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s subsequent statements support this interpretation; she responded “Okay” and identified where the children were inside, as well as informed the officers about her dogs, assuring them they might jump but would not bite.

Based on this, we uphold the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry. Further, we concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers remained within the scope of Lisa’s consent once inside the home. The officers’ articulated objective was to assess the children’s wellbeing—including ensuring that Coleman posed no risk—which naturally entailed surveying the premises, accounting for those present, and making basic inquiries. The knowledge gathered from this resulted in obtaining a warrant to search the home and in recovering three firearms. We find no deficiency in the district court’s finding that the officers abided by the parameters of Lisa’s initial consent after entering the residence.

Given these conclusions, it is unnecessary to reach the government’s alternative arguments based on exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery regarding the officers’ entry and initial search of the Coleman residence. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. In conclusion, we note that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers handled a sensitive situation with careful and sound judgment.
