Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Responding to emergency calls involving domestic violence ranks among the most challenging duties faced by law enforcement officers, and this matter illustrates that point. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police responded to a report from a woman who stated that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the well-being of her children. Officer Kizer then remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” In response, Lisa replied, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and that their dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court concluded that this conversation provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter and determined that neither he nor his fellow officers acted beyond the limits of Lisa’s consent once inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered in the residence. We concur with that assessment and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are derived from the body camera recordings made by Officer Kizer and other officers present at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon their arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and identified himself. Lisa appeared on the porch, informing Officer Kizer that both her son and daughter were inside the house. When asked if her children were safe, Lisa initially hesitated, responding only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer repeated his question, asking whether her children would be alright. Lisa again gave an uncertain reply, stating she “hope[d] so.” Officer Kizer shifted his line of questioning, inquiring about the location of her husband within the residence. Lisa replied that she was unaware of his whereabouts, at which point Officer Kizer requested permission to enter. Lisa, having turned toward the door, hesitated before replying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation paused, after which Lisa indicated she needed to go inside to retrieve her shoes. When Lisa did not promptly reappear, Officer Kizer, concerned for her welfare, partially opened the door and repeatedly called into the house to check if she was alright. Approximately a minute later, Lisa returned to the threshold and stepped outside again, prompting Officer Kizer to inquire once more about the situation inside and, with greater specificity, the name of her husband—whom Officer Kizer believed had threatened her. Lisa identified her husband as Jaison, hereafter referred to as Coleman. At this point, a significant exchange occurred: when Officer Kizer questioned whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and whether the children were safe, the following conversation took place.

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She escorted the officers toward a back bedroom, indicating the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied that she was unaware. Officer Kizer then conducted a brief sweep of the residence, locating Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman consented to Officer Kizer performing a protective frisk. While speaking with the two children, officers discovered that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with Coleman allegedly behaving loudly, aggressively, and unkindly. The children reported that their father had aimed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and also threatened to set the house on fire. Upon further questioning, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun, both that evening and on prior occasions, though they did not currently know its location.

B

After the officers entered the residence and gathered additional information, they applied for and obtained a warrant to search the Coleman residence. The execution of this warrant resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, ultimately leading to a federal indictment against Coleman for being a felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms seized from his home, arguing that his wife had not given consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to initially enter. After holding a hearing, the magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers’ entry and recommended that Coleman’s motion be denied. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa provided consent for entry and also independently rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers exceeded the bounds of that consent once inside the home. After his suppression motion was denied by the district court, Coleman entered a guilty plea while preserving his right to appeal the suppression decision. The present appeal concerns that issue.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are clearly settled, and both parties acknowledge that consent operates as an exception to the Fourth Amendment's requirement of a warrant for a search of a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Likewise, there is no dispute that such consent need not originate from the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has held that valid consent requires it to be “freely and voluntarily” provided. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our precedents follow the same rule. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and employing the Bumper standard). Courts evaluate whether consent was voluntary by looking at the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Factors that are often relevant include, but are not confined to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of detention prior to the consent; (4) whether consent was given immediately or after repeated requests by law enforcement; (5) the presence or absence of physical coercion; and (6) whether the individual was in custody at the time consent was granted.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even if consent is found to be voluntary, any resulting search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The determination of the scope of consent is guided by an “objective reasonableness” standard; specifically, the inquiry is “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the individual granting consent? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our capacity as an appellate court, our review is circumscribed. We assess a district court's findings regarding the voluntariness of consent and any scope objections for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two features of their interaction are particularly notable. First, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor that reflected concern rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to withhold consent, as evidenced by her refusal in response to Officer Kizer’s initial request to enter. She subsequently opted to permit Officer Kizer entry following his second request, which was not accompanied by any coercive behavior. These details are evident from the video evidence. From Officer Kizer’s arrival at the Coleman home, he addressed Lisa in a calm and respectful manner, and at all times, Lisa’s responses reflected her awareness of the ongoing situation. Although Officer Kizer did not explicitly advise her that she could refuse consent, his act of asking for permission, coupled with her initial denial, effectively communicated that refusal was an option. Lisa was never detained during the encounter. While Officer Kizer did make two requests to enter, their discussion was brief, lasting only several minutes. Although Lisa may have experienced some implicit pressure due to the presence of law enforcement, at no stage did Officer Kizer employ coercion. Based on the record, there is no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa’s consent was valid. 

Coleman disagrees, directing attention to Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” during his second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this argument is not without merit, Coleman’s perspective is unduly restrictive. The district court could reasonably conclude that, given the entirety of the circumstances, Officer Kizer’s phrasing did not amount to coercion. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought permission to enter by voicing concern for Lisa’s children’s well-being, and although he conveyed his concern with some urgency, the district court was not clearly mistaken in determining he did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s reaction—saying “Okay,” identifying where her children were located in the house, and reassuring the officers regarding her dogs—supports this conclusion. These facts support our affirmation of the district court’s finding that Lisa voluntarily allowed police to enter her home.

We also concur with the district court that the officers did not exceed the limits of Lisa’s consent once inside. Their articulated purpose was to verify the welfare of the children, which necessarily entailed inspecting the premises, locating individuals, and conducting basic inquiries. Information gained during this entry led to a search warrant and the recovery of three firearms. We find no fault with the district court’s determination that the officers acted within the bounds of Lisa’s consent during their entry. Because of these conclusions, it is unnecessary to address the government’s alternate arguments that exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery justified the police’s entry and initial sweep. 

Accordingly, we AFFIRM. In closing, we note that Officer Kizer and his colleagues exercised prudent and judicious judgment under challenging circumstances.
