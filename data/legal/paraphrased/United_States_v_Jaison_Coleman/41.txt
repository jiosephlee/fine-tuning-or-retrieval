Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers face significant challenges when answering 911 calls involving domestic violence, as demonstrated in this matter. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department, was dispatched following a report that Jaison Coleman had threatened to kill his wife, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside their residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked at the door and engaged Lisa in conversation; when questioned about the welfare of her children, Lisa hesitated. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and subsequently informed Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband occupied another, and her dogs were harmless. The district court determined that Lisa’s statements gave Officer Kizer valid consent to enter the home and also held that he and his fellow officers acted within the boundaries of this consent after entering. As a result, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered inside the residence. We concur with this determination and affirm Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and his team, also moving her dogs aside to allow them in. She guided the officers toward a back bedroom, indicating where the children were located. Officer Kizer, not seeing Coleman present, inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa answered that she was unaware of his location. After a brief search of the residence, Officer Kizer located Coleman in another bedroom. The two engaged in a short conversation during which Coleman consented to Officer Kizer conducting a protective frisk. When the officers spoke with the children, they were informed that an altercation had occurred between Lisa and Coleman, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further stated that their father had aimed a firearm at their mother and had threatened to shoot her and set the house on fire. When asked, the children affirmed that they had observed the gun, both on that evening and previously, but did not know its current location.

B

After obtaining information subsequent to their initial entry into the residence, the officers applied for and were granted a warrant authorizing the search of the Coleman household. During this search, multiple firearms were discovered, which ultimately resulted in a federal indictment charging Coleman with being a felon in possession of a firearm, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms seized from his residence, asserting that his wife did not provide valid consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter the home initially. A hearing was conducted, after which a magistrate judge concluded in a report that Lisa had indeed consented to the officers’ entry and recommended that Coleman’s suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred with this assessment, adopting the magistrate judge’s factual finding regarding Lisa’s consent and separately determining that the officers did not exceed the boundaries of the consent given by Lisa while inside the home. After the district court denied the motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly reserving his appellate right to contest the district court’s suppression ruling. Coleman’s appeal of that issue is now before this Court.

II

A

The applicable legal doctrines are firmly established, and both parties concede that, under the Fourth Amendment, a home may be searched without a warrant where there is consent. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties also do not dispute that consent may be provided by someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has long held that, to be valid, consent must be granted “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our Circuit’s precedent adopts and applies the same test. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and following Bumper). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts evaluate the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Considerations that typically bear on the analysis include, but are not limited to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of detention preceding consent; (4) whether the consent was given promptly or only after repeated government requests; (5) whether physical force was employed; and (6) whether the person was in police custody at the time of consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is found to be voluntary, officers conducting a search must stay within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To determine scope, courts use an “objective reasonableness” approach, asking, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person giving consent? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate court, our function is constrained; we review district court determinations regarding the voluntariness of consent and challenges to scope only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we detect no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the police officers into her residence. Two elements of their interaction are particularly notable. First, throughout, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and manner indicative of concern rather than coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse consent, as evidenced by her denial of Officer Kizer’s initial request. She later opted to let Officer Kizer enter when he made a second request, which, notably, was made without any force or compulsion. These facts are apparent from the video recording. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman home, he addressed Lisa calmly and respectfully, and at all moments, Lisa responded in a way that showed she grasped the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not specifically inform her that she could refuse his entry, his act of requesting permission implicitly conveyed that option, further emphasized by Lisa’s original refusal. Lisa was never in custody during these interactions. While Officer Kizer did repeat his request for entry, the entirety of their conversation lasted only a few minutes. Although Lisa may have experienced some pressure in choosing to admit the officers, there was no evidence that Officer Kizer used coercion. Given this record, there was no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry. 

Coleman disagrees, pointing to Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” in their second significant interaction: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this is a reasonable argument, Coleman’s emphasis is unduly limited. The district court could justifiably conclude that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” did not render his request coercive when considering the overall context. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer asked for Lisa’s permission by conveying concern for the safety of her children, communicating that concern with urgency and seriousness, but the district court did not err in finding his approach stopped short of coercion. Lisa’s response strongly indicates she understood the officer’s statement as a request. She replied “Okay,” then directed Officer Kizer to where the children were located inside and assured him and the other officers that her dogs would jump on them but were not dangerous. All these circumstances support affirmance of the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to police entry. 

We also concur with the district court’s finding that, once inside, the officers stayed within the bounds of the consent given by Lisa. Their declared objective was to check on the welfare of Lisa’s children in the home, including ascertaining that Coleman posed no threat to them or anyone else. Fulfilling this aim necessarily meant surveying the house, finding all present individuals, and making some preliminary inquiries. Information gathered by police then led to their obtaining a warrant and seizing three firearms. We discern no flaw in the district court’s conclusion that the officers acted within the scope of Lisa’s original consent once inside the residence. Accordingly, we need not address the government’s alternate argument that the exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery doctrines justified the police’s entry and initial search of the Coleman home. 

Ultimately, we AFFIRM. In closing, we observe that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers displayed prudent and balanced judgment under sensitive and demanding conditions.
