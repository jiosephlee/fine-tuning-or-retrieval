Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-3051  
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling emergency calls related to domestic violence ranks among the most challenging tasks for law enforcement officers. This matter illustrates that difficulty. A little after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department arrived at a residence after receiving a report that the caller’s stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill the caller’s mother, Lisa Coleman, and was believed to still be inside the house. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and engaged Lisa, who hesitated when the officer inquired about the well-being of her children. In response, Officer Kizer commented, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband occupied another, and the household dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court concluded that this interaction gave Officer Kizer the requisite consent to enter the residence, and further determined that neither he nor the accompanying officers exceeded the boundaries of the consent granted by Lisa once inside. Consequently, the district court denied the motion to suppress the firearms discovered on the premises. We concur with that analysis and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for possessing a firearm unlawfully.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She directed them to a back bedroom, indicating where the children were located. Officer Kizer did not immediately see Coleman and inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied that she did not know. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in another bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman consented to Officer Kizer conducting a protective pat-down. During interactions with the two children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further disclosed that their father had pointed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When questioned, the children affirmed having seen the firearm both that evening and previously, but stated they did not currently know its location.

B

After entering the residence and gathering additional information, the officers applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman home. The execution of this warrant resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which subsequently led to a federal indictment charging Coleman with being a felon in possession of a firearm, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his residence, arguing that his wife never granted consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter initially. A magistrate judge, after conducting a hearing, issued a report concluding that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers' entry and recommending that Coleman’s suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred with the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa had consented and also rejected Coleman’s separate claim that the officers exceeded the boundaries of his wife's consent while inside the home. When the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea but preserved his right to contest the denial of suppression on appeal. That appeal is now before this Court.

II

A

The relevant legal standards in this area are firmly established, and both parties acknowledge that, under the Fourth Amendment, consent constitutes a recognized exception to the requirement that police obtain a warrant prior to searching a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties likewise do not dispute that consent may be provided by someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has held for decades that consent is only effective if it is given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our circuit has consistently reaffirmed this requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and applying the standard from Bumper). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts look to the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). The key considerations frequently encompass, though are not limited to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent has been voluntarily given, the resulting search is still restricted by “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). In assessing whether a search has exceeded its authorized scope, courts apply an “objective reasonableness” standard, considering what a typical reasonable person would have understood as the extent of consent based on “the exchange between the officer” and the individual consenting. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appellate review, our function is circumscribed; we examine a district court’s determinations regarding voluntary consent, as well as objections to its scope, for clear error only. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers to enter her residence. Two key aspects stand out from the interaction. First, throughout the exchange, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor and tone reflecting concern, not coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her ability to withhold consent by initially refusing Officer Kizer’s request to come inside. She subsequently chose to grant permission following a second, non-coercive request by Officer Kizer. These points are evident in the video evidence. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman home, he communicated with Lisa calmly and respectfully; at all times, Lisa responded in a manner that showed her awareness and understanding of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not specifically advise her that consent was unnecessary, his act of seeking permission and her initial refusal both implicitly conveyed that message. At no stage was Lisa detained. Although Officer Kizer twice requested consent to enter, their discussion spanned only a few minutes. While Lisa may have felt some pressure regarding the officers’ entry, there is no evidence Officer Kizer applied coercive tactics. Based on this record, we discern no clear error in the district court’s finding that Lisa gave valid consent for the police entry.

Coleman presents a contrary argument, directing attention to Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” in his second attempt to gain entry: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While Coleman’s argument is not unreasonable, the focus is overly narrow. Considering all circumstances, the district court could reasonably conclude that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” did not render the request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s request for entry was couched in concern for the welfare of Lisa’s children and, though his communication displayed urgency and seriousness, the district court did not plainly err in finding his conduct fell short of coercion. Lisa’s reply essentially confirms this interpretation; upon replying “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer where the children were located inside and reassured both him and the other officers about her dogs’ friendliness. Accordingly, we affirm the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry.

We similarly concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers’ actions upon entering remained within the boundaries of Lisa’s consent. Their declared purpose was to assess the wellbeing of the children, which included ascertaining whether Coleman posed any risk to them or anyone present. This necessarily involved examining the premises, locating individuals inside, and asking routine questions. The officers’ observations and gathered information ultimately led to a warrant to search the residence, resulting in the seizure of three firearms. We discern no error in the district court’s conclusion that the officers, once lawfully inside, honored the scope of Lisa’s original consent. In light of these findings, it is unnecessary to evaluate the government’s alternative arguments based on the exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery doctrines justifying the entry and initial sweep of the Coleman home.

Accordingly, we AFFIRM. In closing, we note that Officer Kizer and his colleagues exercised prudent and judicious decision-making amid a sensitive and challenging situation.
