Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers often face significant challenges when responding to 911 reports of domestic violence, as exemplified by this matter. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department responded to a report indicating that Jaison Coleman, the caller’s stepfather, had allegedly threatened to kill the caller’s mother, Lisa Coleman, and was reportedly still present within the residence. Officer Kizer knocked at the door and engaged Lisa in conversation; when asked about the well-being of her children, Lisa hesitated. In response, Officer Kizer remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded with “Okay,” and then provided information indicating that her children were in one bedroom, her husband in another, and assured Officer Kizer that the dogs in the home were friendly and posed no threat of biting. The district court determined that Lisa’s responses constituted valid consent for Officer Kizer to enter the premises, and further held that neither he nor his fellow officers exceeded the boundaries of that consent during their presence in the home. Consequently, the district court denied suppression of the firearms discovered on the property. We concur in this assessment and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful firearm possession.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, ushering them inside by moving her dogs out of their path. She directed the officers toward a rear bedroom, indicating where the children could be found. When Officer Kizer noticed Coleman was not present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. After briefly searching the residence, Officer Kizer located Coleman in another bedroom. The two had a short conversation, during which Coleman consented to a protective pat down by Officer Kizer. While speaking with the children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in an argument, during which Coleman acted in a loud, aggressive, and hostile manner. The children also recounted that their father had aimed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house ablaze. Upon further inquiry, the children stated that they had seen the gun on that night and previously, though they did not know its current location.

B

After entering the residence and obtaining additional information, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman household. The ensuing search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which eventually led to Coleman being federally indicted for firearm possession by a convicted felon, contravening 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion seeking suppression of the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that his wife, Lisa, had never given consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter the home initially. Following a hearing on this motion, a magistrate judge issued a report determining that Lisa had indeed given her consent for the officers’ entry and recommending that Coleman’s suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred, formally adopting the magistrate judge’s finding regarding Lisa’s consent and separately rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the residence went beyond the limits of his wife’s consent. After the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly preserving his right to appeal the court’s ruling on the motion to suppress. The matter is now presented to us on appeal.

II

A

The foundational legal standards at issue are clear, as both parties concur that under the Fourth Amendment, consent operates as an exception to the requirement for a search warrant when entering a home. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both sides also agree that the defendant is not the sole source from which consent may be obtained: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently required that valid consent must be “freely and voluntarily” provided. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This requirement is similarly reflected throughout this circuit’s jurisprudence. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (relying on Bumper). When evaluating whether consent is voluntary, courts examine the totality of circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations frequently encompass, but are not limited to, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). In addition, any search conducted under voluntary consent must not exceed “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (quoting United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To determine whether the scope was exceeded, the assessment is one of “objective reasonableness,” requiring courts to determine “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting individual? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our function is circumscribed; we assess findings on voluntariness of consent and issues concerning its scope under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After considering the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we do not find any clear error in the district court’s determination that she gave her voluntary consent for the officers to enter her residence. Two particular elements of the encounter are significant. First, Officer Kizer consistently maintained a demeanor and tone indicative of concern rather than coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated the ability to refuse entry—she did so when Officer Kizer initially asked. She ultimately allowed him inside after a second request, which likewise contained no evidence of coercion. These facts are apparent from the video evidence. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived, he maintained a calm, respectful approach to Lisa, who, throughout the interaction, gave responses showing her understanding of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not directly state that consent was unnecessary, the act of seeking permission, combined with Lisa’s original refusal, implied she knew her rights. Lisa was never in custody. Moreover, even though Officer Kizer requested entry twice, their conversation lasted only a few minutes. While Lisa might have felt some implicit pressure to allow entry, nothing in Officer Kizer’s conduct amounted to coercion. The district court’s conclusion that she consented was, therefore, not clearly erroneous.

Coleman takes a different position, focusing on Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the second request, quoting: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this argument has merit, it is too limited in scope. The district court could appropriately determine that the use of “need” was not inherently coercive when viewed in the broader context. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s request to enter reflected concern for the children’s safety, and his urgency did not, according to the district court’s assessment, cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s immediate reaction confirmed her understanding, as she replied, “Okay,” advised the officers of the children’s location, and warned them about her dogs, clarifying they were not a threat.

We therefore uphold the district court’s decision that Lisa consented to police entry. We also concur with the district court’s determination that the officers remained within the boundaries of Lisa’s consent once inside. Their professed reason for entering was to ensure the children’s well-being, which involved verifying that Coleman posed no threat and ascertaining the presence and safety of all persons within. This necessarily entailed observing the residence, identifying everyone inside, and posing rudimentary questions. The officers subsequently relied on this information to obtain a warrant and recover three firearms. The district court rightly found that the officers operated within the confines of Lisa’s consent upon entry.

Our findings obviate the need to consider the government’s alternative theories regarding exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery justifying the entry and subsequent sweep of the Coleman home. Thus, we AFFIRM. In closing, we note that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers exercised prudent and even-handed judgment in a situation demanding sensitivity and balance.
