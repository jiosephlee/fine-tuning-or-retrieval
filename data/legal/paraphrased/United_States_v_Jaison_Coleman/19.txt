Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers face significant challenges when responding to 911 calls involving allegations of domestic violence, and the circumstances in this case illustrate such complexities. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department was dispatched in response to a call indicating that Jaison Coleman, the caller’s stepfather, had threatened to kill the caller’s mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and conversed with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about her children’s safety. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” subsequently informing Officer Kizer that the children were in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and that the family dogs were harmless. The district court determined that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with sufficient consent to lawfully enter the residence, and further concluded that he and the other officers did not act beyond the limits of Lisa’s consent during their presence inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms found within the home. We concur with this determination and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful firearm possession.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door, allowing Officer Kizer and the other officers inside, and helped them enter by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated where the children were located. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unsure. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman agreed to a protective frisk by Officer Kizer. In their conversation with the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, and that Coleman had acted in a loud, aggressive, and hostile manner. The children further reported that their father aimed a firearm at their mother and threatened both to shoot her and to set the house ablaze. When questioned, the children affirmed they had observed the firearm, both that evening and on prior occasions, but stated they did not presently know its location.

B

After entering the residence, the officers obtained additional information that prompted them to apply for and receive a warrant to search the Coleman household. During the execution of this warrant, multiple firearms were discovered, which ultimately resulted in a federal indictment accusing Coleman of violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his home, arguing that Officer Kizer and his team lacked his wife’s consent to enter the premises in the first place. After holding a hearing, a magistrate judge submitted a report concluding that Lisa had indeed given consent for the officers to enter her home, and accordingly recommended the denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination regarding Lisa’s consent and further rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the house went beyond the scope of that consent. When the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman opted to enter a guilty plea but preserved his right to appeal the suppression issue. The matter is now before this court on appeal.

II

A

The foundational legal standards governing this matter are clear, with both parties acknowledging that consent represents an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s requirement of a warrant for home searches. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties also concur that the consent necessary for a search need not originate from the defendant; as stated in United States v. Cellitti, “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). According to longstanding Supreme Court precedent, valid consent to search must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This principle is reaffirmed in our precedent as well. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (relying upon Bumper). Determining whether consent was voluntarily provided requires evaluation of all surrounding circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant factors may include, but do not exhaustively comprise: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the length of time detained prior to giving consent; (4) whether the consent was promptly given or followed repeated requests by law enforcement; (5) whether there was any use of physical force; and (6) whether the individual was in custody when consent was given.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Furthermore, even where consent is deemed voluntary, the subsequent search must be confined to “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (referencing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To ascertain the scope of consent, the central question is that of “objective reasonableness”—specifically, what a typical reasonable person would have understood from the interaction between the law enforcement officer and the consenting individual. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our capacity as a reviewing court, our consideration is constrained. We evaluate a district court’s determinations concerning the voluntariness of consent, as well as any disputes regarding its scope, for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we conclude that the district court did not clearly err in determining that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers into her residence. Two features of their interaction are particularly notable. First, Officer Kizer consistently displayed a tone and manner indicative of concern, rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse consent, which she exercised after the officer’s initial request. Only after being asked a second time—again, without any indication of coercion—did she agree to the entry. These points are confirmed by the video record. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Colemans' home, his communication with Lisa remained calm and respectful; throughout the encounter, Lisa’s responses reflected her comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform her that she could decline his request to enter, his act of seeking permission, as well as her initial refusal, conveyed that understanding. Lisa was never detained during this time. While Officer Kizer made his request twice, the entirety of their conversation was only a few minutes long. Though Lisa may have experienced some sense of obligation to admit the officers, the record shows no coercive conduct from Officer Kizer. Thus, the district court’s finding of voluntary consent was not clearly erroneous.

Coleman contests this conclusion, pointing to Officer Kizer’s statement, “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay],” during the second request. While this is a plausible basis for argument, the focus is unduly narrow. The district court reasonably could have concluded that Kizer’s phrasing did not render the request coercive when considered in the context of the entire situation. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Although Officer Kizer based his request on his concern for the children’s welfare and communicated a sense of urgency, the record supports the district court’s finding that he did so without applying undue pressure. Lisa’s own response reinforces this conclusion: after saying “Okay,” she provided information about the children’s location and reassured the officers regarding her dogs. All of these factors support affirming the district court’s finding of consent.

Additionally, we concur with the district court that the officers’ actions inside the home remained within the scope of Lisa’s consent. The officers articulated that their purpose was to check on the children’s well-being and to ensure Coleman did not pose a danger, which necessarily required them to look through the home and speak with the occupants. The officers gathered information that enabled them to obtain a warrant and ultimately seize three firearms. We find no error in the determination that the officers remained within the limits of Lisa’s consent once inside. As a result of these findings, it is unnecessary to address the government’s alternative argument regarding exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery justifying the entry and protective sweep. Therefore, we AFFIRM. We also note, in closing, that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and measured judgment under sensitive and challenging conditions.
