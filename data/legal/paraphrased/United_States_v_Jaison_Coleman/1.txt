Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers often face significant challenges when responding to 911 calls involving domestic violence, and this case illustrates those difficulties. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a report from a caller who claimed her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the safety of her children. Officer Kizer then remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” subsequently informing Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in a separate bedroom, and their dogs were not aggressive. The district court determined that this conversation constituted valid consent for Officer Kizer to enter and further concluded that Officer Kizer and the other officers did not surpass the bounds of Lisa’s consent while inside the home. As a result, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered within the residence. We concur with the district court’s conclusions and affirm Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are derived from the body camera recordings made by Officer Kizer and other officers present at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon their arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and identified himself. Lisa came out onto the porch and informed Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She was initially reluctant to clarify whether her children were safe, responding only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer asked again, this time inquiring if her children would be all right. Lisa hesitated once more, stating she “hope[d] so.” Changing tactics, Officer Kizer next questioned her about her husband’s whereabouts inside the house. Lisa replied that she did not know, prompting Officer Kizer to request permission to enter. Lisa, after turning toward the door, hesitated before responding, “um … I’d prefer you not.” There was then a pause in the discussion, after which Lisa told Officer Kizer she needed to go inside to retrieve her shoes. When Lisa did not reappear for some time, Officer Kizer, concerned, partially opened the door and called into the residence several times to check on her well-being. After about a minute, Lisa returned to the doorway and stepped back outside, leading Officer Kizer to ask again about the situation inside and, more specifically, to request the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood to have threatened her. Lisa identified him as Jaison, whom we will refer to as Coleman from this point forward. The following key exchange then took place: When Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and if the children were safe, the ensuing conversation was as follows:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, aiding their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers toward a back bedroom, indicating the children's location. When Officer Kizer did not observe Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. A swift search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in a different bedroom. A short exchange ensued, during which Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective frisk. Upon speaking with the two children, officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been involved in an altercation, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father had aimed a firearm at their mother, threatening both to shoot her and to set the house ablaze. When questioned, the children confirmed they had seen the gun both on that night and previously, though they did not know its present location.

B

After entering the residence and gathering further information, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a search warrant for the Coleman residence. During the subsequent search, they discovered multiple firearms, which ultimately resulted in Coleman being federally charged with possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that his wife did not provide consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter the home initially. After a hearing was held, a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa did, in fact, consent to the officers' entry, and recommended denial of Coleman's suppression motion. The district court concurred with the magistrate judge’s determination regarding Lisa’s consent and also independently rejected Coleman's argument that the officers’ conduct inside the residence exceeded the scope of the consent given by his wife. Once the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea but expressly preserved his right to appeal the suppression issue. That appeal is now before this court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are clearly defined, and both parties agree that under the Fourth Amendment, a search of a home generally requires a warrant unless an exception, such as consent, applies. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties further concur that consent to search does not have to be granted by the defendant personally: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has held for decades that consent must be “freely and voluntarily” given to be valid. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This principle is echoed in our precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper). Courts determine whether consent was voluntary by considering all relevant circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Factors frequently considered include, but are not limited to, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is found to be voluntary, the ensuing search must stay within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The test for whether a search stayed within the proper bounds of consent is “objective reasonableness,” requiring the court to ask what “the typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the individual granting consent. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appellate review, our function is limited; we examine district court determinations of voluntariness of consent, as well as challenges to the search’s scope, for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entirety of the interaction between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two particular aspects of their exchange are especially significant. To begin with, throughout the encounter, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and attitude that reflected concern rather than coercion. Additionally, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse consent, as evidenced by her initial denial of Officer Kizer’s request to enter. She subsequently decided to grant Officer Kizer access following his second request, which was not accompanied by any undue pressure. These facts are plainly observable from the video evidence. From his arrival at the Coleman home, Officer Kizer addressed Lisa in a calm and respectful manner, and at every stage, Lisa responded in a manner that indicated she understood the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not directly state that she was not required to consent, his request for permission conveyed this implicitly, as did Lisa’s initial refusal. Lisa was never in custody at any time. While Officer Kizer made two requests for entry, their interaction lasted only a brief period. Lisa may have experienced some pressure in allowing the officers entry, but Officer Kizer did not apply coercion at any point. Under these circumstances, there is no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the entry.

Coleman disputes this, drawing attention to Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this argument is reasonable, Coleman’s focus is too limited. Given the totality of the circumstances, the district court could properly find that Officer Kizer’s reference to “need” did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought consent to enter out of expressed concern for Lisa’s children’s well-being, communicating urgency but not crossing into coercion, as the district court reasonably determined. Lisa’s subsequent actions—responding “Okay,” informing Officer Kizer of the children’s location, and assuring the officers that her dogs would not bite—further support the conclusion that she understood and voluntarily responded to the request. 

On this basis, we uphold the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry. We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers acted within the scope of Lisa’s consent after entering the home. Their articulated purpose was to ensure the safety of her children by confirming Coleman did not pose a threat. Fulfilling this objective required them to survey the home, account for all individuals, and make routine inquiries. The resulting information allowed them to obtain a search warrant and seize three firearms. We find no fault with the district court’s assessment that, once inside, the officers adhered to the scope of Lisa’s initial consent. Consequently, we need not address the government’s alternative arguments concerning exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery to justify the entry and preliminary sweep of the Coleman residence. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. Finally, we note that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and judicious decision-making under sensitive and challenging conditions.
