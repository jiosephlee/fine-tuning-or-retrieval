Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers face some of their most challenging duties when answering 911 calls related to domestic violence. The present case exemplifies that point. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police responded to a report from an individual stating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated to respond when questioned about the safety of her children. Officer Kizer then stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” after which she informed Officer Kizer that her children were in one room, her husband in a separate room, and their dogs would not bite. The district court concluded that Lisa’s statements and conduct gave Officer Kizer the consent required for entry into the home, and that neither he nor the other officers exceeded the boundaries of Lisa’s consent once inside. Consequently, the district court denied the suppression of the firearms eventually discovered in the home. We concur with that assessment and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and his team, further assisting them by moving her dogs aside to allow entry. She guided the officers toward a back bedroom and identified the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. A swift search of the residence led Officer Kizer to Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman consented to a protective pat-down by Officer Kizer. During interviews with the two children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had engaged in an altercation, with Coleman characterized as being loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further recounted that their father aimed a firearm at their mother and threatened both to shoot her and to set the house on fire. Upon further questioning, the children affirmed they had seen the weapon that evening and on previous occasions but did not know its current location.

B

After obtaining information during their initial entry into the residence, the officers applied for and received a warrant to search the Coleman household. This search yielded multiple firearms, which subsequently resulted in Coleman being federally indicted for being a felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman sought to suppress the evidence of the firearms recovered from his residence, arguing that his wife did not initially authorize Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter. After a hearing was conducted, a magistrate judge determined that Lisa had, in fact, granted the officers consent to enter the home and recommended that Coleman’s suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s conclusion regarding Lisa’s consent and independently finding no merit in Coleman’s argument that the officers had exceeded the bounds of his wife’s consent while inside. Once the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman opted to plead guilty, while maintaining his right to contest the suppression ruling on appeal. The present appeal now brings that issue before us.

II

A

The relevant legal standards in this area are well-settled, and both parties acknowledge that the Fourth Amendment’s requirement for a warrant to search a residence includes an exception based on consent. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties further concur that such consent is not required to come from the defendant. As established in United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004), “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party,” citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974). It has been longstanding Supreme Court precedent that valid consent must be given “freely and voluntarily,” as articulated in Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968), a principle reiterated in our own precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper’s standard). The determination of whether consent was voluntary is made by examining the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations may encompass, among others, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even if consent is voluntary, law enforcement must still limit their search to the “scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To evaluate the permissible scope, courts apply an “objective reasonableness” standard, focusing on what “the typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person consenting. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appellate review, our examination is narrow: we review a district court’s determination regarding voluntary consent, as well as challenges to the scope of consent, under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa gave voluntary consent for the officers to enter her home. Two features of the interaction are noteworthy. First, Officer Kizer consistently spoke and acted in a manner that reflected concern, rather than any attempt to pressure or coerce. Second, Lisa demonstrated she could refuse entry, as she did so when Officer Kizer first requested to come in. She subsequently decided to grant entry when Officer Kizer made a second request, which was also not accompanied by any coercion. These facts are plainly apparent from the video recording. Throughout his interaction with Lisa upon arrival at the Coleman residence, Officer Kizer maintained a calm and respectful demeanor, and Lisa’s responses at all times reflected her comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly state that Lisa could decline his request to enter, the very act of seeking her permission implied this option, and her initial refusal further reinforces this interpretation. At no time was Lisa under arrest or detained. Officer Kizer did repeat his request to enter, but the entirety of their discussion lasted only a few minutes. Even if Lisa may have experienced some implicit pressure in allowing the officers in, Officer Kizer did not apply any undue force or coercive tactics. Based on this record, the district court did not clearly err in concluding that Lisa’s consent was freely given.

Coleman disagrees and points to Officer Kizer’s choice of language in the second request, when he said, “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this is a reasonable ground for debate, Coleman’s argument is ultimately too focused on isolated language. Under the totality of the circumstances, the district court could properly conclude that Officer Kizer’s reference to “need” did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s request, motivated by concern for the safety of Lisa’s children and delivered with some sense of urgency, was nonetheless found by the district court to be within bounds and not to have overpowered Lisa’s consent. Lisa’s immediate reaction confirms her understanding: she responded, “Okay,” and then told Officer Kizer where to find the children, also warning the officers about her dogs’ behavior but assuring them the dogs would not bite. These circumstances support our conclusion to uphold the district court’s ruling that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry into her residence.

We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that, upon entering, the officers did not exceed the consent given by Lisa. Their entry was for the explicitly stated purpose of ensuring the welfare of her children and verifying that Coleman did not pose a danger to anyone present. Fulfilling this aim necessarily required the officers to look through the residence, identify everyone present, and ask basic questions. The information collected through this process was later used to obtain a warrant to search the house, which resulted in the recovery of three firearms. There is no error in the district court’s determination that the officers, upon gaining entry, remained within the limits of Lisa’s original consent. Given these findings, we have no reason to address the government’s alternate arguments invoking exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as bases for entry and initial search.

In conclusion, we AFFIRM. We also observe that Officer Kizer and the other officers exercised sound, judicious decision-making when navigating a sensitive and challenging situation.
