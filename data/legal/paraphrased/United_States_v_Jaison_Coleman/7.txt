Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers face significant challenges when responding to 911 calls concerning domestic violence, and this matter illustrates such difficulties. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer, a member of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department, responded to a report from a caller stating her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated when asked about the welfare of her children. Officer Kizer replied, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded affirmatively, saying, “Okay,” and explained to Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and that their dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court determined that this conversation provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter the home, and also found that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers did not exceed the parameters of Lisa’s consent once inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms subsequently located in the home. We concur with the district court and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are established by the footage from the body cameras worn by Officer Kizer and other officers at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon their arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and announced his presence. Lisa came out onto the porch and informed Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. When asked if her children were safe, Lisa initially hesitated and merely indicated that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer repeated his question, asking whether her children would be all right. Lisa remained uncertain, replying that she “hope[d] so.” Changing tactics, Officer Kizer inquired about her husband's location within the house. Lisa replied that she was unsure. At that point, Officer Kizer requested permission to enter the house. Lisa, having turned toward the door, paused and then responded, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation paused, and Lisa informed Officer Kizer that she needed to reenter the home to retrieve her shoes. After some time elapsed and Lisa did not reappear, Officer Kizer, concerned, slightly opened the door and called inside multiple times to check if Lisa was all right. About a minute later, Lisa returned to the door and stepped outside again, leading Officer Kizer to repeat his inquiry about the situation inside and, more specifically, the name of her husband, whom Officer Kizer understood to have threatened her. Lisa identified him as Jaison, whom we will hereafter refer to as Coleman. A significant interaction followed. Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and then inquired if the children were safe, resulting in the following exchange:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers to a rear bedroom, indicating the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not observe Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in another bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman agreed to allow Officer Kizer to perform a protective frisk. Upon interviewing the two children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with the children describing Coleman as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father had aimed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and to set the house on fire. In response to additional questions, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun both on that night and previously, but stated they did not know its present location.

B

After entering the residence and obtaining additional information, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a warrant to search the Coleman household. This search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which ultimately led to a federal indictment accusing Coleman of being a felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his residence, arguing that his wife did not give consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter the home initially. A magistrate judge held a hearing and subsequently issued a report concluding that Lisa did provide consent for the officers’ entry, and thus recommended denying Coleman’s motion. The district court concurred with the magistrate judge’s conclusion, finding that Lisa had consented to the officers entering the residence and also rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the home surpassed the bounds of his wife’s consent. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea while explicitly preserving his right to challenge the suppression decision on appeal. The present appeal concerns that issue.

II

A

The relevant legal standards are firmly established, and both parties acknowledge that, under the Fourth Amendment, consent serves as an exception to the general warrant requirement for searches of a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties likewise concur that such consent does not have to be provided by the defendant personally: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has long made clear that, in order to be effective, consent must be “freely and voluntarily” granted. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This requirement is reflected in our circuit precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (relying on Bumper’s standard). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts must evaluate all circumstances surrounding the consent. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Factors commonly considered in this analysis include, though are not limited to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of detention prior to giving consent; (4) whether consent was provided immediately or only after repeated official requests; (5) the presence or absence of physical coercion; and (6) whether the individual was in custody when providing consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is voluntary, any resulting search must remain within the “scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The appropriate standard for analyzing the scope of consent is “objective reasonableness”; courts inquire as to “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting party? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our review is constrained. We assess a district court’s determinations as to voluntariness and the breadth of consent for clear error only. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entirety of the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers into her residence. Two elements of their encounter are particularly notable. First, throughout their conversation, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and behavior indicative of genuine concern rather than force or intimidation. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse entry, as evidenced by her initial denial of Officer Kizer’s request. She subsequently permitted his entry following a second request, which was not accompanied by any form of coercion. The video evidence supports these findings. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman household, he addressed Lisa in a composed and respectful manner, and at all times Lisa’s reactions reflected comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly state that Lisa could refuse his entry, his request for permission, together with her original denial, implied as much. Lisa was never detained during the interaction. While Officer Kizer requested entry twice, the entirety of their interaction spanned only a few minutes. Although Lisa may have experienced some implicit pressure to consent, at no point did Officer Kizer engage in coercive conduct. The district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented is thus free from clear error.

Coleman contests this, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the crucial second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this is a reasonable argument, it focuses too narrowly on isolated language. The district court could reasonably interpret Officer Kizer’s use of “need” as non-coercive, when viewed within the broader context of their interaction. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s request to enter was grounded in concern for the safety of Lisa’s children, which he conveyed with urgency, but without overstepping the boundaries into coercion, as found by the district court. Lisa’s reaction further corroborates this understanding: after saying “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer about the location of her children inside and reassured the officers that her dogs, though likely to jump, would not bite. These circumstances support affirming the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to police entry.

We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that, once inside, the officers acted within the limits of the consent Lisa had provided. The officers entered to ensure the welfare of the children, including by confirming that Coleman did not pose a threat to anyone present. Fulfilling this purpose required the officers to look throughout the house, locate the individuals present, and ask preliminary questions. The information gathered enabled them to obtain a warrant, resulting in the recovery of three firearms. We find no flaw in the district court’s assessment that the officers did not exceed the scope of Lisa’s initial consent once inside the Coleman residence.

These determinations obviate the need to address the government’s alternative argument invoking exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery as bases for entry and the initial search of the home. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. We close with the observation that Officer Kizer and his colleagues demonstrated prudent and measured judgment in a sensitive and challenging situation.
