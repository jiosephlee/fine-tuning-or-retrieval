Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers often face significant challenges when responding to 911 reports of domestic violence, as demonstrated by the circumstances of this matter. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer, employed by the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department, acted on a report made by a caller who claimed her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and that he remained present in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked at the door and engaged Lisa in conversation. When asked if her children were safe, Lisa hesitated, prompting Officer Kizer to respond, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and informed Officer Kizer that her children were located in one bedroom, her husband was in a separate bedroom, and that her dogs were friendly and not prone to biting. The district court determined that this interaction constituted Lisa’s valid consent for Officer Kizer’s entry into the home, and further concluded that neither Kizer nor the accompanying officers exceeded the boundaries of that consent once inside. On this basis, the court denied the motion to suppress the firearms discovered within the residence. We concur with the district court’s reasoning and therefore affirm Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are drawn from the body camera video captured by Officer Kizer and other officers during their time at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ...” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced his presence. Lisa responded by coming out onto the porch and informing Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. When initially asked whether her children were safe, Lisa hesitated, stating only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer repeated the inquiry, this time asking if her children would be alright, to which Lisa replied she “hope[d] so.” Shifting tactics, Officer Kizer next inquired about her husband’s whereabouts in the residence. Lisa answered that she was unsure of his location, prompting Officer Kizer to ask for permission to enter. Lisa, turning back toward the door, hesitated before stating, “um … I’d prefer you not.” After a pause in the conversation, Lisa informed Officer Kizer that she needed to re-enter the home to put on her shoes. When some time passed without her return, Officer Kizer, growing concerned, slightly opened the door and repeatedly called into the home to check if she was alright. Approximately a minute later, Lisa returned to the entrance and stepped outside again, at which point Officer Kizer resumed his questions about the situation inside, including specifically asking for the name of her husband, who Officer Kizer believed had made threats toward her. Lisa identified her husband as Jaison, hereafter referred to as Coleman. What followed was an important exchange: when Officer Kizer inquired if Coleman was “more calm right now” and whether the children were safe, the following conversation ensued:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, aiding their entry by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom, indicating the location of the children. When Officer Kizer could not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, and Lisa replied that she did not know. Officer Kizer then swiftly searched the house and located Coleman in a different bedroom. A short exchange ensued, during which Coleman agreed to a protective pat-down by Officer Kizer. While speaking with the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had argued, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further recounted that their father aimed a gun at their mother and threatened both to shoot her and to set the house on fire. When questioned, the children affirmed having seen the firearm, both that night and previously, though they were unaware of its current location.

B

After entering the residence, the officers obtained further information that enabled them to apply for and receive a warrant authorizing the search of the Coleman household. This search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which ultimately led to federal charges against Coleman for being a felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that his wife did not consent to the initial entry by Officer Kizer and his associates. After conducting a hearing, a magistrate judge determined that Lisa had, in fact, given consent for the officers to enter her residence, and recommended denying Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s factual finding regarding Lisa’s consent and also specifically rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers' conduct within the home went beyond the scope of any consent provided by his wife. When the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman elected to enter a guilty plea but reserved the right to appeal the suppression decision. That appeal is currently before this Court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are well-settled, and both parties accept that consent operates as an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s warrant requirement for searches of homes. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties likewise concede that the consent may be given by someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). According to the Supreme Court, valid consent must be granted “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our precedent similarly adopts this requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing the rule from Bumper). In evaluating voluntariness, courts consider all surrounding circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant factors may involve, but are not confined to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of his detention prior to consent; (4) if his consent was provided immediately or only after repeated requests by authorities; (5) whether there was any use of physical force; and (6) whether he was in police custody when granting consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). A finding of voluntary consent does not permit police to exceed “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Whether the search stayed within the boundaries of consent depends on “objective reasonableness,” specifically, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenter? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). Our function as an appellate court is restricted; we review findings regarding voluntary consent and objections to its scope for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

Upon reviewing the entirety of the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that she gave consent for the officers to enter her residence. Two elements of their interaction are particularly notable. First, throughout the encounter, Officer Kizer spoke and behaved in a manner suggesting genuine concern rather than any effort to intimidate or compel. Second, Lisa demonstrated that she was capable of refusing consent, as evidenced by her initial denial of Officer Kizer’s first request to come inside. It was only after a subsequent, non-coercive request that she permitted Officer Kizer to enter. These points are substantiated by the video record. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman household, his communication with Lisa was characterized by calmness and respect, and Lisa’s responses at all times indicated that she understood the ongoing situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform Lisa that consent was not obligatory, the very act of seeking her permission, coupled with her initial refusal, suggested as much. At no point during their interaction was Lisa detained. While Officer Kizer repeated his request for entry, the entirety of their discussion was brief, lasting only several minutes. Lisa may have experienced some implicit pressure associated with the police request, but Officer Kizer did not apply any undue influence or compulsion. On the basis of these facts, the district court did not clearly err in concluding that Lisa’s consent to the entry was voluntary. 

Coleman contests this conclusion, focusing on Officer Kizer’s language during the second request—specifically, his use of the word “need”: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this is a plausible argument, Coleman’s focus is overly restrictive. Given the totality of the circumstances, it was reasonable for the district court to find that Officer Kizer’s employment of the word “need” did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer articulated his request for entry by referencing his concern for the children’s safety, and although he conveyed urgency, the district court reasonably determined that his approach stopped short of coercion. Lisa’s response strongly indicates that she perceived the officer’s request accordingly. She replied “Okay” and proceeded to inform the officers where the children were, while reassuring them that her dogs might jump but would not bite. 

These facts support our decision to uphold the district court’s finding that Lisa gave her consent for the officers to enter the home. We further agree with the district court that, once inside, the officers did not exceed the scope of the consent granted by Lisa. The officers’ expressly stated aim was to check on the well-being of the children within the home, which included ensuring that Coleman posed no threat to anyone. Fulfilling this objective required them to look throughout the house, determine everyone’s whereabouts, and pose relevant questions. The information gathered led to the procurement of a search warrant and the recovery of three firearms. We discern no error in the district court’s finding that the officers adhered to the parameters of the consent provided. Consequently, we need not address the government’s alternative arguments concerning exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as justification for the initial entry and protective sweep. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. We close by observing that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers exercised judicious and prudent judgment in a challenging situation.
