Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 domestic violence incidents represents a particularly challenging aspect of law enforcement duties. This matter serves as an illustrative instance. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department, responded to a report from a woman stating her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained present in the residence. After Officer Kizer knocked, he conversed with Lisa, who hesitated to confirm whether her children were alright. Officer Kizer responded, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” In reply, Lisa stated, “Okay,” and further explained that the children were together in one bedroom, her husband was in a separate room, and the family dogs were docile and would not bite. The district court determined that Lisa’s words granted Officer Kizer sufficient consent to enter, and that his actions, along with those of his colleagues, stayed within the bounds of that consent during their time inside. Consequently, the court declined to suppress the firearms discovered on the premises. We concur with this assessment and affirm Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, assisting their entry by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and identified the location of the children. When Officer Kizer inquired about Coleman's whereabouts, Lisa indicated she did not know. Officer Kizer, after a quick search of the residence, located Coleman in another bedroom. A short conversation ensued, during which Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective pat down. While speaking with the two children, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, and that Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further stated that their father aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. Upon questioning, both children said they had seen the gun that night and previously, though they were unaware of its current location.

B

After obtaining information subsequent to their entry into the residence, the officers applied for and were granted a warrant to search the Coleman household. This search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which ultimately led to a federal indictment accusing Coleman of violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his residence, arguing that his wife did not initially consent to Officer Kizer and the other officers entering their home. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge determined that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers’ entry and recommended denial of Coleman’s motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s conclusion regarding Lisa’s consent and, in addition, dismissed Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the house went beyond what his wife had agreed to. With the district court denying his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea, preserving his right to contest the suppression decision on appeal. That challenge is now before this Court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are settled, and both parties acknowledge that, under the Fourth Amendment, a warrantless home search may be justified when there is consent. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties further concede that consent need not originate from the accused; as recognized in United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)), “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” The Supreme Court has consistently held that, in order for consent to be lawful, it must be granted “freely and voluntarily.” See Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our precedent applies the same requirement. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (relying on Bumper’s standard). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts must look to all relevant circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations often encompass: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is voluntary, the subsequent search cannot exceed “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Courts gauge the extent of consent according to an “objective reasonableness” test, asking what a reasonable person would understand from the conversation between the officer and the consenting party. See Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our capacity as a reviewing court, our examination is circumscribed; we assess the district court’s determinations concerning voluntariness of consent, as well as any related challenge to its scope, only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After thoroughly reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we discern no plain error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers into her residence. Two primary factors in the exchange merit particular notice. First, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and behavior consistent with that of someone expressing concern, rather than employing force or intimidation. Second, Lisa demonstrated she was capable of declining entry, as evidenced by her initial refusal to Officer Kizer’s first request. Ultimately, she opted to allow Officer Kizer inside following a second, non-coercive request. These facts are evident in the video evidence. From the outset of Officer Kizer’s presence at the Coleman house, his communication with Lisa was composed and courteous, and Lisa’s reactions consistently exhibited an understanding of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly state that Lisa was under no obligation to allow entry, his verbal request for permission and her initial denial conveyed that implication. Lisa was not at any moment detained. Moreover, even though Officer Kizer sought consent on two occasions, their discussion lasted only a short time. Lisa may have experienced some pressure about admitting the police; nevertheless, Officer Kizer did not apply undue influence or coercion at any point. Based on this record, there was no clear mistake by the district court in concluding that Lisa’s consent was voluntary.

Coleman offers a contrary perspective, centering on Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” in the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this is a plausible argument, it does not account for the broader context. The district court was justified in determining that the use of “need” did not transform Officer Kizer’s request into a coercive demand, when evaluated in light of all the circumstances. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought permission to enter by voicing concern for the wellbeing of Lisa’s children, and while his communication conveyed some urgency, the district court’s finding that this did not become coercive is not clearly erroneous. Lisa’s subsequent statements effectively confirm her perception of the request: after consenting with “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer about her children’s location and reassured the officers about her dogs. All of these facts support the district court’s finding that Lisa gave her consent for the officers to enter the house.

We further agree with the district court’s ruling that the police remained within the confines of Lisa’s consent once inside the home. The officers’ expressly stated objective was to assess the condition of Lisa’s children by verifying both their safety and that Coleman did not pose any harm. Achieving this goal necessarily required the officers to observe the premises, locate all present, and make pertinent inquiries. The resulting information provided the basis for a search warrant, which in turn led to the seizure of three firearms. There is no deficiency in the district court’s conclusion that the officers acted in accordance with Lisa’s original consent upon entry.

Accordingly, it is unnecessary to address the government’s alternative theories of exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as further justification for the officers’ entry and the initial sweep of the Coleman residence.

Therefore, we AFFIRM. In closing, we note more broadly that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and even-handed judgment in a sensitive and challenging situation.
