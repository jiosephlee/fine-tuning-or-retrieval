Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-3051  
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers often face significant challenges when called to intervene in domestic violence situations. This matter demonstrates such difficulties. Around 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer from the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department arrived in response to a report that Jaison Coleman, the caller’s stepfather, had threatened to kill Lisa Coleman, his wife, and remained inside the residence. After knocking, Officer Kizer conversed with Lisa, who hesitated when asked about the safety of her children. Officer Kizer then remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” In response, Lisa stated, “Okay,” and then informed Officer Kizer of the locations of her children and husband in separate bedrooms, adding that their dogs were not aggressive. According to the district court, this dialogue granted Officer Kizer consent to enter, and the officers did not surpass the boundaries of Lisa’s consent while inside the home. As a result, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered during their presence in the house. We concur with this determination and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa subsequently unlocked the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers toward a rear bedroom, indicating the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. After a brief sweep of the residence, Officer Kizer located Coleman in a different bedroom. Following a short exchange, Coleman agreed to a protective pat-down by Officer Kizer. Upon interviewing the two children, the officers were told that Lisa and Coleman had argued, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further stated that their father aimed a firearm at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and also threatened to set the house on fire. When asked, the children affirmed that they had seen the gun that evening as well as previously, but could not say where it currently was.

B

After obtaining information as a result of their initial entry into the residence, the officers applied for and received a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman home. During this search, they discovered multiple firearms, which subsequently led to Coleman’s indictment in federal court for possessing a firearm as a previously convicted felon, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms evidence, arguing that his wife had not given consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter their home in the first place. At a hearing on the motion, a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had indeed consented to the officers’ entry and recommended that Coleman’s suppression motion be denied. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination regarding Lisa’s consent and also dismissing Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the residence went beyond the bounds of his wife’s consent. When the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly reserving the right to appeal the court’s suppression decision. That appeal is presently before this Court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are firmly settled. Both parties acknowledge that, under the Fourth Amendment, consent is a recognized exception to the warrant requirement for the search of a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Additionally, the parties agree that consent need not come exclusively from the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (referencing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). For consent to constitute a valid exception, Supreme Court precedent holds that it must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This standard is consistently reflected in our case law. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper). The question of whether consent was voluntary is analyzed by courts through an examination of all the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Among the relevant considerations are, without limitation: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is voluntary, a search remains bound by “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Courts evaluating the scope of consent apply a standard of “objective reasonableness,” asking what a typical reasonable person would have understood from the interaction between the officer and the consenting individual. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our capacity as a reviewing court, we are tasked with a more restrained role; the district court’s determination on voluntariness of consent and any issues regarding its scope are reviewed for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entirety of the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we discern no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers to enter her residence. Two features of their interaction are particularly noteworthy. First, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor throughout that was concerned rather than coercive. Second, Lisa evidenced her capacity to refuse consent; she did so in response to Officer Kizer’s initial request to enter. Upon the officer’s second, non-coercive request, Lisa opted to allow him entry. The video footage makes these points evident. From the outset of his arrival at the Coleman home, Officer Kizer addressed Lisa with composure and respect, and throughout, Lisa’s responses indicated that she comprehended what was occurring. Although Officer Kizer did not affirmatively state that her consent was not required, the fact that he sought permission and that she first denied it strongly implied such an understanding. At no stage was Lisa under arrest. Officer Kizer did repeat his request, but their discussion lasted only several minutes. While Lisa might have experienced some pressure to admit the officers, Officer Kizer’s actions never rose to the level of coercion. Based on this record, the district court did not clearly err in its finding of Lisa’s consent.

Coleman disagrees, concentrating on Officer Kizer’s language during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” This argument is not without merit, but it is unduly limited. The district court was within reason to conclude that the term “need” did not convert the request into a coercive demand when the circumstances are considered as a whole. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer articulated his concern for the children’s safety—conveying both urgency and seriousness—but, as the district court reasonably found, he did so without improperly pressuring Lisa’s consent. Lisa’s reaction—saying “Okay,” identifying where the children were, and warning the officers about her dogs—supports the conclusion that she understood the request as it was intended.

Accordingly, we uphold the district court’s finding that Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers’ entry. We similarly concur with the district court’s assessment that the officers’ actions inside remained within the parameters of Lisa’s consent. Their explicit objective was to ascertain the children’s well-being, which entailed ensuring Coleman was not a threat and confirming the location and safety of all occupants. This investigation necessarily required the officers to look through the home, find each individual, and ask pertinent questions. The resulting information enabled them to obtain a search warrant, leading to the discovery of three firearms. We detect no flaw in the district court’s conclusion that the officers respected the scope of consent provided by Lisa once inside the residence.

In light of these determinations, we need not address the government’s alternative theories of exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery justifying the officers’ entry and cursory search. We therefore AFFIRM. In doing so, we also note that Officer Kizer and the other officers demonstrated prudent and judicious decision making under challenging conditions.
