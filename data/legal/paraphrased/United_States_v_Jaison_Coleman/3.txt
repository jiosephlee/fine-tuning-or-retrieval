Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Responding to 911 reports involving domestic violence represents one of the most challenging duties for law enforcement personnel. The circumstances of this case illustrate that difficulty. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer, serving with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department, was dispatched after receiving information from a caller that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had made threats to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated when asked about the well-being of her children. Officer Kizer responded, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and that her dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court determined that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with the requisite consent to enter the premises and further found that neither he nor his fellow officers exceeded the boundaries of Lisa’s consent while inside. Accordingly, the district court denied suppression of the firearms discovered within the residence. We concur with this reasoning and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are derived from body camera footage captured by Officer Kizer and other officers at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). When Officer Kizer arrived, he knocked on the door and announced his presence. Lisa came out onto the porch and informed Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside the house. When asked if her children were safe, she hesitated and initially responded only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer inquired again, this time whether her children were going to be all right, to which Lisa ambiguously replied that she “hope[d] so.” Shifting tactics, Officer Kizer then questioned her regarding the whereabouts of her husband inside the residence. Lisa answered that she was unsure of his location, leading Officer Kizer to request permission to enter. After turning back toward the door, Lisa hesitated before stating, “um … I’d prefer you not.” There was a pause in the conversation, after which Lisa informed Officer Kizer that she needed to re-enter the house to get her shoes. After a period passed without Lisa reemerging, Officer Kizer, concerned, slightly opened the door and repeatedly called into the home to check if she was all right. Roughly a minute later, Lisa reappeared in the doorway and stepped outside once again, at which point Officer Kizer resumed his inquiry about the situation inside, specifically asking for the name of her husband, whom Officer Kizer believed had threatened her. Lisa identified him as Jaison, whom we will refer to as Coleman for the remainder of this opinion. A crucial conversation then took place. When Officer Kizer questioned whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and subsequently if the children were safe, the following exchange ensued:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to admit Officer Kizer and his fellow officers by opening the door and moving her dogs aside to allow their entry. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and identified the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not observe Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman agreed to a protective pat down conducted by Officer Kizer. During their conversation with the two children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been involved in an altercation, during which Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further recounted that their father had aimed a firearm at their mother and had threatened both to shoot her and to set the house on fire. In response to officers’ inquiries, the children affirmed that they had seen the firearm, both that night and previously, but were unsure of its current location.

B

After entering the house and obtaining additional information, the officers applied for and received a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman residence. During the search, several firearms were discovered, which ultimately resulted in Coleman being federally indicted for violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms recovered from his residence, arguing that his wife did not consent to the initial entry by Officer Kizer and the other officers. A magistrate judge, after conducting a hearing, concluded in a report that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers’ entry and recommended that Coleman’s motion be denied. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa gave consent for the officers to enter, and additionally rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct within the home exceeded the boundaries of his wife’s consent. After the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman opted to enter a guilty plea, expressly preserving his right to contest the suppression decision on appeal. The appeal is now before this court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards in this area are firmly established, and both parties acknowledge that, under the Fourth Amendment, a warrantless search of a residence is permissible when conducted pursuant to consent. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties further agree that such consent may be provided by someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that valid consent requires that it be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our own precedent reinforces this standard. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and following Bumper). Whether consent was voluntary is determined by considering all relevant circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations frequently include, but are not confined to, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was informed of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of any detention prior to giving consent; (4) whether consent was given promptly or only after repeated official requests; (5) whether physical force was used; and (6) whether the individual was in custody when providing consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is voluntarily provided, the search conducted must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To assess the boundaries of that scope, the test is one of “objective reasonableness,” meaning courts consider “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting individual. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). In our capacity as an appellate court, our review is constrained; we evaluate findings regarding the voluntariness of consent and disputes over its scope under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry into her residence. Two particular elements of the encounter are notable. First, throughout the interaction, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and manner that reflected concern rather than coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her ability to withhold consent when she declined Officer Kizer’s initial request for entry. She later decided to allow Officer Kizer inside after he asked a second time, and there was no evidence of coercion accompanying this subsequent request. These points are evident from the video record. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman household, he interacted with Lisa calmly and with respect, and Lisa’s responses throughout indicated her understanding of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform Lisa that her consent was not required, his act of seeking permission implied as much, as did Lisa’s original refusal. Lisa was not detained at any moment. Although Officer Kizer sought consent on two occasions, the full exchange lasted only several minutes. While Lisa may have experienced some pressure in agreeing to the officers’ entry, Officer Kizer did not engage in any coercive conduct. On these facts, there is no clear error in the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry.

Coleman disputes this, focusing on Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the crucial second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this is a reasonable point, Coleman’s argument is too limited in scope. The district court was justified in concluding that, when considering the circumstances as a whole, Officer Kizer’s reference to a “need” did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s request to enter was premised on concern for the welfare of Lisa’s children and was conveyed with some urgency, but the district court’s finding that he did not cross into coercion is not clearly erroneous. Lisa’s subsequent actions further support the view that she perceived Officer Kizer’s request as non-coercive: upon saying “Okay,” she directed Officer Kizer to where the children were inside and advised him and the other officers about her dogs’ behavior, assuring them the dogs might jump but would not bite.

All these facts support the district court’s conclusion that Lisa gave voluntary consent for the officers to enter her home. We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers remained within the boundaries of Lisa’s consent upon entry. Their declared reason for entering was to ensure the well-being of her children and to verify that Coleman did not pose a threat to anyone. To accomplish this, it was necessary for the officers to look through the home, identify those present, and ask some fundamental questions. The information obtained in this process resulted in their application for a search warrant, which led to the discovery of three firearms. We discern no error in the district court’s determination that the officers, upon entering the Coleman home, acted consistently with the consent Lisa had given. These findings make it unnecessary to address the government’s alternative arguments that the doctrines of exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery justified the officers’ initial entry and search. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. In closing, we note more generally that Officer Kizer and the other officers demonstrated sound and measured judgment under challenging and sensitive conditions.
