Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls involving domestic violence ranks among the most challenging tasks for law enforcement officers, as this matter illustrates. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police responded to a report from a woman stating her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained present in the residence. Officer Kizer knocked on the door and made contact with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the well-being of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and proceeded to inform Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband occupied another, and her dogs were non-aggressive and would not bite. The district court determined that this conversation provided Officer Kizer with the consent required to enter the premises and further concluded that he and the other officers did not act outside the limits of Lisa’s consent once inside. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms later located in the residence. We concur with this reasoning and affirm Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are derived from footage captured by the body cameras of Officer Kizer and other officers present at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon their arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door, identifying himself. Lisa came out onto the porch, informing Officer Kizer that her son and daughter remained inside the house. When asked whether her children were safe, Lisa initially hesitated and stated only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer repeated his inquiry about the children’s safety, to which Lisa replied that she “hope[d] so.” Shifting tactics, Officer Kizer then inquired about the whereabouts of her husband within the house. Lisa answered that she did not know, after which Officer Kizer requested permission to enter. Lisa, turning back toward the door, hesitated and ultimately replied, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation paused, and Lisa indicated to Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After Lisa did not reappear for some time, Officer Kizer, concerned, opened the door slightly and called into the house several times to check on her well-being. Approximately a minute later, Lisa returned to the doorway and stepped back outside, prompting Officer Kizer to again inquire about the situation inside and, with greater specificity, to ask for her husband’s name—believing him to have threatened Lisa. She responded that his name was Jaison, whom we will refer to as Coleman hereafter. At this point, a significant exchange occurred. Officer Kizer questioned whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and, subsequently, whether the children were alright, leading to the following dialogue:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to admit Officer Kizer and his fellow officers into the residence, moving her dogs aside to allow their entrance. She guided the officers toward a rear bedroom and indicated the children's location. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she was unaware. Officer Kizer then conducted a brief search of the house and discovered Coleman in another bedroom. A short exchange ensued, during which Coleman consented to Officer Kizer performing a protective pat down. While speaking with the children, the officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, and that Coleman had been, according to the children, loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father had aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and to set the house on fire. When asked, the children affirmed they had seen the firearm both that evening and previously, though they could not say where it was at that time.

B

After entering the residence and obtaining additional information, the officers applied for and received a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman home. During the subsequent search, they discovered multiple firearms. This ultimately resulted in Coleman being indicted in federal court for possession of a firearm by a convicted felon, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that Officer Kizer and the other officers had initially entered without valid consent from his wife. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa had indeed consented to the officers’ entry, and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s conclusion regarding Lisa’s consent and separately finding that the officers did not act beyond the limits of her consent once inside. After the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea but expressly reserved his right to contest the suppression ruling on appeal. The appeal of that decision is now before this court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards in this area are settled, and both parties acknowledge that consent constitutes an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s requirement for a warrant to conduct a search of a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties further concede that consent does not have to be provided by the defendant; as stated, “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has long held that consent is only valid if given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This same principle is reiterated in our precedent. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing and implementing Bumper’s standard). In evaluating voluntariness, courts must examine the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations may encompass, though are not restricted to, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was informed of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of detention prior to giving consent; (4) whether consent was given immediately or after repeated police requests; (5) whether physical coercion was employed; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when consenting.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even if consent is voluntary, any ensuing search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The determination of scope is governed by “objective reasonableness,” requiring the court to ask, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the individual providing consent? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate court, our function is restricted; we review findings of voluntary consent and any questions regarding its scope for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that she voluntarily allowed the officers into her home. Two features of their interaction are particularly significant. First, Officer Kizer’s behavior and manner were those of someone genuinely concerned rather than attempting to pressure Lisa. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to withhold consent, as she initially refused Officer Kizer’s first request for entry. She then decided to permit entry following his second, non-coercive request. This is evident from the video evidence. From his arrival at the Coleman residence, Officer Kizer communicated in a calm and respectful manner, and Lisa consistently responded in a way that showed she was aware of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform Lisa that she could decline consent, the very fact that he asked permission—and her initial refusal—effectively conveyed this. At all times, Lisa was not in custody. While Officer Kizer made two requests to enter, their entire interaction lasted only a few minutes. Although Lisa might have felt some pressure to admit the officers, at no time did Officer Kizer act in a coercive manner. Based on this record, there is no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa gave valid consent for the officers to enter. Coleman challenges this finding, focusing on Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although Coleman’s argument is reasonable, it is overly narrow. The district court could reasonably conclude, considering all the circumstances, that Officer Kizer’s choice of the word “need” did not render the request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought permission out of apparent concern for the children’s safety, and while his request was delivered with some urgency, the district court did not clearly err in finding he remained non-coercive. Lisa’s response further suggests she understood the request as such. After saying “Okay,” she directed Officer Kizer to the children’s location inside the home and assured the officers about her dogs, noting they might jump but would not bite. Accordingly, we uphold the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry. We also agree with the district court that the officers remained within the scope of Lisa’s consent once inside. Their declared purpose was to ensure the well-being of Lisa’s children and to confirm that Coleman did not pose a threat. Fulfilling this purpose required them to visually check the premises, identify the occupants, and ask essential questions. The information gathered during this process led to obtaining a warrant to search the residence and recovering three firearms. There is no defect in the district court’s conclusion that the officers acted in accordance with the consent given by Lisa. These determinations make it unnecessary to address the government’s alternative argument that exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery justified the entry and sweep of the residence. Therefore, we AFFIRM. We add this final point: Officer Kizer and his colleagues demonstrated prudent and measured judgment in handling a sensitive and challenging situation.
