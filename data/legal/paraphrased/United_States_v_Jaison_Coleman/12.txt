Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Responding to 911 reports of domestic violence represents one of the more challenging tasks undertaken by law enforcement officers. The present matter illustrates this point well. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department answered a call from an individual who stated her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and that he remained within the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and engaged Lisa at the door. When questioned about the wellbeing of her children, Lisa hesitated. Officer Kizer then remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded, “Okay,” and informed Officer Kizer that her children were together in one bedroom, her husband was in a separate room, and that her dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court concluded that this interaction constituted valid consent for Officer Kizer to enter and that neither he nor accompanying officers exceeded the parameters of Lisa’s consent once inside. As a result, the court declined to suppress the firearms discovered on the premises. We concur with this determination and therefore uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant facts are drawn from body camera videos recorded by Officer Kizer and other officers present at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon his arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and identified himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and informing Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside the house. When asked whether her children were safe, Lisa initially hesitated, responding only that the children were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer repeated his question, asking if the children were going to be alright, to which Lisa again showed uncertainty and replied that she “hope[d] so.” Shifting his inquiry, Officer Kizer then asked about the whereabouts of her husband within the residence. Lisa replied she did not know, after which Officer Kizer requested permission to enter. As Lisa, having turned back toward the door, paused, she finally responded, “um … I’d prefer you not.” After a pause in the conversation, Lisa told Officer Kizer that she needed to enter the house to retrieve her shoes. When some time passed without Lisa reappearing, Officer Kizer, out of concern, slightly opened the door and called into the house multiple times to check if Lisa was alright. After about a minute, Lisa came back to the doorway and again exited onto the porch, leading Officer Kizer to again ask about the situation inside and, more particularly, to inquire about her husband’s name—having learned Officer Kizer believed he had threatened Lisa. Lisa answered that his name was Jaison, whom we will refer to as Coleman throughout. A significant exchange then occurred. When Officer Kizer inquired whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and, subsequently, if the children were safe, the following discussion took place:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to admit Officer Kizer and the other officers by opening the door and moving her dogs aside to allow their entry. She guided them to a rear bedroom, indicating where the children could be found. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she did not know. A brief walkthrough of the residence enabled Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in another bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman consented to a protective pat-down. During their conversation with the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had engaged in an argument, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children also stated that their father had aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When asked further, the children affirmed that they had seen the firearm both on the night in question and previously, although they were unaware of its current location.

B

After entering the house, the officers obtained additional information that enabled them to apply for and receive a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman residence. This search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which ultimately led to a federal indictment charging Coleman with being a felon in possession of a firearm, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his home, arguing that his wife did not initially consent to Officer Kizer and the other officers entering the premises. A magistrate judge conducted a hearing and then issued a report concluding that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers’ entry and therefore recommending denial of Coleman’s motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s factual finding regarding Lisa’s consent and also rejecting Coleman’s separate argument that the officers exceeded the limitations of his wife’s consent once inside the residence. After his motion to suppress was denied by the district court, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly maintaining his right to appeal the suppression decision. That appeal is now presented for our review.

II

A

The controlling legal standards in this area are clear, and both parties acknowledge that under the Fourth Amendment, the requirement of a warrant for a home search is subject to an exception for valid consent. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties are further in agreement that the defendant's consent is not necessary: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that for consent to be effective, it must be granted “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our circuit’s decisions reflect the same rule. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (relying on Bumper’s holding). The voluntariness of consent is determined by examining all circumstances present. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Courts often weigh various factors, including—but not confined to—“(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Voluntary consent alone, however, does not authorize a search without limits, as any such search must be confined within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The proper measure of the consent’s scope is “objective reasonableness”; courts must ask “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting party? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate body, our task is a deferential one: we only overturn district court determinations as to voluntariness and the extent of consent if we find clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two factors from the exchange are particularly noteworthy. First, Officer Kizer consistently maintained a concerned and non-coercive tone and manner throughout the interaction. Second, Lisa clearly demonstrated the capacity to withhold consent, as evidenced by her refusal of Officer Kizer’s initial request for entry. Following a second, non-coercive request, Lisa decided to permit Officer Kizer into her home. These conclusions are apparent from the video evidence. From his arrival at the Coleman home, Officer Kizer communicated with Lisa in a calm and respectful way, while Lisa, at all times, gave responses that indicated she comprehended what was occurring. Though Officer Kizer did not specifically inform Lisa that she was not obligated to consent to entry, his act of asking for permission, coupled with her original denial, indicated as much. Lisa was never in custody during this exchange. While Officer Kizer made his request to enter twice, the entire discussion was brief, lasting only a few minutes. Even though Lisa might have felt a certain amount of pressure associated with the police presence, at no point did Officer Kizer exert force or employ coercion. On these facts, there was no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the entry.

Coleman disagrees, directing attention to Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this is a reasonable argument, it is too limited in scope. The district court could permissibly conclude that the officer’s use of “need” did not render the request coercive when evaluated in the context of the entire situation. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer framed his request out of concern for Lisa’s children’s safety and communicated a sense of urgency, but the district court did not clearly err in finding that he did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s reply supports this reading; after responding “Okay,” she pointed out where her children were inside the house and advised the officers about her dogs, assuring them the dogs would not bite. This evidence supports upholding the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the entry.

We also agree with the district court that the officers did not exceed the limits of Lisa’s consent upon entering the home. The officers’ expressed intent was to conduct a welfare check on her children and verify that Coleman did not pose a risk to them or to anyone else. To do so, it was necessary to look through the residence, find those present, and make routine inquiries. The information gathered by the officers led to obtaining a warrant, which allowed them to seize three firearms. We find no deficiency in the district court’s conclusion that the officers honored the scope of Lisa’s initial consent once they were inside the Coleman home.

These holdings make it unnecessary to address the government’s alternative argument that the police entry and initial search were also justified by exigent circumstances or the doctrine of inevitable discovery. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. In closing, we add an overarching observation: Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and judicious decision-making when confronted with sensitive and challenging circumstances.
