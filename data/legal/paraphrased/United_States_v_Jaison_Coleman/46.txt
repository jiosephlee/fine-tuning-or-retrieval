Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Answering 911 calls involving domestic violence ranks among the most challenging duties police officers face. This matter illustrates that point well. At around 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department was dispatched in response to a report from a woman claiming her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and made contact with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the well-being of her children. Officer Kizer then commented, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and explained that the children were in one bedroom, her husband was in a different room, and the family’s dogs were gentle and wouldn’t bite. The district court concluded that, from this exchange, Lisa had given Officer Kizer the requisite consent to enter, and further determined that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers stayed within the boundaries of the consent once inside. Accordingly, the district court refused to exclude the firearms discovered in the home. We concur and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful firearm possession.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, helping them enter by directing her dogs out of the way. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not immediately see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts; Lisa replied she was unaware of his location. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in another bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman agreed to allow Officer Kizer to perform a protective pat down. Upon speaking with the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, and that Coleman had behaved in a loud, aggressive, and unkind manner. The children further reported that their father had aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and also threatened to set the house on fire. In response to the officers' inquiries, the children confirmed having seen the gun both on that night and on prior occasions, but they did not know its current location.

B

After entering the residence and obtaining additional information, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a warrant to search the Coleman residence. During the execution of this warrant, they discovered multiple firearms, which subsequently resulted in a federal indictment accusing Coleman of being a felon in possession of a firearm, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion seeking to suppress the firearms recovered from his residence, asserting that his wife had not authorized Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter at the outset. A hearing was conducted, after which a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers’ initial entry and thus recommended denying Coleman’s motion to suppress. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination regarding Lisa’s consent and independently concluding that the officers did not exceed the boundaries of her consent during their presence inside the home. Once the district court refused his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea while explicitly preserving his right to challenge the suppression decision on appeal. That issue is now presented before this Court.

II

A

The controlling legal doctrines are firmly rooted, and both sides acknowledge that an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s warrant requirement exists when there is consent to search a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties similarly concur that a defendant's consent is not necessary: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (referencing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has established that valid consent must be “freely and voluntarily” given. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This standard has been reiterated in our circuit’s case law. See, for instance, United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts evaluate the totality of circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant factors commonly considered include, but are not confined to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) the length of detention prior to giving consent; (4) whether consent was given immediately or was the result of persistent police requests; (5) whether any physical force was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when consent was provided.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is voluntarily given, the search that follows must stay within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The assessment of the scope of consent employs an “objective reasonableness” standard, focusing on what a typical reasonable person would have interpreted from the interaction between the officer and the consenting individual. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As a court of review, our examination is constrained: we assess district court determinations regarding voluntary consent and any disputes over its scope under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After examining the entirety of the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we discern no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily permitted the officers to enter her residence. Two elements of their interaction are particularly notable. First, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor and manner of speaking indicative of concern, rather than of coercion, throughout the encounter. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to withhold consent by refusing the officer’s initial request for entry. She ultimately allowed Officer Kizer to enter following his renewed request, which was made without any show of coercion. These points are evident upon review of the video recording. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman home, his interactions with Lisa were calm and respectful; in return, Lisa’s reactions consistently conveyed her understanding of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly state that Lisa could withhold consent, his act of asking for permission, and her initial refusal, both implicitly recognized her right to decline entry. Lisa was not at any time placed in custody. While Officer Kizer requested entry twice, the entire discussion spanned only several minutes. Even if Lisa experienced some measure of pressure in the situation, at no time did Officer Kizer apply coercion. Based on this record, there was no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry. Coleman argues to the contrary, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s statement during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this contention is understandable, it narrows the focus excessively. In the broader context, the district court reasonably concluded that Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” was not coercive, considering all relevant factors. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought permission to enter based on his expressed concern for Lisa’s children, conveying urgency and earnestness, but did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s reactions strongly suggest she understood the request accordingly: after assenting by saying “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer of the children’s whereabouts inside the house and reassured the officers that, though her dogs might jump on them, they would not bite. These circumstances warrant affirmation of the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the officers’ entrance. We also concur with the district court’s determination that the officers remained within the scope of the consent Lisa granted once inside. Their articulated aim was to ensure the wellbeing of her children, in part by making sure Coleman posed no threat to them or others. Fulfilling this purpose logically required the officers to survey the premises, account for all occupants, and ask some straightforward questions. The facts uncovered enabled the officers to obtain a search warrant, which in turn led to the discovery of three firearms. We detect no deficiency in the district court’s finding that the officers, once inside, adhered to the bounds of consent Lisa originally gave. These findings preclude the need to analyze the government’s alternate arguments regarding exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as alternative justifications for the entry and initial sweep of the Coleman residence. Ultimately, we AFFIRM. In doing so, we add this overarching observation: Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and well-reasoned judgment in a sensitive and challenging setting.
