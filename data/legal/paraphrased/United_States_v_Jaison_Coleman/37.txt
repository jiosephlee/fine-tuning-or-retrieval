Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa subsequently opened the door, allowing Officer Kizer and his fellow officers inside, and helped by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and indicated the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not observe Coleman, he inquired as to Coleman's whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. Officer Kizer conducted a brief search of the residence and found Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman allowed Officer Kizer to perform a protective frisk. Upon speaking with the two children, the officers were told that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in an argument, during which Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father pointed a firearm at their mother and threatened both to shoot her and to set the house on fire. When questioned, the children confirmed they had seen the gun, not only that evening but previously as well, although they did not know its current location.

B

After entering the residence and obtaining additional information, the officers applied for and received a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman household. During that search, they discovered multiple firearms, which ultimately resulted in a federal indictment accusing Coleman of being a felon in possession of a firearm, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms recovered from his residence, arguing that Officer Kizer and the other officers did not have initial consent to enter from his wife. At a subsequent hearing, the magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had indeed provided consent for the officers’ entry and recommended denial of the suppression motion. The district court concurred with the magistrate judge’s findings and adopted the conclusion that Lisa had permitted the officers to enter, and, in addition, overruled Coleman’s separate argument that the officers’ conduct inside the residence exceeded the boundaries of his wife’s consent. Once the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea but expressly reserved the right to contest the suppression decision on appeal. That appeal is now presented before this Court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are firmly established, and both parties acknowledge that consent serves as an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s requirement that searches of homes be authorized by a warrant. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both sides further concur that consent may be granted by someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has held for many years that valid consent requires it to be “freely and voluntarily” given. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This standard is consistently reiterated in our circuit’s decisions. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and implementing the rule stated in Bumper). When determining whether consent was voluntary, courts analyze the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Courts often look to several factors, including but not limited to, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (referencing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Additionally, a lawful search pursuant to consent must not exceed “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (referencing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The appropriate test for assessing scope is one of “objective reasonableness,” prompting courts to examine “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person providing consent? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate tribunal, our function is narrower. We evaluate a district court’s determinations regarding whether consent was voluntary and its scope only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entirety of the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we discern no clear mistake in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the police officers to enter her residence. Two elements of the encounter are particularly noteworthy. First, throughout the interaction, Officer Kizer spoke and acted in a manner expressing genuine concern, rather than attempting to pressure Lisa. Second, Lisa clearly demonstrated her capacity to withhold consent to entry, as evidenced by her initial refusal when Officer Kizer first requested permission. However, she then permitted Officer Kizer to enter after a subsequent, non-coercive request. These facts are plainly observable in the video recording. From the moment of Officer Kizer’s arrival at the Coleman home, he maintained a calm and respectful demeanor towards Lisa, and at all times, Lisa’s responses indicated that she comprehended the unfolding events. Although Officer Kizer never specifically informed Lisa that she was not obligated to consent, the very act of asking her permission suggested as much, as did her original denial. Lisa was never placed in custody at any point. Furthermore, while Officer Kizer did ask for consent twice, the entire dialogue between them lasted only a few minutes. Although Lisa may have felt some pressure to admit the officers, there is no indication that Officer Kizer acted coercively at any stage. On these grounds, there was no clear error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa gave her consent for the police to enter. Coleman contests this, concentrating on Officer Kizer’s statement during the second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While that objection is understandable, it narrows the focus too much. The district court was entitled to determine that, when considering the entire interaction, Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” was not coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s request to enter was motivated by concern for the welfare of Lisa’s children and, though he conveyed this with some urgency, the district court reasonably found he did so without surpassing the boundaries into coercion. Lisa’s subsequent statements reinforce this conclusion; after agreeing by saying “Okay,” she directed Officer Kizer to the location of her children and reassured the officers regarding her dogs, noting that while the dogs might jump, they would not bite. Taken together, these facts support the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the police’s entry. We also concur with the district court’s determination that, upon entering, the officers acted within the bounds of Lisa’s initial consent. Their objective was to ensure the children’s safety, including verifying that Coleman posed no threat to them or anyone else, a task that necessarily entailed surveying the premises, accounting for all occupants, and asking basic questions. The subsequent information obtained led to the issuance of a search warrant and the recovery of three firearms. We detect no flaw in the district court’s finding that the officers remained within the limits of the consent granted by Lisa. These conclusions render it unnecessary to reach the government’s alternative arguments regarding exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery as justification for the entry and initial sweep of the Coleman home. Ultimately, we AFFIRM. We close with a general observation: Officer Kizer and the other officers displayed prudent and judicious conduct in the face of complex and sensitive circumstances.
