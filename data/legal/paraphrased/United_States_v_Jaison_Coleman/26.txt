Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-3051  
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls related to domestic violence is among the most challenging tasks faced by law enforcement officers. The present matter aptly illustrates such difficulty. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police responded to a report from an individual stating her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned regarding the welfare of her children. In response, Officer Kizer remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and informed Officer Kizer that the children were located in one bedroom, her husband occupied another, and her dogs were safe and would not bite. The district court determined this interaction provided sufficient consent for Officer Kizer to enter the residence and, further, concluded that the actions of Officer Kizer and his colleagues once inside did not surpass the scope of Lisa’s consent. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms found during the search. We concur with these determinations and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, assisting their entrance by moving her dogs aside. She directed the officers to a rear bedroom and identified where the children were located. When Officer Kizer did not immediately find Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she was unaware. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in a different bedroom. Following a short exchange, Coleman consented to Officer Kizer performing a protective pat-down. During conversations with the children, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been engaged in an altercation, with Coleman characterized as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further stated their father had aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and also to set the house on fire. When questioned further, the children affirmed they had seen the firearm, both on the current evening and previously, although they did not know its current location.

B

After obtaining information during their initial entry, the officers applied for and received a search warrant for the Coleman residence. The subsequent search uncovered multiple firearms, which eventually resulted in Coleman being federally charged with firearm possession as a previously convicted felon, violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms discovered in his home, arguing that his wife had not given consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter at the outset. After a hearing, the magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa did provide consent for the officers' entry and recommended denying Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s finding regarding Lisa’s consent and, in addition, rejected Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the house went beyond what his wife had permitted. Once the district court denied the suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea while preserving his right to appeal the denial of suppression. That appeal is presently before this Court.

II

A

The foundational legal doctrines in this area are settled, and the parties do not dispute that consent constitutes a recognized exception to the Fourth Amendment’s warrant requirement for searches of a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties further acknowledge that the defendant need not be the individual providing consent: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (referencing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that consent must be provided “freely and voluntarily” in order to be valid. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This Circuit’s precedents adhere to the same standard. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and applying Bumper’s rule). To determine whether consent was voluntary, courts examine the totality of circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations generally include, but are not limited to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is voluntary, a subsequent search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Courts measure the scope of consent under an “objective reasonableness” standard, inquiring as to “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting party. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our authority is circumscribed; we only examine the district court’s findings concerning voluntary consent and its scope for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After carefully reviewing the entire encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear mistake in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa consented to the officers' entry into her home. Two elements of the exchange are particularly notable. First, Officer Kizer consistently used a tone and exhibited behavior suggestive of concern rather than coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her ability to withhold consent when she denied Officer Kizer’s initial request to enter. She subsequently agreed to allow Officer Kizer inside after he made a second, non-coercive request. These points are substantiated by the video evidence. From Officer Kizer’s arrival at the Coleman home, his communication with Lisa remained calm and respectful, and Lisa’s responses throughout suggested that she fully grasped what was occurring. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform Lisa that her consent was not mandatory, his act of seeking permission, as well as Lisa’s first denial, sufficiently implied this right. Lisa was not detained at any point, and though Officer Kizer repeated his request to enter, their discussion only lasted several minutes. Lisa might have experienced some implicit pressure, but Officer Kizer never resorted to coercion. Accordingly, the district court did not clearly err in deciding Lisa’s consent was voluntary.

Coleman objects by emphasizing Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need” during the second, key exchange: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this point is not without merit, it draws too narrowly on a single word. The district court was justified in determining that Officer Kizer’s phraseology did not transform his request into coercion when considering the totality of the situation. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer sought entry to verify the children’s safety, conveying urgency and concern, but the district court found this did not amount to undue pressure. Lisa’s own reply supports this interpretation—after answering “Okay,” she informed Officer Kizer of the children’s whereabouts inside and reassured him and the other officers about her dogs’ temperament. Based on this, we uphold the district court’s determination that Lisa consented to the police’s entry.

Similarly, we concur with the district court that the officers’ actions within the home remained within the scope of Lisa’s consent. The officers’ stated intent was to check on the welfare of the children, including ensuring Coleman posed no threat. This task necessarily involved surveying the house, finding all occupants, and asking some rudimentary questions. The information they gathered then formed the basis for obtaining a search warrant and recovering three firearms. We perceive no defect in the district court’s ruling that the officers, once inside, adhered to the limitations of Lisa’s initial consent.

These findings render unnecessary any consideration of the government’s alternative theories regarding exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery as justifications for the entry and preliminary search. Accordingly, we AFFIRM. Finally, we note that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers exercised prudent and thoughtful judgment in a challenging and sensitive situation.
