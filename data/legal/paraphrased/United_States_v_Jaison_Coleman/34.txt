Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls regarding domestic violence represents one of the most challenging aspects of law enforcement duties. This matter illustrates that reality. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer, a member of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police force, responded to a report in which a caller stated her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and was still present within the residence. Officer Kizer knocked on the door and conversed with Lisa, who hesitated when questioned about the welfare of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and then explained that her children were in one bedroom, her husband in a separate room, and her dogs were friendly and would not bite. According to the district court’s findings, this interaction provided Officer Kizer with valid consent to enter and, moreover, determined that he and the other officers did not go beyond the boundaries of Lisa’s consent upon entry. Accordingly, the district court declined to exclude the firearms discovered in the home. We concur with that conclusion and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for being a felon in possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his team, helping them enter by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers toward a rear bedroom, indicating where the children were located. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. A swift search of the residence led Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in another bedroom. A short exchange ensued, and Coleman agreed to let Officer Kizer perform a protective pat down. While conversing with the two children, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been arguing, with Coleman described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father aimed a gun at their mother and threatened to shoot her and to set the house on fire. In response to the officers’ questions, the children confirmed they had seen the firearm, both on that evening and in the past, but did not know its current location.

B

After obtaining information subsequent to their entry into the residence, the officers applied for and were granted a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman household. During the execution of the search warrant, officers discovered multiple firearms, which eventually resulted in Coleman being federally charged with firearm possession by a convicted felon, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the evidence of the firearms seized from his home, asserting that his wife did not give consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to initially enter. A hearing was held, after which the magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers’ entry and recommended denying Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s conclusion regarding Lisa’s consent and independently concluding that the officers’ conduct within the house did not surpass the boundaries of the consent granted by his wife. After his suppression motion was denied by the district court, Coleman entered a guilty plea but preserved his right to contest the suppression decision on appeal. The appeal of that decision is now before this court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are settled: the parties are in agreement that consent serves as an exception to the Fourth Amendment’s general warrant requirement for home searches. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Both parties likewise concur that the defendant need not be the person to provide consent: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (referencing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has established that valid consent requires that it be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This voluntary standard is equally reflected in our own precedents. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and employing the rule from Bumper). Determinations of whether consent was voluntary are made by considering all the circumstances as a whole. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant factors to this analysis may include, though are not strictly limited to, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was informed of his constitutional rights; (3) the duration of detention before consent was given; (4) the promptness or repetition of police requests for consent; (5) any use of physical force or threats; and (6) whether the person consenting was already in police custody.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Moreover, even where consent is found voluntary, law enforcement's search is confined by the “scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). This scope is evaluated by an “objective reasonableness” standard, assessing, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting party? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our review is constrained; we examine the district court’s determinations of voluntary consent and disputes over its scope only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her home. Two particular elements of the interaction are noteworthy. First, Officer Kizer’s behavior and speech throughout conveyed concern rather than any suggestion of coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated that she could refuse entry—indeed, she did so when Officer Kizer first requested access. Only after he made a second, non-coercive request did Lisa permit him to enter. The video evidence supports these observations. From his arrival at the Colemans’ home, Officer Kizer addressed Lisa in a calm and courteous manner, while Lisa consistently responded in a way that showed her comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not specifically inform her that she could withhold consent, his act of seeking permission carried that implication, and Lisa’s initial denial further supports it. Lisa was never detained at any time during the encounter. Despite Officer Kizer’s two requests for entry, the entire conversation lasted only a few minutes. While it is possible Lisa experienced some pressure to allow entry, there was no point at which Officer Kizer exerted any form of coercion. Based on these facts, we conclude that the district court’s finding of consent was not clearly erroneous.

Coleman disputes this, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the second, crucial request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this is a reasonable argument, Coleman’s focus is too narrow. The district court was entitled to find that using the word “need” did not make the request coercive, given the overall circumstances. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer explained that his motive was concern for the children’s safety and expressed his request with some urgency, yet the district court did not clearly err by finding there was no coercion. Lisa’s reaction essentially confirms this interpretation: she responded “Okay,” informed Officer Kizer where to find the children, and reassured the officers about the family’s dogs. 

All these facts support upholding the district court’s conclusion that Lisa gave consent for entry. We also agree with the district court that, once inside, the officers remained within the bounds of the consent Lisa had provided. Their stated reason for entering was to check on the welfare of the children, which entailed locating everyone present and asking a few pertinent questions. This investigation supplied grounds for obtaining a warrant, ultimately leading to the recovery of three firearms. We detect no deficiency in the district court’s ruling that, upon entry, the officers respected the scope of Lisa’s initial consent. Given these conclusions, we do not reach the government’s alternative arguments regarding exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery justifying the entry and sweep. Ultimately, we AFFIRM. In conclusion, we observe that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and well-balanced decision making under sensitive and challenging conditions.
