Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Addressing 911 calls alleging domestic violence ranks among law enforcement’s most challenging duties. The present case illustrates this reality. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer, serving with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department, responded to a report from a caller claiming her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained in the residence. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and initiated conversation with Lisa. When questioned about her children’s safety, Lisa hesitated. In response, Officer Kizer remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” then informed the officer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband occupied another, and that her dogs were friendly and not aggressive. The district court concluded that this interaction granted Officer Kizer consent to enter the premises and determined that neither he nor the other officers exceeded the parameters of Lisa’s permission once inside. On these grounds, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered during the search. We concur with these determinations and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, helping them enter the residence by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers to a back bedroom and indicated where the children were located. When Officer Kizer did not immediately see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. Officer Kizer conducted a brief search of the house and located Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman agreed to let Officer Kizer perform a protective pat-down. While conversing with the two children, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been involved in an argument, and that Coleman was described as being loud, aggressive, and mean. The children also stated that their father pointed a gun at their mother and threatened both to shoot her and to set the house on fire. When asked, the children confirmed that they had seen the firearm, both on that evening and at earlier times, but were uncertain of its current location.

B

After entering the residence, the officers obtained additional information and subsequently applied for and received a warrant to search the Coleman household. The resulting search uncovered multiple firearms, which ultimately led to Coleman being federally charged under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) for possessing a firearm as a felon. Coleman filed a motion to suppress the evidence of the firearms recovered from his residence, arguing that his wife never authorized Officer Kizer and his team to enter the home initially. After conducting a hearing, a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa had given consent for the officers to enter the premises and recommended denying Coleman’s motion to suppress. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s factual finding regarding Lisa’s consent and independently dismissing Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the house surpassed the boundaries of his wife's consent. After the district court rejected his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly preserving his ability to contest the suppression decision on appeal. The matter is now presented before this Court on appeal.

II

A

The foundational legal rules applicable here are not in dispute; both parties acknowledge that consent constitutes a recognized exception to the requirement of a warrant for home searches under the Fourth Amendment. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties further concur that the defendant himself need not provide consent: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (referencing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). For consent to be effective, the Supreme Court has long held it must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our circuit precedents reaffirm this rule. See, for instance, United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (quoting and applying Bumper’s standard). In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts examine all circumstances surrounding the consent. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations can include, though are not confined to: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was informed of his constitutional rights; (3) the length of any detention prior to consent; (4) whether consent was granted immediately or after persistent requests from authorities; (5) whether any form of physical force was involved; and (6) the individual’s custodial status at the time of consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is given voluntarily, law enforcement must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). To determine the scope, courts apply an “objective reasonableness” test, asking, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the person granting consent? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate tribunal, our review is limited; we assess district court findings regarding voluntariness of consent and the breadth of the consent under the clear error standard. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear mistake in the district court's determination that Lisa consented to the officers' entrance into her residence. Two elements of the interaction are particularly significant. To begin, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and demeanor indicative of genuine concern, not coercion, throughout the encounter. Additionally, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to withhold consent, as evidenced by her initial refusal to Officer Kizer’s first request to enter. She later permitted Officer Kizer inside following his renewed—yet non-coercive—request. These facts are plainly observable from the video evidence. From the outset of Officer Kizer's arrival at the Coleman home, his communication with Lisa was calm and respectful, and at all times, Lisa replied in a manner that indicated her comprehension of the situation. While Officer Kizer did not explicitly inform Lisa that her consent was not obligatory, the act of seeking permission itself, coupled with her initial denial, implied she was not compelled. At no moment was Lisa detained. Though Officer Kizer requested entry twice, the entirety of the discussion lasted only a few minutes. While Lisa might have felt a certain pressure to allow the officers entry, no evidence shows that Officer Kizer engaged in any coercive conduct. Given these circumstances, the district court’s finding that Lisa voluntarily consented is supported by the record.

Coleman, however, disagrees, centering on Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this argument has merit, it is ultimately too restricted in focus. The district court could reasonably conclude, considering all the circumstances, that Officer Kizer’s employment of the word “need” did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer, motivated by concern for the children’s welfare, communicated that concern urgently yet did not overstep into coercion, according to the district court’s reasonable view. Lisa’s own reaction confirms that she interpreted Officer Kizer’s request as non-coercive: she responded “Okay” and directed Officer Kizer to the children’s location in the home, informing him and his colleagues that her dogs might jump on them but would not bite.

Based on this, we uphold the district court’s conclusion that Lisa voluntarily allowed police entry. We also concur with the district court that the officers acted within the bounds of Lisa’s consent upon entering. Their clear objective was to assess the welfare of the children present, which necessarily involved visually surveying the premises, identifying individuals inside, and asking routine questions. The resulting information led officers to secure a search warrant and recover three firearms. There is no flaw in the district court’s determination that, once inside, the officers acted consistent with the scope of Lisa’s consent. Because these findings resolve the key issues, we need not address the government’s alternate arguments premised on exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery doctrines regarding the officers’ entry and initial protective sweep.

Consequently, we AFFIRM the judgment. We do so while noting, in closing, that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers demonstrated prudent and balanced judgment under sensitive and challenging conditions.
