Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls involving domestic violence ranks among the most challenging duties for law enforcement officers. The present matter illustrates this point. A little after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department responded to a report from a caller who stated that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and that he remained inside the residence. Upon arriving and knocking on the door, Officer Kizer engaged Lisa in conversation; Lisa hesitated when asked about the safety of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and went on to inform Officer Kizer that her children were together in one bedroom, her husband occupied another room, and her dogs were friendly and would not bite. The district court concluded that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with the necessary consent to enter and that, once inside, neither he nor the other officers surpassed the scope of the consent given by Lisa. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered in the residence. We concur with this reasoning and affirm Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant details are derived from body-worn camera recordings captured by Officer Kizer and other officers while at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and announced his presence. Lisa emerged onto the porch and informed Officer Kizer that both her son and daughter were inside. When asked if her children were safe, Lisa initially hesitated and merely replied that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then repeated his question regarding the wellbeing of her children, to which Lisa responded uncertainly, stating that she “hope[d] so.” Officer Kizer shifted his inquiry to the location of her husband within the home. Lisa replied that she did not know where he was, after which Officer Kizer asked for permission to come inside. As Lisa was turning back towards the door, she stopped briefly before replying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation paused, after which Lisa stated she needed to reenter the home to retrieve her shoes. When Lisa failed to promptly return, Officer Kizer, concerned, opened the door slightly and repeatedly called inside to check on her welfare. Approximately a minute later, Lisa came back to the doorway and stepped outside once more, leading Officer Kizer to again ask for an update about the situation inside, specifically requesting the name of her husband—who, according to Officer Kizer’s understanding, had threatened her. Lisa identified him as Jaison, whom this opinion will refer to as Coleman. A crucial interaction then ensued. Officer Kizer inquired whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and followed up by asking if the children were safe, leading to the following conversation:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to admit Officer Kizer and the other officers by opening the door and moving her dogs aside to clear their path. She then escorted the officers to a rear bedroom, indicating the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she was unaware. A brief sweep of the residence allowed Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in another bedroom. Following a short exchange, Coleman agreed to a protective pat-down by Officer Kizer. Upon speaking with the children, officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had engaged in a fight, during which Coleman was described as loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father had aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house ablaze. When questioned, both children confirmed that they had seen the firearm, both that evening and previously, but stated they did not currently know its location.

B

After entering the residence and acquiring additional information, law enforcement officers applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman residence. During the execution of this search, multiple firearms were discovered, which ultimately resulted in a federal indictment against Coleman for being a felon in possession of a firearm, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion seeking to suppress the evidence of the firearms found in his home, arguing that his wife had not provided consent for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers to initially enter the house. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge concluded that Lisa did in fact give consent for the officers to enter her residence and therefore recommended denial of Coleman’s motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge's determination regarding Lisa’s consent to the entry, and also expressly rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the residence went beyond the scope of his wife’s consent. Once the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea but did so with the express reservation of his right to appeal the district court’s ruling on suppression. That appeal is presently before this Court.

II

A

The controlling legal standards are well settled, and both parties acknowledge that consent operates as an exception to the requirement under the Fourth Amendment for a search warrant of a home. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Furthermore, the parties agree that consent may originate from someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that valid consent must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our circuit precedents apply this identical standard. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and utilizing the Bumper rule). In assessing whether consent was voluntary, courts take into account the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations may include, but are not confined to, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (referencing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is voluntary, any search undertaken must not exceed “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The question of scope is judged by “objective reasonableness,” which asks “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consent-giver? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appellate review, our task is constrained: determinations by the district court regarding voluntary consent and challenges related to the scope of consent are subject to review only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no manifest error in the district court's determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two features of the exchange are especially notable. First, Officer Kizer's manner throughout reflected concern rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to withhold consent; she did so when Officer Kizer initially sought entry. She ultimately permitted Officer Kizer to enter after a subsequent, non-coercive request. These facts are readily apparent from the video record. From his arrival, Officer Kizer maintained a calm and respectful tone with Lisa, who responded throughout in a way that indicated she comprehended the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly state that Lisa could refuse entry, the very act of seeking her approval, coupled with her initial refusal, inherently conveyed this information. Lisa was not detained at any time. Even though Officer Kizer sought consent twice, the entire interaction transpired in a matter of minutes. While some implicit pressure may have existed for Lisa to allow the officers in, Officer Kizer refrained from any coercive conduct. On this record, there is no clear error in the district court's conclusion that Lisa consented. Coleman contends otherwise, drawing attention to Officer Kizer's use of the word “need” during the critical second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this point is not without merit, Coleman’s argument focuses too narrowly. The district court could properly conclude that, under the full circumstances, the use of “need” did not render the request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer articulated concern for the welfare of Lisa’s children and did so with a degree of urgency, but the district court reasonably found this was not coercion. Lisa’s reply supports that finding: after saying “Okay,” she directed Officer Kizer to where the children were and reassured the officers about her dogs’ behavior. These facts support the district court’s decision that Lisa provided consent to the entry. We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers remained within the bounds of Lisa's consent upon entering. Their declared purpose was to ensure the well-being of the children, confirming Coleman posed no threat. This required surveying the premises, locating individuals, and making basic inquiries. The information thus obtained enabled the officers to obtain a warrant and ultimately led to the recovery of three firearms. There is no error in the district court’s finding that, after entry, the officers observed the limits of the consent granted. Given these determinations, we need not address the government’s alternative arguments regarding exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery justifying the entry and sweep of the Coleman home. In summary, we AFFIRM. We also observe that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers exercised prudent and measured judgment under sensitive and challenging circumstances.
