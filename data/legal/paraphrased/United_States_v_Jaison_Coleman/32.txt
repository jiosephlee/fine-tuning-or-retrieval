Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-3051  
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Addressing reports of domestic violence is among the most challenging tasks that law enforcement officers confront. This matter illustrates that reality. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a report from a woman who claimed that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside their residence. Officer Kizer knocked and made contact with Lisa, who hesitated when asked about her children’s welfare. In response, Officer Kizer remarked, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” after which she informed Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband was in another, and that the dogs in the home would not bite. The district court concluded that Lisa’s responses furnished Officer Kizer with the necessary consent to enter, and also determined that neither Officer Kizer nor his fellow officers acted beyond the limitations of Lisa’s consent once they were inside. Accordingly, the district court denied the motion to suppress the firearms discovered within the residence. We concur with this reasoning and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, also clearing her dogs out of their way to aid their entrance. She guided the officers to a back bedroom and indicated where the children were located. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. A swift search of the residence led Officer Kizer to discover Coleman in a different bedroom. Following a short exchange, Coleman agreed to let Officer Kizer conduct a protective frisk. Upon interviewing the two children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had engaged in an argument, and that Coleman had acted in a manner that was loud, aggressive, and mean. The children further reported that their father had aimed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to set the house on fire. When asked, the children affirmed having seen the gun, both that evening and on prior occasions, though they were unsure of its current location.

B

After obtaining information during their initial entry, law enforcement officers applied for and received a search warrant for the Coleman residence. The ensuing search uncovered multiple firearms, which eventually resulted in Coleman being federally charged with possession of a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress evidence of the firearms seized from his residence, arguing that his spouse never granted Officer Kizer and his fellow officers permission to enter the home at the outset. At a subsequent hearing, the magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa had in fact consented to the officers' entry and therefore recommended denying Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred with this recommendation, expressly adopting the magistrate judge’s finding regarding Lisa’s consent and, in addition, overruling Coleman’s argument that the law enforcement officers had exceeded the boundaries of the consent given while inside the home. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea but explicitly reserved his right to appeal the suppression decision. The present appeal now brings that issue before this Court.

II

A

The foundational legal standards at issue are undisputed, as both parties concede that, under the Fourth Amendment, searches of a residence typically require a warrant, with consent constituting an exception to that rule. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The parties further agree that this consent may be provided by someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently required that consent must be “freely and voluntarily” granted to be valid. Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Our circuit has repeatedly applied this principle. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (referencing Bumper’s standard). Courts make determinations about whether consent was voluntary by evaluating all circumstances in context. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Factors that are commonly weighed include: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Importantly, even when consent is given voluntarily, any resulting search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). Courts utilize a standard of “objective reasonableness” when interpreting the scope of consent, considering “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting individual. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our review is narrowly circumscribed: we assess district court determinations regarding the voluntariness and scope of consent only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After considering the entire encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we conclude that the district court did not clearly err in determining that she voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two elements of the exchange are particularly notable. First, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and attitude that reflected concern rather than coercion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to refuse entry, as evidenced by her initial denial of Officer Kizer’s first request. Upon his second, non-coercive inquiry, she then opted to grant permission. The video evidence substantiates these points. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman home, he communicated calmly and courteously with Lisa, who consistently responded in ways suggesting her awareness and comprehension of the situation. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly state she could decline his entry, his request for consent implicitly conveyed this right, as did her initial refusal. Lisa was not in custody at any stage. Although Officer Kizer sought entry permission twice, the entirety of their exchange lasted only a few minutes. Lisa may have experienced some implicit pressure regarding the police presence, but there is no evidence of coercion by Officer Kizer. Accordingly, the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to entry is supported by the record.

Coleman argues otherwise, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s choice of the word “need” in his second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Although this point is reasonably raised, Coleman’s focus is overly narrow. Under the totality of the circumstances, the district court could reasonably determine that the use of “need” did not render Officer Kizer’s request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer explained his desire to enter by conveying concern for the children’s safety, delivering the message with urgency and earnestness; however, the district court reasonably concluded that his approach did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa’s own reaction—saying “Okay,” telling Officer Kizer the children’s locations, and assuring the officers about the non-threatening nature of her dogs—supports the conclusion that she received his request as such.

Accordingly, we uphold the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to police entry. We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers acted consistently with the scope of that consent upon entry. Their purpose in entering was to check on the welfare of Lisa’s children and to confirm that Coleman did not present a threat. These objectives necessitated a visual inspection of the premises, locating all individuals present, and asking relevant questions. The information obtained subsequently led to the issuance of a warrant and the seizure of three firearms. We discern no error in the district court’s determination that the officers honored the consent Lisa had given once inside the home.

Because of these conclusions, it is unnecessary to address the government’s alternate argument that the entry and protective sweep were justified under the doctrines of exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery. For these reasons, we AFFIRM. In closing, we acknowledge that Officer Kizer and his fellow officers acted with judgment and composure in challenging circumstances.
