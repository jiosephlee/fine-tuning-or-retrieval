Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-20143  
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud based on their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. According to the superseding indictment, defendants encouraged their social media audience to buy securities by posting false information about their own trading activity and potential future prices, causing the securities' prices to rise and enabling defendants to reap profits. The district court dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it described only an attempt to withhold potentially important economic information from followers, as opposed to depriving them of a traditional property right. Upon review, we find that the indictment adequately alleges a fraudulent scheme and intent. Therefore, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment.

I

The government accuses Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight of orchestrating a securities “pump and dump” scheme. According to the allegations, the defendants amassed substantial audiences on multiple social media platforms, represented themselves as expert stock traders, and regularly shared their trading actions online. The government contends that, in implementing their alleged scheme, defendants would acquire a security, “pump” its price by disseminating false and misleading statements regarding the security on social media so as to entice other investors to buy and artificially raise the price, and then “dump” their holdings by surreptitiously selling at a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. It is further alleged in the indictment that the defendants obtained $114 million in illicit profits. The indictment charged them with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and with multiple counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Daniel Knight pleaded guilty, while the others filed motions to dismiss. The district court granted dismissal, finding that the indictment did not properly allege that the defendants intended to deprive the victims of a recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We examine the adequacy of an indictment without deference. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must presume the indictment’s factual assertions are accurate and assess whether it properly alleges an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To be valid, an indictment should: “(1) include every essential element of the alleged crime; (2) clearly advise the accused of the charges he faces; and (3) protect the accused against double jeopardy by future prosecutions for the same offense.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Ultimately, the indictment must set forth all material components of the crime charged to be deemed sufficient. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The statute addressing securities fraud prohibits both schemes intended “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” and those devised “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “caselaw interpreting the securities fraud statute in this circuit is limited,” § 1348 “adopts key elements from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” resulting in courts treating these statutory provisions similarly and frequently referencing mail and wire fraud jurisprudence when considering securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying those legal standards, securities fraud consists of (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) execution of the scheme with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The first element, “scheme to defraud,” necessitates a material misstatement “designed to mislead others to obtain something valuable, for instance, money, from the party deceived.” Id. (modification in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). Regarding the “intent to defraud” component, it requires “a purpose to (1) deceive, and (2) inflict some injury via such deception.” Id. (citing Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Thus, securities fraud is committed when a defendant “‘perpetrated a deception’” and “[t]he acquisition of the victim’s money or property” was “‘a goal’ of the fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants admit the indictment sufficiently pleads deception. They argue, however, that the indictment falls short of pleading a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud, asserting it only claims they sought to deprive followers of economically valuable information (as opposed to money or property) to personally profit (rather than to harm the followers). Given the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject their argument.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to charge a scheme aimed at depriving their followers of a protected property right. Although it is accurate that fraud convictions cannot be based solely on depriving someone of potentially valuable economic information, in this case the indictment advanced further by adequately alleging that the defendants schemed to deprive their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest exclusively on depriving someone of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist to secure contracts for state-funded projects. Id. at 309–10. During Ciminelli’s prosecution for wire fraud, the government based its case solely on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the government sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Put differently, under this theory, the victim’s property is defined as “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that because federal fraud statutes are intended to safeguard only traditional property rights, and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

The present case stands in contrast to Ciminelli. The indictment does not reference the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that defendants persuaded their followers to purchase securities through false representations—meaning, they convinced them to part with their money or property. The indictment relies on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In addressing the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that this approach “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Distinct from the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here “protects money and property.” Id. Accordingly, the indictment complies with Ciminelli, as it charges that the defendants fraudulently deprived their followers of a protected property interest by inducing them to buy securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment does not allege an intent to defraud because it asserts that their goal was to profit from the alleged scheme, rather than to inflict harm upon any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable argument, clarifying that the fraud statutes do not make a victim’s economic loss a necessary element of the offense. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the accused made false representations to secure contracts with a state transportation department, where contractors were mandated to subcontract to disadvantaged businesses. Kousisis misrepresented that his company would comply with this requirement, but after being awarded the contracts, he used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor only as a “mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When the deception was uncovered, Kousisis faced prosecution under the “fraudulent-inducement theory”—which posits that a defendant commits fraud by using a significant misrepresentation to lead a victim into a contract necessitating transfer of money or property, irrespective of whether the defendant intended the victim to suffer a net economic loss. Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contended that the fraudulent-inducement theory failed because it did not require showing that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that a fraud statute violation occurs whenever a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

Here, the indictment adequately alleges the defendants’ intent to defraud their followers, specifically alleging that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the statutes do not require a showing of actual financial harm, the mere fact that the followers acquired securities at market value does not negate the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (noting liability for fraud can exist even where the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants assert their aim was to profit, not to inflict harm; yet, in these circumstances, this difference is immaterial. As pled in the indictment, the objective of the alleged scheme was to obtain money by deceiving followers into purchasing securities. The indictment’s account that defendants “pumped and dumped” securities for profit demonstrates that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—regardless of the presence or absence of a net loss to the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s charge that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to purchase securities sufficiently alleges an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (clarifying that the fraud statutes do not demand a “mirror image” requirement between victim loss and defendant gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in an analogous situation).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
