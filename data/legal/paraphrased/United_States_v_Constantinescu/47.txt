Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-20143  
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud arising out of their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants caused their social-media followers to purchase securities by posting false information regarding their own trading activities and the anticipated value of securities, thereby artificially boosting prices and generating profits for themselves. The district court dismissed these charges, finding that the indictment only accused the defendants of depriving their followers of beneficial economic information, not of any conventional property interest, and thus failed to allege an offense. However, because we find the indictment adequately pleads both a fraudulent scheme and fraudulent intent, we REVERSE the district court’s order of dismissal.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. Each defendant amassed a substantial following on social media platforms, where they portrayed themselves as expert stock traders and routinely shared information about their trading activity. According to the government’s allegations, the scheme operated by having defendants acquire a security, “‘pump’ the price of that security by disseminating false and misleading statements about the security on [social media], thereby inducing other investors to purchase and thereby inflate the price,” after which defendants would “‘dump’” the security by selling it surreptitiously at a profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts that the defendants collectively realized $114 million in profits from their actions. The indictment charged defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as several counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea; the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, finding the indictment insufficient because it did not assert that defendants plotted to deprive victims of any traditional property right. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We conduct a de novo review to assess whether an indictment is sufficient. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept all factual assertions in the indictment as true and ascertain if a valid offense is charged. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). The indictment must (1) state each essential element of the alleged offense, (2) adequately notify the defendant of the specific accusations, and (3) protect the defendant from subsequent prosecution for the same conduct by providing a double jeopardy safeguard. United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). To be adequate, an indictment must allege every substantive element of the charged crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute bars schemes intended to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as those intended “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “caselaw interpreting the securities fraud statute in this circuit is limited,” § 1348 “incorporates key elements from the mail and wire fraud statutes.” Accordingly, courts have applied “similar interpretations” to the relevant terms, frequently referencing mail and wire fraud precedent when evaluating securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Given these legal frameworks, liability for securities fraud arises when there exists (1) a “scheme to defraud” (2) committed with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” component necessitates a material misrepresentation that is “meant to mislead others with the goal of obtaining a valuable asset, such as money, from the deceived party.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” component, on the other hand, consists of “a purpose to (1) deceive, and (2) create some harm through that deception.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Essentially, a defendant is guilty of securities fraud if he “‘committed deception’” and obtaining “‘the victim’s money or property’” was one of his “‘objectives.’” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants concede that the indictment properly alleges deceptive conduct. However, they maintain that the indictment fails to set forth a scheme to defraud or the requisite intent to defraud. They argue that, at most, the indictment asserts that their intent was to deprive followers of useful economic information (rather than money or property) for their own enrichment (rather than to inflict harm on the followers). Based on the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject that argument.

A

Defendants contend that the district court rightly dismissed the indictment since it fails to claim that their followers were deprived of a legally protected property right. Although a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on depriving someone of potentially advantageous economic information, the indictment in this case made additional allegations, appropriately asserting that defendants intended to defraud their followers of monetary assets.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of access to valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist to secure jobs funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The government’s wire fraud prosecution against Ciminelli relied exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. Under this theory, the government sought to prove wire fraud by demonstrating that the defendant engaged in a scheme to withhold from the victim important economic information necessary for discretionary economic choices. Id. at 310. Essentially, the theory held that the property at issue was “the right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that since the federal fraud statutes only guard traditional property interests, and because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the right-to-control theory does not support a fraud conviction. Id. at 316.

The present circumstances differ from those in Ciminelli. Here, the indictment does not refer to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it accuses the defendants of misleading their followers into buying securities—that is, of fraudulently causing victims to surrender their money or property. This indictment proceeds on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant secures a victim’s agreement to a contract involving payment of money or property by means of a material misstatement. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In its review of this theory, the Supreme Court expressly stated that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory dismissed in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory presented here “protects money and property.” Id. Accordingly, the indictment is not prohibited by Ciminelli because it asserts that the defendants obtained a protected property interest—money—by fraudulent inducement to purchase securities.

B

Defendants then contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege that they intended to defraud, asserting instead that it merely claims defendants sought personal gain from their scheme, not to inflict harm on any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed a comparable position, ruling that fraud statutes do not require the victim to experience economic injury as a result of the defendant's actions. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, Kousisis misrepresented information to secure contracts with a state transportation department, which mandated that contractors subcontract with disadvantaged businesses. Kousisis falsely claimed his company would meet this requirement, but after receiving the contracts, he used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor solely as "a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When the deception was uncovered, the government prosecuted Kousisis under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which is defined as when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis maintained that this theory was untenable, as it allowed conviction without proof that the “defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this reasoning, clarifying that a violation of the fraud statutes occurs whenever a defendant "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,'" even if the defendant does not intend for the victim to be left economically disadvantaged. Id. at 1392.

The current indictment meets the requisite standard by alleging defendants acted with fraudulent intent towards their followers. For instance, it states that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not require proof of actual economic harm, the fact that the followers received securities at market value does not negate the basis for the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding a defendant may be culpable for fraud “even when he provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants claim that their goal was profit, not to disadvantage victims economically. Here, that distinction is immaterial. As set forth in the indictment, defendants intended to obtain money by fraudulently inducing their followers into purchasing securities. The charge that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme for profit is evidence they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. It is sufficient to complete the fraud that “the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—whether or not the victim sustains a net financial loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment's averments concerning the fraudulent inducement of followers to purchase securities adequately allege a cognizable injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that fraud statutes do not necessitate a direct correlation between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (following Kousisis in a similar case).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and an intent to defraud, we VACATE the district court's order dismissing the indictment and RETURN the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
