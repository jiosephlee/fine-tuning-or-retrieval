Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud arising from their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, they persuaded followers on social media to buy securities by making posts that falsely represented their own trading activities and the anticipated price movements of those securities, thereby manipulating prices upward so they could gain financially. The district court dismissed the indictment, finding it insufficient because it claimed only that the defendants attempted to deprive followers of potentially valuable economic information, rather than of a conventional property interest. However, since we determine that the indictment adequately sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the government, each defendant maintained significant followings on several social media platforms. They presented themselves as expert stock traders and regularly shared their trading behavior online. The government’s allegations describe the defendants’ method: purchasing a security, “pumping” its price upward by disseminating false or misleading statements about the security via social media in order to induce other investors to buy and inflate its price, and then “dumping” the security by selling it covertly for a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts that the defendants realized $114 million in profits from this conduct. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed motions to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted dismissal, determining that the indictment did not adequately allege that defendants intended to deprive victims of any recognized property right. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We assess whether an indictment is sufficient by conducting a de novo review. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). In this analysis, the court accepts the indictment’s factual assertions as accurate and evaluates whether the allegations establish the commission of a criminal offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment is required to: “(1) set forth each essential element of the crime charged; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the accusations; and (3) protect the defendant from being tried again for the same conduct.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Thus, an indictment is considered sufficient if it charges every material element of the alleged crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids both “schemes to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” and schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” resulting in “courts [giving] the terms similar treatment” and frequently turning to mail and wire fraud precedent to interpret securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). In view of those principles, a conviction for securities fraud requires proof of (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. To satisfy the “scheme to defraud” prong, there must be a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” component demands “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In summary, a person commits securities fraud when that individual “engaged in deception” and when “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” constituted “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants concede that the indictment sufficiently alleges deceptive conduct. However, they assert that the indictment fails to plead a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. According to the defendants, the indictment only accuses them of intending to deprive their followers of valuable economic information (not money or property) for their own gain (not to harm their followers). Based on the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject that argument.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it failed to allege a scheme to deprive their followers of a protected property interest. While a defendant cannot be found guilty of fraud merely for depriving someone of potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in this case does more, properly asserting that the defendants engaged in a scheme to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a fraud conviction cannot rest solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction company compensated a lobbyist to assist in securing jobs funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The government, in charging Ciminelli with wire fraud, relied exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the government sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Stated differently, the right-to-control theory treats the victim’s “property” as “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes safeguard only traditional property interests and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” thereby precluding reliance on the right-to-control theory for a fraud conviction. Id. at 316.

This case presents a different scenario from Ciminelli. The indictment here does not invoke the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that the defendants persuaded their followers—using misrepresentations—to buy securities, thereby relinquishing money or property. The indictment advances a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When addressing the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here “protects money and property.” Id. Thus, the indictment does not contravene Ciminelli, as it claims that defendants, through fraudulent inducement, deprived their followers of a protected property interest by encouraging them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, arguing instead that it asserts defendants aimed to profit from their scheme rather than to inflict harm on any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed such an argument, holding that under the fraud statutes, it is not necessary for a victim to incur economic loss as a result of a defendant’s conduct. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant made false statements to secure contracts from a state department of transportation, which required contractors to hire a disadvantaged business as a subcontractor. Kousisis falsely claimed his company would satisfy this requirement, but after winning the contracts, used the subcontractor only as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When his deception was revealed, the government prosecuted him under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which finds liability when a defendant employs a material misrepresentation to induce a victim to enter into a contract involving a transfer of money or property, “regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis challenged the theory, claiming it would permit convictions absent proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this position, clarifying that a violation occurs when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The present indictment adequately pleads that defendants intended to defraud their followers, including allegations that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the statutes governing fraud do not necessitate actual economic loss, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not preclude prosecution for fraud. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding that a defendant may be found liable for fraud even if he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants assert they were motivated by profit rather than by any intent to harm, but in this context, that distinction is irrelevant. According to the indictment, the defendants’ objective was to obtain money, which they did by deceitfully causing their followers to purchase securities. The fact that the indictment charges defendants with orchestrating a pump-and-dump scheme to realize profits demonstrates that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The commission of fraud is established “when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—regardless of whether the victim sustained a net financial loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Therefore, the indictment’s allegation that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to purchase securities sufficiently asserts an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” correlation between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying the Kousisis holding in an analogous setting).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
