Case Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 24-20143  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Kurt D. Engelhardt

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud based on their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. According to the superseding indictment, defendants misled their social-media audience by disseminating false statements about their own trading positions and about the expected value of certain securities, causing prices to rise artificially so the defendants could benefit financially. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, finding the indictment insufficient because it claimed only a deprivation of valuable economic information—rather than a classic property interest. Determining that the indictment adequately asserts both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a “pump and dump” securities scheme. Each defendant possessed a significant presence on various social media platforms, presenting themselves as expert stock traders and routinely sharing their trading conduct online. According to the government’s allegations, the defendants executed their “pump and dump” operation by initially acquiring a security, then “‘pumping’ the security’s price by disseminating false and misleading information about it on [social media],” thereby encouraging other investors to buy the security and artificially inflating its price, before “‘dumping’” their own holdings by covertly selling for a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts that the defendants realized $114 million in profits from this activity. The charges brought against the defendants included conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in contravention of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed motions seeking dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motions, finding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege that the defendants intended to deprive victims of a traditional property right. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We assess the adequacy of an indictment using a de novo standard of review. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must “accept the allegations in the indictment as true and decide whether the indictment states an offense.” United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To satisfy requirements, an indictment should “(1) list every prima facie element of the offense charged; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the nature of the charge against him; and (3) give the defendant the ability to assert a double jeopardy defense in subsequent proceedings.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Ultimately, “an indictment is sufficient only if it alleges every essential element of the crime.” United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids both schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” and those aimed “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although this circuit has “scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes”; thus, “courts have given the terms similar treatment,” frequently looking to mail and wire fraud precedent to assess securities fraud claims. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying those concepts, the elements of securities fraud are: (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) execution of the scheme with an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. Satisfying the “scheme to defraud” requirement means showing a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). For “intent to defraud,” there must be “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Summarizing, securities fraud occurs when a defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and where “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants admit the indictment sufficiently pleads deception. However, they assert that the indictment does not allege a scheme or intent to defraud. They maintain that, at most, the indictment claims they sought to deprive followers merely of valuable economic information (not tangible money or property) for the purpose of enriching themselves rather than causing injury to followers. We reject this argument, guided by the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis.

A

Defendants contend that the district court was correct in dismissing the indictment, asserting that it fails to allege any scheme aimed at depriving their followers of a legally protected property right. Although fraud convictions cannot rest solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information, the indictment in this case alleges more, as it specifically sets forth defendants’ plan to defraud their followers of actual money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest exclusively on the loss of access to valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction business compensated a lobbyist in an effort to secure state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution based its wire fraud charge solely on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory allowed the government to seek a conviction for wire fraud by demonstrating that the defendant devised a scheme to deprive the victim of information vital to making discretionary economic decisions. Id. at 310. Put differently, the property interest asserted under this theory was the “right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Court concluded that federal fraud statutes only protect conventional property interests and that “the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” precluding the right-to-control theory from serving as a valid basis for a fraud conviction. Id. at 316.

The facts here are distinguishable from those in Ciminelli. The indictment contains no reference to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it alleges that the defendants misled their followers through false statements in order to persuade them to purchase securities, thereby relinquishing their money or property. The charging document asserts a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which involves a defendant making a material misrepresentation to cause a victim to enter into a contract obligating the victim to transfer money or property. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When the Supreme Court examined the fraudulent-inducement theory, it clarified that this theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the rejected right-to-control theory, the fraudulent-inducement approach asserted here “protects money and property.” Id. Because the indictment claims that defendants caused their followers to relinquish a protected property interest by fraudulently inducing securities purchases, it is not inconsistent with Ciminelli.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not assert that they intended to defraud; they argue that the indictment only claims they aimed to profit from their scheme rather than to cause harm to any alleged victim. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed a comparable argument, clarifying that the fraud statutes do not require victims to incur financial loss for a scheme to be prosecutable. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant made false statements to secure contracts from a state department of transportation, which mandated that contractors subcontract with a disadvantaged business. Kousisis misrepresented his company’s intent to satisfy this requirement. After obtaining the contracts, he merely used the disadvantaged business as a “mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. Upon the exposure of his deceit, Kousisis was charged under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which criminalizes the act of “us[ing] a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis claimed this theory was invalid as it permitted conviction without proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, affirming that violating the fraud statutes merely requires “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The indictment at hand sufficiently alleges defendants’ fraudulent intent toward their followers, asserting that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the statutes governing fraud do not necessitate actual economic loss, the mere fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not undermine the validity of the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding that fraud liability attaches even if the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants maintain their goal was simply to gain profit, not to inflict harm upon victims, but under these circumstances, such a distinction is immaterial. As alleged in the indictment, the intent was to obtain money by deceptively inducing their followers into purchasing securities. The claim that defendants executed a pump-and-dump to earn a profit substantiates their financial motivation, or that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. As stated by the Court, “[a] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—whether or not the victim experienced a net loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Therefore, the indictment’s allegations concerning defendants’ scheme to induce followers to acquire securities suffice to establish an alleged injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that the fraud statutes do not require the victim’s loss and defendant’s gain to be exact opposites); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in an analogous scenario).

IV

Since the indictment adequately asserts both a scheme and the intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
