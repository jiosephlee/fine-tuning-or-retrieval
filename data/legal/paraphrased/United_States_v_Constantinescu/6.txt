Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced indictment for securities fraud due to their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. According to the superseding indictment, defendants encouraged their social-media followers to buy securities by posting false information regarding their own trading activities and the likely future value of the securities, thereby causing an artificial increase in price from which the defendants profited. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that it did not allege an offense since it only claimed the defendants deprived followers of possibly valuable economic information, rather than a conventional property interest. However, as we determine that the indictment adequately pleads both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. Each defendant maintained a substantial following on social media platforms and presented themselves as expert stock traders, routinely sharing details of their trading activities online. According to the government, the defendants executed their scheme by purchasing securities and then “‘pump[ing]’ the price of those securities by disseminating false and misleading statements about them on [social media], thereby encouraging other investors to buy and artificially drive up the price.” The defendants would then “dump” their holdings by selling the securities secretly for personal gain. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts that the defendants realized profits totaling $114 million from the scheme. Charges were brought against them for conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and for multiple counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, finding the indictment insufficient because it did not allege that the defendants intended to deprive victims of any traditional property right. The government filed a timely appeal from that decision.

II

We conduct a de novo review of whether an indictment is sufficient. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court “is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.” United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment must: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Ultimately, an indictment is adequate only if it sets out each essential element of the charged crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute makes it unlawful to engage in schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” or to use “false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises” to acquire “any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” See U.S.C. § 1348. Although “caselaw interpreting the securities fraud statute in this circuit is limited,” § 1348 “incorporates significant concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and therefore “courts have treated the relevant terms similarly,” often turning to mail and wire fraud precedents when evaluating securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Against this backdrop, a securities fraud charge requires (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) action taken with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. For the “scheme to defraud” element, there must be a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others so as to secure something of value, such as money, from the deceived party.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” prong demands “an intent both to deceive and to bring about some harm resulting from that deception.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Ultimately, securities fraud occurs where a defendant has “‘engaged in deception’” and “‘obtaining the victim’s money or property’” constituted “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently alleges deceptive conduct. However, they argue that it does not set forth either a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. Their position is that the indictment only claims they intended to deprive their followers of valuable economic information—rather than money or property—for their own enrichment, not to harm their followers. Based on the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject this argument.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment, asserting that it fails to claim a scheme to deprive their followers of a property interest safeguarded by law. Although it is accurate that fraud convictions cannot rest merely on the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in this case extends further by adequately alleging that defendants engaged in a scheme to defraud their followers of actual money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud may not be predicated exclusively on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that matter, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist to secure state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution in Ciminelli relied only on the right-to-control theory for its wire fraud case. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory posits that the government may prove wire fraud by demonstrating that a defendant conspired to deprive a victim of economic information important for discretionary economic decisions. Id. at 310. In essence, the theory defines the victim’s property as “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that the federal fraud statutes are limited to protecting traditional property interests and that “the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” making the right-to-control theory an improper basis for a fraud conviction. Id. at 316. The current case is distinguishable from Ciminelli. The indictment here does not invoke the right-to-control theory. Instead, it charges that defendants, through misrepresentations, persuaded their followers to buy securities—in other words, to relinquish their money or property. This constitutes a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” where a defendant employs a material misrepresentation to deceive a victim into entering a contract that entails surrendering money or property. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When the Supreme Court examined the fraudulent-inducement theory, it clarified that this approach “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Contrary to the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory asserted here “protects money and property.” Id. The indictment therefore complies with Ciminelli, as it asserts that defendants fraudulently caused their followers to part with a protected property interest by inducing them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient for failing to allege an intent to defraud, asserting that it only claims defendants sought personal gain from their scheme rather than intending harm to any victims. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable argument, determining that the federal fraud statutes do not mandate that a victim incur financial loss as a result of the defendant’s actions. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, the defendant secured contracts with a state department of transportation by submitting false representations regarding his compliance with requirements to subcontract with a disadvantaged business. Despite these assertions, Kousisis’s company employed the purported subcontractor solely as a “mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. After his deception was exposed, Kousisis was prosecuted under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which posits that a defendant can be held liable for “us[ing] a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis challenged this basis for liability, arguing it allowed conviction absent evidence that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that a violation of the fraud statutes occurs when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

Here, the indictment properly alleges the defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. It states, for instance, that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not require actual financial harm, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not negate the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding liability may exist even when a defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Although defendants claim their goal was to profit rather than to injure any victim, that distinction is immaterial under these circumstances. As stated in the indictment, defendants’ aim was to secure money through the fraudulent inducement of their followers to buy securities. The indictment’s allegations that defendants manipulated and sold for personal gain demonstrate that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The law provides that “[a] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—even if the victim did not experience a net financial loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Accordingly, the indictment’s claims that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to purchase securities suffice to allege an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that fraud statutes do not demand a “mirror image” scenario where the victim’s monetary loss equates exactly to the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (extending Kousisis’s reasoning to an analogous situation).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
