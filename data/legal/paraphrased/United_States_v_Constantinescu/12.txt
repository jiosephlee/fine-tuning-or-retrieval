Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced indictment for securities fraud arising out of their participation in a "pump and dump" scheme. According to the superseding indictment, they persuaded their followers on social media to buy securities by posting false information regarding their own trading positions and the projected price of those securities, thereby inflating prices artificially and securing profits for themselves. The district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that it did not establish an offense, reasoning that the indictment only claimed the defendants aimed to deprive followers of potentially beneficial economic information, rather than of a conventional property interest. However, finding that the indictment sufficiently sets out both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a securities “pump and dump” scheme. Each defendant reportedly maintained substantial social media audiences across different platforms, representing themselves as adept stock traders and regularly sharing details of their trades online. According to the government, defendants would acquire a security and subsequently “‘pump’ the price...by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” then covertly “dump” the security for personal gain. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts that the defendants realized $114 million in profits from this conduct. Charges brought included conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, along with several counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants moved to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the dismissal, reasoning that the indictment did not properly allege the defendants intended to deprive victims of a traditional property interest. The government then filed a timely appeal.

II

We conduct a de novo review to assess whether an indictment is sufficient. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The allegations presented in the indictment must be accepted as true, and the court must evaluate whether they establish a crime. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment is required to: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Thus, an indictment’s adequacy depends on whether it sets forth every material element of the alleged offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids schemes designed to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as schemes seeking “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is little case law interpreting the securities fraud statute within this circuit, § 1348 “adopts key concepts” from the mail and wire fraud provisions; consequently, courts typically analyze these terms similarly and frequently reference mail and wire fraud precedent when assessing securities fraud cases. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Utilizing these shared concepts, a charge of securities fraud necessitates (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the presence of “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” prong requires a significant misrepresentation that “is intended to mislead others in order to gain something valuable, such as money, from the party deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” component means “an intent (1) to deceive and (2) to cause harm as a result of such deception.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Therefore, a person is guilty of securities fraud when he “‘engaged in deception’” with the goal that “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently sets forth deceptive conduct. However, they argue that the indictment fails to properly allege either a scheme to defraud or intent to defraud. They claim the indictment simply accuses them of intending to deprive their followers of valuable economic data (rather than money or property), for self-enrichment (as opposed to injuring their followers). After considering the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reach a contrary conclusion.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to allege a scheme to deprive their followers of a property interest protected by law. Although it is correct that a person cannot be found guilty of fraud solely for withholding valuable economic information, the indictment at issue here alleges more, specifically that defendants schemed to defraud their followers out of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a fraud conviction cannot rest exclusively on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm hired a lobbyist for assistance in securing government-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution’s wire fraud charge relied entirely on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory posited that the government could “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. According to the right-to-control theory, the purported property interest is “the right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes only safeguard classic property interests, and since “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the right-to-control approach cannot sustain a fraud conviction. Id. at 316.

The present case is distinct from Ciminelli. The indictment does not invoke the right-to-control theory; instead, it asserts that the defendants, through misrepresentations, persuaded their followers to buy securities—thus, to relinquish money or property. According to the indictment, this constitutes a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” under which an individual “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court, when addressing the fraudulent-inducement theory, made clear that this doctrine “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory dismissed in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory applied here “protects money and property.” Id. Consequently, the indictment does not violate Ciminelli, as it alleges that the defendants used fraudulent inducement to deprive their followers of a protected property interest by causing them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting instead that it charges defendants with seeking personal profit rather than causing harm to any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable argument, holding that under the relevant fraud statutes, it is not necessary that the scheme cause an economic loss to the victim. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, Kousisis misrepresented his company’s compliance with a state requirement to subcontract with a disadvantaged business in order to secure state transportation contracts. His company, after winning the contracts, used the purported subcontractor only as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When this was discovered, the government charged Kousisis based on a “fraudulent-inducement theory”—specifically, that fraud involves a defendant using a significant misrepresentation to mislead the victim into entering a contract that obligates the transfer of money or property, “regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis maintained that such a theory should not apply unless it is shown that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that fraud statutes are violated when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. 

Here, the indictment adequately pleads defendants’ fraudulent intent toward their followers. For instance, it claims the defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the relevant statutes do not require proof of actual economic injury, the fact that the victims received securities at market value does not defeat the fraud allegations. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding that a defendant can be guilty of fraud even if he gives something—such as money, property, or services—of equivalent value in exchange). Defendants insist that their motive was self-enrichment rather than to damage their victims. This, however, is immaterial in this context. The indictment specifically alleges that defendants’ goal was to obtain money by means of fraudulently convincing followers to buy securities. Their alleged “pump and dump” actions designed to yield profit plainly show that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses,” no matter whether the victim is ultimately left with a net financial loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. By alleging that defendants deceitfully induced their followers to make purchases, the indictment sufficiently pleads an actionable injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that the fraud statutes do not require a one-to-one correspondence between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis to a similar factual scenario).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
