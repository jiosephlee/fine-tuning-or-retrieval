Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud arising from their participation in a "pump and dump" operation. According to the superseding indictment, defendants used social media posts containing false information regarding their trading activity and the projected value of certain securities, thereby encouraging followers to buy these securities. This conduct allegedly caused the prices to be artificially inflated, permitting the defendants to obtain profits. The district court determined that the indictment did not articulate an offense, reasoning that the allegations were limited to depriving victims of valuable economic information rather than of conventional property rights, and dismissed the charges. Finding that the indictment adequately sets forth a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a securities “pump and dump” scheme. Each defendant maintained a substantial following on various social media outlets and represented themselves as accomplished stock traders, regularly sharing details of their trading activity online. According to the government, the defendants’ scheme involved purchasing a security, then “‘pumping’ its price by disseminating false and misleading statements about the security on [social media], thereby inducing other investors to buy and causing the price to inflate artificially,” followed by “dumping” the security through concealed sales at a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts the defendants amassed $114 million in profits from this activity. Charges brought against the defendants included conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and multiple counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed motions to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the motions, finding the indictment insufficient because it did not allege that the defendants intended to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We conduct a de novo examination of whether an indictment is sufficient. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). “[T]he court must accept the indictment's factual allegations as true and decide if the indictment establishes a criminal offense.” United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). For an indictment to be valid, it must “(1) include each essential element of the crime charged; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the allegations against him; and (3) allow the defendant to invoke protection against double jeopardy in later prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). “In essence, the sufficiency of an indictment depends on whether all necessary elements of the crime are alleged.” United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute makes it unlawful to execute schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is little precedent interpreting the securities fraud statute in this circuit, § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” leading courts to treat its provisions in a similar manner and frequently consult mail and wire fraud decisions when evaluating securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Based on these principles, a securities fraud offense must establish both (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the presence of an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. For the “scheme to defraud” requirement, there must be a material misrepresentation made with the aim of deceiving others for the purpose of acquiring something of value, such as money, from the intended target. Id. (alteration in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” prong calls for “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In summary, securities fraud occurs when a defendant has “engaged in deception” and the scheme was designed so that “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants accept that the indictment properly alleges deception, but argue it fails to plead either a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. Specifically, they maintain that the indictment only accuses them of seeking to deprive their followers of valuable economic information—rather than money or property—for personal enrichment rather than for injuring their followers. Taking into account the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we disagree with Defendants’ assertions.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to assert a scheme aimed at depriving their followers of a legally protected property right. Although the mere deprivation of potentially valuable economic information does not, by itself, constitute fraud, in this case the indictment alleges more; specifically, it sufficiently claims that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest exclusively on the loss of significant economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm provided payments to a lobbyist to secure state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution, pursuing wire fraud charges against Ciminelli, relied exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the government sought to prove wire fraud by demonstrating that the defendant devised a scheme to withhold from the victim economic information critical to making informed discretionary decisions. Id. at 310. Stated differently, under the right-to-control approach, the purported property interest was the “right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that the federal fraud statutes only safeguard conventional property rights and that, because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” reliance on the right-to-control theory cannot support a conviction for fraud. Id. at 316.

The circumstances in this matter differ from Ciminelli. The indictment here makes no reference to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it charges that the defendants, by making misrepresentations, persuaded their followers to acquire securities—essentially causing them to part with money or property. The indictment relies on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which holds that a defendant employs material misstatements to deceive a victim into entering a contract that obligates relinquishing her money or property. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When considering the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that this approach “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory, which was rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory presented here “protects money and property.” Id. Thus, the indictment does not conflict with Ciminelli because it asserts that defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities, thereby depriving them of a recognized property interest.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud; they assert that the indictment claims the defendants acted to secure profit, rather than to cause harm to any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed this type of reasoning, determining that the federal fraud statutes do not require that a victim incur economic loss as a result of a defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the accused individual misrepresented facts in order to secure contracts with a state department of transportation. State regulations mandated contractors to enter into subcontracts with a disadvantaged business. Kousisis fraudulently stated that his company would adhere to that stipulation, but after obtaining the contracts, merely used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. Upon discovery of this deception, the government prosecuted Kousisis under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which posits that a defendant can be found guilty if he uses material misrepresentation to deceive a victim into a contract that necessitates transferring her money or property—regardless of whether the wrongdoer, who may provide something of value in return, intends to inflict a net financial loss on the victim. Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contended that this theory was invalid because it could result in conviction absent proof the defendant aimed to injure the victim’s finances. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this position and held that a fraud statute violation occurs when the defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. 

Here, the indictment sufficiently charges the defendants with an intent to defraud their followers. For instance, it asserts that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not necessitate an actual financial loss, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not eliminate the possibility of fraud. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding a defendant may be criminally liable for fraud even where he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). The defendants maintain that their intention was to obtain profit, not to harm anyone; however, this difference is not legally significant in this context. As the indictment outlines, defendants’ objective was to obtain money, accomplished by fraudulently inducing their followers into purchasing securities. The claim that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme for profit demonstrates that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—regardless of any net economic loss to the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. Therefore, the indictment’s allegation that the defendants fraudulently induced their followers to acquire securities adequately asserts an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (holding the fraud statutes do not require the defendant’s gain to be the “mirror image” of the victim’s loss); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying the Kousisis decision in a comparable scenario).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we VACATE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
