Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud based on their participation in a "pump and dump" operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants prompted their followers on social media to buy securities by making false statements about their own trading activity and about the likely future price of the securities. This conduct allegedly led to artificial price inflation, which enabled the defendants to realize profits. The district court dismissed these charges, finding that the indictment did not establish an offense, since it alleged only that the defendants deprived their followers of economically valuable information, rather than a conventional property interest. However, because we find that the indictment adequately pleads both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a plan to execute a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the government, the defendants all maintained substantial followings on several social media platforms, presented themselves as experienced stock traders, and regularly shared their trading activity online. The government contends that in furtherance of their scheme, the defendants purchased securities and then “‘pump[ed]’ the price of those securities by disseminating false and misleading statements about them on [social media], thereby inducing other investors to purchase and thus artificially inflate the price,” before “dump[ing]” the securities through undisclosed profitable sales. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment alleges defendants obtained $114 million in proceeds from this activity. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as several counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants moved to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted dismissal, reasoning that the indictment did not adequately allege a scheme to deprive victims of a conventional property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We conduct a de novo review to determine whether an indictment is sufficient. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment’s assertions as true, and evaluate if it alleges an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To be adequate, an indictment should: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Thus, a sufficient indictment must assert every material element of the alleged crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids schemes designed “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as plans “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” the provisions of § 1348 “borrow key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and courts thus “have given the terms similar treatment,” frequently referring to mail and wire fraud precedents when assessing securities fraud claims. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Considering these principles, a conviction for securities fraud necessitates: (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) execution of that scheme with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The requirement of a “scheme to defraud” involves a material false statement or misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” criterion demands “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In essence, securities fraud occurs if a defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and the pursuit of “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently asserts deception. Nevertheless, they argue that the indictment fails to plead either a scheme to defraud or the requisite intent to defraud. Their position is that the indictment alleges only that they intended to withhold valuable economic information from their followers (not money or property) to enrich themselves (rather than harm the followers). In view of the Supreme Court’s opinions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject this argument.

A

The defendants contend that the district court’s dismissal of the indictment was proper because it failed to allege any scheme depriving followers of a recognized property right. Although a person cannot be found guilty of fraud merely for withholding potentially advantageous economic information from someone, the present indictment went further by appropriately asserting that the defendants schemed to defraud their followers out of money.

The Supreme Court in Ciminelli determined that a fraud conviction cannot rest solely on depriving another of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist to secure jobs financed by the state. Id. at 309–10. Prosecutors based their wire fraud case exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. Under this approach, the government attempted to establish wire fraud by claiming that the defendant sought to deny the victim crucial economic information essential to discretionary economic decisions. Id. at 310. That is, in the right-to-control theory, the “property” in question is “the right to control [the victim’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court explained that federal fraud statutes only shield established property interests and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest”; thus, “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

The present matter is distinguishable from Ciminelli. The right-to-control theory is not invoked anywhere in the indictment. Instead, the indictment asserts that defendants deceived their followers via misrepresentations to get them to purchase securities—in effect, to relinquish their money or property. The indictment advances a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” where a defendant uses a material misstatement to induce a victim into an agreement involving the transfer of money or property. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When the Supreme Court examined the fraudulent-inducement theory, it affirmed that the theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control rationale dismissed in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory asserted here “protects money and property.” Id. As such, the indictment is consistent with Ciminelli because it alleges that the defendants fraudulently caused their followers to part with a protected property interest by inducing them to invest in securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege a specific intent to defraud, but merely asserts that defendants sought to benefit financially from their conduct, rather than cause loss to any particular victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently addressed and dismissed a comparable contention, finding that the federal fraud statutes do not necessitate that a defendant’s conduct result in actual economic harm to a victim. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that matter, Kousisis had provided false statements to secure contracts with a state transportation agency, which mandated engagement of a disadvantaged business as a subcontractor. Kousisis misrepresented his intent to honor that requirement, and after receiving the contracts, he used the subcontractor merely as a nominal participant. Id. at 1389. Upon discovery of his misrepresentation, the government charged Kousisis under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contended that such a theory was invalid absent proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this line of reasoning, holding that liability attaches under the fraud statutes when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

In this case, the indictment sufficiently asserts that defendants intended to defraud their followers, including by alleging that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Given that the fraud statutes do not demand proof of actual economic injury, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not negate the validity of the fraud allegations. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding fraud liability can arise even when the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants claim their intent was to profit rather than inflict harm; however, for present purposes, this distinction is immaterial. The indictment charges that defendants’ purpose was to obtain money through deceit by enticing their followers into purchasing securities. By alleging a pump-and-dump scheme aimed at profit, the indictment indicates that defendants “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—and this holds true whether or not the victim suffers a net loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s assertion that defendants induced their followers into purchasing securities through fraudulent means is adequate to plead injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” where the victim’s loss directly corresponds to the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in an analogous factual setting).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
