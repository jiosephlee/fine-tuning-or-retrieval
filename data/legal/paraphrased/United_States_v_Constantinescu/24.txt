Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud based on their alleged participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants persuaded their followers on social media to buy certain securities by making posts that misrepresented both their own trading activities and the possible future value of those securities, which inflated the market price and enabled the defendants to benefit financially. The district court dismissed these charges, reasoning that the indictment only claimed that the defendants tried to deprive their followers of economically useful information, and not a traditional form of property. We hold, however, that the indictment properly asserts both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, and therefore we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a securities “pump and dump” scheme. Each defendant maintained substantial followings on various social media sites. They portrayed themselves as expert stock traders and regularly shared their trading actions online. According to the government, the defendants’ scheme entailed purchasing a security, “‘pumping’ the security’s price by disseminating false or misleading statements regarding that security on [social media], thereby encouraging other investors to buy and artificially inflate its price,” followed by “dumping” their own holdings covertly to secure profits. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts that the defendants gained $114 million in profits through this conduct. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud in contravention of 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and various securities fraud counts under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought to have the indictment dismissed. The district court granted the motion, holding that the indictment did not allege a scheme to deprive victims of a conventional property interest. The government filed a timely appeal from this decision.

II

We conduct a de novo review of whether an indictment is sufficient. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court “must treat the allegations in the indictment as true and decide if they constitute an offense.” United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment must “(1) list every prima facie element of the alleged offense; (2) properly notify the defendant of the charges brought; and (3) afford the defendant protection against double jeopardy in subsequent proceedings.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). “Ultimately, an indictment is adequate if it alleges each essential element of the charged crime.” United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The statute addressing securities fraud outlaws both schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” and plans “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” which has led courts to interpret its terms in a similar fashion, frequently drawing on mail and wire fraud precedents when assessing securities fraud cases. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Accordingly, a violation of securities fraud consists of (1) the presence of a “scheme to defraud” (2) carried out with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The requirement for a “scheme to defraud” involves making a material misrepresentation that is “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (modification in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” requirement entails “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (citing Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). To summarize, a person is guilty of securities fraud if he or she “‘engaged in deception’” and “the obtaining of the victim’s money or property” was “an object of the fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently alleges deceptive conduct. However, they assert that the indictment falls short of alleging either a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. According to defendants, the indictment only claims they sought to deprive their followers of valuable economic information (rather than money or property) for their personal gain (not to injure their followers). Considering the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we disagree with defendants’ argument.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the basis that it fails to assert a plan to deprive their followers of any legally protected property interest. Although it is accurate that fraud convictions cannot rest solely on the deprivation of merely potentially valuable economic information, the present indictment alleged more than that, sufficiently claiming that the defendants executed a scheme aimed at defrauding their followers of actual money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest exclusively on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist in exchange for assistance in securing government-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution charged Ciminelli with wire fraud based only on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the prosecution sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. In essence, the right-to-control theory defines the relevant property as “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that the federal fraud statutes only protect conventional property rights, and because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” convictions cannot be based on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 316.

The circumstances of the present case are distinct from Ciminelli. The indictment makes no reference to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that defendants, by means of false representations, persuaded their followers to acquire securities—in other words, to surrender their money or property. The indictment articulates a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In examining the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court expressly stated that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the disapproved right-to-control theory in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory pursued in this case “protects money and property.” Id. Therefore, the indictment does not conflict with Ciminelli, since it alleges that defendants fraudulently deprived their followers of a protected property interest by inducing the purchase of securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting instead that the indictment claims defendants aimed to benefit themselves without harming any specific victim. The Supreme Court has recently dismissed such an argument, ruling that the fraud statutes do not require a victim to incur financial loss as a consequence of the defendant's conduct. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant misrepresented information in order to secure contracts with a state department of transportation, which required contractors to use a subcontractor classified as a disadvantaged business. Kousisis falsely claimed compliance with this requirement but only used the designated subcontractor as a superficial “pass-through” company. Id. at 1389. When the deceit was uncovered, Kousisis was prosecuted under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which posits that a defendant who utilizes a significant falsehood to deceive someone into a contract that transfers money or property—regardless of whether the defendant, who may provide something in exchange, intends to cause an overall financial loss to the victim—can be convicted of fraud. Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis maintained that the fraudulent-inducement doctrine was invalid because it enabled a conviction without proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, concluding that the fraud statutes are breached when a defendant attempts to “‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’” irrespective of whether the defendant aims to leave the victim at a financial disadvantage. Id. at 1392.

In the present case, the indictment properly sets out defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. Specifically, it claims defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not mandate proof of actual financial loss, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities valued at market rates does not negate the charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding that a fraud conviction can be sustained even if the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants contend their motive was profit rather than victim harm. However, in this instance, that distinction is inconsequential. The indictment avers that defendants’ goal was to obtain money, which they pursued by misleading their followers into purchasing securities. The allegations that defendants manipulated and rapidly sold securities for profit establish that they were “motivated by money.” Id. at 1397. “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—even if no net financial loss occurs to the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment's claim that defendants fraudulently prompted their followers to purchase securities meets the requirement to allege injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (stating the fraud statutes do not require a strict correlation between victim’s loss and defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (using Kousisis in an analogous context).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court's decision to dismiss the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
