Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud in connection with their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. The amended indictment asserts that defendants persuaded their social-media followers to buy securities by posting false statements concerning their own holdings and the projected prices of certain securities, thereby inflating the prices and enabling the defendants to profit. The district court found that the indictment merely claimed the defendants deprived followers of possible advantageous economic facts, rather than of a traditional property right, and on that basis dismissed the indictment for failure to state an offense. Since we determine that the indictment properly alleges both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

According to the government, Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a fraudulent scheme commonly referred to as a “pump and dump” involving securities. Each defendant maintained a significant following on multiple social media platforms and represented themselves as expert stock traders, often sharing their trading activities online. The government contends that, in executing their scheme, defendants would acquire shares of a particular security, artificially “pump” its price by disseminating false or misleading information about the security via social media to induce additional investors to buy and inflate the price, and then “dump” their shares by secretly selling them to realize profits. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts that defendants collectively earned $114 million as a result of the alleged scheme. Defendants faced charges including conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Daniel Knight entered a guilty plea, while the remaining defendants sought to have the indictment dismissed. The district court granted dismissal, finding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege an intent to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government subsequently filed a timely appeal.

II

The adequacy of an indictment is subject to de novo review. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). In conducting this review, the court assumes the indictment’s allegations are true and evaluates whether they establish a criminal offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To satisfy legal requirements, the indictment must (1) list each essential element that constitutes a prima facie case of the alleged crime; (2) sufficiently notify the accused of the nature of the charges; and (3) afford protection against being tried again for the same offense by enabling a double jeopardy defense. United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Thus, an indictment is legally sufficient only if it charges every material element of the offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute outlaws plans to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as efforts “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” See U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is minimal precedent interpreting the securities fraud statute within this circuit, § 1348 incorporates essential concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes; consequently, “courts have given the terms similar treatment” and have frequently relied upon mail and wire fraud authorities when assessing securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). With those parallels in mind, securities fraud consists of (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. Satisfying the “scheme to defraud” prong requires a material misstatement “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). For the “intent to defraud” requirement, there must be “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Thus, securities fraud is established when a defendant has “‘engaged in deception’” and “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” served as “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently pleads deception. However, they argue that the indictment does not adequately allege either a scheme or an intent to defraud. Specifically, Defendants maintain that the indictment merely asserts an intent to deny their followers access to valuable economic information (as opposed to money or property), and that their aim was to profit themselves, not harm their followers. We reject this argument in view of the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it failed to assert a scheme to deprive their followers of any recognized property right. Although it is correct that fraud does not encompass depriving someone solely of valuable economic information, the indictment in this case additionally and adequately charged the defendants with scheming to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist to secure contracts funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The government prosecuted Ciminelli for wire fraud, relying exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. That theory sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Essentially, under the right-to-control approach, the asset at issue is the victim’s “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court found that federal fraud statutes safeguard only conventional property interests, and because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” a “right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The present circumstances are distinct from those in Ciminelli. The indictment here does not refer to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that defendants persuaded their followers, through false representations, to purchase securities—causing them to relinquish money or property. The indictment adopts a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” in which the defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When examining the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court stated that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control approach that the Court rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory put forth here “protects money and property.” Id. Because the indictment asserts that the defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities and thereby deprived them of a protected property interest, it does not contravene the holding in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants subsequently contend that the indictment is deficient for failing to plead an intent to defraud, asserting that it only claims defendants sought profit rather than intended to injure any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed a comparable contention, ruling that fraud statutes do not mandate that a victim incur an economic loss because of a defendant's actions. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant made false statements to secure contracts with a state transportation department, which required contractors to subcontract with disadvantaged businesses. Kousisis misrepresented compliance, but used the disadvantaged-business entity merely as a “pass-through.” Id. at 1389. After the deception was discovered, Kousisis was charged under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” where liability arises if a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued this theory failed because it would permit conviction absent evidence the defendant aimed to damage the victim financially. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, concluding that fraud is established where a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. 

In the present case, the indictment adequately alleges defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. It specifies, for example, that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not necessitate proof of actual financial harm, the fact that defendants’ followers may have received securities at market value does not negate the fraud allegations. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding a defendant can be guilty of fraud even where he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants claim their objective was personal gain, not to inflict harm; however, under the circumstances, this distinction is immaterial. As alleged in the indictment, defendants’ purpose was to secure money by fraudulently enticing their followers to buy securities. The charge that defendants executed a pump-and-dump for profit indicates they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. A “fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—whether or not the victim ultimately sustains a net monetary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment's charge that defendants fraudulently caused their followers to buy securities sufficiently sets forth the requisite injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (interpreting Kousisis in a like factual scenario).

IV

Since the indictment adequately asserts both a scheme and the intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in line with this opinion.
