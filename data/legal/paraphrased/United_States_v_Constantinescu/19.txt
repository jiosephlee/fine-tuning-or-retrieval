Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud related to their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants encouraged their followers on social media to buy securities by publishing posts that misrepresented their own trading activity and the expected value of the securities. This misinformation artificially boosted security prices and enabled the defendants to secure illicit gains. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the charges merely claimed the defendants deprived their followers of potential access to valuable economic information rather than a conventional property interest. However, as we find the indictment adequately pleads both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government accuses Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight of executing a "pump and dump" securities scheme. Each defendant possessed a substantial social media audience spanning different platforms. They portrayed themselves as experienced stock traders, routinely sharing their trading activity online. The government contends that their scheme involved acquiring a security, subsequently “pumping” its price by disseminating “false and misleading information about the security on [social media],” thereby enticing other investors to purchase and artificially inflate the price, and then secretly “dumping” the security by selling it at a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. According to the indictment, the defendants collectively earned $114 million from this conduct. Charges brought against the defendants include conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, along with multiple securities fraud counts under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought to have the indictment dismissed. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, determining that the indictment did not adequately allege an intent to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government appealed the decision in a timely manner.

II

We assess whether an indictment is sufficient using a de novo standard. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept as true the indictment's allegations and decide if an offense is properly alleged. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). For an indictment to be adequate, it must: (1) specify each essential element that constitutes the charged offense; (2) clearly notify the defendant about the charges against him; and (3) enable the defendant to assert double jeopardy should future prosecutions arise. United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Therefore, an indictment’s sufficiency depends on whether it alleges every material element of the charged offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids engaging in schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as schemes designed “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” such that “courts have given the terms similar treatment,” and routinely reference mail and wire fraud cases when interpreting securities fraud charges. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Considering these principles, a conviction for securities fraud requires (1) a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) action taken with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” prong involves a material misrepresentation intended “to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” requirement means there must be “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In short, a securities fraud offense is committed when a defendant has “engaged in deception” and securing “the victim’s money or property” served as “an object” of that fraud. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants do not dispute that the indictment sufficiently alleges deceptive conduct. However, they maintain that the indictment fails to state a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. According to their argument, the indictment only asserts that their goal was to deprive followers of valuable economic information (rather than money or property) for the purpose of self-enrichment (rather than harming their followers). Taking into account the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject this argument.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment, asserting that it fails to charge any scheme intended to divest their followers of a legally protected property interest. Although deprivation of merely potentially valuable economic information does not suffice to support a conviction for fraud, the indictment in this case went beyond such allegations and appropriately charged that the defendants engaged in a scheme to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot be based exclusively on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli's construction company provided payments to a lobbyist in order to secure jobs funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. When prosecuting Ciminelli for wire fraud, the government depended solely upon the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory permits the government to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Essentially, the property at issue according to the right-to-control theory is “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that because federal fraud statutes only safeguard conventional property interests, and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the right-to-control theory cannot underpin a fraud conviction. Id. at 316. The present matter differs from Ciminelli. The indictment contains no reference to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that defendants misled their followers through false representations into purchasing securities—effectively, to give up their money or property. The indictment is grounded in a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court, in examining this fraudulent-inducement theory, made clear that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory asserted here “protects money and property.” Id. Since the indictment alleges that defendants deprived their followers of a protected property interest by fraudulently inducing the purchase of securities, it does not violate Ciminelli.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient for not alleging an intent to defraud, asserting that it accuses them of seeking personal gain rather than intending harm to victims. However, the Supreme Court has recently rejected a comparable position, determining that fraud statutes do not mandate that a victim experience an economic loss as a consequence of a defendant's actions. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, Kousisis misrepresented his compliance with a state transportation department’s requirement for contractors to hire disadvantaged businesses, promising to do so but then merely using the subcontractor as a “pass-through” entity after securing the contracts. Id. at 1389. When authorities discovered this deception, Kousisis was prosecuted under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” wherein a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued that this theory was invalid since it allowed for conviction even if there was no intent to diminish the victim's financial standing. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court dismissed this line of argument, ruling that a defendant contravenes fraud statutes by “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

Here, the indictment adequately pleads defendants’ intent to defraud, including the allegation that they “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not require proof of actual economic loss, it is immaterial that defendants’ followers received securities valued at the market rate; such facts do not negate the fraud allegations. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding a defendant can commit fraud even by providing something—such as money, property, or services—of equivalent value in exchange). While defendants insist their motive was self-enrichment rather than to injure any victim, the distinction is irrelevant in this context. As stated in the indictment, the goal was to obtain money, achieved through deceitfully inducing their followers into purchasing securities. Allegations that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme to generate profits make clear that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—it is not necessary that the victim have experienced a net economic loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s description of how defendants fraudulently induced their followers to buy securities is enough to establish injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that the fraud statutes do not require that every loss to a victim matches a defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in a related situation).

IV

As the indictment properly sets forth a scheme as well as intent to defraud, we VACATE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
