Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud stemming from participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants persuaded their social media audience to buy certain securities by publishing posts that contained false statements about their own holdings and misleading information about the securities’ future value, resulting in an artificial increase in market prices from which the defendants benefited financially. The district court ruled in favor of dismissing the indictment, finding that the allegations merely established an attempt to withhold potentially lucrative economic details from followers, rather than implicating a conventional property right, and therefore did not state an offense. However, because we find that the indictment adequately alleges both a fraudulent scheme and fraudulent intent, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” scheme involving securities. According to the government, each defendant maintained a substantial presence on social media across different platforms and portrayed themselves as expert stock traders, regularly sharing their trading behavior online. As described by the government, the defendants executed the “pump and dump” by purchasing securities and then inflating (“pumping”) their prices through the dissemination of materially false or misleading information on social media, with the intent that this would induce other investors to buy, thereby artificially boosting the security’s price. Subsequently, the defendants would sell (“dump”) their holdings covertly to make a profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. It is further alleged in the indictment that the defendants realized profits totaling $114 million from this course of conduct. Charges against defendants included conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted dismissal on the basis that the indictment did not sufficiently allege that the defendants’ scheme intended to deprive victims of any recognized property right. The government filed a timely appeal of this decision.

II

We examine the adequacy of an indictment under a de novo standard. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment's allegations as true and assess whether they set forth a criminal offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). For an indictment to be valid, it must “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In short, a sufficient indictment must charge every essential element of the crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids engaging in schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as using “false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises” to acquire “any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” See U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “incorporates important concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” resulting in courts often interpreting these terms similarly and frequently referencing mail and wire fraud case law when assessing securities fraud cases. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Based on these principles, a prosecution for securities fraud necessitates (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) that the defendant acted with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” prong calls for a material misrepresentation “made to deceive others with the objective of obtaining something of value, such as money, from the [entity] being misled.” Id. (modification in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” element demands “the intent both to deceive and to bring about some harm as a consequence of the deception.” Id. (citing Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In essence, a defendant is guilty of securities fraud when the defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and acquiring the victim’s “money or property” served as “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants accept that the indictment sufficiently alleges deceptive conduct. However, they argue it does not allege either a scheme or an intent to defraud. They claim that the indictment merely charges an intention to deprive their followers of valuable economic information (but not of money or property), for their own benefit (rather than to harm their followers). We find, particularly in view of the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, that this argument lacks merit.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it failed to charge a scheme to deny their followers a legally protected property interest. Although it is accurate that fraud cannot be established solely on the basis of depriving someone of potentially valuable financial information, the indictment in this case alleged more, sufficiently claiming that defendants engaged in a scheme to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of access to valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm had compensated a lobbyist to assist in securing government-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. For its wire fraud prosecution, the government proceeded entirely on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this approach, the government sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. In essence, the right-to-control theory posits that the asset at issue is “the right to control [one's] assets.” Id. at 311. The Court ruled that federal fraud statutes extend only to conventional property interests, and because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

The circumstances in the present matter differ from those in Ciminelli. The indictment here does not rely on or refer to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that the defendants persuaded their followers, through false statements, to acquire securities—that is, to give up money or property. The indictment puts forward a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” wherein the accused “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When considering this fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory asserted in this case “protects money and property.” Id. Therefore, because the indictment alleges the defendants fraudulently caused their followers to relinquish a protected property interest through the purchase of securities, it does not violate the rule established in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud; instead, it claims the defendants sought to profit from their actions without aiming to harm any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed a comparable argument, concluding that the relevant fraud statutes do not mandate that a victim incur financial harm due to a defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In the Kousisis case, the defendant had misrepresented facts to secure contracts from a state department of transportation that required subcontracting to a disadvantaged business, and falsely stated that his company would satisfy that requirement. After being awarded the contracts, Kousisis utilized the disadvantaged-business subcontractor solely as a “mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When this falsehood emerged, the government prosecuted Kousisis based on the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” in which a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued against the fraudulent-inducement theory, contending it allowed conviction even without proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, determining that the fraud statutes are violated when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The present indictment adequately sets forth defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. It specifies, for instance, that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not necessitate actual economic loss, the fact that defendants’ followers acquired securities at market value does not negate the fraud allegations. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding that fraud liability may exist even if the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Although defendants assert that their intention was to profit rather than to harm any victim, that distinction holds no significance under these circumstances. As alleged in the indictment, the defendants’ objective was to obtain money by deceitfully prompting their followers to purchase securities. That the indictment charges defendants with engaging in a “pump and dump” scheme to secure profits underscores that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—even if the victim does not experience a net monetary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s claim that defendants used fraudulent means to cause their followers to purchase securities sufficiently pleads an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that the fraud statutes do not create a “mirror image” requirement tying the victim’s loss directly to the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying the reasoning in Kousisis in a related context).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth a scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court's order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the matter for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
