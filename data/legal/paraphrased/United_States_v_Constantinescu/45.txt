Case Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket Number: 24-20143  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Kurt D. Engelhardt  

The defendants were charged with securities fraud based on their participation in a so-called "pump and dump" operation. According to the superseding indictment, defendants persuaded their social-media followers to buy securities by issuing posts that falsely represented both their own trading interests and the potential price performance of those securities, thereby causing an artificial rise in market price and enabling themselves to earn profits. The district court ruled to dismiss the indictment, finding that it did not allege a prosecutable offense since it claimed only that defendants deprived their followers of potentially important economic information, rather than of a more traditional property right. However, after reviewing the allegations, we find the indictment adequately sets out a fraudulent scheme and requisite intent to defraud; therefore, we REVERSE the dismissal by the district court.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a plan to manipulate securities prices through a “pump and dump” scheme. According to the allegations, each defendant amassed substantial audiences on several social media platforms. They presented themselves as expert stock traders and routinely shared details of their trades online. To execute the purported “pump and dump” scheme, the government asserts, the defendants would first acquire a security, then “‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” before “dumping” their holdings by discreetly selling them for financial gain. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further claims that the defendants reaped $114 million in profits from these actions. The charges brought include conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and several counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the remaining defendants filed motions to dismiss. The district court granted dismissal, determining the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme intended to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We assess the adequacy of an indictment using a de novo standard of review. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment’s factual assertions as true and decide whether they establish the commission of an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). The indictment is required to: “(1) list every element necessary to make out a prima facie case for the crime charged; (2) adequately advise the defendant about the accusations he faces; and (3) afford protection from repeated prosecutions on double jeopardy grounds.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Thus, an indictment is only sufficient if it sets forth each essential element of the charged crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute makes it unlawful to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as to “obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is limited precedent interpreting the securities fraud statute within this circuit, § 1348 draws from foundational principles underlying the mail and wire fraud statutes. Courts have, therefore, commonly analyzed the securities fraud statute in parallel with mail and wire fraud, often consulting those bodies of case law when addressing securities fraud prosecutions. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Using this framework, securities fraud consists of two primary elements: (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the perpetrator’s “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The first element—the “scheme to defraud”—requires proof of a material misstatement or omission designed to deceive someone in order to wrongfully obtain something valuable, such as money, from the targeted entity. Id. (alteration in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The second element—“intent to defraud”—necessitates an intent both “to deceive” and to produce “some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Collectively, the offense is established if a defendant has “engaged in deception” and the act of “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of the fraudulent conduct. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, the defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently pleads deceptive conduct. Nonetheless, they argue the indictment fails to allege a scheme to defraud or an accompanying intent to defraud. Specifically, they maintain that the indictment only asserts that their goal was to withhold valuable economic information from their followers (which does not equate to money or property) to enrich themselves (without the aim of causing injury to their followers). Based on the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we conclude otherwise.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the basis that it fails to claim a scheme intended to deprive their followers of a legally protected property right. Although depriving another of mere potentially valuable economic information, by itself, does not support a fraud conviction, in this case, the indictment contains additional allegations—namely, that defendants schemed to defraud their followers out of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the withholding of important economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm gave payments to a lobbyist in order to secure jobs funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution charged Ciminelli with wire fraud based exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory allows the government to “prove wire fraud by demonstrating the defendant schemed to deny the victim information of economic value necessary for making discretionary financial choices.” Id. at 310. Put differently, under the right-to-control approach, the victim’s property is identified as “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that since the federal fraud statutes only protect traditional property rights, and because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . does not constitute a traditional property interest,” the “right-to-control theory cannot provide grounds for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

This case differs materially from Ciminelli. The indictment in question does not refer to or rely upon the right-to-control theory. Instead, it contends that the defendants made false statements to persuade their followers to buy securities, effectively causing them to relinquish money or property. The indictment proceeds on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” under which a defendant “employs a significant misrepresentation to deceive a victim into entering a contract requiring the transfer of her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When evaluating the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that this theory “is not simply a rebranded version of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the right-to-control theory that the Court rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory alleged here “aims to protect money and property.” Id. Thus, the indictment does not violate Ciminelli because it asserts that the defendants fraudulently caused their followers to give up a legitimate property interest by purchasing securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient in alleging intent to defraud, asserting that it describes their intent as seeking profit from their scheme rather than causing harm to victims. However, this position was recently dismissed by the Supreme Court, which ruled that fraud statutes do not mandate that victims experience financial loss due to a defendant’s conduct. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant misrepresented facts to secure contracts from a state transportation department. The state’s contractual terms required contractors to subcontract with a disadvantaged business; Kousisis falsely claimed that his company would satisfy this mandate. After securing the contracts, Kousisis employed the disadvantaged-business subcontractor merely as a “pass-through entity.” Id. at 1389. When the fraudulent conduct was revealed, authorities charged Kousisis based on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” under which a defendant uses a significant misrepresentation to mislead a victim into entering a contract requiring the transfer of money or property, “regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued that this theory permitted conviction absent proof “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Court rejected this notion, holding that fraud statutes are violated when the defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. The present indictment sufficiently alleges that defendants intended to defraud their followers. For example, it states that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since actual economic loss to victims is not required by the fraud statutes, the fact that followers received securities at fair market value does not negate the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding defendants may be found guilty of fraud even when providing something—money, property, or services—of equal value). Although defendants claim they were motivated by profit rather than intent to harm, in this context, the distinction is immaterial. According to the indictment, defendants’ purpose was to acquire money by fraudulently convincing their followers to buy securities. That the indictment asserts defendants executed a pump-and-dump scheme for financial gain is sufficient to show they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses,” regardless of any pecuniary loss to the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s allegation that defendants deceived their followers into purchasing securities suffices to allege harm. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting fraud statutes do not require a direct correspondence between victim loss and defendant gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in a comparable case).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme to defraud and the requisite intent, we VACATE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and RETURN the case for further proceedings in line with this opinion.
