Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-20143  
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

Defendants faced charges of securities fraud based on their participation in a "pump and dump" operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants persuaded followers on social media to buy securities by posting false statements about their own trading activity and the expected price movement, thereby driving up the value of those securities and securing a profit for themselves. The district court ruled to dismiss the indictment, finding it insufficient because it claimed the defendants intended only to withhold possibly useful economic facts from their followers, rather than to deprive them of a tangible property right. As we determine that the indictment properly alleges both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court's dismissal of the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight were involved in a scheme known as a “pump and dump” involving securities. Each defendant maintained substantial followings on social media platforms and presented themselves as experienced stock traders, regularly sharing details about their trades online. According to the government, the defendants executed their “pump and dump” scheme by purchasing securities, then “pumping” up the price by disseminating false or misleading information about those securities on social media, thereby inducing other investors to buy and causing an artificial price increase. The defendants would then “dump”—covertly selling their holdings for a profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment alleges that this conduct resulted in profits of $114 million. The charges included conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed motions to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the motion, finding that the indictment did not adequately allege that the defendants sought to deprive victims of any conventional property right. The government appealed this decision in a timely manner.

II

We assess the adequacy of an indictment without deference, applying a de novo standard. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must “accept the allegations contained in the indictment as true and evaluate whether they constitute an offense.” United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To be sufficient, an indictment must: “(1) list each essential element of the crime charged; (2) adequately apprise the defendant of the nature of the accusation; and (3) enable the defendant to invoke the protection against double jeopardy in future proceedings.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). “Ultimately, an indictment is sufficient if it asserts every material element of the alleged offense.” United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute outlaws schemes intended “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as schemes designed “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]his circuit has little caselaw interpreting the securities fraud statute,” Section 1348 “incorporates essential principles from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” so “courts treat the language similarly,” frequently turning to precedent from mail and wire fraud cases to analyze securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Considering these principles, securities fraud consists of (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” prong involves a material misrepresentation “meant to deceive others so as to secure something valuable, such as money, from the [party] who is deceived.” Id. (modification in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The requirement of “intent to defraud” involves “an intention to (1) deceive and (2) produce harm as a result of that deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Summarizing, a person is guilty of securities fraud if he “engaged in deception” with the aim that “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of his fraudulent scheme. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants admit that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception. They argue, however, that the indictment fails to charge a scheme to defraud or the intent to defraud. According to defendants, the indictment states only that they meant to deprive their followers of important economic information (but not money or property), for their own enrichment (not to harm followers). In view of the Supreme Court’s opinions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject this argument.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to assert a scheme aimed at divesting their followers of a protected property right. Although the law does not permit a conviction for fraud merely for withholding potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in question goes beyond this; it specifically and sufficiently alleges that defendants intended to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot be grounded merely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist for assistance in securing state-financed contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution’s wire fraud charges against Ciminelli relied entirely on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the government sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. In essence, the right-to-control theory defines the victim’s property as “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court reasoned that federal fraud statutes safeguard only conventional property rights and concluded that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” rendering the right-to-control theory an insufficient basis for a fraud conviction. Id. at 316. The present matter is distinguishable from Ciminelli. The charging instrument does not refer to the right-to-control theory; instead, it accuses defendants of persuading their followers to invest in securities by making false statements—meaning the followers were induced to relinquish money or property. The indictment sets forth a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In its review of the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that this approach “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here “protects money and property.” Id. The indictment thus does not contravene Ciminelli, since it alleges that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to purchase securities, thereby depriving them of a property interest recognized as protected by law.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is deficient for not pleading intent to defraud, asserting that it only alleges defendants aimed to profit rather than to cause injury to any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently rejected such a contention, clarifying that the fraud statutes do not require a showing of economic loss to the victim stemming from the defendant's scheme. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant secured contracts with a state department of transportation by making false statements. Specifically, the state mandated that contractors engage a disadvantaged business as a subcontractor, and Kousisis falsely claimed his company would comply. After being awarded the contracts, Kousisis used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor merely as a "pass-through" entity. Id. at 1389. When the deception was discovered, the government prosecuted Kousisis under the "fraudulent-inducement theory," under which a defendant can be liable for “us[ing] a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—even if the fraudster, who sometimes provides something in exchange, is not intent on causing the victim a net financial loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued this theory was invalid because it allowed conviction without proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that the fraud statutes are violated when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. 

Here, the indictment properly alleges that defendants intended to defraud their followers. For instance, it states that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not demand proof of actual economic damage, the fact that the followers received securities at market value does not defeat the charges of fraud. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (“defendant may be liable in fraud even when he ‘provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return’”). Defendants claim their objective was to profit, not to injure any victim, but, as the indictment reflects, this is a distinction without consequence. The charging document asserts that the aim of defendants’ scheme was to acquire money, and that this was done by fraudulently inducing their followers to buy securities. By alleging that the defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump operation for profit, the indictment establishes that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. "[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—no matter whether the victim ends up suffering a net financial loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Accordingly, the indictment’s allegation that defendants fraudulently solicited their followers to purchase securities is sufficient to plead an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting fraud statutes do not require that the victim’s loss mirror the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (relying on Kousisis in a comparable setting).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth a scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we VACATE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in line with this opinion.
