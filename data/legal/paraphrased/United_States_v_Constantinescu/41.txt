Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-20143  
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud arising from their participation in a so-called “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, defendants posted misleading information to their social-media followers regarding both their own trading positions and expected securities prices, thereby artificially inflating prices for their own financial gain. The district court dismissed the indictment, finding that it did not properly allege an offense because it claimed only that defendants sought to deny followers access to valuable economic information rather than deprive them of a conventional property right. Since we determine that the indictment adequately sets forth a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. Each defendant maintained a substantial following on various social media platforms, presented themselves as experienced stock traders, and regularly shared information about their trading activities online. According to the government's allegations, the defendants would acquire a security, “‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media],” which allegedly induced other investors to purchase the security, thereby inflating its price artificially. Defendants then would “dump” by covertly selling their holdings at a profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment claims that the scheme generated $114 million in profits for the defendants. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, along with several counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed a motion to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the motion, finding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government appealed from that decision in a timely manner.

II

We evaluate whether an indictment is sufficient through a de novo standard of review. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must “accept the allegations in the indictment as true and assess whether they constitute an offense.” United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). For an indictment to satisfy legal requirements, it must “(1) list each prima facie element of the alleged crime; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the accusations against him; and (3) afford the defendant protection from double jeopardy in subsequent prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). “In short, a sufficient indictment must set forth each material element of the charged offense.” United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute makes it unlawful to engage in schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” or to use “false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises” to acquire “any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is limited precedent in this circuit specifically interpreting the securities fraud statute, § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes.” Accordingly, courts have generally construed the terms in a consistent manner, frequently referencing mail and wire fraud jurisprudence to assess securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying these principles, the offense of securities fraud consists of: (1) a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) execution of that scheme with an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. To satisfy the “scheme to defraud” prong, there must be a material misstatement “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” component requires showing “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Therefore, to establish securities fraud, it must be shown that a defendant “engaged in deception” and that “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of that fraudulent conduct. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants acknowledge that the indictment properly pleads deception. They argue, however, that it fails to allege a scheme or intent to defraud, asserting that it only claims they sought to withhold valuable economic information from their followers for their own benefit, rather than intending to injure their followers or deprive them of money or property. Considering the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we do not find their argument persuasive.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to claim any scheme targeting their followers’ protected property rights. Although merely withholding potentially profitable economic information does not suffice for a fraud conviction, the present indictment advances beyond that, adequately asserting that the defendants devised a plan to swindle their followers out of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud may not rest solely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that matter, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist to assist in securing jobs funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. When the government charged Ciminelli with wire fraud, it relied exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory posits that “wire fraud is established if the defendant engages in a scheme depriving a victim of valuable economic information required for discretionary economic choices.” Id. at 310. Essentially, according to the right-to-control theory, the property at stake for the victim is “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that because the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, and since “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the “right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The present case differs from Ciminelli. The indictment contains no reference to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that the defendants, by making misrepresentations, led their followers to invest in securities—that is, to relinquish money or property. The indictment rests on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” in which a defendant “employs a material falsehood to deceive a victim into entering a contract that obliges her to give up money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When the Supreme Court examined the fraudulent-inducement theory, it confirmed that this theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory that was rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here “protects money and property.” Id. Since the indictment charges that defendants fraudulently deprived their followers of a protected property interest by inducing them to purchase securities, it does not violate Ciminelli.

B

Defendants next contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not plead an intent to defraud, asserting that it accuses them of seeking personal gain rather than intending to harm a victim. However, the Supreme Court recently addressed and rejected this line of argument, holding that the fraud statutes do not necessitate that a victim experience financial loss due to the defendant’s scheme. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant misrepresented facts in order to secure contracts with a state transportation department. The state had a mandate that contractors must subcontract with a disadvantaged business; Kousisis falsely stated that his company would comply. After receiving the contracts, he employed the disadvantaged-business subcontractor merely as a nominal participant. Id. at 1389. When this falsehood was exposed, Kousisis was charged under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which posits that a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contested this theory, arguing it improperly allowed conviction without evidence that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court dismissed that view, concluding that a defendant commits fraud by “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. Here, the indictment adequately alleges that defendants intended to defraud their followers. For instance, it charges that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not require proof of actual economic loss, the fact that followers received securities at market value does not negate the possibility of fraud. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (noting that a defendant can be guilty of fraud even if he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants claim that their intention was to realize a profit, not to injure anyone. Yet in this case, that is a distinction without significance. The indictment states that the defendants aimed to obtain money, achieved by fraudulently encouraging their followers to buy securities. Alleging that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump for profit suffices to show they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—even where the victim sustains no net financial loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s assertion that defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities adequately alleges harm. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (clarifying that the fraud statutes do not demand a “mirror image” showing of the victim’s loss equating to the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (employing Kousisis in a comparable setting).

IV

As the indictment properly sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and intent, we VACATE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
