Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud arising from their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants used misleading posts on social media to convince followers to buy certain securities by misrepresenting both their own trading activities and the projected future prices of the securities, thereby causing the prices to inflate artificially and enabling them to profit. The district court granted a motion to dismiss, ruling that the indictment did not establish an offense, as it only claimed that the defendants deprived followers of valuable economic information, not of a conventional property right. However, finding that the indictment adequately sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and an intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s decision to dismiss the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a scheme to “pump and dump” securities. Each defendant maintained a significant following on several social media platforms, where they portrayed themselves as adept stock traders and routinely shared details of their trades. According to the government, the defendants would acquire securities and then artificially “pump” up their prices by disseminating false or misleading information about the securities via social media, thereby enticing other investors to purchase and inflate the price. The defendants would then “dump” their holdings by selling them surreptitiously to secure profits. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts that these activities yielded $114 million in profits for the defendants. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, determining that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme to deprive victims of a traditional property interest. The government then filed a timely appeal.

II

We conduct a de novo review of whether an indictment is sufficient. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). In this analysis, the court must accept all allegations in the indictment as accurate and assess if the indictment charges a criminal offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). The indictment is required to: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In essence, an indictment meets the sufficiency standard when it sets forth every material element of the alleged crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute outlaws any plan to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as schemes designed “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” section 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” prompting courts to “give the terms similar treatment” and to frequently use mail and wire fraud precedents when evaluating securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Accordingly, to prove securities fraud, the government must show (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) action undertaken with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The requirement of a “scheme to defraud” is satisfied by a material misrepresentation that is “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The element of “intent to defraud” demands a showing of “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Ultimately, a defendant perpetrates securities fraud by “‘engag[ing] in deception’” where “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” serves as “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants admit that the indictment properly alleges deception. However, they argue that the indictment is deficient in alleging a scheme to defraud or intent to defraud. Specifically, they maintain that the indictment charges only an intent to deprive followers of valuable economic information (rather than money or property) for defendants’ self-enrichment (as opposed to injuring their followers). Considering the Supreme Court’s guidance in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reach a different conclusion.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the basis that it fails to allege a scheme involving the deprivation of a protected property interest from their followers. Although a fraud conviction cannot rest solely on the deprivation of potentially significant economic information, the indictment in question extends beyond this by adequately alleging that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In the Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli, it was determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest exclusively on the loss of access to economically significant information. U.S. at 316. In that matter, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist in an effort to secure government-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. When the government prosecuted Ciminelli for wire fraud, its case was predicated solely on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory posits that “wire fraud is established by demonstrating the defendant schemed to deprive the victim of potentially valuable economic information required for discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Simply put, the property interest according to the right-to-control theory is “the right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Court determined that, since federal fraud statutes only safeguard conventional property interests, and because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

The circumstances at issue differ from those in Ciminelli. Here, the indictment does not reference the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that the defendants, through false representations, induced followers to buy securities—thereby causing them to relinquish money or property. The indictment proceeds under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which is when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In addressing the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that this doctrine “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the right-to-control theory disallowed in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory presented here is intended to “protect[] money and property.” Id. Because the indictment alleges that the defendants fraudulently caused their followers to part with a recognized property interest by purchasing securities, it is not precluded by the holding in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting instead that they were motivated by personal gain rather than a desire to injure a victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable argument, concluding that fraud statutes do not require a showing that the victim experienced economic harm as a result of the defendant's scheme. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant provided false information to secure contracts with a state department of transportation. The state mandated that contractors partner with a disadvantaged business, and Kousisis fraudulently claimed that his company would satisfy this requirement. Upon award of the contracts, Kousisis in fact used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor merely as a "pass-through" entity. Id. at 1389. When his deceit was uncovered, he was prosecuted under a "fraudulent-inducement theory" which provides that a defendant commits fraud by using a material misrepresentation to induce a party into a contract that involves handing over money or property, "regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis maintained that this theory could not be upheld because it permitted conviction without proof that "the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line." Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this argument, holding that a violation occurs when the defendant "schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." Id. at 1392.

In the present case, the indictment adequately alleges defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. For instance, it asserts that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because proof of actual economic injury is not a requirement under the fraud statutes, it is immaterial that the followers ultimately received market-value securities—the fraud charges stand. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding liability may attach even if the defendant "provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return"). Defendants claim their goal was profit rather than harm to anyone else, yet this argument is inconsequential here. As alleged, their intent was to obtain money through fraudulent means by enticing their followers into buying securities, satisfying the requisite mental state. The indictment’s assertion that the defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme for profit clearly demonstrates their financial motivation. Id. at 1397. "A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses," even if no net pecuniary loss is incurred by the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. Therefore, the indictment’s charge that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to purchase securities is sufficient to establish an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (explaining that the fraud statutes do not require a "mirror image" correlation between victim’s loss and defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis to an analogous circumstance).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and an intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND the matter for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
