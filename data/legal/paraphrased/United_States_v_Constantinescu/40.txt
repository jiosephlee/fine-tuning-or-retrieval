Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced indictment on securities fraud charges arising from their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. The superseding indictment charges that, by making misleading posts about their trading positions and the potential prices of securities, the defendants persuaded followers on social media to buy those securities, thus artificially boosting the prices and securing profits for themselves. The district court determined that the indictment should be dismissed, reasoning that it only asserted the defendants intended to deprive their followers of potentially valuable financial information rather than a recognized property interest. However, we find that the indictment adequately sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud. Accordingly, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the government, each defendant maintained significant followings on various social media platforms, portraying themselves as adept stock traders and regularly sharing their trading actions online. The alleged scheme involved defendants acquiring a security and then “‘pumping’ its price by posting deceptive and misleading statements about the security on [social media], thereby persuading others to purchase it and causing an artificial price increase.” The defendants would subsequently “dump” the security by covertly selling it for a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further asserts that their scheme yielded $114 million in profits. The charges included conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted dismissal, finding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme to deprive victims of a traditional property interest. The government subsequently filed a timely appeal.

II

The adequacy of an indictment is subject to de novo review. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). As stated in United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)), “[T]he court must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and assess whether those allegations constitute an offense.” To be valid, an indictment is required to: “(1) set forth each prima facie element of the offense charged; (2) adequately advise the defendant of the accusations against him; and (3) afford protection against double jeopardy in later prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). As summarized by United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up), “An indictment is sufficient when it includes each essential element of the offense.”

III

The securities fraud statute bars any scheme to "defraud any person in connection with . . . any security," as well as any scheme "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security." See U.S.C. § 1348. Although "caselaw interpreting the securities fraud statute in this circuit is limited," § 1348 "incorporates essential concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes." Consequently, "courts have interpreted these terms similarly," frequently referencing mail and wire fraud precedents to adjudicate securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Accordingly, securities fraud is established by showing (1) the existence of a "scheme to defraud" (2) that was undertaken with "intent to defraud." Id. at 339. The "scheme to defraud" prong requires a material misstatement "intended to deceive others for the purpose of gaining something of value, such as money, from the [entity] misled." Id. (modification in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The "intent to defraud" component necessitates "an intent both (1) to deceive and (2) to inflict some harm resulting from the deception." Id. (citing Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Thus, a defendant is guilty of securities fraud if he "'engaged in deception'" and if "'[o]btaining the victim’s money or property' was 'an object' of his fraud." Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently pleads deception. Nevertheless, they assert that the indictment does not sufficiently allege a scheme to defraud or intent to defraud. They argue instead that the indictment charges merely that their intent was to deprive followers of valuable information (not of money or property), thereby benefiting themselves rather than harming their followers. Taking into account the Supreme Court's opinions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject defendants’ argument.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to claim any scheme aimed at depriving their followers of a legally protected property interest. Although deprivation of merely potentially useful economic information is insufficient to support a conviction for fraud, the indictment in this case alleged more, appropriately charging that defendants intended to defraud their followers out of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm made payments to a lobbyist with the aim of securing government-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. In prosecuting Ciminelli for wire fraud, the government relied exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory permitted the government to seek a wire fraud conviction by “showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Put differently, the theory defined the victim’s property as “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that, since federal fraud statutes only safeguard traditional property interests and “the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” a conviction cannot be sustained on the basis of the right-to-control theory. Id. at 316. The present matter is not analogous to Ciminelli. Here, the indictment does not rely on the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that the defendants misled their followers into purchasing securities, thereby causing them to relinquish money or property. This constitutes a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” wherein a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When evaluating the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court affirmed that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast with the right-to-control theory dismissed in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue “protects money and property.” Id. As such, the indictment does not conflict with Ciminelli because it claims that the defendants, through fraudulent inducement, deprived their followers of a constitutionally protected property interest by persuading them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting that it only claims they sought to benefit financially rather than inflict harm on any victims. However, the Supreme Court recently addressed and rejected an analogous contention, clarifying that fraud statutes do not require a showing that a victim experienced financial loss due to a defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, the defendant had secured contracts with a state department of transportation by falsely claiming compliance with a mandate to subcontract work to a disadvantaged business. After being awarded the contracts, Kousisis employed the purported subcontractor as a nominal, pass-through participant. Id. at 1389. The government prosecuted Kousisis under a fraudulent-inducement theory, wherein a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued that this theory was flawed because it allowed for conviction without evidence that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning, concluding that a person violates the fraud statutes when they “schem[e] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

In the matter at hand, the indictment sufficiently alleges that the defendants acted with fraudulent intent towards their followers. Specifically, it states that the defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. As actual financial harm is not a prerequisite under the fraud statutes, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not nullify the basis for fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding that liability may attach for fraud even when the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). While defendants claim their intent was simply to gain profit, rather than harm victims, that distinction is immaterial in this context. According to the indictment, the purpose was to obtain money by deceitfully persuading their followers to purchase securities. The claim that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme to realize profits further demonstrates that their motivation was financial. Id. at 1397. “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses,” irrespective of the victim’s net pecuniary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s assertion that the defendants fraudulently induced their followers to buy securities adequately pleads a cognizable injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (stating that the fraud statutes do not require the victim’s loss to mirror the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (interpreting Kousisis in a related scenario).

IV

As the indictment adequately charges both a scheme and an intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
