Case Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 24-20143  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Kurt D. Engelhardt

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud arising from their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. According to the superseding indictment, defendants utilized misleading social-media posts about their trading positions and the potential value of securities to encourage their followers to buy, thereby causing artificial inflation of those securities’ prices and enabling defendants to earn profits. The district court ruled to dismiss the indictment, reasoning that its allegations did not constitute an offense since they only claimed defendants deprived their followers of possibly valuable economic information, rather than of a conventional property interest. However, as the indictment, in our view, adequately sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and fraudulent intent, we REVERSE the district court’s decision to dismiss the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the government, these defendants maintained significant followings on various social media platforms, represented themselves as adept stock traders, and frequently shared their trading activities online. To execute the alleged scheme, the government claims that defendants would buy a security, “’pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” then covertly “dump” the security by selling it for profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further charges that the scheme resulted in $114 million in profits for the defendants. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, finding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme by defendants to deprive victims of any traditional property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

Our assessment of whether an indictment is sufficient is conducted under a de novo standard. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment's factual allegations as true and decide if the indictment establishes an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment is required to: “(1) state every prima facie element of the alleged crime; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the nature of the accusation; and (3) afford the defendant protection against subsequent prosecutions for the same offense through a double jeopardy defense.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). “In short, an indictment is only sufficient if it charges each essential element of the crime.” United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids “schemes to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” Section 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” meaning “courts have given the terms similar treatment,” and frequently look to mail and wire fraud precedent in addressing securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying these principles, to establish securities fraud the government must prove (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” element involves a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” requirement calls for “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). To summarize, securities fraud is committed when a defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and the goal of his fraud was “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants do not contest that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception. However, they argue that the indictment fails to charge either a scheme or an intent to defraud, maintaining that it only claims they sought to deny their followers valuable economic information (rather than money or property) for their own enrichment (rather than to cause harm to their followers). Considering the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject this argument.

A

Defendants contend that dismissal of the indictment by the district court was warranted on the grounds that it failed to allege a scheme depriving their followers of a property interest safeguarded by law. Although conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in this case went beyond that, sufficiently charging that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of monetary assets.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist to secure contracts funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution based its wire fraud case against Ciminelli exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to that theory, the government sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. That is, within this framework, the asset in question is the victim’s “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that the federal fraud statutes safeguard only conventional property interests, and because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the “right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

The facts of this case are distinguishable from Ciminelli. Here, the indictment does not reference the right-to-control theory. Instead, it charges that defendants, through false representations, persuaded their followers to purchase securities—thereby relinquishing their money or property. The indictment puts forth a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court, in addressing the fraudulent-inducement theory, made clear that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the rejected right-to-control theory in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory invoked here “protects money and property.” Id. Since the indictment alleges that the defendants deprived their followers of a recognized property interest by fraudulently inducing them to buy securities, it does not violate the rule in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is insufficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting that it only claims they intended to benefit financially from their scheme rather than to cause harm to any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed an analogous contention, ruling that the fraud statutes do not demand that a victim experience financial harm as a result of the defendant’s actions. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant misrepresented facts to secure contracts with a state department of transportation. The relevant state regulations obligated contractors to subcontract work to a disadvantaged business, which Kousisis claimed his company would satisfy, but once awarded the contracts, he merely used the disadvantaged business as a “pass-through” without meaningfully involving them. Id. at 1389. When these misrepresentations surfaced, Kousisis was prosecuted under the “fraudulent-inducement theory”—that is, where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis challenged this theory, asserting it enabled conviction without showing the defendant intended “to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this argument and clarified that a defendant contravenes the fraud statutes by “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. The indictment in the current matter adequately pleads that defendants acted with intent to defraud their followers; for instance, it alleges that the defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the statutes governing fraud do not necessitate a showing of actual monetary harm, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities worth the current market value does not negate the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding defendants may be culpable for fraud even when they provide something of commensurate value in exchange). The defendants contend their goal was profit rather than to injure a victim; however, in this instance, that distinction is immaterial. As set forth in the indictment, the purpose was to obtain money, achieved by means of fraudulently enticing their followers to purchase securities. The allegations that defendants executed a “pump and dump” for their financial benefit demonstrates that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The offense of fraud is complete “when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—regardless of the presence or absence of net financial harm to the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s claims regarding the defendants’ inducement of their followers to buy securities by fraudulent means suffice to allege the requisite injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (stating fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in a similar situation).

IV

Since the indictment adequately sets forth a scheme and fraudulent intent, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
