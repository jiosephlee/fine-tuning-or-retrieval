Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the allegations, each defendant amassed substantial followings on multiple social media platforms, where they portrayed themselves as experienced stock traders and frequently disclosed their trades online. The government contends that the defendants executed their scheme by purchasing certain securities, then “pumping” the prices by sharing “false and misleading information about the security on [social media],” thereby encouraging other investors to buy and artificially drive up the price, before subsequently “dumping” the securities by covertly selling them for a gain. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. It is alleged in the indictment that the defendants generated $114 million in profits from this activity. The defendants faced indictment on charges of conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, in addition to several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought to have the indictment dismissed. The district court granted the dismissal, reasoning that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme by defendants to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We assess the adequacy of an indictment anew. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court “must assume the factual assertions of the indictment are accurate and decide whether they establish a criminal offense.” United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment is required to “(1) specify each essential element constituting the alleged crime; (2) adequately notify the accused of the charges against him; and (3) equip the accused with protection against double jeopardy in any subsequent prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Ultimately, for an indictment to be legally sufficient, it “must charge all the key elements of the crime.” United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The statute governing securities fraud forbids plans to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as schemes designed “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” leading courts to “give[] the terms similar treatment,” and frequently turn to mail and wire fraud precedents when considering securities fraud claims. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Given this legal backdrop, a conviction for securities fraud requires (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the presence of “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” prong demands a material misstatement meant to mislead others to procure something valuable—such as money—from the deceived entity. Id. (alteration in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). As for “intent to defraud,” that element necessitates “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Taken together, the offense of securities fraud is established where a defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and securing “the victim’s money or property” constituted “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (citing Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants admit that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception but argue that it fails to charge a scheme to defraud or the requisite intent to defraud. According to defendants, the indictment only asserts that they sought to deny their followers valuable economic information (as opposed to money or property) for their own benefit (not to harm their followers). We find, especially in view of the Supreme Court’s recent guidance in Ciminelli and Kousisis, that this argument is unpersuasive.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly granted dismissal of the indictment, asserting that it fails to allege a plan to take a legally protected property interest from their followers. Although a fraud conviction cannot stand where the only deprivation alleged is of potentially useful economic information, the indictment in this case does more by adequately charging that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of actual monetary assets.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest exclusively on the denial of access to significant economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction business paid a lobbyist in pursuit of state-financed contracts. Id. at 309–10. The government’s wire fraud prosecution of Ciminelli was based entirely on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the government’s position was that wire fraud may be shown if the defendant executed a scheme to deprive the victim of potentially significant economic information needed for discretionary business choices. Id. at 310. Essentially, this approach views the relevant property as the victim’s “right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes cover only conventional property rights, and since “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” a conviction cannot be sustained under the right-to-control theory. Id. at 316.

The present circumstances are distinct from those in Ciminelli. The indictment does not reference or rely on the right-to-control theory. Instead, it charges that the defendants caused their followers, through false statements, to invest in securities, thereby separating those individuals from their money or property. This constitutes a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant deploys a materially false statement to deceive a victim into entering a contract that requires surrendering money or property. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. Upon review of the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court made clear it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the disapproved right-to-control theory from Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory involved here “protects money and property.” Id. Therefore, because the indictment asserts the defendants fraudulently deprived followers of a protected property interest by causing them to purchase securities, it does not contravene Ciminelli.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is deficient because it alleges an intent to profit from their scheme rather than an intention to harm a victim, arguing this shows a lack of intent to defraud. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed a similar position, making clear that the relevant fraud statutes do not require the victim to have suffered an actual economic loss as a result of the defendant’s conduct. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, the defendant obtained state transportation contracts by falsely asserting that a disadvantaged business would perform subcontracting work as required by the state. Upon being awarded the contracts, Kousisis simply utilized the supposed disadvantaged subcontractor as a nominal participant. Id. at 1389. When this deception was discovered, authorities prosecuted Kousisis on the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” meaning that he used a significant misrepresentation to cause another to enter a contract resulting in a transfer of money or property to him—regardless of whether he provided any value or caused a financial loss to the other party. Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis claimed this theory was flawed, arguing it should not support conviction without evidence that the defendant intended economic harm to the victim. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that a fraud conviction may be sustained whenever the defendant attempts to obtain the victim’s money or property by fraudulent means, even if the victim is not left in a worse financial position. Id. at 1392.

The present indictment adequately charges the defendants with having an intent to defraud their followers. As one example, it claims the defendants disseminated “false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the law does not demand that the victims actually incur monetary harm, the fact that the followers ultimately received securities at market value does not invalidate the prosecution. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding that liability for fraud remains even if the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants insist they merely intended to make money, not injure anyone. Here, however, that claimed distinction is immaterial. The indictment sets forth that the purpose was to procure money, which they sought by fraudulently soliciting their followers to buy securities. In alleging a pump and dump scheme to generate profit, the indictment establishes that the defendants “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The commission of fraud is satisfied once the defendant has, through material falsehoods, caused the deprivation of money or property, whether or not a net loss to the victim results. Id. at 1394 n.5. The assertion in the indictment that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to invest in securities sufficiently alleges the necessary injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that the statutes governing fraud do not require that the victim’s loss correspond exactly to the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (discussing the application of Kousisis in a comparable scenario).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court's order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
