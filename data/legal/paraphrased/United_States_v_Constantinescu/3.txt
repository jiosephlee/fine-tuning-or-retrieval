Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud based on their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, they persuaded their followers on social media to buy securities by making posts that falsely represented both their own trading activities and the potential value of the securities, thereby artificially increasing the securities’ prices and enabling themselves to gain financially. The district court dismissed the indictment, finding that it did not properly allege an offense, as it claimed only that the defendants deprived followers of possibly valuable economic information rather than a conventional property right. We determine, however, that the indictment adequately sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent, so we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the government, each defendant amassed a significant following on several social media platforms, portrayed themselves as expert stock traders, and regularly shared their trading activities online. As described by the government, the defendants would acquire a security and then “‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] to induce other investors to buy the security, thereby artificially inflating its price,” before “dumping” it by covertly selling for a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further claims that the defendants realized $114 million in profits through this scheme. The charges brought against the defendants included conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion, finding that the indictment did not allege a scheme to deprive victims of any conventional property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We examine the adequacy of an indictment anew. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). As stated in United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)), the court must accept the indictment’s factual allegations as true and assess whether they constitute a criminal offense. To satisfy the requirements, an indictment must: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Stated otherwise, an indictment is only sufficient if it charges every essential element of the crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids both schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” and efforts “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “caselaw interpreting the securities fraud statute in this circuit is limited,” § 1348 “incorporates core principles from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” which has led courts to “treat the terms similarly,” frequently referencing mail and wire fraud decisions when considering securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). In this context, the offense of securities fraud consists of (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” requirement involves a material misrepresentation made “with the intention to deceive others to acquire something of value, such as money, from the party to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” prong demands “an intention both (1) to deceive and (2) to cause harm as a result of the deception.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Therefore, securities fraud occurs when a defendant has “‘engaged in deception’” with the acquisition of “the victim’s money or property” as “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants admit that the indictment sufficiently pleads deception. However, they argue that the indictment fails to set forth either a scheme to defraud or intent to defraud. They maintain that the indictment only alleges that their aim was to deprive followers of important economic information (rather than money or property) for personal gain (without injuring their followers). In view of the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject that argument.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment, asserting that it fails to allege any scheme to deprive their followers of a legally protected property interest. Although a person may not be convicted of fraud solely for denying another access to potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in this case goes beyond that, properly alleging that the defendants orchestrated a scheme to defraud their followers of actual money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest exclusively on the denial of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that matter, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist in pursuit of state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution, in seeking to convict Ciminelli of wire fraud, relied entirely on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. Under this theory, the government attempted to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. That is, under the right-to-control theory, the relevant property interest is the victim’s “right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, and “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” thus “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The present case differs from Ciminelli. The indictment does not invoke the right-to-control theory; instead, it charges that defendants used misrepresentations to induce followers to buy securities—meaning, to give up money or property. The indictment relies on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When examining the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court stated it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory disallowed in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here “protects money and property.” Id. Thus, the indictment does not violate Ciminelli, as it alleges the defendants, through fraud, deprived their followers of a protected property interest by inducing them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege that they intended to defraud, arguing instead that it merely accuses them of seeking personal gain rather than aiming to inflict harm on any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable argument, finding that the federal fraud statutes do not mandate that a victim experience financial loss as a result of a defendant’s actions. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant misrepresented facts in order to secure contracts with a state department of transportation. The contracts required engagement with a disadvantaged business subcontractor, and Kousisis falsely claimed his company would satisfy this prerequisite. Upon being awarded the contracts, Kousisis utilized the disadvantaged-business entity as little more than a "pass-through." Id. at 1389. When this deception surfaced, Kousisis faced charges under the "fraudulent-inducement theory," which covers cases where a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued that this theory was flawed because it allowed for conviction even without proof that the defendant sought to financially injure the victim. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that fraud statutes are violated when a defendant "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." Id. at 1392.

The indictment at issue here adequately alleges the defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. It states, for example, that defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." Indictment ¶ 13. Since fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, the fact that the followers received securities at market value does not negate the possibility of fraud. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (noting liability for fraud may exist even if the defendant "provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return"). Defendants maintain that their goal was profit, not causing harm. Yet, in this context, such a distinction is immaterial. As the indictment asserts, the objective was to obtain money by fraudulently inducing their followers to buy securities. Allegations that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme to realize profits demonstrate that they "had money in mind." Id. at 1397. "A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—even if the victim did not sustain a net financial loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment's charge that defendants fraudulently caused their followers to purchase securities suffices to allege injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (holding the fraud statutes do not require a "mirror image" where the victim's loss directly corresponds to the defendant's gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in a related scenario).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
