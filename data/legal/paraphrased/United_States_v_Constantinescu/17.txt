Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud based on their participation in a "pump and dump" scheme. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants used social media posts to falsely represent their trading positions and the expected prices of certain securities, thereby encouraging their followers to buy these securities, which led to artificial price increases and subsequent profits for the defendants. The district court dismissed the indictment, finding that it did not properly allege an offense as it claimed only a deprivation of potentially useful economic information from followers, not the deprivation of a classic property interest. We find that the indictment adequately sets forth a scheme and intent to defraud, and therefore, we REVERSE the district court’s decision dismissing the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a scheme commonly referred to as “pump and dump” regarding securities. According to the allegations, each defendant maintained a significant presence and following across different social media networks. They portrayed themselves as experienced stock traders and regularly disclosed their trading activities on these platforms. The government asserts that, in furtherance of the scheme, defendants would buy a security, then artificially “pump” its price by disseminating materially false and misleading statements about the security via social media, with the goal of prompting other investors to purchase the security and thus drive up its market price; thereafter, they would “dump” their holdings by discreetly selling the securities at a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further claims the defendants collectively realized profits totaling $114 million from this conduct. The charges against them included conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as multiple counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Daniel Knight entered a guilty plea; the other defendants filed a motion seeking dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted their motion, finding that the indictment failed to sufficiently allege a scheme depriving victims of a recognized property interest. The government subsequently filed a timely appeal.

II

Our review of an indictment’s sufficiency is conducted de novo. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment's factual assertions as accurate and ascertain whether it properly alleges a criminal offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To satisfy legal requirements, an indictment needs to: “(1) set out each prima facie element of the charged crime; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the accusations brought; and (3) enable the defendant to claim double jeopardy protection in later proceedings.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Essentially, an indictment’s sufficiency depends on whether it states every essential element of the alleged offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids schemes that “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” See U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and, accordingly, “courts have given the terms similar treatment,” frequently turning to mail and wire fraud decisions to interpret securities fraud offenses. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying these principles, to prove securities fraud, the government must show both (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) that it was executed with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. For the “scheme to defraud” requirement, there must be a material misrepresentation meant “to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). As for the “intent to defraud” component, it necessitates “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Thus, a person is guilty of securities fraud if he “‘engaged in deception’” and where “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently pleads deceptive conduct. Nonetheless, they assert the indictment fails to allege a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. Their argument is that the indictment only claims they sought to deprive their followers of valuable economic information for their own enrichment, rather than depriving them of money or property for the purpose of injuring their followers. Considering the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we do not agree with Defendants’ position.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it failed to allege any scheme to deprive their followers of a legally protected property interest. Although it is accurate that a person cannot be found guilty of fraud merely for causing someone to lose access to potentially valuable economic information, the present indictment went further by properly alleging that the defendants intended to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a fraud conviction cannot rest solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm made payments to a lobbyist in exchange for assistance in securing contracts funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The government based its wire fraud prosecution exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. Under this theory, the government sought to establish wire fraud by demonstrating that the defendant’s scheme aimed to deprive the victim of vital economic information necessary for making discretionary financial decisions. Id. at 310. Essentially, the property at issue under the right-to-control theory is the individual’s “right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes guard only traditional property rights, stating: “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” so “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

This matter, however, differs from Ciminelli. The right-to-control theory is not referenced in the present indictment. Instead, the indictment asserts that the defendants used false statements to persuade their followers to invest in securities, resulting in the transfer of money or property. Here, the indictment presents a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which occurs when a defendant makes material misstatements that lead a victim into an agreement necessitating the transfer of money or property. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court, in reviewing the fraudulent-inducement theory, clarified that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Contrary to the right-to-control theory that the Court rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here serves to “protect[] money and property.” Id. Because the indictment alleges that the defendants fraudulently induced their followers to buy securities, thereby depriving them of protected property interests, it does not violate the rule in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege that they intended to defraud, asserting instead that it merely claims they sought personal profit rather than to inflict harm on a victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently addressed and dismissed a comparable contention, clarifying that the federal fraud statutes do not require the victim to incur an economic detriment due to the defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, Kousisis misrepresented facts to secure contracts with a state transportation department, falsely assuring compliance with a mandate to engage a disadvantaged business as a subcontractor. After winning the contracts, Kousisis merely used the disadvantaged business as a nominal participant. See id. at 1389. Following discovery of his deception, the government prosecuted Kousisis under the “fraudulent-inducement theory”—a doctrine recognizing fraud where a defendant obtains a victim’s money or property through a material misrepresentation meant to secure a contract, regardless of whether the perpetrator intends to create a net loss for the victim, who may receive something of comparable value in exchange. Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis maintained that fraudulent-inducement could not support conviction absent proof that the defendant aimed to damage the victim’s economic position. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning, determining that the fraud statutes are breached whenever a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

In the present matter, the indictment adequately alleges defendants’ fraudulent intent toward their followers. Specifically, the indictment states that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the federal fraud statutes do not make economic injury a required element, the fact that defendants’ followers acquired securities at market value does not negate the sufficiency of the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding liability for fraud even when the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants posit their goal was personal profit, not victim harm, but in this instance, that difference is immaterial. As alleged, defendants’ goal was to obtain money through inducing their followers, by fraudulent means, to buy securities. The indictment’s reference to defendants engaging in a pump-and-dump scheme to garner profit supports the notion that their object was monetary gain. Id. at 1397. The offense of fraud is considered consummated “when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—without regard to whether the victim endures a net pecuniary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s claims of defendants deceiving followers into purchasing securities suffice to allege injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (clarifying that the fraud statutes do not mandate a “mirror image” correlation between a victim’s loss and a defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (citing Kousisis under analogous facts).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
