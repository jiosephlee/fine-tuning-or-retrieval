Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud stemming from their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, they misled their social-media followers into purchasing securities by posting false claims about their own holdings and the likely future prices of those securities, which artificially drove up prices and enabled the defendants to gain financially. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that it merely asserted the defendants attempted to withhold advantageous economic information rather than a conventional property right, and therefore did not sufficiently allege an offense. However, upon review, we find that the indictment adequately pleads both the existence of a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud. Accordingly, we REVERSE the dismissal issued by the district court.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a fraudulent “pump and dump” operation involving securities. According to the allegations, each defendant maintained a substantial following on various social media platforms. They presented themselves as proficient stock traders and routinely shared details of their trading activities online. The government contends that, in furtherance of their scheme, defendants would acquire a security, then “‘pump’ the price of that security by disseminating false and misleading statements about the security on [social media] in order to prompt other investors to buy the security and thus artificially drive up its price,” subsequently “dumping” the security by covertly selling their holdings for profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment claims the defendants generated $114 million in illicit gains through this conduct. The charges include conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, along with several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed a motion seeking dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion, determining that the indictment did not sufficiently allege that defendants intended to deprive victims of any conventional property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We assess whether an indictment is sufficient on a de novo basis. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment's allegations as true and decide if it adequately charges an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment should: “(1) specify every prima facie element of the offense alleged; (2) sufficiently notify the defendant of the accusations; and (3) equip the defendant to assert double jeopardy should subsequent prosecutions arise.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In essence, for an indictment to be adequate, it must set forth each material element of the charged crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids the use of schemes intended “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is “scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 incorporates significant concepts from the statutes addressing mail and wire fraud. Consequently, “courts have given the terms similar treatment,” frequently referencing mail and wire fraud case law when evaluating securities fraud allegations. See United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). With those principles in mind, the elements of securities fraud consist of: (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The first element, “scheme to defraud,” involves a material misrepresentation that is “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” prong means “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Thus, the commission of securities fraud occurs where a defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants admit that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception. However, they argue the indictment fails to allege either a scheme or intent to defraud, asserting that it only claims they intended to deny their followers economically valuable information (rather than money or property) for personal gain (rather than to harm the followers). We disagree with their assessment in light of the Supreme Court's rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis.

A

Defendants contend that the district court’s dismissal of the indictment was appropriate since the indictment fails to assert a scheme to deprive their followers of a protected property right. Although fraud convictions cannot be based solely on the deprivation of potentially useful economic information, the indictment in this case made additional allegations, specifically stating that defendants intended to defraud their followers of money.

In the Ciminelli case, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the denial of access to valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that matter, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist to secure state-financed projects. Id. at 309–10. In bringing wire fraud charges against Ciminelli, the government exclusively proceeded on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the prosecution sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Essentially, under this approach, the property interest at issue was characterized as the “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that the federal fraud statutes are limited to safeguarding traditional property interests, and because “the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the “right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

The present case differs from Ciminelli. The indictment here does not reference the right-to-control theory. Instead, it charges that defendants, by making false statements, persuaded their followers to invest in securities—that is, to relinquish money or property. The indictment asserts a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” in which a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When the Supreme Court reviewed the fraudulent-inducement theory, it made clear that this theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory disapproved in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here “protects money and property.” Id. Thus, because the indictment alleges that defendants fraudulently deprived their followers of a property interest through inducing securities purchases, it does not contravene the holding in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is insufficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting that it merely claims they intended to profit rather than cause harm to any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed a comparable argument, clarifying that under the fraud statutes, it is not necessary for a victim to experience economic loss as a result of the defendant’s actions. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant misrepresented facts to secure contracts with a state transportation department that required subcontracting with disadvantaged businesses; he falsely asserted his company would comply, but used the purported subcontractor only as a "pass-through" entity. Id. at 1389. After discovery of this deception, the government prosecuted Kousisis under a "fraudulent-inducement theory"—whereby a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued that this theory was untenable, since it allowed conviction without proof that "the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line." Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that the fraud statutes are violated when a defendant "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim’s 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." Id. at 1392.

Here, the indictment adequately sets forth defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. As stated, the indictment charges that defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not require proof of actual financial loss, it is immaterial that defendants’ followers received securities at market value. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding a defendant may be found liable for fraud even if "he provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return"). Defendants claim that their goal was profit, not victimization. Yet, for purposes of the fraud statutes, this distinction is immaterial. As outlined in the indictment, defendants aimed to secure money by fraudulently persuading their followers to purchase securities, and the allegation of pumping and dumping demonstrates their intent for personal financial gain—indicating they "had money in mind." Id. at 1397. "[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—regardless of whether the victim ends up with a net pecuniary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s claims that defendants misled their followers to purchase securities are thus sufficient to establish an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting the fraud statutes lack a “mirror image” requirement tying the defendant’s gain to a corresponding victim loss); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in a similar scenario).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth a scheme and fraudulent intent, we VACATE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
