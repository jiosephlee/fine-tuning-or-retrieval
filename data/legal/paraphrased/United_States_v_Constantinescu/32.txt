Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud in connection with their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants persuaded individuals following them on social media to buy securities by disseminating posts containing false information regarding both their own trading activities and the prospective price movements of those securities. This conduct allegedly led to an artificial increase in the securities’ market prices, generating profits for the defendants. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the indictment did not establish an offense because it merely claimed that defendants intended to deprive followers of potentially important financial information rather than a recognized property right. We disagree and hold that the indictment properly sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud. Therefore, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government accuses Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight of orchestrating a securities “pump and dump” scheme. According to the government, these defendants amassed sizable audiences on various social media platforms, presenting themselves as expert stock traders and routinely sharing their trades online. The government claims their method involved acquiring a particular security, then “‘pumping’ up its price by disseminating false and misleading statements on [social media] to persuade others to buy, thereby inflating the price,” and ultimately “dumping” the security by surreptitiously selling it at a profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment charges that the defendants reaped $114 million as a result of this conduct. The charges brought against them include conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and several counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the remaining defendants sought to have the indictment dismissed. The district court granted the motion, holding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege that defendants intended to deprive victims of a conventional property right. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We evaluate the adequacy of an indictment without deference to the district court’s decision. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). At this stage, the court must accept the indictment’s assertions as true, considering whether they articulate a prosecutable offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). The requirements for an indictment are that it must: “(1) set forth each prima facie element of the offense charged; (2) sufficiently apprise the defendant of the nature of the accusations; and (3) enable the defendant to invoke protection against future prosecution for the same act.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Stated otherwise, for an indictment to be valid, it must charge every essential element of the crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The statute concerning securities fraud makes it illegal to devise schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as plans “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” Section 1348 incorporates essential ideas from the mail and wire fraud statutes, leading courts to interpret these statutes similarly and often to utilize mail and wire fraud case law when considering securities fraud charges. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Based on these principles, the elements for securities fraud include: (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) the scheme being carried out with an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. Satisfying the “scheme to defraud” requirement necessitates a material misrepresentation intended “to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (modification in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” prong requires “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (citing Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Put succinctly, a person is guilty of securities fraud when he “‘engaged in deception’” and “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently pleads deception. However, they argue that it fails to allege either a scheme or an intent to defraud. Specifically, they assert the indictment only claims they intended to deny their followers access to valuable economic information, rather than depriving them of money or property, and sought to enrich themselves rather than harm their followers. Taking into account the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject this argument.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to assert a plan to withhold a protected property interest from their followers. Although the law does not permit conviction for fraud when only the deprivation of potentially advantageous economic information is involved, the indictment in question alleged more, adequately charging defendants with orchestrating a scheme to defraud their followers out of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the denial of access to important economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli's construction firm made payments to a lobbyist to secure contracts for projects funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution charged Ciminelli with wire fraud, basing its case exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the government sought to "establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." Id. at 310. Essentially, under the right-to-control approach, the property interest at stake for the victim is "the right to control [his] assets." Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes are designed to protect only conventional property interests, explaining that "[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest," and therefore, "the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction." Id. at 316.

The facts of this case differ from those in Ciminelli. Here, the indictment does not assert the right-to-control theory. Instead, it claims that defendants misled their followers into purchasing securities, thereby causing them to relinquish money or property. The indictment follows a "fraudulent-inducement theory," which asserts that a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When addressing the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court emphasized that this theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the discredited right-to-control theory at issue in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory advanced in this case is aimed at "protect[ing] money and property." Id. Because the indictment alleges that defendants, by fraudulent means, deprived their followers of a constitutionally protected property interest through inducing the purchase of securities, it does not violate the rule set forth in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege they intended to defraud, asserting instead that they merely sought personal profit and did not aim to inflict harm on any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently addressed a comparable claim and held that federal fraud statutes do not necessitate that a victim incur an actual economic loss as a result of the defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, Kousisis made false statements to secure contracts from a state department of transportation, which required its contractors to subcontract a portion of work to disadvantaged businesses. Kousisis misrepresented his compliance, while in reality, the disadvantaged business served only as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. Upon discovery of the deception, authorities prosecuted Kousisis under a “fraudulent-inducement theory.” Under this theory, a person is liable for using a significant misrepresentation to persuade someone into a contract that involves the transfer of their money or property—“regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contended that convictions cannot rest on such theory without proof the defendant intended to “hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Court rejected this argument, finding that violating the fraud statutes simply requires “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

Similarly, the indictment in this case adequately charges that defendants intended to defraud their followers. Specifically, it states that the defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because actual financial harm to the victim is not a prerequisite for fraud under the statutes, it is immaterial that defendants’ followers received securities at market value. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding that liability for fraud exists even if the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants insist that their goal was to profit rather than to injure anyone; however, in this instance, that distinction is inconsequential. According to the indictment, defendants' objective was to obtain money by deceiving their followers into buying securities. The indictment’s assertion that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme to achieve a profit demonstrates they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The crime of fraud is “complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—even if the victim did not suffer a net economic loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Therefore, the indictment’s claims regarding the fraudulent inducement of followers to purchase securities properly allege an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” scenario in which the victim’s loss exactly equals the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (interpreting Kousisis in an analogous setting).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
