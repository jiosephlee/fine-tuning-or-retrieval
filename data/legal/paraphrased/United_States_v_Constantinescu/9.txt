Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced indictment for securities fraud in connection with their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. The superseding indictment claims that the defendants persuaded their followers on social media to buy securities by posting misleading information about their own trading positions and the prospective prices of those securities, thereby artificially raising the securities’ values and creating opportunities for personal profit. The district court granted dismissal of the indictment, determining that it did not establish a valid offense since it only asserted that defendants attempted to withhold potentially valuable economic information from followers rather than depriving them of a more conventional property interest. However, finding that the indictment adequately asserts both a fraudulent scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the allegations, each defendant had a substantial following on various social media platforms and presented themselves as expert stock traders, routinely sharing their trades online. The government asserts that, as part of the scheme, the defendants would acquire a security, then “pump” its price by disseminating false or misleading information about it on social media, causing other investors to purchase the security and thereby inflating its price artificially. The defendants would then “dump” their holdings by covertly selling the security at a profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment claims that defendants gained $114 million through this conduct. Charges brought against them included conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and several counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted dismissal, holding that the indictment did not adequately allege a scheme to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government subsequently filed a timely appeal.

II

Our review of an indictment’s sufficiency is conducted without deference to the district court’s decision. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and assess whether the indictment properly charges an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). For an indictment to suffice, it must: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Stated succinctly, a sufficient indictment must charge every material element of the offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute makes it unlawful to execute schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “there is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 draws important elements from the mail and wire fraud statutes; accordingly, “courts have given the terms similar treatment,” frequently using mail and wire fraud decisions as guidance when analyzing securities fraud. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying these frameworks, securities fraud consists of (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the existence of an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). For “intent to defraud,” the government must show “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Put simply, a person is guilty of securities fraud if he “‘engaged in deception’” and “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants admit that the indictment sufficiently sets forth acts of deception, but argue it fails to properly allege a scheme to defraud or the intent to defraud. Specifically, they argue that the indictment accuses them only of intending to withhold valuable economic information from their followers (rather than money or property) for their own gain (not for the purpose of harming their followers). However, given the Supreme Court’s opinions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reach a contrary conclusion.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment because it fails to allege a scheme to deprive their followers of a recognized property interest. Although it is accurate that fraud cannot be established merely on the basis that a defendant deprived someone of potentially useful economic information, the indictment at issue goes further by properly alleging that defendants’ scheme sought to defraud followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a fraud conviction cannot rest only on the deprivation of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist to secure jobs funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The government's wire fraud prosecution depended exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the government sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. In essence, the right-to-control theory considers the victim’s property interest to be the “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Court ruled that the federal fraud statutes safeguard only traditional property rights and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest.” Consequently, “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The present circumstances differ from Ciminelli. The indictment here does not rely on the right-to-control theory, but rather asserts that defendants persuaded followers, through false representations, to purchase securities, thereby relinquishing money or property. The indictment presents a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In considering the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that this theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Distinct from the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory advanced here “protects money and property.” Id. Therefore, the indictment is not inconsistent with Ciminelli, since it alleges that defendants fraudulently deprived their followers of a protected property interest by inducing them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants also contend that the indictment is deficient because it fails to assert that they intended to defraud; according to them, the charges only suggest that their goal was to profit, not to cause harm to any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently rejected a comparable argument, concluding that fraud statutes do not require proof that a victim actually suffers economic loss as a result of the defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant misrepresented facts in order to secure contracts from a state transportation department. Contractors bidding for those contracts were obligated to subcontract with disadvantaged businesses. Kousisis falsely certified that his firm would comply, but after receiving the contract, used the purportedly disadvantaged subcontractor merely as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When these misrepresentations were discovered, Kousisis was prosecuted under the “fraudulent-inducement theory”—that is, when “a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis objected, arguing that the fraudulent-inducement theory allowed conviction without proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this position, ruling that it suffices under the fraud statutes for a defendant to “scheme[] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The present indictment adequately sets forth defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. Among other things, it claims that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not require an actual pecuniary loss, the fact that victims received market-value securities does not nullify the charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (determining that liability for fraud attaches even if the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants assert their motive was to make a profit, rather than to inflict injury, but under these circumstances, that is a distinction without legal significance. According to the indictment, defendants’ purpose was to secure money through fraudulent inducement of security purchases by their followers. The assertion that defendants engaged in pump-and-dump activity to generate profits demonstrates they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—without regard to any net pecuniary loss experienced by the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. The allegations that defendants fraudulently encouraged their followers to buy securities adequately allege injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (explaining that there is no “mirror image” requirement in the fraud statutes mandating that the victim’s loss exactly equal the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in an analogous context).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
