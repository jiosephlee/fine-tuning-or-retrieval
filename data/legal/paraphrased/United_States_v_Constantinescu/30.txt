Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-20143  
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

Defendants faced charges of securities fraud stemming from their participation in a "pump and dump" operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants used social-media posts containing false statements about their trading activities and the expected value of certain securities to persuade followers to buy those securities, thereby driving up their market prices and enabling defendants to gain financially. The district court dismissed the indictment, finding that it did not constitute an offense as it only asserted that the defendants aimed to withhold potentially important financial information from followers rather than a more conventional property right. Finding that the indictment adequately sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and intent, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight executed a “pump and dump” securities fraud scheme. According to the allegations, each defendant maintained a significant social media presence across several platforms and portrayed themselves as expert stock traders, frequently sharing their trading activities online. The government contends that, as part of their scheme, the defendants would acquire a security, then “‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media],” leading other investors to buy and artificially drive up the price, after which the defendants would “dump” their holdings by covertly selling at a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further alleges the defendants realized $114 million in profits through these activities. Charges were brought against them for conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, along with multiple counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Daniel Knight pleaded guilty, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion, determining the indictment did not adequately allege that the defendants conspired to deprive the victims of any conventional property interest. The government appealed this dismissal in a timely manner.

II

We conduct a de novo review when evaluating whether an indictment is sufficient. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the facts as alleged in the indictment as true and assess if a crime is properly charged. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To satisfy legal requirements, an indictment must: (1) set forth every prima facie element of the offense charged; (2) adequately advise the defendant of the nature of the accusations; and (3) enable the defendant to invoke protection against being prosecuted again for the same offense. United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In essence, an indictment's sufficiency turns on whether it states all essential elements of the alleged offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids both engaging in a “scheme to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” and pursuing a plan “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “there is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “incorporates significant concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” leading courts to interpret the statute in parallel with those statutes, frequently referencing mail and wire fraud precedent to evaluate securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). With these principles in mind, establishing securities fraud requires proof of (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) that such scheme was pursued with an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” prong necessitates a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] being deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” criterion requires “an intent both to (1) deceive and (2) to cause some harm as a consequence of the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Therefore, a violation of the securities fraud statute occurs if a defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and if “‘obtaining the victim’s money or property’” constituted “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants admit that the indictment sufficiently charges deception; however, they argue that the indictment is deficient in that it does not allege a scheme to defraud or the necessary intent to defraud. Specifically, they assert that the indictment merely claims they sought to deprive followers of valuable economic information (and not of money or property), to their own benefit (rather than to the harm of their followers). We reject this position, guided by the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly granted dismissal of the indictment, asserting it fails to charge a scheme aimed at depriving their followers of a legally protected property interest. Although a conviction for fraud cannot be premised solely on the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in this case surpasses that threshold by sufficiently alleging that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest exclusively on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist to secure state-funded projects. Id. at 309–10. The government, in its wire fraud prosecution against Ciminelli, based its case entirely on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the prosecution sought to establish wire fraud by demonstrating that the defendant devised a scheme intended to deprive a victim of important economic information required to make discretionary business decisions. Id. at 310. In this context, the property interest involved was characterized as “the right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that, because federal fraud statutes are limited to safeguarding conventional property interests and “the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the right-to-control theory could not underpin a fraud conviction. Id. at 316.

The present matter differs materially from Ciminelli. The indictment here makes no reference to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that defendants, by means of false representations, persuaded their followers to buy securities—that is, to relinquish their own money or property. The indictment relies on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which posits that a defendant uses a significant misrepresentation to deceive a victim into entering a contract obligating the transfer of money or property. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In its review of the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court affirmed that this theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory here “protects money and property.” Id. As a result, the indictment does not violate Ciminelli, since it claims that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to part with protected property interests by purchasing securities.

B

Defendants then contend that the indictment is deficient for not pleading an intent to defraud, reasoning that it only alleges they sought personal profit from their scheme rather than aimed to cause harm to any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently addressed and dismissed a comparable contention, clarifying that federal fraud statutes do not require the victim to experience a monetary loss due to the defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, the defendant secured contracts from a state transportation department by misrepresentation; state requirements mandated that contractors subcontract work to a disadvantaged business, and Kousisis falsely claimed his firm would comply. After securing the contracts, he merely funneled work through the disadvantaged-business subcontractor as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. Upon discovery of his deceit, authorities prosecuted Kousisis using the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” a doctrine under which a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis asserted that convictions under this theory were impermissible absent evidence that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, ruling that a defendant contravenes the fraud statutes by “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The indictment in this case clearly sets forth defendants’ fraudulent intent. For instance, it states that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the applicable fraud statutes do not require proof of actual economic harm, the fact that defendants’ followers received market-value securities does not negate the possibility of fraud. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (determining liability under fraud statutes even where the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants maintain that their aim was profit, not to inflict loss on others; nevertheless, for purposes of the law, such distinction is immaterial here. The indictment alleges that defendants’ goal was to secure money by fraudulently persuading followers to buy securities. Their “pump and dump” strategy, as described, is sufficient to demonstrate they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The fraud is accomplished upon inducing a deprivation of money or property by material misrepresentation—whether or not the victim ultimately suffers a net pecuniary detriment. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s charges that defendants fraudulently caused their followers to buy securities is therefore enough to plead injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting fraud statutes do not require a precise symmetry between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (employing Kousisis in a comparable case).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and the intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court's decision to dismiss the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in line with this opinion.
