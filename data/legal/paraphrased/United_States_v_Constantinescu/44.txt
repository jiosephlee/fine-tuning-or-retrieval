In United States v. Constantinescu, before the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit on October 2, 2025 (No. 24-20143), Circuit Judge Kurt D. Engelhardt authored the opinion.

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud related to their participation in a so-called “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, they persuaded followers on social media to purchase certain securities by making false statements about their own trading activities and the likely future value of those securities. This led to an artificial rise in the securities’ market prices, providing the defendants with financial gains. The district court had dismissed the indictment, reasoning that it did not allege deprivation of a conventional property interest but, at most, a denial of possibly valuable economic information. Upon review, we determine that the indictment adequately alleges both a fraudulent scheme and intent to defraud. Therefore, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a “pump and dump” securities scheme. Each defendant maintained a substantial presence on multiple social media platforms, presented themselves as expert stock traders, and regularly shared their trading behavior online. According to the government, the scheme involved defendants acquiring securities, artificially inflating their prices by disseminating false or misleading statements about them on social media to encourage outside investors to purchase and thereby further boost the price, and then covertly selling their own holdings at a profit—i.e., “dumping.” Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment claims the defendants realized gains of $114 million from this activity. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and various counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants moved to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, finding the indictment did not sufficiently allege that defendants conspired to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We assess whether an indictment is sufficient using a de novo standard of review. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the factual assertions within the indictment as true and decide if they constitute an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). The indictment is required to: “(1) set forth every prima facie element of the offense charged; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the accusations; and (3) enable the defendant to assert a double jeopardy claim against subsequent prosecution.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In short, for an indictment to meet the sufficiency requirement, it must charge every essential element of the crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids executing schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as any plan “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” section 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” which has led “courts [to] treat the terms similarly,” frequently referring to mail and wire fraud precedent when evaluating securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Taking these principles into account, prosecution for securities fraud requires: (1) the presence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the scheme's execution with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The requirement of a “scheme to defraud” necessitates a material falsehood “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [party] to be deceived.” Id. (modification in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). For the “intent to defraud” element, the government must show “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). To summarize, a defendant is guilty of securities fraud when he or she has “‘engaged in deception’” and the goal of “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently asserts deception. However, they argue the indictment does not set forth either a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. According to their argument, the indictment only accuses them of intending to deprive their followers of valuable financial information (rather than money or property), for the purpose of personal gain (as opposed to injuring their followers). Based on the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reach the opposite conclusion.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment, asserting that it fails to allege a scheme to take from their followers a property interest protected by law. Although mere deprivation of potentially valuable economic information does not by itself suffice for a conviction of fraud, the indictment in this case does more; it specifically and properly alleges that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that matter, Ciminelli’s construction firm provided payments to a lobbyist to secure jobs financed by the state. Id. at 309–10. When prosecuting Ciminelli for wire fraud, the government based its case exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory allowed the government to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Stated differently, within the right-to-control theory, the supposed property interest is “the right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court ruled that the federal fraud statutes are intended to protect only traditional property interests, and since “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” a conviction under the right-to-control theory is improper. Id. at 316.

The facts at hand differ from those in Ciminelli. The indictment here does not invoke the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that the defendants misled their followers through false representations to invest in securities—that is, to relinquish money or property. The indictment advances a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” under which a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When the Supreme Court examined the fraudulent-inducement theory, it expressly stated that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement approach pursued here “protects money and property.” Id. Thus, because the indictment alleges that the defendants fraudulently caused followers to part with a recognized property interest by purchasing securities, it does not contravene the holding in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is insufficient because it does not allege that they intended to defraud, asserting instead that it only claims they aimed to profit rather than inflict harm upon any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently addressed and rejected this line of reasoning, clarifying that the fraud statutes do not demand a showing that a victim has incurred financial loss due to a defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant made false statements to secure contracts with a state transportation department, which required the use of a disadvantaged business subcontractor. Kousisis falsely stated that his company would comply with that condition but, after receiving the contracts, utilized the subcontractor solely as a “pass-through” entity. Id. at 1389. Once these misrepresentations surfaced, the government charged Kousisis under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which posits that a person commits fraud by using material misrepresentations to induce a victim into a contractual agreement that involves transferring money or property, irrespective of whether the defendant—who might offer something in exchange—intends to cause the victim a net monetary loss. Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contended that this theory could not be sustained because it allowed for conviction even without proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, finding that a fraud statute violation occurs when someone “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ whether or not the person means to leave the victim financially disadvantaged.” Id. at 1392.

The indictment here clearly sets forth allegations of the defendants’ fraudulent intent toward their followers. It asserts, among other things, that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the law does not require proof of actual monetary harm, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at prevailing market values does not negate the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding that a defendant can be guilty of fraud even where he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). While defendants maintain that their aim was personal gain, not to harm anyone, that distinction is immaterial in this context. The indictment alleges that their primary objective was to obtain money, accomplished through fraudulent inducement of their followers to purchase securities. By alleging that the defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme for profit, the indictment demonstrates that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The fraud is effectuated once the defendants have caused the victim to part with money or property through materially false statements—regardless of whether the victim experiences a net pecuniary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s averment that defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities suffices to state an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that the fraud statutes do not impose a strict one-to-one correspondence between a victim’s loss and a defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (citing Kousisis in a similar setting).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and an intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in line with this opinion.
