Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced indictment on charges of securities fraud arising from their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants made social-media posts containing false statements about their trading positions and the projected value of certain securities, prompting their followers to purchase these securities, thereby causing an artificial price increase and securing profits for themselves. The district court decided to dismiss the indictment, holding that it did not properly state an offense because it alleged only that defendants deprived followers of valuable economic information rather than a conventional property right. Upon review, we find that the indictment adequately sets forth a fraudulent scheme and requisite intent, so we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. Each defendant reportedly amassed substantial followings on social media platforms, represented themselves as expert stock traders, and frequently shared details of their trades online. According to the government, the defendants executed the alleged scheme by acquiring securities, then “pumping” their prices through the dissemination of false or deceptive information on social media to encourage other investors to buy, artificially inflating the security’s price, and subsequently “dumping”—selling—their holdings without disclosure to realize profits. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts the defendants gained $114 million from this scheme. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and multiple counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, finding the indictment did not sufficiently allege that the defendants schemed to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government appealed this ruling in a timely manner.

II

We consider the adequacy of an indictment using de novo review. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept as true the indictment's allegations and assess whether the indictment adequately alleges an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). The indictment is required to “(1) set forth each essential element of the crime charged; (2) clearly notify the defendant of the accusation; and (3) furnish the defendant with protection against double jeopardy in the event of later proceedings.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In short, an indictment’s sufficiency depends on its inclusion of each material element constituting the charged crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute makes it unlawful to engage in any scheme “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” or any scheme “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit is scarce,” § 1348 incorporates fundamental principles from the mail and wire fraud statutes, leading “courts [to] treat the terms similarly” and frequently look to mail and wire fraud precedents when interpreting securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Given that background, the offense of securities fraud consists of (1) a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) the “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. To satisfy the “scheme to defraud” prong, there must be a material misrepresentation made “with the intent to deceive others in order to secure something valuable, such as money, from the deceived [party].” Id. (alteration in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” prong requires “a purpose to (1) deceive, and (2) bring about some harm by means of the deception.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In summary, securities fraud occurs when a defendant has “‘engaged in deception’” and when “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants do not dispute that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception. They argue, however, that it fails to allege both a scheme and intent to defraud, asserting that the indictment only accuses them of seeking to deprive their followers of economic information (rather than money or property) for their own benefit (and not to harm their followers). We reject this argument in light of the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the basis that it failed to claim a scheme aimed at depriving their followers of a legally protected property right. Although it is accurate that mere deprivation of possibly valuable economic information is insufficient for a fraud conviction, in this instance the indictment advanced beyond that point and adequately charged that defendants intended to defraud their followers out of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of important economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist to secure contracts funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. When prosecuting Ciminelli for wire fraud, the government relied exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. The government’s approach was to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Essentially, under this theory, the alleged property at stake is the victim’s “right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Court concluded that the federal fraud laws only safeguard conventional property rights and clarified that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” meaning that “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The facts here differ from those in Ciminelli. The indictment does not rely on the right-to-control theory; instead, it claims that defendants misled their followers into buying securities—essentially, convincing them to relinquish their money or property through deception. The indictment pursues a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” in which a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court’s consideration of the fraudulent-inducement theory reaffirmed that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the rejected right-to-control approach in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory applied here “protects money and property.” Id. The indictment is consistent with Ciminelli because it alleges that defendants deprived their followers of a protected property interest by fraudulently persuading them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting instead that they aimed merely to profit from their conduct, not to harm any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed a comparable contention, determining that the fraud statutes do not necessitate that a victim actually incur financial loss from a defendant's scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant misrepresented facts in order to secure contracts from a state department of transportation. The department required that contractors subcontract with a disadvantaged business, and Kousisis falsely claimed that his company would do so. After obtaining the contracts, Kousisis used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor only as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When the deceit was uncovered, the government charged Kousisis on the basis of the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which posits that a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued that this theory should fail because it would allow a conviction without evidence that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this argument, holding that the fraud statutes are violated when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The indictment in the present case adequately pleads the defendants' intent to defraud their followers. For example, the indictment asserts that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not mandate a showing of actual financial harm, the fact that the followers obtained securities at market value does not nullify the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding liability for fraud even if the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). While defendants claim their motive was profit rather than causing injury to any victim, the distinction is immaterial here. As set forth in the indictment, their aim was to obtain money by means of fraudulently inducing their followers to acquire securities. The fact that the indictment describes a pump-and-dump scheme for the purpose of profit demonstrates that the defendants “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—even if there is no resulting net pecuniary loss for the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. Therefore, the indictment's allegations that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to buy securities sufficiently alleges injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (clarifying that the fraud statutes do not require a perfect correspondence between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in a similar case).

IV

Since the indictment adequately sets forth a scheme and an intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
