Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud in connection with a "pump and dump" operation. According to the superseding indictment, they persuaded individuals following them on social media to buy securities by posting false statements about their own trading positions and the likely future price of the securities. These actions allegedly resulted in artificial price increases, which the defendants exploited for profit. The district court dismissed the indictment, holding that it failed to properly allege an offense since it only claimed that the defendants aimed to withhold economically valuable information from their followers, rather than a conventional property right. Finding that the indictment sufficiently sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a securities “pump and dump” scheme. Each defendant maintained significant audiences across multiple social media channels. They represented themselves as experienced stock traders, routinely sharing details of their trading on these platforms. According to the government, the scheme involved defendants buying certain securities, subsequently “pumping” their prices by posting deceptive and false statements about those securities on social media in order to persuade other investors to buy and thereby artificially drive up prices, and then “dumping” the securities by covertly selling them at a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment claims defendants generated $114 million in gains through this activity. The charges include conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, along with various counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants moved for dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion, reasoning that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme to deprive victims of a recognized property right. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We evaluate the adequacy of an indictment under a de novo standard. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and decide whether it sets forth a criminal offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To satisfy legal requirements, an indictment must: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In essence, an indictment is sufficient only if it alleges every essential element of the offense charged. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute bars both schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” section 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and, accordingly, “courts have given the terms similar treatment,” frequently turning to mail and wire fraud precedents to evaluate securities fraud charges. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Accordingly, securities fraud is comprised of (1) a “scheme to defraud” carried out with (2) an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The element of a “scheme to defraud” involves a material misstatement that is “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). As for “intent to defraud,” this requires both “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). To summarize, securities fraud is committed when a defendant “engaged in deception” and “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of that deception. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, the defendants concede that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception but argue that it fails to set forth a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. According to them, the indictment claims only that they sought to deprive their followers of significant financial information (as opposed to money or property) for their own gain (rather than to harm their followers). Given the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reach a contrary conclusion.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to assert any plan to deprive their followers of a property interest safeguarded by law. Although a person cannot be found guilty of fraud based solely on depriving another of potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in this case goes beyond that, asserting that defendants schemed to defraud their followers out of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a fraud conviction cannot stand merely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that matter, Ciminelli’s construction business engaged a lobbyist to facilitate obtaining state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The government's prosecution of Ciminelli for wire fraud rested exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory involves showing “that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. According to this theory, the property interest in question is the “right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes only cover traditional property interests and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest”; hence, “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The current case presents a different scenario than Ciminelli. The indictment does not assert the right-to-control theory; instead, it alleges that the defendants, through false statements, induced their followers to purchase securities—in effect, causing them to give up money or property. It proceeds on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” under which a perpetrator “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court, in evaluating the fraudulent-inducement theory, explicitly stated that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here “protects money and property.” Id. The indictment, therefore, is consistent with Ciminelli, as it charges that defendants, through fraudulent inducement, deprived their followers of a protected property interest by persuading them to buy securities.

B

Defendants subsequently contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege that they intended to defraud; they assert it only claims that defendants sought personal gain, not that they intended any victim harm. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed an analogous contention, holding that fraud statutes do not require proof that a victim experienced monetary loss due to a defendant's scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant made false statements to secure state transportation contracts. The relevant state regulations mandated contracting with a disadvantaged business, but Kousisis falsely stated his company would satisfy this requirement. Upon receiving the contracts, his company used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor purely as a “pass-through” entity. Id. at 1389. Once the falsehood surfaced, the government prosecuted Kousisis based on a “fraudulent-inducement theory” which posits liability when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis asserted that the fraudulent-inducement theory was invalid, as it could justify a conviction even without evidence the defendant intended to reduce the victim’s economic standing. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this argument, ruling that violating fraud statutes occurs whenever a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The present indictment adequately sets forth defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. For instance, it states that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not require proof of economic loss, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not eliminate the basis for the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding liability for fraud may attach even when the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants maintain that their intent was to profit, not to injure any victim, but this distinction is immaterial under the law. As pleaded in the indictment, defendants’ aim was to acquire money, which they accomplished by fraudulently prompting their followers to buy securities. The assertion that defendants used a pump-and-dump strategy for personal profit establishes their intent to “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—even if the victim does not endure a net pecuniary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. The charge that defendants fraudulently induced followers to buy securities is therefore sufficient to allege injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” where the defendant’s gain matches the victim’s loss); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (implementing Kousisis in a related scenario).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
