Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

Defendants faced charges of securities fraud based on their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. The superseding indictment asserts that defendants persuaded their social-media followers to buy securities by making posts that falsely represented both their own trading positions and the likely future prices of the securities. This conduct allegedly resulted in artificially increased security prices from which defendants benefitted financially. The district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that it did not allege deprivation of a traditional property interest, asserting instead that defendants deprived followers only of potentially useful economic information. We determine, however, that the indictment adequately pleads a scheme and intent to defraud. Accordingly, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities fraud scheme. According to the government, each defendant amassed a substantial audience on several social media platforms, portrayed themselves as expert stock traders, and routinely shared their trading activities online. The alleged scheme involved purchasing a security, then “‘pumping’ the security’s price by disseminating false and misleading statements about the security on [social media] to induce other investors to buy in and inflate the price artificially,” after which the defendants would “dump” the security by covertly selling it for profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further alleges that the defendants realized $114 million in gains from the purported scheme. The defendants were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, along with multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, reasoning that the indictment did not sufficiently claim that the defendants conspired to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

Our examination of whether an indictment is sufficient is conducted anew. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept as accurate the indictment’s allegations and assess if they state an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment is required to: “(1) specify every essential element constituting the alleged offense; (2) adequately advise the accused of the charges against him; and (3) allow the accused to raise double jeopardy as a bar to any subsequent prosecution.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Ultimately, an indictment satisfies sufficiency requirements only when it asserts each material element of the crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids engaging in schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” so “courts have given the terms similar treatment,” frequently referring to mail and wire fraud precedents when examining securities fraud offenses. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Within this framework, securities fraud necessitates (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the action be carried out with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. To establish the “scheme to defraud” element, there must be a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” requirement is satisfied by showing “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (citing Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In essence, an individual commits securities fraud if he “‘engaged in deception’” and if “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants acknowledge that the indictment properly alleges deceptive conduct. However, they argue that it fails to allege either a scheme to defraud or intent to defraud. According to Defendants, the indictment only asserts that they aimed to withhold valuable economic information from their followers to benefit themselves, not that they intended to deprive their followers of money or property. Upon consideration of the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we do not agree with Defendants’ position.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the basis that it fails to allege a scheme in which their followers were deprived of a constitutionally protected property interest. Although it is accurate that fraud convictions cannot rest on the mere deprivation of potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in this case alleges more, specifically that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of actual money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot be founded exclusively on the deprivation of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction business paid a lobbyist to secure government-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution, in seeking Ciminelli’s conviction for wire fraud, rested solely on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory asserts that the government can “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. That is, the victim’s property is identified as “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court held that federal fraud statutes are designed to protect traditional property rights, and since “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the “right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The present case is distinguishable from Ciminelli. The indictment here does not invoke the right-to-control theory; instead, it claims that defendants, by means of misrepresentations, persuaded their followers to purchase securities—thereby parting with their money or property. The indictment advances a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court has recognized that this theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory presented here “protects money and property.” Id. As such, the indictment does not violate the holding in Ciminelli, as it charges that defendants deprived their followers of a protected property interest by fraudulently inducing them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants subsequently contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting that it claims their motive was to gain profit from their conduct rather than to injure any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed a comparable argument, holding that the fraud statutes do not require proof that the victim experienced a financial loss due to the defendant’s actions. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, the defendant, Kousisis, had made false statements to secure contracts from a state transportation department that mandated contractors to engage a disadvantaged business as a subcontractor. Kousisis misrepresented that his business would comply with this requirement. After securing the contracts, Kousisis merely used the disadvantaged business as a “pass-through” entity. Id. at 1389. Once his deception was uncovered, the government prosecuted him under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which provides that a defendant violates the law by using a significant misrepresentation to lure a victim into a contract that necessitates transferring money or property, “regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contended that this theory was improper because it allowed a conviction without showing that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this position, ruling that a defendant breaches the fraud statutes by scheming “to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The indictment in this case adequately charges the defendants with the intent to defraud their followers. Specifically, it claims defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not demand a showing of actual economic damage, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not defeat the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding liability for fraud even where the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). While defendants assert that their purpose was to generate profit and not to harm any victims, this is not a meaningful distinction in this context. As stated in the indictment, the aim was to secure money by fraudulently influencing followers to buy securities, demonstrating that defendants “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The crime of fraud is established once the defendant secures a deprivation of money or property through materially false representations, regardless of whether the victim ultimately loses money. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s assertion that defendants fraudulently induced purchases by their followers suffices to allege injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that fraud statutes do not require that every loss to a victim be a direct gain to the defendant); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (citing Kousisis in a related setting).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
