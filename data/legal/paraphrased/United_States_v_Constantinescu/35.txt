Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-20143  
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud based on their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, they encouraged their social-media followers to buy securities by making posts that falsely described both their trading activities and the future prices of certain securities, thereby manipulating the prices upward for their own gain. The district court dismissed the indictment, holding that it did not adequately state an offense because it only claimed that the defendants deprived followers of possible valuable economic information rather than a conventional property interest. Since we find the indictment adequately pleads both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a “pump and dump” scheme involving securities. According to the allegations, each defendant maintained substantial followings on multiple social media platforms, portraying themselves as expert stock traders and routinely sharing their trading actions online. To execute the purported scheme, the government claims the defendants would acquire a security, “‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” and subsequently “dump” their holdings by covertly selling the securities for a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further claims the defendants realized $114 million in profits from this conduct. Charges brought include conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and various counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed motions to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the motions to dismiss, finding the indictment insufficient because it did not allege the defendants engaged in a scheme to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We assess the adequacy of an indictment with fresh review. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The allegations set forth in the indictment must be accepted as true, and the inquiry is whether those allegations state a criminal offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To satisfy legal requirements, an indictment needs to: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Ultimately, an indictment is sufficient only if it charges every essential element of the crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids the use of schemes to "defraud any person in connection with... any security" as well as schemes "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of... any security." 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit," § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," leading courts to interpret those terms similarly and to look to mail and wire fraud decisions when evaluating securities fraud cases. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Under these shared principles, securities fraud is established by proof of: (1) a "scheme to defraud"; and (2) execution with "intent to defraud." Id. at 339. For the "scheme to defraud" requirement, there must be a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived." Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The "intent to defraud" requirement necessitates "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Accordingly, securities fraud occurs when the defendant has "'engaged in deception'" and "'obtaining the victim’s money or property' was 'an object' of his fraud." Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants do not dispute that the indictment sufficiently charges deception. However, they assert that the indictment fails to allege a scheme to defraud or the intent to defraud, arguing it charges only that they sought to deprive followers of valuable economic information—rather than money or property—so as to enrich themselves, not to harm their followers. Based on the Supreme Court’s holdings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject this argument.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to assert a scheme aimed at depriving their followers of a legally protected property right. Although mere deprivation of potentially valuable economic information is not sufficient for a fraud conviction, in this instance, the indictment alleges beyond that—specifically, that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In the Ciminelli case, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm made payments to a lobbyist to secure contracts funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. When prosecuting Ciminelli for wire fraud, the government relied exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory allows the government to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Essentially, according to the right-to-control theory, the relevant “property” at issue is the “right to control [one's] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that because federal fraud statutes only safeguard traditional property rights, and “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the right-to-control theory “cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

The circumstances of the present case differ from those in Ciminelli. The indictment does not invoke the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that the defendants misled their followers into buying securities, i.e., relinquishing their money or property, through false representations. The indictment sets forth a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In its discussion of the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court made clear that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory disapproved in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here “protects money and property.” Id. The indictment is therefore consistent with Ciminelli because it alleges that the defendants deprived their followers of a protected property right by fraudulently inducing them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient for failing to allege intent to defraud, arguing instead that it only claims the defendants aimed to make a profit from their actions rather than to inflict harm on victims. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable contention, clarifying that fraud statutes do not necessitate that a victim actually experience financial loss as a result of the defendant’s conduct. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant secured contracts with a state department of transportation through false representations; the state’s contracts mandated partnerships with disadvantaged businesses, but Kousisis misrepresented compliance with this criterion. After obtaining the contracts, the disadvantaged-business subcontractor was merely used as a “pass-through” entity. Id. at 1389. Upon discovery of this deception, the government prosecuted Kousisis under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” under which a defendant violates the law by “us[ing] a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contended that this theory was improper because it would permit conviction without proof that the defendant intended to negatively affect the victim’s finances. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this position, holding that one can be found to have committed fraud under the statute simply by “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. The present indictment adequately asserts that defendants intended to defraud their followers, stating, for instance, that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the law does not demand that economic harm befall the victim, the mere fact that followers received securities at fair market value does not eliminate the basis for the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (“defendant may be liable in fraud even when he ‘provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return’”). Defendants' argument that their intent was profit, not victim harm, is immaterial here. According to the indictment, defendants’ objective was to acquire money through fraudulent inducement of securities purchases by their followers. Their alleged pump-and-dump scheme to realize a profit demonstrates that they were “motivated by money.” Id. at 1397. It is established that “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—irrespective of whether the victim incurred a net economic loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s charge that defendants fraudulently persuaded followers to purchase securities sufficiently alleges an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that fraud statutes do not impose a “mirror image” requirement necessitating that the victim’s loss mirror the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in a comparable setting).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and an intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
