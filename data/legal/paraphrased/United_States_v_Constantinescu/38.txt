Title: United States v. Constantinescu  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 2, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-20143  
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud stemming from their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. The superseding indictment claims that the defendants persuaded their followers on social media to buy securities by publishing posts that falsely represented both their own trading activities and the anticipated prices of securities. This conduct purportedly caused an artificial increase in the securities' prices, enabling the defendants to profit. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, finding that the indictment did not adequately allege an offense since it claimed only that the defendants aimed to withhold potentially important economic information from their followers, rather than deprive them of a conventional property right. However, because we find that the indictment properly sets forth a scheme to defraud and requisite intent, we REVERSE the district court's dismissal.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a plan to conduct a “pump and dump” of securities. According to the government, each defendant amassed a sizable following on multiple social media platforms, where they portrayed themselves as knowledgeable stock traders and routinely shared details of their trades online. The government asserts that, as part of the alleged “pump and dump” operation, defendants acquired securities and then “’pumped’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” after which they would “dump” by covertly selling their holdings for a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further claims defendants earned $114 million from this scheme. Charges filed included conspiracy to commit securities fraud in contravention of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and various counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed motions to dismiss. The district court granted those motions, finding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme to deprive victims of a conventional property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

The sufficiency of an indictment is reviewed de novo. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). As explained in United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)), the court must treat the indictment’s allegations as true and assess whether an offense is properly alleged. For an indictment to suffice, it must: (1) list each essential element of the alleged crime; (2) adequately apprise the defendant of the accusations; and (3) enable the defendant to plead double jeopardy in the event of later prosecutions. United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In short, a sufficient indictment sets forth every material element of the charged offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids both schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” and plans “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “there is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “incorporates key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” leading courts to interpret the language similarly and to frequently consult mail and wire fraud cases when assessing securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying these standards, securities fraud consists of (1) participation in a “scheme to defraud” and (2) acting with an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” prong requires a material misstatement intended to mislead others with the aim of securing something of value, such as money, from the deceived party. Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). To satisfy the “intent to defraud” requirement, there must be an intent both “to deceive” and “to cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In summary, securities fraud is established when a defendant “engaged in deception” and “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of the fraudulent conduct. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception. Nevertheless, they argue that it fails to set forth facts showing a scheme or intent to defraud. Their position is that the indictment only claims they sought to withhold valuable economic information from their followers (rather than money or property) for their own benefit, without intent to harm the followers. However, considering the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject that argument.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly granted dismissal of the indictment on the grounds that it fails to claim a scheme that would deprive their followers of a legally protected property right. Although fraud convictions cannot rest solely on depriving a person of valuable economic information, the indictment in this case did more; it sufficiently alleged that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely upon the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm hired a lobbyist to assist in acquiring contracts funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution charged Ciminelli with wire fraud exclusively under a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory contends that wire fraud can be demonstrated when a defendant engages in a scheme to deprive a victim of material economic information necessary for making discretionary financial decisions. Id. at 310. Stated differently, the victim’s property, according to the right-to-control approach, is understood as “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes are limited to safeguarding conventional property interests and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” thereby precluding convictions based on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 316.

The facts of this case differ from those in Ciminelli. The indictment here does not reference the right-to-control theory; instead, it asserts that defendants, by making misrepresentations, caused followers to invest in securities—that is, to relinquish money or property. The indictment is predicated on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which involves a defendant deploying a material misrepresentation to deceive a victim into agreeing to a contract that obliges the victim to hand over money or property. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. Upon reviewing the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that this theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Contrary to the rejected right-to-control theory in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory as pled here “protects money and property.” Id. The indictment is not contrary to the holding in Ciminelli because it charges the defendants with fraudulently causing their followers to part with a recognized property interest by inducing them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient for failing to allege an intent to defraud, asserting that the indictment merely accuses them of seeking profit, rather than inflicting harm upon any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a similar contention, holding that the federal fraud statutes do not necessitate that a victim actually experience economic loss as a result of a defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, the defendant misrepresented his company’s qualifications to procure contracts with a state department of transportation, which mandated that contractors engage a disadvantaged business as a subcontractor. Kousisis falsely indicated his company would comply, but after obtaining the contracts, he merely used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor as a nominal “pass-through” entity. Id. at 1389. After this deception was uncovered, prosecutors charged Kousisis under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” under which “a defendant employs a material falsehood to deceive a victim into entering a contract resulting in the transfer of money or property—irrespective of whether the perpetrator, who may offer something in exchange, intends to cause the victim a net financial loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis objected to this theory, arguing it permitted conviction without showing “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected that argument, clarifying that fraud statutes are violated when an individual “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ whether or not the victim is left economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

Here, the indictment adequately alleges that the defendants had intent to defraud their followers. For instance, it states that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not require proof of actual economic injury, the fact that followers acquired securities at market value does not negate the existence of fraud. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding that a defendant may still be held liable for fraud even if they “provide something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants’ assertion that they were motivated by profit, not by a desire to harm victims, is legally immaterial in this context. The indictment alleges that their goal was to obtain money, achieved by deceptively convincing their followers to purchase securities. Allegations that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme for profit demonstrate that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The law provides that “[a] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses,” regardless of whether the victim ultimately sustains a net financial loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. The allegation that defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities is therefore sufficient to allege injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (explaining that fraud statutes do not demand a “mirror image” between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis to similar facts).

IV

As the indictment properly sets forth a fraudulent scheme and intent, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
