Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

Defendants faced charges of securities fraud arising from their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, defendants persuaded individuals who followed them on social media to buy securities by posting misleading information about their own trading activities and the likely value of those securities, thereby artificially increasing the securities’ prices and enabling themselves to benefit financially. The district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that it did not properly allege an offense, asserting it only claimed defendants deprived followers of potentially advantageous economic data rather than of a conventional property interest. However, finding that the indictment adequately alleges both a fraudulent scheme and the intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a securities “pump and dump” scheme. According to the government, each defendant maintained a significant presence and following across several social media platforms. They promoted themselves as expert stock traders, regularly sharing their trading transactions on social media. The prosecution contends that the defendants would initiate the scheme by buying a security and then “pumping” its price—that is, disseminating false and deceptive information regarding the security on social media to convince other investors to purchase the security and thereby artificially inflate its value—before “dumping,” or covertly selling the security to realize profits. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment further alleges that defendants gained $114 million in profits through this scheme. The defendants were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, along with several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea; the other defendants filed motions seeking to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted dismissal, finding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme by the defendants to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We examine the adequacy of an indictment without deference. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the factual assertions within the indictment as true and assess whether those allegations constitute an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To meet the requirements, an indictment needs to: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Stated differently, an indictment is adequate if it charges every essential element of the crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute makes it unlawful to engage in any scheme “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” or to obtain money or property “by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises” tied to the purchase or sale of a security. See U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is limited authority interpreting this statute within this circuit, § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes.” Courts therefore typically interpret its provisions in parallel with those statutes and often refer to precedents in mail and wire fraud when evaluating securities fraud cases. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Under these principles, the government must prove two main elements for securities fraud: (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the scheme’s execution with an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The first element requires that the defendant made a material misrepresentation with the purpose of deceiving others to acquire something valuable, like money, from the victim. Id. (modification in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). To establish intent to defraud, the prosecution must show an intent both “to deceive” and to cause some harm as a result of the deception. Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Accordingly, securities fraud occurs when the defendant has “‘engaged in deception’” with the aim that “‘[o]btaining the victim’s money or property’” constituted an objective of the fraudulent conduct. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants in this case admit the indictment sufficiently alleges deception; however, they maintain it fails to plead either a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. Specifically, they argue the indictment only claims they sought to deprive their followers of economic information for their personal gain rather than to inflict financial harm. We conclude, in view of the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, that their position is incorrect.

A

Defendants contend that the district court’s dismissal of the indictment was correct, asserting that the indictment fails to claim a scheme to deprive their followers of any protected property interest. Although a fraud conviction cannot rest solely on depriving someone of potentially valuable economic information, the present indictment goes beyond that and sufficiently alleges that defendants aimed to defraud their followers out of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a fraud charge cannot be upheld merely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that matter, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist in pursuit of securing state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution’s wire fraud case relied exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This approach sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. The core of the right-to-control theory is that the victim’s property interest lies in the “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that the federal fraud statutes only shield traditional property rights, stating that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” and thus, “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

The circumstances of the current case differ from Ciminelli. The indictment in this matter does not refer to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it accuses defendants of inducing their followers through false representations to purchase securities—effectively causing them to part with money or property. This constitutes a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” wherein a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court, when considering the fraudulent-inducement theory, emphasized that this theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control approach rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory presented here “protects money and property.” Id. Accordingly, the indictment does not conflict with Ciminelli, as it asserts defendants used fraud to deprive their followers of a protected property interest by persuading them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting that the indictment states only that defendants intended to benefit financially from their actions rather than inflict harm on any victim. However, a comparable contention was recently rejected by the Supreme Court, which found that fraud statutes do not mandate that victims actually incur an economic loss due to a defendant’s conduct. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant obtained state transportation contracts through false representations, specifically by claiming compliance with a requirement to use a disadvantaged-business subcontractor, when in fact this entity functioned only as a “‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. After discovery of his falsehood, the government charged him under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which covers instances where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contended that this theory was invalid since it permitted liability without showing an intent “to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected that claim, deciding that a defendant violates the fraud statutes by “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. 

In this case, the indictment adequately alleges that defendants had an intent to defraud their followers. For example, it specifies that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not necessitate proof of actual economic loss, the fact that defendants’ followers acquired securities at market value does not preclude fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding that fraud liability may attach even where the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants maintain that their aim was personal profit rather than causing injury, but in this context, this distinction is immaterial. According to the indictment, defendants’ goal was to obtain money, which they accomplished by fraudulently inducing others to purchase securities. The indictment’s description of defendants engaging in a “pump and dump” scheme to realize a profit demonstrates that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—whether or not the victim incurs a net pecuniary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s claim that defendants induced their followers to buy securities through fraudulent means suffices to allege injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” relationship between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in a similar situation).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
