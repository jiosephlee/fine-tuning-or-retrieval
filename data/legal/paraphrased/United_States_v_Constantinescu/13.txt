Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud related to their participation in a "pump and dump" operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants persuaded their social-media followers to buy securities by posting false statements about their own trading positions and the securities' expected prices, thereby artificially raising the prices and profiting as a result. The district court dismissed these charges, finding that the indictment did not allege an offense, since it only claimed the defendants aimed to withhold possibly valuable financial information from their followers instead of depriving them of a conventional property interest. However, because we find that the indictment adequately alleges both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s decision to dismiss the indictment.

I

The government accuses Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight of orchestrating a “pump and dump” scheme involving securities. According to the government, each defendant maintained a substantial social media presence on several platforms, representing themselves as knowledgeable stock traders and frequently sharing updates about their trades online. The scheme allegedly involved the defendants buying particular securities, then attempting to “‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media], thereby encouraging other investors to purchase and artificially inflating the price,” before “dumping” the security by covertly selling it for a profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. It is further alleged that the defendants generated $114 million in profits from this conduct. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as multiple securities fraud counts under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted their motion, finding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege that defendants intended to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government promptly appealed that decision.

II

We examine an indictment's adequacy using a de novo standard of review. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment's allegations as accurate and assess whether a crime is properly alleged. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment is required to: “(1) allege every essential element of the offense charged; (2) adequately notify the accused of the specific accusations; and (3) enable the accused to invoke protection against double jeopardy in later prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In short, an indictment is legally sufficient only if it sets forth all material elements of the charged offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids engaging in schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as efforts “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” the statute “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” prompting courts to interpret its terms in a similar manner, frequently referring to mail and wire fraud precedent when reviewing securities fraud claims. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying these concepts, securities fraud consists of (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) a concurrent “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. To establish the “scheme to defraud” prong, a material misrepresentation must be made “with the intent to deceive others in order to secure something of value, such as money, from the [entity] being deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). For the “intent to defraud” requirement, it must be shown that there was “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm as a result of that deception.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Therefore, the act of securities fraud is established when a defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and the “obtaining of the victim’s money or property” served as “an object” of his fraudulent scheme. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently pleads deception; however, they argue the indictment does not allege the existence of a scheme to defraud or the requisite intent to defraud. They assert the indictment only claims they intended to deprive their followers of valuable economic information, rather than money or property, for the purpose of self-enrichment instead of causing harm to their followers. Given the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject this argument.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to assert a scheme depriving their followers of a protected property interest. Although a fraud conviction cannot be predicated solely on depriving someone of potentially valuable economic information, in this case, the indictment does more by sufficiently alleging that the defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest merely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. The facts in Ciminelli involved a construction company, led by Ciminelli, paying a lobbyist to secure contracts funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution charged Ciminelli with wire fraud, relying only on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. Under this theory, the government sought to prove wire fraud by demonstrating that the defendant devised a scheme to deny a victim crucial economic information needed for discretionary decision-making. Id. at 310. Essentially, the theory posited that the relevant property interest was “the right to control [the victim’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes safeguard only traditional property interests, and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest;” thus, “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The present circumstances differ from Ciminelli. Nowhere does the indictment refer to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that the defendants, via misrepresentations, persuaded their followers to buy securities—specifically, to relinquish money or property. The indictment advances a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” defined as a scenario where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court, upon reviewing the fraudulent-inducement theory, clarified that this theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the right-to-control theory, which Ciminelli rejected, the fraudulent-inducement theory “protects money and property.” Id. Because the indictment alleges that the defendants deprived their followers of a protected property interest through fraudulent inducement to purchase securities, it does not conflict with the Ciminelli decision.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, claiming it only accuses them of seeking to profit from their actions rather than causing harm to any victims. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable contention, holding that fraud statutes do not require victims to suffer financial loss as a result of a defendant’s scheme. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In the Kousisis case, the defendant misrepresented information to secure contracts with a state transportation department. The department mandated contractors to partner with a disadvantaged business; Kousisis falsely asserted that his company would meet this requirement. After obtaining the contracts, Kousisis engaged the disadvantaged-business subcontractor merely as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When these falsehoods were discovered, the government prosecuted Kousisis under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” in which a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis argued against this theory, asserting that it allows for conviction even without proof that the defendant aimed to damage the victim’s financial standing. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that a violation of the fraud statutes occurs if a defendant is “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. Here, the indictment adequately sets forth defendants’ intent to defraud their followers, stating, among other allegations, that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not mandate a showing of actual economic injury, the fact that followers received securities at market value does not negate the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding a defendant may be guilty of fraud even if he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants assert that their goal was personal profit rather than injury to victims. Nevertheless, in this context, such a distinction is immaterial. The indictment specifies that defendants sought to obtain money through their fraudulent actions in encouraging followers to purchase securities. Their alleged pump and dump for personal gain establishes that their motive was monetary. Id. at 1397. “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses,” whether or not the victim has sustained a net financial loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s assertion that defendants fraudulently persuaded followers to acquire securities sufficiently alleges a legally cognizable injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting the fraud statutes do not necessitate a “mirror image” where the victim’s loss directly corresponds to the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying the Kousisis standard in analogous circumstances).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
