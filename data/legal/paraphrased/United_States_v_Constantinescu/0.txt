Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced indictment for securities fraud arising from their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants persuaded their followers on social media to buy securities by making posts that falsely described their own trading positions and the likely price trajectory of those securities, thus artificially boosting prices for their benefit. The district court dismissed the indictment, finding it inadequate because it claimed only that the defendants aimed to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information, not a conventional property interest. However, as we determine that the indictment properly sets forth a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a securities “pump and dump” scheme. According to the allegations, each defendant maintained a substantial presence on social media platforms and portrayed themselves as experienced stock traders, regularly sharing their trading activity online. The government contends that, in executing their “pump and dump” scheme, defendants would acquire a security, then artificially inflate the security’s price by disseminating “false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” before covertly selling the security at a profit—actions described as “dumping.” See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment claims that defendants gained $114 million from this conduct. Defendants faced charges for conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and multiple counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed motions to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted dismissal, finding that the indictment did not allege a scheme to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government subsequently filed a timely appeal.

II

We consider the adequacy of an indictment using a de novo standard of review. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). As stated in United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)), “[t]he court must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and assess whether they constitute an offense.” To be sufficient, an indictment is required to: “(1) set forth each essential element of the charged crime; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the charges brought against him; and (3) enable the defendant to invoke a double jeopardy defense in any later prosecution.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Thus, as summarized by United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up), an indictment must allege every material element of the offense in order to be sufficient.

III

The securities fraud statute forbids both schemes “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” and schemes intended “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” resulting in courts generally treating the terms in a similar manner and frequently looking to mail and wire fraud cases for guidance when interpreting securities fraud charges. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). In this framework, a charge of securities fraud must show (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the presence of an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. To establish a “scheme to defraud,” there must be a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The requirement of “intent to defraud” is satisfied by demonstrating “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). Summarizing, a person commits securities fraud if he “engaged in deception” and securing the victim’s “money or property” was “an object” of the fraudulent activity. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception. However, they argue that the indictment fails to state either a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. They assert that the indictment only claims they aimed to deprive their followers of valuable economic information (not money or property) for their own benefit (not to harm their followers). Based on the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we do not agree with defendants’ argument.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment, asserting it fails to allege a scheme to deprive their followers of a protected property interest. Although a defendant cannot be found guilty of fraud for merely depriving someone of potentially valuable economic information, the present indictment goes beyond that by adequately alleging that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a fraud conviction cannot rest solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist in an effort to secure state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. When prosecuting Ciminelli for wire fraud, the government relied exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory allowed the government to attempt to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Thus, under this theory, the property at issue is “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that because federal fraud statutes only protect traditional property interests, and since “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” a fraud conviction cannot be supported by the right-to-control theory. Id. at 316. The facts here differ from those in Ciminelli. The indictment does not invoke the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that defendants, by making misrepresentations, convinced their followers to purchase securities—in effect, to relinquish money or property. The indictment proceeds under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In its examination of the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Distinct from the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory asserted here “protects money and property.” Id. Therefore, the indictment is consistent with Ciminelli, as it claims that defendants, by fraudulent inducement, caused their followers to surrender a protected property interest by purchasing securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient for failing to allege an intent to defraud, arguing that it charges them with seeking profit from their scheme rather than intending to harm any victims. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable claim, determining that the fraud statutes do not require that a victim actually sustain an economic loss due to the defendant’s actions. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant made false statements to secure contracts with a state transportation department, which mandated that contractors hire a disadvantaged business as a subcontractor. Kousisis misrepresented his compliance with this requirement and, upon obtaining the contracts, merely used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor as a “pass-through” entity. Id. at 1389. After this deception was uncovered, the government charged Kousisis under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which involves a defendant making a significant misrepresentation to trick a victim into a contract that entails transferring money or property, regardless of whether the perpetrator, who may provide something of equal value, aims to inflict a net pecuniary loss on the victim. Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis maintained that this theory should fail because it allows for conviction even without evidence that the defendant intended to negatively affect the victim’s financial position. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, finding that a fraud violation occurs when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. Here, the indictment adequately alleges that defendants intended to defraud their followers; for instance, it claims defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not require proof of actual economic loss, the fact that defendants’ followers may have received securities at market value does not defeat the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding that fraud liability may attach even where the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants assert they intended only to make a profit, not to harm anyone, but in this case, such a distinction is immaterial. The indictment specifies that defendants’ goal was to acquire money by fraudulently encouraging their followers to buy securities. That the defendants sought profit through a pump-and-dump scheme indicates their objective was monetary gain. Id. at 1397. As stated, “[a] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—even if the victim did not suffer a net economic loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s allegation that defendants fraudulently induced followers to buy securities sufficiently pleads an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (clarifying that fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” loss-to-gain correspondence); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis to a similar fact pattern).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
