Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced indictment on securities fraud charges arising from their participation in a so-called “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants encouraged their followers on social media to buy particular securities by posting false statements concerning both their own trading activities and the likely market price of those securities; this conduct allegedly caused an artificial increase in the securities’ prices, permitting the defendants to benefit financially. The district court dismissed the indictment on the grounds that its allegations amounted only to efforts by the defendants to withhold possibly valuable economic information from their followers, rather than depriving them of a conventional property interest. Since we determine that the indictment properly alleges both a scheme to defraud and the requisite intent, we REVERSE the district court’s decision to dismiss the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight executed a “pump and dump” securities fraud scheme. Each defendant maintained substantial followings on numerous social media outlets. They represented themselves as expert stock traders and routinely shared their trading decisions online. According to the government’s allegations, the defendants would acquire securities, then “‘pump’ the price of that security by disseminating false or deceptive statements about the security on [social media] to prompt other investors to purchase the security and thereby unnaturally drive up its price,” before “dumping” their holdings covertly by selling for a gain. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment asserts that the defendants obtained $114 million in profits from their activities. Charges brought included conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and multiple counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the others sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted dismissal, determining that the indictment did not allege a scheme by defendants to deprive victims of any conventional property interest. The government appealed in a timely fashion.

II

We conduct a de novo assessment of whether an indictment is sufficient. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment’s factual assertions as accurate and evaluate if those allegations state an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To be valid, an indictment has to: “(1) list each essential element of the alleged crime; (2) adequately advise the defendant of the charges against him; and (3) enable the defendant to plead double jeopardy as a defense to any later prosecution for the same conduct.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In summary, for an indictment to satisfy legal requirements, it must include all material elements of the crime. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids engaging in schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as using “false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises” to obtain “any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “this circuit” has “scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes.” Accordingly, “courts have given the terms similar treatment” and frequently consult mail and wire fraud precedent when evaluating securities fraud cases. See United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). In accordance with these concepts, the elements required for securities fraud are: (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. To satisfy the “scheme to defraud” requirement, there must be a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (modification in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” element demands “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In essence, a defendant is guilty of securities fraud where he has “‘engaged in deception’” and obtaining the victim’s “money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (citing Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants here acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception. However, they argue the indictment does not properly allege the existence of a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud, claiming instead that it merely asserts an intent to deprive followers of valuable economic information (as opposed to money or property), for their personal gain (not to harm the followers). We find, contrary to their assertions and based on the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, that their argument is unpersuasive.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to allege a scheme to deprive their followers of a property interest protected by law. Although it is accurate that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in this case extends beyond that and appropriately alleges that defendants devised a scheme to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist to secure contracts financed by the state. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution's wire fraud case against Ciminelli was based exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to that theory, the government aimed to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Put differently, the property at issue under the right-to-control theory is the victim’s “right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes safeguard only conventional property rights and, since “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the right-to-control theory cannot serve as the basis for a fraud conviction. Id. at 316.

The circumstances presented here differ from Ciminelli. The indictment does not reference the right-to-control theory. Instead, it charges that the defendants used misrepresentations to induce their followers to buy securities, meaning they allegedly caused the victims to relinquish money or property. The indictment is premised on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which occurs when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court’s review of this fraudulent-inducement theory made clear that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). In contrast to the right-to-control approach rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory invoked here “protects money and property.” Id. Since the indictment charges that the defendants unlawfully obtained a recognized property interest through fraudulent inducement, it does not violate the principles set forth in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting instead that the indictment claims defendants sought personal profit without intending harm to any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable argument, clarifying that fraud statutes do not necessitate that a victim experience monetary loss as a result of the defendant’s scheme. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In the Kousisis decision, the defendant misrepresented facts to secure contracts with a state department of transportation. The state’s requirements obligated contractors to subcontract with a disadvantaged business, and Kousisis inaccurately asserted that his company would meet this stipulation. Once the contracts were awarded, Kousisis utilized the disadvantaged-business subcontractor merely as a “pass-through” entity. Id. at 1389. Following discovery of this deception, charges were brought against Kousisis under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which allows conviction where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis’s defense was that the fraudulent-inducement theory was invalid unless the prosecution could demonstrate that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this proposition, affirming that the fraud statutes are violated when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The present indictment adequately asserts that the defendants intended to defraud their followers. As an example, the indictment avers that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the statutes governing fraud do not demand proof of actual economic injury, the fact that followers received securities at market value does not negate the fraud allegations. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding a defendant may be culpable for fraud even when he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants maintain that their aim was profit, not injury to any victim. Nonetheless, in this context, such a distinction lacks significance. According to the indictment, defendants' goal was to acquire money through fraudulently influencing their followers’ purchases of securities. The claim that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme to earn profit shows that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—irrespective of any net financial loss by the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. The allegations in the indictment regarding fraudulent inducement of security purchases are sufficient to allege injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” exchange where the defendant’s gain equals the victim’s loss); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (interpreting Kousisis similarly).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and an intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s decision to dismiss the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
