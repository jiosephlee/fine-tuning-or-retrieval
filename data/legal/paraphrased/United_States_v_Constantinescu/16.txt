Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud in connection to their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants persuaded individuals following them on social media to buy certain securities by posting false information regarding their own trading activities and the likely future value of those securities. This conduct allegedly resulted in artificial inflation of the securities’ prices, from which the defendants allegedly benefited financially. The district court dismissed these charges, reasoning that the indictment did not properly state an offense since it only asserted that the defendants attempted to withhold possibly valuable economic information, not deprive anyone of a conventional property interest. However, as we determine that the indictment adequately asserts both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent, we REVERSE the district court’s decision to dismiss the indictment.

I

According to the government, Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight were involved in executing a “pump and dump” scheme related to securities. Each defendant maintained a significant presence and following on multiple social media outlets. They represented themselves as experienced stock traders and regularly shared details about their trading actions online. As part of their alleged “pump and dump” operation, the government asserts that defendants acquired securities and proceeded to “‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” before “dumping” the securities by covertly selling them for financial gain. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment claims that the defendants earned $114 million through this activity. Charges brought against them included conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed motions to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, reasoning that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme to deprive victims of a conventional property interest. The government filed a timely appeal from that decision.

II

Our examination of an indictment’s sufficiency is conducted de novo. See United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court “must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and assess whether they constitute an offense.” United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To be sufficient, an indictment must: “(1) set forth each element necessary to establish a prima facie case of the alleged crime; (2) adequately apprise the defendant of the specific charges brought; and (3) equip the defendant with protection from subsequent prosecution under the double jeopardy clause.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). “Ultimately, a valid indictment must charge every essential element of the crime.” United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute makes it unlawful to engage in schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “there is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” leading courts to treat the terms similarly and frequently look to mail and wire fraud case law when examining securities fraud offenses. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Based on these principles, a securities fraud offense consists of (1) a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) the “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The element of a “scheme to defraud” requires that there be a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). For the “intent to defraud,” what is required is “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). To summarize, securities fraud is committed when a defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and when “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” is “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently alleges deception. However, they argue that the indictment fails to allege a scheme to defraud or the requisite intent to defraud. According to them, the indictment only claims they sought to deprive followers of valuable economic information—not money or property—to benefit themselves, not to harm followers. We disagree with that argument given the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to set forth a scheme to deprive their followers of a legally protected property interest. Although depriving an individual solely of potentially valuable economic information cannot support a fraud conviction, as a general matter, the indictment in question goes further by properly alleging that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot be predicated only on depriving someone of valuable economic information, U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction company paid a lobbyist in an effort to secure state-financed jobs. Id. at 309–10. During Ciminelli’s wire fraud prosecution, the government relied exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to the right-to-control theory, prosecutors aimed to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Put simply, under that theory, the property at issue is “the right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that only traditional property interests are protected by the federal fraud statutes, and that “the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” making the right-to-control theory an improper foundation for a fraud conviction. Id. at 316. The facts of this case are distinguishable from those in Ciminelli. The indictment in this case does not reference the right-to-control theory. Rather, it alleges that the defendants, by making misrepresentations, persuaded their followers to purchase securities, thereby relinquishing money or property. The indictment asserts a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which applies when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In considering the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory that was rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue “protects money and property.” Id. Because the indictment asserts that defendants obtained a protected property interest from their followers by fraudulently inducing them to purchase securities, it does not contravene Ciminelli.

B

Defendants subsequently contend that the indictment is deficient for failing to allege intent to defraud, asserting that the allegations indicate defendants sought to benefit financially from their conduct rather than inflict harm on any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently dismissed an analogous contention, clarifying that the fraud statutes do not mandate the victim to sustain economic harm as a result of the defendant’s actions. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant obtained contracts with a state transportation department by misrepresentation. The state mandated that contractors work with disadvantaged businesses, but Kousisis falsely attested that his company would satisfy this requirement. Once awarded the contracts, his business merely used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor as a “pass-through” entity. Id. at 1389. When these misrepresentations were discovered, authorities prosecuted Kousisis under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which posits a violation occurs when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis objected to this theory, asserting it permits conviction without evidence showing the defendant intended financial harm to the victim. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that fraud is established when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. The indictment in this case adequately pleads defendants’ intent to defraud their followers. It specifically states that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because actual economic loss is not required under the fraud statutes, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not nullify the charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding that fraud liability attaches even when the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants maintain that their intent was to secure profit, not inflict loss. In this instance, however, this distinction is irrelevant. As the indictment states, defendants’ objective was to acquire money by fraudulently inducing purchases of securities by their followers. The allegation that defendants engaged in a “pump and dump” for profit establishes their financial motive. Id. at 1397. “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—regardless of actual pecuniary loss to the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s claim that defendants deceived their followers into purchasing securities adequately pleads an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that fraud statutes lack any requirement that the defendant’s gain mirrors the victim’s monetary loss); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (following Kousisis in a comparable case).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
