Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud based on their participation in a "pump and dump" scheme. According to the superseding indictment, they encouraged their social-media audiences to buy securities by posting misleading statements about their own trading activity and the future price potential of those securities, which artificially boosted the prices and enabled defendants to secure profits. The district court decided to dismiss the indictment, determining that it failed to allege an offense, reasoning that the claims centered on depriving followers of potentially useful economic information rather than a conventional property right. We hold, however, that the indictment adequately sets forth a fraudulent scheme and intent to defraud, and thus, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the allegations, each defendant maintained significant followings on social media platforms and publicly presented themselves as adept stock traders by regularly sharing their trading activity online. The prosecution asserts that, in advancing their scheme, the defendants would first acquire shares of a particular security, then proceed to “‘pump’ the price of that security by disseminating false and misleading statements about the security on [social media] to entice other investors into purchasing and thus artificially inflating the price,” and subsequently “dump” their shares by covertly selling for a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment charges that defendants reaped $114 million in profits through this conduct. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and several counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Daniel Knight entered a guilty plea; the other defendants sought to have the indictment dismissed. The district court granted the motion, finding the indictment insufficient as it did not allege a scheme to deprive victims of a traditional property right. The government filed a timely appeal of that ruling.

II

We evaluate the adequacy of an indictment on a de novo basis. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and assess whether they establish the commission of an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To be valid, an indictment is required to “(1) set forth each essential element of the charged crime; (2) sufficiently notify the accused of the charges against him; and (3) allow the accused to raise a double jeopardy defense against subsequent prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In short, for an indictment to satisfy the requirements, it must charge every essential element of the offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids schemes designed “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security” as well as schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is “scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 adopts central principles found in the mail and wire fraud statutes, leading courts to interpret these terms similarly and to reference mail and wire fraud decisions when evaluating securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying those principles, prosecution for securities fraud must establish (1) a “scheme to defraud” (2) carried out with “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” prong calls for a material misrepresentation made “with the goal of deceiving others for the purpose of acquiring something valuable, like money, from the party targeted by the deceit.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” prong necessitates “the intention both (1) to deceive and (2) to cause some harm through that deceit.” Id. (citing Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In short, a defendant perpetrates securities fraud if he “‘engaged in deception’” with “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” as “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Here, Defendants acknowledge the indictment sufficiently alleges deceptive conduct. Nevertheless, they argue that the indictment fails to sufficiently plead a scheme to defraud or intent to defraud, asserting it charges only an intent to deny their followers important economic information (as opposed to money or property) for their own enrichment (not to harm followers). We do not agree with that assessment, especially in view of the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to assert a scheme intended to divest their followers of a legally protected property right. Although a defendant cannot be found guilty of fraud solely for withholding information that might possess economic value, the present indictment goes beyond such an allegation by appropriately claiming that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of monetary assets.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court concluded that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of important economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli's construction firm paid a lobbyist to secure state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. In prosecuting Ciminelli for wire fraud, the government relied entirely on the right-to-control doctrine. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory permitted the government to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Essentially, under the right-to-control approach, the “property” at issue is “the right to control [one’s] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court determined that the federal fraud statutes safeguard only conventional property rights, and since “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the right-to-control theory could not support a fraud conviction. Id. at 316.

The facts of this case differ from those in Ciminelli. The indictment here does not invoke the right-to-control concept. Instead, it asserts that the defendants deceived their followers through false representations, prompting them to buy securities—meaning they surrendered money or property. The indictment proceeds on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which involves a defendant “us[ing] a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In its examination of this theory, the Supreme Court affirmed that the fraudulent-inducement approach “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the discredited right-to-control theory from Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue “protects money and property.” Id. Accordingly, the indictment is consistent with Ciminelli, as it charges that defendants fraudulently deprived followers of a recognized property interest by inducing the purchase of securities.

B

Defendants next contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege that defendants intended to defraud, asserting that the indictment only describes a motive to profit rather than to cause harm to any victims. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed a comparable claim, holding that fraud statutes do not require that a scheme result in economic harm to a victim. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant submitted false information in order to secure contracts with a state transportation department. The state mandated that contractors collaborate with a disadvantaged business, but Kousisis falsely claimed his company would satisfy this requirement. After receiving the contracts, Kousisis used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor solely as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When this deception was uncovered, he was charged under a "fraudulent-inducement theory," under which a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis maintained that the fraudulent-inducement theory should fail because it allowed conviction even when there was no proof the “defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this, ruling that a defendant breaches the fraud statutes by “schem[ing] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392. The current indictment adequately alleges an intent to defraud the defendants' followers; for example, it asserts that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the fraud statutes do not demand proof of economic loss, the fact that followers received securities valued at market rates does not negate the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (determining liability may attach for fraud even if the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants claim they intended only to benefit themselves, not to injure anyone. Yet, in this context, that distinction is immaterial. The indictment charges that defendants’ goal was to acquire money by deceptively inducing their followers to purchase securities. Their “pump and dump” operation aimed at making a profit is enough to demonstrate they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—regardless of the victim's net pecuniary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. By alleging that defendants tricked their followers into buying securities, the indictment properly claims an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (finding no “mirror image” requirement for the victim’s loss to correspond exactly to the defendant’s gain under fraud statutes); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (following Kousisis in a similar circumstance).

IV

As the indictment adequately sets forth both a scheme and an intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and REMAND the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
