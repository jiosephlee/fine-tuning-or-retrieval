Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud related to their participation in a “pump and dump” operation. According to the superseding indictment, they persuaded their social media followers to buy certain securities by posting false statements about their own trading activities and the likely future value of those securities, thereby causing artificial price inflation and securing profits for themselves. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the charges only claimed the defendants deprived followers of possibly important economic information, not a conventional property right, and thus did not establish an offense. However, because we find that the indictment adequately sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a “pump and dump” scheme involving securities. According to the allegations, each defendant maintained a substantial presence on multiple social media platforms and portrayed themselves as adept stock traders, regularly sharing their trading decisions online. The government contends that to execute their scheme, the defendants would first acquire a security, then “pump” its price by disseminating false and misleading statements about the security on social media to persuade other investors to buy, thereby causing the price to rise artificially, and subsequently “dump” by surreptitiously selling their holdings at a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment claims that the defendants gained $114 million as a result of this conduct. The charges against them included conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the others filed motions seeking dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted the motion, holding that the indictment did not sufficiently allege that defendants intended to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government subsequently filed a timely appeal.

II

We examine the adequacy of an indictment without deference. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court “must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and assess whether they describe an offense.” United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment is required to “(1) state each prima facie element of the alleged crime; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the accusations against him; and (3) enable the defendant to assert double jeopardy protection in any future proceedings.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In essence, “an indictment is only sufficient if it asserts each essential element of the crime charged.” United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids both schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as efforts “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “there is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 incorporates “key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” resulting in courts often treating these terms in a similar manner and frequently relying on mail and wire fraud precedents to interpret securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying these principles, securities fraud consists of (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” requirement entails a material misstatement “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” requirement means there must be “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). To summarize, securities fraud occurs when a defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and “‘obtaining the victim’s money or property’ was ‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently alleges deceptive conduct. However, they assert that the indictment fails to charge a scheme or intent to defraud, arguing that it only alleges an intent to deny their followers valuable economic information for the purpose of self-enrichment, not to deprive them of money or property. Based on the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we reject that argument.

A

The defendants contend that the district court appropriately dismissed the indictment on the basis that it fails to allege a scheme to deprive their followers of a property interest protected by law. Although it is correct that fraud convictions cannot rest solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information, in this case, the indictment advances beyond that point by properly asserting that defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest merely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm compensated a lobbyist to assist in securing state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. When the government prosecuted Ciminelli for wire fraud, its case rested exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. Under this theory, the prosecution sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Stated differently, under the right-to-control theory, the alleged property interest was “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes protect only conventional property interests and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” and therefore, “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The circumstances here differ from those in Ciminelli. The indictment at issue does not refer to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it claims that defendants used misrepresentations to induce their followers to buy securities, thereby causing them to relinquish money or property. The indictment is based on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which holds that a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. In addressing the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court clarified that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory that was disapproved in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue here “protects money and property.” Id. The indictment, therefore, does not violate the principles established in Ciminelli because it asserts that defendants, through fraudulent inducement, deprived their followers of a property interest protected by law by persuading them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege that they intended to defraud; instead, it claims only that defendants aimed to profit from their conduct rather than to injure any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently addressed and dismissed a comparable argument, determining that the fraud statutes do not necessitate proof that a victim suffered economic loss as a result of the defendant’s scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant secured contracts with a state department of transportation by submitting false statements. The contracting process required that contractors engage a disadvantaged business as a subcontractor; Kousisis misrepresented that his company would meet this requirement. After being awarded the contracts, he utilized the disadvantaged-business subcontractor solely as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. Upon discovery of this deception, Kousisis was prosecuted under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis maintained that this theory was invalid because it would permit conviction without evidence showing the defendant intended to financially harm the victim. Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected this position, clarifying that a defendant breaches the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

In this case, the indictment adequately alleges that defendants intended to defraud their followers. Specifically, it charges that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Because the statutes governing fraud do not require that actual economic harm occur, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not negate the fraud allegations. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding that a defendant can be liable for fraud even if he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants assert that their intention was solely to profit, not to injure any victim. Yet, in the context of these allegations, that distinction is immaterial. The indictment claims that defendants’ purpose was to obtain money, and they accomplished this by fraudulently convincing their followers to buy securities. Their “pump and dump” scheme, as described in the indictment, demonstrates that they were motivated by profit, or that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—regardless of whether the victim incurred a net pecuniary loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s allegation that defendants fraudulently persuaded their followers to purchase securities sufficiently alleges the requisite injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” correlation between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis under similar circumstances).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
