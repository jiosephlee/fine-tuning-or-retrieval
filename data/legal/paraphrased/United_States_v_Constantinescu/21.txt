Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud in connection with their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants prompted their social-media followers to buy securities by making posts that falsely represented their own trading positions and the prospective value of certain securities. This conduct allegedly led to an artificial increase in the securities’ prices, from which the defendants gained financially. The district court decided to dismiss the indictment, reasoning that it did not allege an offense since the claims involved only a deprivation of potentially important economic information rather than an actual property interest. However, because we find that the indictment adequately sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the dismissal of the indictment by the district court.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a securities “pump and dump” scheme. According to the government, each defendant maintained a substantial social media audience on different platforms and presented themselves as expert stock traders, regularly sharing their trading actions online. To execute their “pump and dump” operation, the government asserts that defendants acquired shares, “‘pumped’ the price of those securities by disseminating false and misleading statements regarding the securities on [social media] so that other investors would be persuaded to buy and thus artificially drive up the price,” and “dumped” by covertly selling their holdings for profit. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment charges that the defendants obtained $114 million from this conduct. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and with several counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought to have the indictment dismissed. The district court granted dismissal, determining that the indictment did not sufficiently allege that the defendants’ scheme deprived victims of a traditional property interest. The government then filed a timely appeal.

II

Our review of whether an indictment is sufficient is conducted without deference. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept all facts alleged in the indictment as true and decide if the indictment alleges a criminal offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). A valid indictment is required to: “(1) set forth every essential element of the offense charged; (2) give the defendant adequate notice of the accusations; and (3) enable the defendant to raise a defense of double jeopardy in the event of a subsequent prosecution for the same offense.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Ultimately, a sufficient indictment must include each substantive element of the charged offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The statute governing securities fraud forbids engaging in schemes that "defraud any person in connection with... any security," as well as schemes aimed at "obtain[ing], by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of... any security." See U.S.C. § 1348. Although "[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit," § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," and courts generally treat those terms similarly, frequently turning to precedents under mail and wire fraud statutes to interpret securities fraud charges. See United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Applying those principles, securities fraud consists of (1) a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) the existence of “an intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. For the “scheme to defraud” prong, there must be a material misrepresentation designed “to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The element of “intent to defraud” requires “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). To summarize, a defendant perpetrates securities fraud when he “‘engaged in deception’” and the aim of “‘[o]btaining the victim’s money or property’” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants acknowledge that the indictment sufficiently alleges acts of deception. However, they maintain that the indictment does not state facts constituting a scheme to defraud or a specific intent to defraud. According to the defendants, the indictment only accuses them of trying to deprive followers of useful economic information (instead of money or property) in order to benefit themselves, not to harm their followers. We do not agree with this interpretation in light of the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis.

A

Defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it failed to assert a scheme to deny their followers a protected property right. Although a conviction for fraud cannot be sustained solely on the basis of depriving someone of information that might have economic value, the indictment in this case asserted more, specifically alleging that defendants engaged in a scheme intended to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist to secure state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The prosecution based its wire fraud case exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to this theory, the prosecution sought “to establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Simply put, under this approach, the property at issue is the victim’s “right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Court concluded that the federal fraud statutes are limited to conventional property interests, and because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The facts here differ from Ciminelli. The indictment omits any reference to the right-to-control theory. Instead, it claims that defendants misled their followers through false statements, prompting them to buy securities—thereby relinquishing money or property. The indictment proceeds on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which applies when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court, in addressing the fraudulent-inducement theory, stated that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Distinct from the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory asserted here “protects money and property.” Id. Since the indictment asserts that defendants fraudulently caused followers to purchase securities, thus losing a protected property interest, it does not contravene Ciminelli.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment does not properly allege intent to defraud, claiming it states that defendants sought personal gain from their actions rather than intending to inflict harm on any victim. However, the Supreme Court has recently dismissed an analogous argument, concluding that the fraud statutes do not mandate that a victim experience financial loss due to a defendant's scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant deceived a state department of transportation in order to secure contracts. The state’s regulations required contractors to subcontract work to a disadvantaged business; Kousisis made fraudulent representations to meet that criterion. Upon award of the contracts, Kousisis engaged the disadvantaged-business subcontractor merely as a “pass-through” entity. Id. at 1389. Once this misrepresentation surfaced, Kousisis was charged under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” wherein a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contested this theory, asserting it improperly permitted convictions even absent proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that the fraud statutes are violated when a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

Similarly, the indictment in this case adequately sets forth that defendants intended to defraud their followers, specifically alleging that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the statutes governing fraud do not necessitate the victim to suffer actual financial harm, the fact that the followers received securities of market value does not negate the fraud allegations. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding a defendant may be liable for fraud even if he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants maintain their aim was profit, not to inflict injury on any victim, but in the context of this indictment, that distinction is immaterial. The indictment avers that defendants sought to acquire money by misleadingly inducing followers to purchase securities. The claim that the defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme for personal profit demonstrates that their objective was financial gain. Id. at 1397. As the Supreme Court observed, “[a] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—regardless of whether the victim incurred a net loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. The indictment’s assertion that defendants fraudulently persuaded followers to purchase securities sufficiently alleges an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (explaining that the fraud statutes do not require the defendant’s gain to be a “mirror image” of the victim’s loss); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in a comparable setting).

IV

Since the indictment properly sets forth a scheme and an intent to defraud, we VACATE the district court's dismissal of the indictment and RETURN the case for further proceedings in accordance with this opinion.
