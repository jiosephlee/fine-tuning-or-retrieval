Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s decision denying their motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s refusal to grant a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons set forth below, we overturn the denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, both of which concern methods for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. To prevent signal interference, radios typically operate on separate frequencies; however, their transmission signals may interact with physical obstructions—such as metal fences, poor cable connections, or corroded connectors—which can cause the formation of additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise from passive physical obstacles, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent based on AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the suit. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that every asserted claim was both valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum of $166,303,391 as damages covering the entire remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. After trial, Appellants filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and for a new trial, all of which were denied by the district court. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal. Our jurisdiction is based on 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL according to the law of the pertinent regional circuit—in this case, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, a district court’s denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo and will be reversed “only if, when considering the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence weighs so heavily and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that no reasonable jury could decide otherwise.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). We examine jury determinations regarding infringement for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A finding of fact is sustained by substantial evidence where a reasonable jury could have returned a verdict in favor of the party that prevailed, based on the record evidence.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Claim construction is evaluated de novo, while underlying factual determinations relied upon by the district court in its construction are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process intended to diminish IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 exemplifies the claimed invention.

1. A method comprising:

[a] over-sampling a selected frequency range of the incoming signals to produce a stream of bits, with the incoming signals comprising both the desired signals and interfering signals, where the interfering signals may produce intermodulation products within the frequency band of the signals of interest; [b] utilizing one or more decimating filters to separate the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] employing one or more decimating filters to extract the source signals responsible for generating one or more intermodulation products falling within the band of interest; [d] generating an estimate for each such intermodulation product from the corresponding source signals; [e] utilizing these estimates to remove one or more inband intermodulation products; and [f] carrying out closed-loop phase and amplitude correction on the estimated interfering intermodulation signals, where said adjustment involves executing sub-sample phase shifts to effectuate phase modifications on these estimated interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As plaintiff, Finesse bore the responsibility of demonstrating that the radios accused of infringement sample a frequency band containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During the trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, provided testimony that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by relying upon a Nokia technical document explaining the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (“RF ADC,” shown inside a blue box) acts as a receiver, sampling the so-called “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each represented by a dashed red path) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently assigned the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, both illustrated with dashed red lines as explained in the red box within the legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Likewise, Dr. Wells’s expert report opined that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel highlighted that the RF ADC is incapable of sampling the modeled PIM path, as this signal is produced after the sampling operation is performed downstream of the RF ADC, making it impossible for signals to be sampled before their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Consequently, the modeled PIM path does not qualify as the claimed “interference generating signals.” When questioned, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony to state, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, he did not equate x1 and x2 with the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; instead, he maintained that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked if he was asserting “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Once again, he failed to directly map x1 or x2 onto the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as required by the claims.

The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. At JMOL, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells linked “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, and thus there was no infringement, since the receiver in the accused radios does not sample a passband that contains interference generating signals, as those signals come into being only after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the “interference generating signals” of the claim, limitation 1[a]

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, co-located with a transmitter, the described method includes: [a] the ongoing, near real-time creation of intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that neutralize passive IMPs in the receiver, utilizing replicas of the transmitter’s own signals; [b] these passive IMPs are produced both in passive components of the transmitter and in parts of the receiver, subsequent to a high powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter's filter; [c] said transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and an antenna shared by the transmitter; [d] the process for generating ICSs derives from a power series representation of a non-linear mechanism that generates the IMPs, and entails generating an n-th order ICS, where, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, the method involves the digital multiplication and filtering of S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n representing an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases retained). In deciding summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as being “separately identifiable,” without requiring that they be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court found the intrinsic record did not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, and also took into account testimony from the inventor, serving as extrinsic evidence, that “S1, S2 and S3” indicate signals in general, and thus could either be identical or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The main claim limitation contested on appeal concerns the necessity for the seven specified third-order multiplications among the three signals to fulfill the claim’s language. At trial, Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios infringed the claims of the ’775 patent based on a Nokia technical document illustrating how the accused radios function. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). In particular, Dr. Wells testified that the accused products multiply three separately identifiable signals, equating the three distinctive multiplications recorded in the Nokia documentation to the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In its ruling on JMOL, the district court found that Appellants had waived their argument regarding “S1, S2 and S3” being required to be unique signals, as they did not present it during the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court held that employing two signals plus an additional copy of one of these signals sufficed under its construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Moreover, it found that only three unique multiplications occur with two unique signals, and this was sufficient to establish infringement, since each of those three different multiplications corresponds to two or three out of the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, asserting that these radios operate with just two signals: x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, Appellants maintain that, by amending the claims during prosecution to specify “three signals” rather than “two or three signals,” Finesse disclaimed claim coverage involving only two signals in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art, which discloses generating cancellation signals from two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants argue that a reasonable jury could not have determined the accused radios conduct the seven claimed multiplications, as Dr. Wells pointed to only three multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for arrangements involving two signals. The examiner had initially rejected a claim that included “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious in light of Filipovic along with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, overcame this rejection in part by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the claimed method addresses both active and passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to incorporate the requirement of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” via seven multiplications, this action does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope for devices, such as the accused radios, that operate using only two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The effect of Finesse’s amendment, at most, is to require three individually recognizable signals; it does not specify a minimum number of distinct input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that there is no basis upon which a reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications required by the claims. At trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document as evidence that the accused radios implement the claimed multiplication process. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the referenced document describes modulus and complex conjugate calculations, not the performance of all seven claimed multiplications by the accused radios. J.A. 26485–86. The document merely identifies three specific multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no indication or evidence of repetition among these to achieve the total of seven multiplications mandated by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony explaining how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications asserted in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse seeks to clarify this mapping during the appeal, such evidence was not introduced before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, the referenced document does not disclose multiplication of x1 and x2, but rather their complex conjugates and moduli; therefore, Finesse’s mapping is deficient because it necessarily requires, for instance, S3 to be mapped onto the signal x2, as well as its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation reveals that these radios actually perform ten multiplications in total. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (mentioning p(n) = a0 through a9). Nowhere do either Finesse or Dr. Wells clarify which of the ten multiplications described in the Nokia document are aligned with the seven required by the claims. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they outline a correspondence from the ten listed multiplications to the three unique multiplications that Finesse equated to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. On this record, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as substantial evidence does not support the jury’s verdict of infringement.

II. Damages

A district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial is reviewed under the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard when reviewing such denials. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit precedent). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants requested judgment as a matter of law on damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we set aside the denial of JMOL on noninfringement and nullify the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
