Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court's refusal to grant a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) possesses the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Typically, radios prevent signal interference by transmitting at distinct frequencies; nevertheless, when their signals encounter certain obstructions, such as metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors, these signals may combine and generate additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). See J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs result from non-active, external sources, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought an infringement action against AT&T, asserting that use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability in Nokia radios infringes claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the action. J.A. 389–90. A jury determined that every asserted claim was both valid and infringed, and it awarded lump-sum damages of $166,303,391 for the patents’ remaining terms. J.A. 169–71. Appellants subsequently filed motions for JMOL of noninfringement, JMOL regarding the damages, and for a new trial; the district court denied each motion. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now seek review. Our appellate jurisdiction is established under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of a district court’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) is guided by the precedent of the relevant regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Within the Fifth Circuit, denials of JMOL are subject to de novo review, and such a denial will be reversed “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit law). The jury’s determination regarding infringement is reviewed for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). As explained therein, “[a] factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Claim construction is reviewed de novo, whereas any underlying factual determinations that underpin the district court’s construction are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM results from mechanical conditions within the radio, whereas external PIM originates from sources outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process to diminish IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through utilization of a calculated estimation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as an illustrative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a chosen rate, a passband containing received signals to produce a bit stream, where these received signals comprise both signals of interest and signals responsible for generating interference, and where the interference-generating signals are capable of producing inband intermodulation products that overlap with the signals of interest; [b] extracting signals of interest from the bit stream through the use of one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting source signals that are responsible for creating one or more inband intermodulation products using one or more decimating filters; [d] generating an estimate of each of the relevant intermodulation products by analyzing the source signals which produce those intermodulation products; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products utilizing the corresponding estimates; and [f] conducting adjustments to phase and amplitude of the estimates of the intermodulation product interference signals in a closed-loop process, where such phase and amplitude adjustments specifically include implementing sub-sample phase shifts to effectuate the phase adjustment on the estimates of the intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

As the party asserting the claim, Finesse was obligated to demonstrate that the accused radios sample a passband that contains both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants maintain that Finesse did not prove the accused radios sample both types of signals—namely, the “signals of interest” as well as “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment.

During trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, Finesse’s infringement expert, testified that the accused radios practice the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document that illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, reproduced below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) functions as a receiver sampling both the asserted “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each illustrated in the dashed red path), converting them into a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells continually aligned “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and identified “interference generating signals” as the modeled PIM path (both depicted via the dashed red path as detailed in the red box of the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Similarly, Dr. Wells’ expert report stated that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and asserted that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants contended that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path, since this path is created downstream from the RF ADC after sampling, meaning signals cannot be sampled before generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. As a result, the modeled PIM path cannot be the “interference generating signals” as claimed. Dr. Wells then altered his testimony, asserting, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, he did not specify that x1 and x2 represent the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” instead reiterating, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When pressed if he was equating “x1” with the modeled PIM path, Dr. Wells responded, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Again, he did not explicitly associate x1 or x2 with either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as required by the claims.

The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. Upon JMOL, Appellants contended Dr. Wells identified “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, meaning infringement is precluded because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband of received signals inclusive of interference generating signals, given these signals only arise post-sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court noted “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path corresponds to the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation 1[a] is unsatisfied, as the accused receiver cannot sample

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver, with both the transmitter and receiver situated together, the method includes: [a] generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) in order to mitigate passive IMPs within the receiver continuously and nearly in real time, utilizing duplicates of the transmitter's signals; [b] noting that the passive IMPs arise in passive components of the transmitter as well as the receiver components, following the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and its transmitter filter; [c] specifying that the transmitter filter connects between the HPA and the antenna used by the transmitter; [d] indicating that ICSs are derived from a power series representation of the nonlinear process responsible for generating IMPs, including producing an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally performing multiplication and filtering on combinations such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n as an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added). 

During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but determined they need not be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined, based on the intrinsic evidence, that there was no restriction limiting “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, and also relied on the inventor's testimony as extrinsic evidence that “S1, S2 and S3” generally represent signals which may be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. 

On appeal, the primary claim limitation in dispute concerns the requirement for seven specific third order multiplications involving the three signals, as stated in the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios meet the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by reference to a Nokia technical document illustrating the accused radios' operation. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells explained that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals and equated the three different multiplications identified in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications required by the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). 

The jury found that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court denied Appellants’ argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals as it found that Appellants had waived this contention by not raising it at the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court held that utilizing two signals together with a duplicate of one sufficed to fulfill the requirement of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court found that using two unique signals yields only three different multiplications, which was enough to prove infringement because each of these three distinct multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven required by the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios produce cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, as the radios in question utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. Appellants specifically assert that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to specifically require “three signals” rather than “two or three signals.” This amendment, appellants argue, was made to distinguish the claims from the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses cancellation signal generation based on two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). In addition, appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications recited in the claims, as Dr. Wells identified only three multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse disclaimed claim scope with respect to two signals. The examiner rejected a claim containing the phrase “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references, see J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse countered this rejection partially by stating that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which the claimed method also covers. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse subsequently revised the claims to recite digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, this amendment does not constitute the kind of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim coverage regarding products, such as the accused radios, that utilize two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. If anything, Finesse’s amendment simply requires the presence of three individually distinguishable signals, not a particular minimum number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. However, we concur that a reasonable jury could not conclude the accused radios carry out the seven specific multiplications as claimed. At trial, Dr. Wells based his opinion on a Nokia document to suggest the accused radios conduct the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Nevertheless, this document addresses modulus and complex conjugate operations and fails to demonstrate the accused radios execute the seven specified multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, the document merely identifies three discrete multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no indication that any of the multiplications are repeated so as to fulfill all seven multiplications stipulated by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony explaining how the three specific multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse endeavors to clarify this mapping on appeal, such evidence was not introduced to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, the document does not describe multiplying x1 and x2 themselves, but rather their complex conjugates and moduli, leading to a flaw in Finesse’s mapping; for instance, this would entail assigning S3 to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications on the basis that Nokia’s documents indicate the radios perform a total of ten multiplications, not just seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells specifies which of the ten multiplications enumerated in the Nokia document correspond to the seven that are claimed. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they describe how those ten multiplications are mapped to the three unique multiplications that Finesse previously mapped to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. In this context, no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. Thus, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s finding of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support. 

II. Damages

We assess the denial of a motion for a new trial under the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of new trials for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury delivered its verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both motions. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the award of damages.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the judgment denying JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
