Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the decision of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas denying their motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s refusal to grant a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. Radios can typically prevent interference by transmitting on separate frequencies; however, their signals may interact with physical obstructions, such as metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors, resulting in the creation of new frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs originate from passive structures, they are termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, stemming from AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened. J.A. 389–90. The jury found in favor of Finesse, determining that all challenged claims were valid and infringed, and awarded $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages for the anticipated duration of both patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, for JMOL regarding damages, and for a new trial, each of which the district court denied. J.A. 1–72. Appellants have now appealed. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) following the standards of the pertinent regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). In the Fifth Circuit, denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo, with reversal warranted “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit precedent). Findings of infringement by a jury are examined for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We analyze claim construction issues de novo and review any underlying factual determinations made by the district court during claim construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a method aimed at mitigating IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling, at a selected frequency, a received signal passband to generate a bit stream, where the received signals comprise both signals of interest and signals causing interference, with the latter being able to produce inband intermodulation products within the range of the signals of interest; [b] employing one or more decimating filters to isolate the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] utilizing one or more decimating filters to separate the source signals responsible for producing inband intermodulation products; [d] calculating an estimation for each of the inband intermodulation products generated by these source signals; [e] removing one or more of these inband intermodulation products using the calculated estimation; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude modifications to the estimates of the interfering intermodulation product signals within a closed loop, wherein these modifications specifically include sub-sample phase adjustments to alter the estimates of the interfering intermodulation product signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As the party asserting the claim, Finesse had the obligation to demonstrate that the accused radios perform sampling of a passband comprising both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this contention. During the trial, Finesse presented expert testimony from Dr. Jonathan Wells, who asserted that the accused radios infringe the claims of the ’134 patent based upon a Nokia technical document illustrating the operational features of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, displayed below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, illustrated within a blue box) serves as a receiver that samples the recited “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (both represented by a dashed red path) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently associated “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and designated “interference generating signals” as the modeled PIM path—both traced by the dashed red path, as indicated in the red box found under the legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). His expert report reinforced this, stating that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

However, on cross-examination, counsel for Appellants argued that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path, since the modeled PIM path is generated further downstream from the RF ADC, meaning that sampling would occur before these signals actually exist, so it would be impossible to sample them beforehand. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This implies that the modeled PIM path does not qualify as the recited “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his position, testifying, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Nevertheless, Dr. Wells did not state that x1 and x2 corresponded to the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals”; he continued to assert that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned more directly whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, he never matched x1 or x2 specifically to the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as the claims required.

Ultimately, the jury returned a verdict finding infringement of all asserted claims of the ’134 patent. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants argued that, because Dr. Wells linked “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, infringement was impossible: the receivers in the accused radios do not sample a received signal passband that includes interference generating signals because these signals are not present until after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (referencing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system where a transmitter and receiver are positioned together, the following method is employed: [a] intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) are generated to eliminate passive IMPs within the receiver, on a continuous and near real-time basis, by utilizing copies of the transmitter signals; [b] the passive IMPs originate in the transmitter’s passive components and the receiver components after the transmitter’s high power amplifier (HPA) and filter; [c] the transmitter filter connects the HPA to an antenna utilized by the transmitter; [d] the ICSs are produced based on a power series model of the nonlinear process that generates the IMPs, which includes forming an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering terms such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n represents an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). At summary judgment, the district court determined that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” are required to be “separately identifiable,” but need not be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court held that the intrinsic evidence did not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to only unique input signals, and the court credited extrinsic evidence in the form of inventor testimony stating that “S1, S2 and S3” could generally refer to any signals, whether the same or different. J.A. 109–14. The main point of contention on appeal concerns the claim limitation requiring that seven third-order multiplications of the three signals must occur in order to satisfy the claim limitation. During trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document describing how the radios function. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically asserted that the accused devices multiply three separately identifiable signals, and equated the three unique multiplications shown in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found infringement as to all asserted claims of the ’775 patent. J.A. 169. Upon JMOL, the district court determined that the Appellants’ argument—contending that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals—was waived since Appellants failed to bring it at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court ruled that using two signals along with a duplicate of one sufficed to meet the construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court concluded that only three distinct multiplications result when two unique signals are used, and that this was adequate to establish infringement because each of those three distinct multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven claim-recited multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, since, according to Appellants, the accused radios utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished any claim scope covering two signals when, during prosecution, the claims were amended from "two or three signals" to "three signals," in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art reference which disclosed generating cancellation signals using two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants assert that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios engage in the seven claimed multiplications, as Dr. Wells only identified three multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope with respect to two signals. The examiner had initially rejected a claim covering “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious over Filipovic and other secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. However, Finesse was able to overcome this rejection in part by asserting that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” while the claimed invention also addresses passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend its claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, this change does not constitute the sort of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope that would exclude products, such as the accused radios, utilizing two distinct input signals. See Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, Finesse’s amendment establishes a requirement for three separately identifiable signals, but does not specify the necessity for any particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications recited in the claims. Dr. Wells, at trial, pointed to a Nokia document as evidence that the accused radios engage in the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document refers to modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not demonstrate that the accused devices perform all seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, the document mentions three specific multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no indication that any of the multiplications are repeated in such a way as to satisfy the requirement for all seven claimed multiplications set out in limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

In addition, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony connecting the three distinct multiplications to the seven multiplications described in the asserted claims. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse seeks to clarify the mapping during this appeal, such evidence was not submitted to the jury. See Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, the relevant document indicates the multiplication of the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2, not x1 and x2 directly. Finesse’s proposed mapping is defective because, for instance, it would require S3 to correspond to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios satisfy the requirement of the seven claimed multiplications because documents from Nokia show that the accused products in fact carry out ten multiplications, not just seven. See Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referencing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (citing p(n) = a0 through a9). Nowhere, however, do Finesse or Dr. Wells clarify which particular seven of the ten multiplications in Nokia’s document correspond to the seven multiplications required by the claims. See Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they identify a mapping between the ten Nokia multiplications and the three distinct multiplications that Finesse proposed as mapping onto the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications. Consequently, we reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s infringement finding lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

We review a district court's denial of a motion for new trial according to the regional circuit’s law. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard for reviewing such denials. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury’s verdict, Appellants requested judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
