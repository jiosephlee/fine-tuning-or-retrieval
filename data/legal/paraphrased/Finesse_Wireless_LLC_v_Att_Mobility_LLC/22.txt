Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket Number: 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s decision to deny their motion for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the holder of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which are generally directed to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. While radios typically prevent signal interference by transmitting on distinct frequencies, their signals can merge upon contact with certain obstacles—such as metal fences, improperly connected cables, or corroded connectors—to generate additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When IMPs originate from passive obstructions, they are described as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse filed suit against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent based on AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature found in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the case. J.A. 389–90. The jury concluded that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, awarding $166,303,391 as a lump-sum for damages covering the remaining terms of both patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants subsequently sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL with respect to damages, and a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now seek review on appeal. Jurisdiction is proper pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) adheres to the precedent set by the applicable regional circuit, which, in this instance, is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit standards, a denial of JMOL is subject to de novo review; reversal is warranted "only if, when the evidence is viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence so overwhelmingly favors one party that reasonable jurors could not reach a different conclusion." Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit authority). We assess a jury's verdict of infringement under the substantial evidence standard. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). "Substantial evidence supports a factual determination where a reasonable jury could find in favor of the party prevailing, considering the evidence introduced at trial." Id. (internal citations omitted). With respect to claim construction, our review is de novo, but any factual determinations made by the district court to support its construction are examined for clear error. See Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM arises from mechanical defects within the radio, whereas external PIM originates from factors outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process designed to mitigate IMP interference by first separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently removing the “interference generating signals” through an estimated calculation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] performing over-sampling at a specified frequency on a passband comprising received signals so as to generate a bit stream, where these received signals consist of both signals of interest and signals responsible for interference, with the interference-generating signals being able to produce intermodulation products that fall within the band of the signals of interest; [b] utilizing one or more decimating filters to separate out the signals of interest present in the bit stream; [c] employing one or more decimating filters to extract source signals responsible for producing one or more intermodulation products located in the same band as the signal of interest; [d] determining an estimate for each of the intermodulation products, this estimation derived from the source signals generating those products; [e] using these computed estimates of intermodulation products to remove one or more of the inband intermodulation products; and [f] carrying out adjustments to phase and amplitude on the estimations of intermodulation product interference signals in a closed loop fashion, with the process of phase and amplitude adjustment of these estimations including sub-sample phase shifting to effectuate the phase adjustment on estimations of the intermodulation product interference signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

As plaintiff, Finesse was required to demonstrate that the radios accused of infringement perform sampling of a passband of signals encompassing both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals." Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants maintain that Finesse failed to establish that the accused radios actually sample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment. During the trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who served as Finesse’s expert on infringement, testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document which outlines the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, as reproduced below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, represented within the blue box) functions as a receiver that samples both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals"—each illustrated in the dashed red path—to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently associated the "signals of interest" with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and the "interference generating signals" with the modeled PIM path, both of which appear in the dashed red path and are described in the legend’s red box. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In his expert report, Dr. Wells also opined that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and characterized “the ‘modeled PIM signal’” as “the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

During cross-examination, Appellants' counsel challenged this by pointing out that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path, given that the modeled PIM path is created after sampling in the signal chain and thus cannot be sampled prior to its existence. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Accordingly, the modeled PIM path cannot satisfy the claim’s “interference generating signals” requirement. In response, Dr. Wells altered his position, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells never testified that x1 and x2 correspond to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; rather, he persisted in stating “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked to clarify if “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Nevertheless, at no point did he identify x1 or x2 as being the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as required by the asserted claims.

The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells had mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, and that infringement could not be established because the accused radio’s receiver did not sample a received passband containing the interference generating signals, as those signals only arose after the sampling had occurred. J.A. 19–20. The court acknowledged that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and recognized “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Thus, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver, where both the transmitter and the receiver are located together, the method includes: [a] generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to suppress passive IMPs in the receiver continuously and in near real time, by utilizing copies of the transmitter’s own signals, [b] with the passive IMPs forming in the passive transmitter components of the transmitter and in the receiver components of the receiver, occurring after the transmitter’s high-powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter, [c] with the transmitter filter positioned between the HPA and the antenna employed by the transmitter, [d] in which creating the ICSs depends upon a power series model describing a non-linear mechanism for IMP generation, and this process incorporates forming an n-th order ICS through, provided with three signals S1, S2, and S3, the digital multiplication and filtering of S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases original). During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as meaning signals that are “separately identifiable,” rather than requiring that they be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court concluded that the intrinsic evidence did not require that “S1, S2 and S3” be unique input signals, and relied also on testimony from the inventor—considered extrinsic evidence—that “S1, S2 and S3” refer to signals in general and could represent either the same or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation challenged on appeal centers on the requirement that seven distinct third-order multiplications involving the three signals are to be performed in order to satisfy the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells testified that the radios accused of infringement meet the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document that describes the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). More specifically, Dr. Wells stated that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals and equated the three individual multiplications illustrated in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications called for by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court found Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals had been waived since Appellants did not raise it during the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that employing two signals along with a duplicate of one of those signals fulfilled the claim requirement of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court determined that when two distinct signals are used, only three individual multiplications result, but that this satisfied the infringement requirement because each of those three multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, since the radios in question utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for two signals when, during prosecution, it amended the claims to specify “three signals” instead of “two or three signals,” in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generating cancellation signals from two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants assert that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications, because Dr. Wells identified only three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We reject the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding two signals. The examiner initially found a claim that recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals" obvious in light of Filipovic in combination with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, overcame that rejection in part by asserting that "Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs" and does not address passive IMPs, which the claimed method also covers. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Although Finesse subsequently amended the claims to include the requirement of digitally multiplying "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in a series of seven multiplications, this amendment does not constitute a "clear and unambiguous" surrender of claim scope as to products, such as the accused radios, that involve two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. Finesse’s amendment, at best, necessitates three separately identifiable signals but does not mandate a particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. Dr. Wells at trial referenced a Nokia document to suggest the accused radios perform the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, this document relates to modulus and complex conjugate functions and does not show that the accused radios undertake the seven specified multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, the document lists only three unique multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence suggesting any multiplications are duplicated so that all seven required by limitation 1[d] are performed. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells failed to specify how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse seeks to clarify this mapping on appeal, such evidence was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, the relevant document does not disclose multiplication of x1 and x2 themselves, but rather their complex conjugate and modulus. Thus, Finesse’s proposed mapping is deficient, as it would, for instance, require mapping S3 onto signal x2, as well as its complex conjugate x2', and modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation indicates that the accused radios in fact perform not only seven, but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (describing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identify which specific subset of these ten multiplications, as listed in the Nokia document, aligns with the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). They also fail to establish a mapping from the ten documented multiplications to the three unique multiplications that Finesse had associated with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, it would be unreasonable for a jury to find that the accused radios practice the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because the jury’s verdict of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

The standard of review for a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial follows the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit law, such denial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury reached its verdict, Appellants filed a motion for JMOL regarding damages or, in the alternative, sought a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we hereby vacate the award of damages.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the refusal to grant JMOL on noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
