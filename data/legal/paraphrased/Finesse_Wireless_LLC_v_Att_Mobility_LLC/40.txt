Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court's decision denying a motion for a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for addressing interference in radios caused by intermodulation products (IMPs). Radios generally prevent signal interference by operating on distinct frequencies; however, when transmit signals encounter certain physical obstacles—such as metallic fences, deteriorated cable connections, or corroded connectors—they may combine to produce new frequencies identified as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs result from passive obstacles, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging that AT&T had infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through the implementation of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability within Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the proceedings. J.A. 389–90. The jury concluded that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, awarding lump-sum damages in the amount of $166,303,391 for the remaining patent terms of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL concerning damages, and a new trial, all of which requests were denied by the district court. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now bring this appeal. This court has jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess the denial of JMOL by a district court according to the law applicable in the regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). In the Fifth Circuit, denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo and will be reversed “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). We examine jury verdicts of infringement to determine if substantial evidence exists. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Mechanical issues inside the radio lead to internal PIM, whereas external PIM originates from sources beyond the radio. J.A. 15223–24. We review claim construction issues de novo, but review any subsidiary factual determinations made by the district court underlying that construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a technique aimed at minimizing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by removing the “interference generating signals” through use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling a selected frequency band of received signals in order to produce a bit stream, where the received signals comprise both desired signals ("signals of interest") and interfering signals, with the interfering signals capable of producing intermodulation products that fall within the frequency band of the signals of interest; [b] using one or more decimating filters to extract the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] applying one or more decimating filters to extract the source signals responsible for generating intermodulation products that overlap the frequency band of the signals of interest; [d] estimating each of the intermodulation products based on the source signals that produce them; [e] using these estimates to cancel one or more in-band intermodulation products; and [f] implementing closed-loop phase and amplitude adjustments to the estimates of the intermodulation product interfering signals, which includes making sub-sample phase shifts to effectuate phase changes in these interference estimates. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases added). Finesse, as plaintiff, was required to demonstrate that the accused radios sample a frequency band containing both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not prove the accused radios sample both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse's expert witness on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, based his assertion that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent on a Nokia technical document explaining the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), shown inside the blue box, operates as a receiver that samples the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals”—both of which appear along the dashed red path—producing a bit stream. See J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently described mapping the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path; these are depicted in the dashed red path, as clarified in the legend’s red box. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Similarly, Dr. Wells wrote in his expert report that the “DL (TX) reference” signal “meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest,’” and asserted that the “modeled PIM signal” is “the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel emphasized that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path, since this path is created after the RF ADC performs its sampling, and signals cannot be sampled before they exist. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This establishes that the modeled PIM path cannot be the “interference generating signals” required by the claim. When questioned further, Dr. Wells adjusted his testimony, stating “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells never specifically testified that x1 and x2 were equivalent to the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals”; he persisted in identifying “the two signals” as the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked explicitly whether he was saying “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, he did not connect x1 and x2 directly to either the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as the claims require.

The jury found that every asserted claim of the ’134 patent was infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants contended that because Dr. Wells identified the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path—which is not produced until after the sampling step—the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband that includes interference generating signals, so infringement cannot occur. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged, “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Consequently, if the “modeled PIM path” is synonymous with the claimed “interference generating signals,” element 1[a]

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver system where both the transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) for the purpose of suppressing passive IMPs within the receiver, on a continuous and nearly real-time basis, through the use of replicas of signals from the transmitter; [b] the passive IMPs arise in passive components of the transmitter and components of the receiver following both a high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter within the transmitter; [c] where the transmitter filter connects the HPA and the antenna used by the transmitter; [d] generating the ICSs utilizes a power series representation describing the nonlinear process that gives rise to the IMPs, including creating an nth order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, conducting digital multiplication and filtering of S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n represents an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original).

At the summary judgment phase, the district court interpreted "three signals S1, S2 and S3" as being "separately identifiable" rather than "unique." J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record did not confine "S1, S2 and S3" to necessarily unique input signals and also gave weight to the inventor's statements, offered as extrinsic evidence, that "S1, S2 and S3" denotes signals in general, which can either be the same signal or distinct signals. J.A. 109–14. On appeal, the primary disputed claim limitation concerns the requirement that seven third-order multiplications be performed among these three signals in order to satisfy the claim.

During trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document that described the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). More specifically, Dr. Wells stated that the accused radios perform multiplication of three separately identifiable signals and equated the three different multiplications illustrated in the Nokia document with the seven multiplications set out in the patent claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169.

On a motion for JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must represent unique signals, treating it as forfeited since the issue was not raised during the Rule 50(a) proceedings. J.A. 38, 40. The court determined that employing two signals, along with a duplicate of one of those signals, fulfilled the court’s construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court held that with two unique signals, only three unique multiplications are produced; nonetheless, this sufficed to prove infringement because each of these three unique multiplications matches with two or three of the seven claim-required multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios produce cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, given that these radios employ solely two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. More particularly, Appellants assert that Finesse relinquished claim scope covering two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to specify "three signals," rather than "two or three signals," in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses the generation of cancellation signals utilizing two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants argue that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios conduct the seven multiplications recited in the claims, as Dr. Wells pointed out only three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope encompassing two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim that recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in view of Filipovic together with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. However, Finesse succeeded in overcoming this rejection, in part, by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which are also covered by the claimed method. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse amended the claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, this amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope as applied to products—such as the accused radios—employing two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, this amendment requires the presence of three separately identifiable signals, without mandating any particular quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could determine the accused radios perform the seven required multiplications set forth in the claims. At trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to substantiate that the accused radios execute the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, this document addresses modulus and complex conjugate operations and fails to demonstrate that the accused radios perform all seven multiplications required by the claims. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it references only three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence indicating that any of these multiplications are repeated so as to result in the execution of all seven multiplications demanded by claim limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony explaining how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse attempts to clarify this mapping; however, such clarification was not presented to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Moreover, because the document does not disclose multiplication of x1 and x2 themselves, but rather discloses their complex conjugates and moduli, Finesse’s mapping is incorrect because, for instance, it necessitates mapping S3 onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications identified in the claims because Nokia’s documents show the radios actually perform not only seven, but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells have identified which specific seven of the ten multiplications described in the Nokia documentation correspond to the claimed seven multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor is any explanation provided for how the ten multiplications align with the three distinct multiplications that Finesse attempted to equate with the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, because substantial evidence does not support the jury’s finding of infringement.

II. Damages

We assess a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial in accordance with the law of the relevant regional circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, a decision denying a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). After the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law with respect to damages or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Because we have now reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we accordingly vacate the award of damages.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the denial of JMOL regarding noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
