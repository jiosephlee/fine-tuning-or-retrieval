Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas's refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court's decision to deny their request for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court's denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Radio signals generally operate on distinct frequencies to prevent interference; however, transmission signals may mix when confronted with barriers such as metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors, producing additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs are produced by passive objects, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging that the use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia radios infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent, as well as claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the litigation. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum payment of $166,303,391 for the duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on grounds of noninfringement and damages, and also requested a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the appropriate regional circuit—in this case, the Fifth Circuit—when evaluating a district court’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). According to Fifth Circuit precedent, such denials are subject to de novo review; reversal is warranted “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit authority). The question of infringement as determined by a jury is reviewed for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). Substantial evidence exists “if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Claim construction presents a legal question subject to de novo review, while factual findings underlying the district court’s construction are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM results from mechanical problems occurring inside the radio, whereas external PIM originates from sources outside of the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a technique aimed at lessening IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently negating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling, at a chosen frequency, a received signal passband to generate a bit stream, with the received signals comprising both the desired signals and interference-producing signals, where the interference-producing signals are capable of causing intermodulation products that fall within the frequency band of the signals of interest; [b] employing one or more decimating filters to isolate the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] employing one or more decimating filters to isolate the source signals responsible for producing one or more intermodulation products within the frequency band of interest; [d] estimating each of the intermodulation products based on the identified source signals that give rise to those products; [e] mitigating one or more in-band intermodulation products through cancellation using the estimated intermodulation products; and [f] carrying out adjustments to phase and amplitude on the estimations of the interfering intermodulation signals in a closed-loop fashion, wherein phase and amplitude adjustment involves conducting sub-sample phase shifts to adjust the phase of the estimations of the interfering intermodulation signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As plaintiff, Finesse had the responsibility to demonstrate that the accused radios sample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. At trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, relying on a technical document from Nokia that shows the functioning of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, depicted below). Finesse Br. 15 (referencing annotation to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), shown inside a blue box, functions as a receiver that samples both the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" (each illustrated along a dashed red path), thereby generating a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently associated the "signals of interest" with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the "interference generating signals" with the modeled PIM path (each indicated on the dashed red path, as explained in the red box under the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Likewise, Dr. Wells' expert report stated, “the ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants argued that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path because this path is generated only after the RF ADC has already performed sampling—thus, a signal cannot be sampled before its generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Therefore, the modeled PIM path could not be the claimed "interference generating signals." In reply, Dr. Wells shifted his answer, stating that “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not specify that x1 and x2 are the "signals of interest" or "interference generating signals," but rather continued to describe the two signals as the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked explicitly if he meant “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, he did not directly link x1 or x2 to the "signals of interest" or "interference generating signals" as described in the patent claims.

The jury ultimately found all asserted claims of the ’134 patent to be infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells tied "interference generating signals" to the modeled PIM path, which precludes infringement, as the accused radios' receiver could not sample received signals including "interference generating signals" because those are produced after sampling occurs. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and noted that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Accordingly, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the “interference generating signals,” then limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied because the accused receivers do not sample a signal pass

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver system, where the transmitter and receiver are situated at the same location, the claimed method involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) in real-time and on a continuous basis to suppress passive IMPs within the receiver, by utilizing copies of the transmitter's signals; [b] these passive IMPs originate from passive components in both the transmitter and receiver, downstream of a high-powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter’s filter; [c] where the transmitter filter is placed between the HPA and an antenna shared by the transmitter; [d] the creation of ICSs is predicated on a power series model representing a nonlinear IMP-generating process, and the method entails forming an n-th order ICS by, using three signals labeled S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering combinations such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added). 

At the summary judgment phase, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as needing to be “separately identifiable,” though not “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that nothing within the intrinsic record restricted “S1, S2 and S3” solely to unique input signals, and it credited, as extrinsic evidence, inventor testimony indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” could be either the same or different signals, representing signals in general. J.A. 109–14. The central claim limitation on appeal concerns the requirement that seven third-order multiplications involving the three signals are performed to satisfy the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of these accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that these radios multiply three separately identifiable signals, equating the three distinct multiplications found in the Nokia document with the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. 

In deciding the motion for JMOL, the district court ruled that Appellants' contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals had been waived, since they failed to raise the argument at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court held that use of two signals along with a duplicate of one satisfied its interpretation of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court concluded that only three distinct multiplications arise when using two unique signals, and this sufficed to prove infringement because each of these three distinct products mapped onto two or three of the seven claim-recited multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by performing multiplication involving three signals, as the radios in question operate solely with two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. They further assert that Finesse disclaimed any claim scope covering the use of two signals by amending the claims during prosecution from “two or three signals” to explicitly “three signals,” doing so to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generation of cancellation signals utilizing two inputs. Refer to J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). In addition, Appellants maintain that a reasonable jury could not have found the accused radios engage in the seven claimed multiplications, as Dr. Wells’s testimony identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope covering two signals. The examiner had found a claim reciting “digitally multiplying two or three signals” to be obvious in view of Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse addressed this rejection partly by stating that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the method claimed also addresses passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Finesse did modify its claims to specify that digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” occurs in seven multiplications; nevertheless, this amendment does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of coverage for products—such as the radios at issue—that utilize only two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. If anything, the amendment requires three signals that are separately identifiable, not a specific count of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications claimed. During trial, Dr. Wells relied upon a Nokia document to attempt to establish that the accused devices performed the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document pertains to operations involving modulus and complex conjugate, and fails to demonstrate that the accused products carry out the seven specific multiplications as claimed. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it identifies only three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no record evidence to suggest that any of these multiplications are repeated such that all seven required multiplications occur, as specified by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not specify how the three unique multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications stated in the asserted claims. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse tries to clarify this correspondence on appeal, but such evidence was not introduced before the jury. See Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, the document in question does not describe multiplying x1 and x2 directly, but instead addresses their complex conjugate and modulus; therefore, Finesse’s attempted correspondence is incorrect because it would, for instance, require S3 to relate to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios satisfy the seven claimed multiplications based on Nokia’s documentation showing that the accused radios perform not seven, but ten, multiplications. See Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referencing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells clarify which seven out of the ten multiplications described in Nokia’s documents align with the seven claimed multiplications. See Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Likewise, there is no explanation as to how the ten described multiplications are associated with the three distinct multiplications Finesse claims correspond to the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 17–18. Under these conditions, a reasonable jury could not have found that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications. We therefore reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as substantial evidence does not support the jury’s infringement finding.

II. Damages

The denial of a motion for a new trial by a district court is reviewed pursuant to the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard to review such denials. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages or, as an alternative, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. Both requests were denied by the district court. J.A. 42–57 (order denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (order denying new trial on damages). Since we have reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we accordingly set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the denial of JMOL regarding noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
