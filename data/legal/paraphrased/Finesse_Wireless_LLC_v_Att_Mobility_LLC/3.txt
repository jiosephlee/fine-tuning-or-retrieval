Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the decision of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, which denied their motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court’s refusal to grant a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the award of damages.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain generally to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. While radios typically prevent interference by functioning on separate frequencies, their transmission signals may interact upon encountering obstacles such as metal fences, poorly connected cables, or corroded connectors, leading to the creation of new frequencies termed “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs are generated by passive barriers, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse filed suit against AT&T, claiming infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, based on AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the action. J.A. 389–90. A jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and awarded $166,303,391 as a lump-sum for damages covering the remaining terms of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and requested a new trial, but the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal. Our jurisdiction arises under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL by applying the legal standards of the relevant regional circuit, in this case, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo, and reversal is appropriate “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit authority). Our review of a jury’s determination on infringement is for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We examine claim construction decisions de novo, and we review any underlying factual determinations made by the district court that support its construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at minimizing IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” through use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a frequency band of incoming signals to generate a sequence of bits, where these incoming signals contain both desired signals and signals that produce interference, and where the interference signals are capable of causing intermodulation products that fall within the band of the desired signals; [b] extracting the desired signals from the bit sequence utilizing one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting source signals responsible for producing one or more inband intermodulation products of the desired signal using one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimate for each of the intermodulation products based on the source signals that give rise to such products; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products using the computed estimates of these products; and [f] making phase and amplitude corrections to the estimated intermodulation product interference signals within a closed-loop framework, wherein such phase and amplitude adjustments include performing phase shifts smaller than one sample interval to adjust the phase of the intermodulation product interference signal estimations. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

As the plaintiff, Finesse was required to establish that the accused radios sample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not demonstrate that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who testified as Finesse’s infringement expert, stated that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document which illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (with annotations to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) functions as a receiver, sampling both the so-called “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (both indicated by the dashed red path) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly explained that he associated “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, both of which appear along the dashed red path as clarified in the red box in the legend. For example, see J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report was consistent, asserting that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants highlighted that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path since that path is created downstream of the RF ADC after sampling has occurred; consequently, signals cannot be sampled prior to their creation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Therefore, the modeled PIM path cannot represent the claimed “interference generating signals.” When confronted with this, Dr. Wells shifted his position, testifying, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not testify that x1 and x2 were equivalent to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; instead, he continued to claim “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked directly if he was testifying “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, he did not assign x1 or x2 to either “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” as the claims require.

The jury determined all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, but infringement was impossible because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a received signal passband that includes interference generating signals, as such signals are only generated after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and also noted Dr. Wells’ testimony that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA. J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Thus, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the claimed “interference generating signals,” then limitation

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, where both the transmitter and receiver are located together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to mitigate passive IMPs in the receiver, performed continuously and in near real time, utilizing replicas of the transmitter signals; [b] the passive IMPs originate from passive components of the transmitter and receiver after the high powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter’s filter; [c] the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the antenna used by the transmitter; [d] generation of the ICSs is grounded in a power series model describing the non-linear process responsible for the IMPs, and encompasses generating an n-th order ICS by, provided with three signals S1, S2, and S3, carrying out digital multiplications and filtering for S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original).

During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean signals that are “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic evidence did not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” solely to unique input signals, and gave weight to inventor testimony—considered extrinsic evidence—that “S1, S2 and S3” refers to signals in a general sense and could be either the same or different signals. J.A. 109–14.

On appeal, the principal claim limitation in dispute is that seven third-order multiplications involving the three signals must be performed to satisfy the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his testimony on a Nokia technical document describing the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells explained that the accused radios perform multiplication on three separately identifiable signals, and equated the three distinct multiplications indicated in the Nokia document with the seven multiplications specified in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17).

The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. Upon consideration of judgment as a matter of law, the district court dismissed Appellants’ assertion that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals as waived, noting that Appellants did not present this argument during the Rule 50(a) proceedings. J.A. 38, 40. The court found that using two signals along with a duplicate of one sufficed to satisfy the interpretation of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court determined that employing two unique signals results in three distinct multiplications, and that this sufficed for infringement because each of those three distinct multiplications aligns with two or three of the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, since the accused devices allegedly employ only two signals, namely x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants assert that Finesse disclaimed claim coverage of two signals during prosecution when it amended the claims to specifically require “three signals,” as opposed to the original language of “two or three signals,” in order to distinguish the invention from the Filipovic prior art, which discloses cancellation signal generation with two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Further, Appellants maintain that the evidence could not support a jury finding that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications recited in the claims, arguing that Dr. Wells only identified three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage regarding two signals. The examiner had found a claim including “digitally multiplying two or three signals” to be obvious based on Filipovic and other references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse responded to this rejection by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which the claimed invention does, J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven separate multiplications, this amendment does not constitute the sort of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim coverage for devices, such as the accused radios, which have only two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The amendment, at most, necessitates the presence of three distinguishable signals, but does not mandate a particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. However, we concur that no reasonable jury could determine the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications as claimed. During the trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document as evidence that the accused radios performed these multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Nevertheless, that document refers to modulus and complex conjugate operations, and fails to demonstrate that the accused radios execute all seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. It lists only three different multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no evidence that any multiplication is repeated such that the total of seven, as specified by limitation 1[d], is satisfied. J.A. 26486 (see below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony establishing how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications required by the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse attempts to explain this mapping, but such evidence was not introduced to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the referenced document discusses the multiplication of the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2 rather than x1 and x2 themselves, the mapping proposed by Finesse is incorrect; for instance, it requires S3 to be associated with x2, x2’s complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications based on Nokia’s documentation indicating the radios actually perform ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referencing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do Finesse or Dr. Wells identify which subset of these ten Nokia multiplications match the seven recited in the claims. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they set forth how the ten listed multiplications correspond to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse mapped to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, a reasonable jury could not have found that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the infringement verdict lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

We apply regional circuit law when reviewing a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit law, a denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). After the jury returned its verdict, Appellants moved for JMOL on the issue of damages or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Since we have reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we hereby vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
