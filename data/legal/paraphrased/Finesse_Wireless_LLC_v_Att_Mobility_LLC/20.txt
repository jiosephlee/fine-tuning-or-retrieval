Caption: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Case No.: 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia) (together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) finding noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s rejection of their request for a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the rights to the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. To prevent interference, radios typically transmit on separate frequencies; however, their signals may interact upon encountering obstacles—such as metal fences, loose cabling, or corroded connectors—resulting in the formation of additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise from passive sources, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, stemming from AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia radio products. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the case. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, awarding lump-sum damages totaling $166,303,391 for the duration of the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) for noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and requested a new trial; the district court denied each motion. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal, and this court exercises jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL according to the legal standards of the relevant regional circuit, which in this matter is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). In the Fifth Circuit, a denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo, and reversal is appropriate “only if, when the evidence is viewed most favorably to the verdict, it so overwhelmingly favors one party that reasonable jurors could reach no other conclusion.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit precedent). The determination of infringement by a jury is evaluated under the substantial evidence standard. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “Substantial evidence exists where a reasonable jury, in consideration of the trial evidence, could have returned a verdict for the prevailing party.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We examine claim construction anew and review any factual determinations underlying the district court’s interpretation for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process intended to minimize IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently removing the “interference generating signals” based on a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a chosen frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a specified frequency range of incoming signals to produce a series of digital bits, where the incoming signals comprise both target signals and signals responsible for generating interference, with such interference signals having the potential to produce intermodulation products that fall within the frequency band occupied by the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the generated bit stream through the application of one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting source signals responsible for generating at least one intermodulation product located within the frequency band of the signals of interest, employing one or more decimating filters; [d] forming an estimated value of each intermodulation product based on the identified source signals producing those products; [e] eliminating at least one intermodulation product present within the band by making use of its computed estimate; and [f] applying closed-loop phase and amplitude corrections to the estimations of the intermodulation product interference signals, where this adjustment involves employing phase shifts of less than a full sample period to implement phase modifications to the estimations of the intermodulation product interference signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As plaintiff, Finesse was required to demonstrate that the accused radios perform sampling of a frequency band that comprises both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not prove that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. At the trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, claimed that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document that described the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, cited below). Finesse Br. 15 (citing J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) functions as a receiver that samples both the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" (each represented by a dashed red path), and generates a bit stream. See J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly associated the "signals of interest" with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the "interference generating signals" with the modeled PIM path, both of which are illustrated by the dashed red path as detailed in the legend’s red box. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report expressed similar views, stating “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel noted that the RF ADC is incapable of sampling the modeled PIM path since this path is produced downstream of the RF ADC, following sampling, making it impossible to sample signals that have yet to be generated. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Therefore, the modeled PIM path cannot serve as the claimed “interference generating signals.” In reaction, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony and stated, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not assign x1 or x2 as corresponding to either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” and maintained that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned whether his testimony was that “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet, again, he failed to map x1 or x2 to the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as the claims require. The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In its JMOL motion, Appellants argued that because Dr. Wells equated “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path—and since these signals are only created after sampling—the accused radios' receiver does not sample a passband including interference generating signals, thereby precluding infringement. J.A. 19–20. The district court observed that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Accordingly, if the modeled PIM path is the claimed “interference generating signals,” then the limitation in 1[a] cannot be satisfied because

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, where both a transmitter and the receiver are located together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) for mitigating passive IMPs in the receiver, performed continuously and almost in real time, by utilizing copies of the transmitter's signals, [b] where the passive IMPs originate in passive transmitter components of the transmitter and components of the receiver after the transmitter's high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter, [c] with the transmitter filter positioned between the HPA and an antenna employed by the transmitter, [d] where ICSs generation is derived from a power series model characterizing the non-linear process of IMP formation, including generating an n-th order ICS, where, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, ICSs are generated by digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n being an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined from the intrinsic record that “S1, S2 and S3” was not restricted to unique input signals and also relied on the inventor’s testimony as extrinsic evidence, indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” are generally representative and can be identical or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The pivotal claim limitation on appeal involves the requirement that seven third-order multiplications among the three signals be performed to satisfy the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the radios accused of infringement met the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document describing the functioning of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically stated that the accused radios perform multiplication on three separately identifiable signals and equated the three distinct multiplications mentioned in the Nokia document to the seven multiplicative combinations required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all the asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. Upon JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ assertion that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals on grounds of waiver, noting Appellants had not raised this at the Rule 50(a) juncture. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that employing two signals along with a duplicate of one satisfied the construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court found that, when two unique signals are utilized, only three distinct multiplications result, which sufficed to demonstrate infringement because each of these distinct multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven required claim multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

The appellants contend that no rational jury could have determined that the accused radios create cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, since, according to them, the accused radios utilize only two signals: x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, the appellants assert that Finesse relinquished claim coverage over two-signal embodiments when, during prosecution, it amended the claims to require “three signals” instead of the original “two or three signals,” in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art reference, which disclosed the generation of cancellation signals with two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, the appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications claimed, since Dr. Wells had only identified three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope covering two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim that recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in light of Filipovic along with additional references, as noted at J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, responded by arguing that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the claimed method addresses passive IMPs as well, successfully overcoming the rejection in part. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did further revise the claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplication steps, this amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of claim scope for devices, including the accused radios, which utilize two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The amendment at most imposes a requirement that there be three distinguishable signals, without mandating any definite count of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios execute the seven specific multiplications claimed. During trial, Dr. Wells relied upon a Nokia document as purported evidence that the accused radios perform the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, this document describes modulus and complex conjugate procedures and fails to demonstrate that the accused radios conduct the seven multiplications in question. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it mentions only three separate multiplication operations—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no supporting evidence to indicate that any multiplications are performed in repetition to satisfy the requirement that all seven claimed multiplications are performed, as mandated by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not offer testimony regarding how those three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the asserted claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse endeavors to elucidate this mapping during the appeal, such evidence was never introduced before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the relevant document does not describe multiplication of x1 and x2 but rather of their complex conjugate and modulus, the mapping set forth by Finesse is problematic, as it purports, for example, to map S3 onto signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications identified in the claims because Nokia’s documents establish that the accused devices carry out not only seven, but in fact ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (citing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identify any specific subset of the ten multiplications detailed in the Nokia document that is equivalent to the seven multiplications claimed. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they provide a mapping that connects these ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications which Finesse previously equated with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. On this record, a reasonable jury could not have found that the accused radios carried out the seven multiplications required by the claims. Therefore, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement with regard to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, since the verdict of infringement lacks the support of substantial evidence.

II. Damages

Our standard of review for a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial follows the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, denials of motions for new trial are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit precedent). After the jury rendered its verdict, Appellants filed a motion for JMOL concerning damages, or alternatively, for a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (order denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (order denying new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement on all asserted claims of both the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the denial of JMOL regarding noninfringement and set aside the awarded damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
