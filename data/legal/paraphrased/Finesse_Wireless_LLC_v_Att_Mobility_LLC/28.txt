Case Name: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Decision Date: September 24, 2025  
Case Number: No. 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s decision not to grant a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the denial of JMOL and set aside the award of damages.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. Radios typically prevent interference by transmitting at distinct frequencies; however, their signals can interact when obstructed by objects like metal fences, loose cables, or corroded connectors, leading to new frequencies referred to as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs are generated by non-powered obstacles, they are termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, based on AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia-manufactured radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia joined the case as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that every asserted claim was valid and infringed, and it awarded a lump-sum payment of $166,303,391 for the patents’ remaining duration. J.A. 169–71. The appellants subsequently filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, JMOL on damages, and for a new trial; all such motions were denied by the district court. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal, and we exercise jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL follows the law of the pertinent regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo, with reversal warranted “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit authority). Our review of a jury's finding as to infringement is for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “‘A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.’” Id. (internal citations omitted). We consider claim construction issues de novo, but any subsidiary factual determinations underlying the district court’s interpretation are examined for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process designed to lessen IMP interference through the separation of “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by neutralizing the “interference generating signals” via a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 exemplifies the invention.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling, at a selected frequency, a passband of incoming signals to produce a stream of bits, where the incoming signals comprise both signals of interest and signals that cause interference, with these interference-generating signals having the potential to produce intermodulation products within the band occupied by the signals of interest; [b] using one or more decimating filters to extract the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] employing one or more decimating filters to separate out the source signals responsible for creating one or more inband intermodulation products affecting the signal of interest; [d] deriving an estimate for each of the inband intermodulation products based on the identified source signals; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products with the use of the derived estimate; and [f] carrying out closed loop phase and amplitude corrections on estimations of the interfering intermodulation product signals, with such phase and amplitude adjustment involving sub-sample phase shifting to achieve the required phase correction for these estimated interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases added). As plaintiff, Finesse was responsible for demonstrating that the accused radios sampled a passband containing both “signals of interest” as well as “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sampled both categories of signals. Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with appellants. During the trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, the infringement expert for Finesse, asserted that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing his opinion on a technical document from Nokia that illustrated the functioning of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, presented below). Finesse Br. 15 (showing annotation to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in a blue box) operates as a receiver, sampling both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals" (each illustrated by dashed red lines), and generates a bit stream as a result. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently identified the “signals of interest” as the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” as corresponding to the modeled PIM path (both shown as dashed red lines, as detailed in the red legend box). For example, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Similarly, his expert report concluded that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants noted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because this path is generated downstream, i.e., after sampling, so it would not be possible to sample signals before their creation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. As a consequence, the modeled PIM path cannot be the claimed “interference generating signals.” Dr. Wells, in response, asserted “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, he never explicitly mapped x1 and x2 to “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals.” Instead, he maintained his stance: “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When clarification was sought as to whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells stated, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Again, he failed to map x1 or x2 onto the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as claimed.

The jury found infringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 patent. J.A. 169. During JMOL, Appellants contended that since Dr. Wells aligned the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, there could be no infringement because the accused radios’ receiver could not sample a passband containing interference generating signals that do not exist until after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and further noted that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Consequently, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the “interference generating signals” as claimed, limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied, since the accused receiver does not sample these signals, as they do not yet exist

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver system, where both the transmitter and receiver are located together, the process includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that counteract passive IMPs within the receiver in a continuous, near real-time manner by using versions of the transmitter's signals; [b] wherein the passive IMPs are produced within passive transmitter elements of the transmitter and within receiver components of the receiver, occurring downstream from the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter; [c] wherein the transmitter filter is situated between the HPA and the antenna utilized by the transmitter; [d] wherein the creation of ICSs relies on a power series representation of a non-linear process that results in the IMPs, including producing an n-th order ICS by, when provided with three signals S1, S2, and S3, performing digital multiplication and filtering on combinations S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer value. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases original). In summary judgment proceedings, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic evidence does not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to be exclusively unique inputs, further relying on inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence that “S1, S2 and S3” are representative of signals generally, and may refer to identical or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The central claim dispute on appeal concerns whether all seven third-order multiplications among the three signals must occur to satisfy the claim restriction. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document outlining the operation of the radios at issue. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused radios perform multiplications on three separately identifiable signals and equated the three distinct multiplications described in the Nokia document to the seven required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. During JMOL, the district court concluded that Appellants had waived their contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals, as this argument was not raised during the Rule 50(a) motion. J.A. 38, 40. The court found that using two signals, together with a repeat of one of those signals, sufficed to satisfy its construction requiring three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court determined that, when employing two unique signals, only three distinct multiplications are performed, which was adequate for a finding of infringement since each of the three distinct multiplications corresponds to either two or three of the seven multiplications mandated by the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the accused radios actually use only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished any claim scope covering two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to require “three signals” instead of “two or three signals” in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generating cancellation signals from two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Furthermore, Appellants argue that it would not have been reasonable for a jury to conclude the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications, pointing out that Dr. Wells identified only three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope relating to two signals. The examiner had previously rejected a claim that included “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in light of Filipovic and secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. However, Finesse overcame this rejection, at least in part, by noting that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” and does not concern itself with passive IMPs, which the claimed invention addresses. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend its claims to recite the step of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” across seven multiplications, this amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope as it applies to products, such as the accused radios, that involve two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, the amendment requires the presence of three individually discernible signals but does not stipulate a specific number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios execute the seven multiplications recited in the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document as evidence that the accused radios perform the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the document addresses modulus and complex conjugate operations, rather than showing performance of the seven claimed multiplications by the accused radios. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it enumerates only three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no evidence presented that any multiplications are repeated so as to satisfy the requirement for all seven claimed multiplications as specified by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony explaining how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications identified in the asserted claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to clarify this correspondence on appeal; however, such evidence was not presented before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, the document in question describes calculations involving the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2, but not the multiplication of x1 and x2 themselves. Therefore, the mapping Finesse proposes is defective; for instance, it would have S3 correspond to x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios execute the seven required multiplications because Nokia's documentation demonstrates these radios perform a total of ten multiplications, not just seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (noting p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identifies which seven of the ten multiplications described in the Nokia document align with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Similarly, they do not supply an explanation connecting the ten described multiplications to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse initially tied to the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 17–18. Under these circumstances, a reasonable jury could not conclude that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications required by the claims. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because the jury's finding of infringement lacks support from substantial evidence. 

II. Damages

Our standard for reviewing a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial is governed by the law of the relevant regional circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, an abuse of discretion standard applies when reviewing denial of a motion for new trial. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit precedent). After the jury rendered its verdict, Appellants moved for JMOL on damages or, alternatively, for a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both motions. J.A. 42–57 (order denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (order denying new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award must be vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
