Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia), together referred to as Appellants, challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s rejection of their request for a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons discussed below, we reverse the order denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which broadly concern techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radio systems. Radios typically operate on different frequencies to prevent signal interference; however, when their transmitted signals encounter obstructions—such as metallic fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors—the signals may interact to generate additional frequencies referred to as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs originate from passive obstacles, they are termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T, asserting that AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia radios infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the case. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and granted $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages to cover the remaining lifespan of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants sought JMOL of noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and a new trial, but the district court denied all post-trial motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now appeal. This court has jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) is governed by the law of the regional circuit—in this case, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit applies de novo review to denials of JMOL and will overturn such a denial “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit precedent). Our standard for reviewing a jury’s infringement verdict is whether there is substantial evidence to support it. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). Substantial evidence exists when the record permits a reasonable jury to have found in favor of the prevailing party based on the evidence submitted at trial. Id. (internal citations omitted). As for claim construction, we review it de novo while reviewing for clear error any factual determinations that form the basis of the district court’s construction. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM arises from mechanical issues inside the radio, while external PIM originates from sources outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a method that mitigates IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently uses a calculated estimate of the IMPs to cancel the “interference generating signals.” See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a chosen frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a band of received signals so as to produce a digital bit stream, where these received signals contain both target signals and signals responsible for generating interference, with such interference-generating signals being capable of causing inband intermodulation products affecting the signals of interest; [b] extracting the target signals from this bit stream through the use of one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting the source signals responsible for producing one or more inband intermodulation products through one or more decimating filters; [d] calculating an estimate for each such intermodulation product based on the identified source signals; [e] removing one or more inband intermodulation products by utilizing the computed intermodulation product estimates; and [f] adjusting the phase and amplitude of the estimated intermodulation product interference signals within a feedback (closed-loop) process, wherein the phase and amplitude adjustment involves applying sub-sample phase shifts to the estimations to achieve the required phase correction for the interference signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). Because Finesse was the plaintiff, it carried the responsibility of demonstrating that the accused radios sample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both types of signals. Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment. During the trial, Finesse’s expert witness, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing his opinions on a Nokia technical document which illustrated the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (highlighting J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) functions as a receiver, sampling both the asserted “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each illustrated with a dashed red line) to produce a bit stream. See J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells persistently linked the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path (each referenced along the dashed red path, as described within the red box in the legend). For instance, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Consistent with this, Dr. Wells' expert report stated, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

On cross-examination, counsel for Appellants argued that the RF ADC is not able to sample the modeled PIM path since this path comes into existence downstream from the RF ADC after the sampling process, meaning that sampling cannot occur before the relevant signals exist. See J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. The implication is that the modeled PIM path cannot fulfill the claim requirement of “interference generating signals.” In reply, Dr. Wells shifted his position, claiming, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not specifically link x1 or x2 to the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” and he continued to equate “the – two signals” with the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked for clarification whether x1 is the modeled PIM path, Dr. Wells answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, he never identified x1 or x2 directly as the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as claim language requires.

The jury found all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants contended Dr. Wells equated “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, thus making infringement impossible because, in the accused radios, the receiver does not sample any received signal passband that contains interference generating signals—those are generated post-sampling. J.A. 19–20. The trial court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (referencing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the “interference generating signals” as claimed, then

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system comprising a receiver co-located with a transmitter, the method includes: [a] generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to neutralize passive IMPs within the receiver, in a continuous and nearly real-time fashion, by utilizing copies of signals produced by the transmitter; [b] passive IMPs are produced within passive components of the transmitter and the components of the receiver, occurring after both a high powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter’s filter; [c] this transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the antenna used by the transmitter; [d] ICSs are produced by referencing a power series representation of the nonlinear process causing IMPs, and specifically involve creation of an n-th order ICS by, with three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally performing multiplications and filtering of S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as meaning “separately identifiable,” rather than “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic evidence did not require “S1, S2 and S3” to be distinct input signals, and gave weight to the inventor’s testimony (as extrinsic evidence) that “S1, S2 and S3” could generally refer to any signals, including those that may be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation disputed on appeal is the necessity that all seven third-order multiplications of these three signals occur to satisfy the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells stated that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a technical document from Nokia which illustrated how these radios functioned. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused radios executed multiplications with three separately identifiable signals and correlated the three individual multiplications shown in the Nokia documentation to the seven multiplications described in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. In the judgment as a matter of law, the district court considered and rejected Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must refer to unique signals as untimely, finding it had been waived since it was not raised during the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court determined that using two distinct signals along with a repeated version of one of those signals satisfied the construction requiring three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. It also found that only three distinct multiplications are generated when two unique signals are employed, and this was sufficient to demonstrate infringement because each of these three unique multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 38–39.

The appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that these radios utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. They specifically claim that Finesse relinquished claim coverage concerning two signals during prosecution by amending the claims to specify "three signals" rather than "two or three signals," in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art, which discloses the generation of cancellation signals with two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Further, appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios conduct the seven claimed multiplications, as Dr. Wells pointed to only three multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope with respect to two signals. The examiner previously rejected a claim encompassing “digitally multiplying two or three signals,” citing Filipovic and various secondary references as rendering it obvious, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, successfully responded to that rejection, partly by pointing out that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which are covered by the claimed method. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend its claims to include the limitation of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, this amendment does not amount to an “explicit and unequivocal” disclaimer of claim scope with regard to products—such as the accused radios—that utilize two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The most that can be derived from Finesse’s amendment is a requirement for three identifiable signals, without mandating any particular quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios perform the seven required multiplications recited in the claims. At trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to demonstrate that the accused radios engage in the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Nevertheless, the Nokia document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not establish that the accused radios execute the seven distinct multiplications claimed. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it identifies three specific multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no evidence that any are repeated so as to satisfy the requirement that all seven claimed multiplications, as stipulated in limitation 1[d], are performed. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony establishing how the three different multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to clarify this correlation in its appeal, but such information was not presented to the jury during trial. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, because the referenced document describes multiplication of the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2, not x1 and x2 themselves, Finesse's correspondence is defective—for instance, it seeks to assign S3 to x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications because documentation from Nokia indicates the radios actually perform not only seven, but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do Finesse or Dr. Wells delineate which of the ten listed multiplications in the Nokia record match the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor is there any explanation linking the ten multiplications to the three unique multiplications Finesse related to the seven asserted. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these circumstances, a reasonable jury could not have found that the accused devices practice the seven multiplications as claimed. We therefore reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s verdict on infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

We evaluate the district court’s refusal to grant a new trial under the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit assesses denials of motions for a new trial under an abuse-of-discretion standard. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants requested JMOL regarding damages, or alternatively, sought a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both of these requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). As we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award must also be vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we reverse the judgment denying JMOL on noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
