Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas's refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s denial of their request for a new trial on damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s JMOL denial and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, both of which pertain to techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radio communications. While radios typically use distinct frequencies to prevent interference, their transmitted signals can interact after reflecting off certain obstacles—such as metal fences, degraded cable connections, or corroded connectors—thereby creating additional frequencies known as intermodulation products (IMPs). See J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs result from passive structures, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). See J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated a lawsuit against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (“PIM-C”) capability incorporated in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the proceedings. J.A. 389–90. The jury found each asserted claim both valid and infringed, and it granted a lump-sum damages award of $166,303,391 for the residual terms of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants subsequently sought judgment as a matter of law (“JMOL”) of noninfringement, JMOL as to damages, and requested a new trial, but the district court denied each motion. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now pursue this appeal. This court’s jurisdiction is based on 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We examine a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL in accordance with the precedent of the regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit law, review of JMOL denials is conducted de novo and reversal is warranted only “if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Our standard of review for a jury’s finding of infringement is whether it is supported by substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). A factual determination is based on substantial evidence when, considering the evidence admitted at trial, a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party. Id. (internal citations omitted). Additionally, claim construction is subject to de novo review, and any factual determinations underlying the district court’s interpretation are assessed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at minimizing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] Oversampling is performed at a chosen frequency over a passband containing the received signals, thus generating a bit stream; these received signals comprise both signals of interest and interference-generating signals, with the interference-generating signals capable of producing inband intermodulation products that fall within the signals of interest; [b] the bit stream is then filtered using one or more decimating filters to isolate the signals of interest; [c] additional decimating filters are used to isolate source signals responsible for generating one or more inband intermodulation products within the signal of interest; [d] an estimate of each intermodulation product is then calculated from these source signals; [e] this estimate is used to cancel out one or more inband intermodulation products; and [f] phase and amplitude adjustments are then made to the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals in a closed-loop process, where these adjustments involve sub-sample phase shifts to accomplish the required phase adjustment on the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As the party with the burden of proof, Finesse was required to demonstrate that the accused radios sample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants assert that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample the required “signals of interest” along with “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, serving as Finesse’s infringement expert, asserted that the accused radios infringe the claims at issue from the ’134 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document that describes the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, as shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), indicated by a blue box, functions as a receiver that samples both the asserted “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (illustrated by a dashed red path), thereby generating a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently maintained that the “signals of interest” mapped to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” mapped to the modeled PIM path, both shown in the dashed red path and as explained in the red box beneath the legend. For example, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report likewise concluded that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel asserted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because this path is created after the RF ADC has performed sampling, meaning such signals cannot be sampled before they actually exist. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Thus, the modeled PIM path cannot be regarded as the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted and stated, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not directly associate x1 and x2 with “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals.” Instead, he continued to assert, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When prompted to clarify if “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, he did not explicitly associate x1 or x2 with either “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as required by the claims.

The jury ultimately determined all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants argued that Dr. Wells mapped “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, which forecloses infringement because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample any passband of received signals including “interference generating signals,” since such signals are not generated until after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The trial court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Accordingly, if the modeled PIM path is indeed the claimed “interference generating signals,” then limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied because the accused radios

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver that is positioned together with a transmitter, the described method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to actively suppress passive IMPs occurring in the receiver, on an ongoing, nearly real-time basis, utilizing duplicated signals generated by the transmitter, [b] with the passive IMPs originating in non-active transmitter components of the transmitter and receiver parts of the receiver situated downstream from a high powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter’s filter, [c] and where the transmitter filter is connected between the HPA and an antenna utilized by the transmitter, [d] and wherein the ICSs are generated based on a power series characterization of a nonlinear IMP generation process, and entail producing an n-th order ICS by, for three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). 

At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as requiring that the signals be “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined the intrinsic record did not limit “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals and further gave weight to the inventor's testimony—considered extrinsic evidence—that “S1, S2 and S3” could refer to either the same or different signals. J.A. 109–14.

The core claim limitation disputed on appeal concerns whether seven third-order multiplications among the three signals are required to fulfill the limitation. At trial, Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by relying on a Nokia technical document that illustrated the operation of the accused products. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused radios performed multiplications of three separately identifiable signals and equated the three discrete multiplications described in the Nokia document with the seven required by the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17).

The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In the JMOL proceedings, the district court found that Appellants had waived their contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique, since this was not raised during the Rule 50(a) motion. J.A. 38, 40. The district court concluded that utilizing two signals along with a duplicate of one satisfied the requirement for three separately identifiable signals according to its prior construction. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court found that when using two unique signals, only three discrete multiplications would result, and this was adequate to demonstrate infringement since each of those multiplications mapped to two or three of the seven claimed combinations. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios generate cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, asserting that the radios in question operate with only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. They specifically assert that Finesse relinquished any claim scope involving two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to require “three signals,” in place of the prior “two or three signals,” in order to distinguish the claims from the Filipovic prior art, which discloses generation of cancellation signals utilizing two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Appellants further assert that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform all seven of the claimed multiplications, contending that Dr. Wells identified only three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage with respect to two signals. The examiner had rejected a claim that recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07, yet Finesse countered this rejection partly by arguing that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which are also covered by the claimed process. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to require digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven separate multiplications, this amendment does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope for products—such as the accused radios—that involve only two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. Finesse’s amendment, at most, mandates the presence of three distinguishable signals, but does not specify any minimum number of distinct input signals. See J.A. 968–69. We concur, however, that the evidence presented could not have supported a jury finding that the accused radios executed the seven multiplicative operations as claimed. At trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document to contend that the accused radios performed the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Nevertheless, that document discusses modulus and complex conjugate calculations and fails to establish that the accused devices execute all seven multiplicative steps delineated in the claim. J.A. 26485–86. The document identifies only three different multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no supporting evidence that any operations are duplicated to reach all seven multiplications as the first limitation, 1[d], requires. J.A. 26486 (see below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three distinct multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications asserted in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse attempts to clarify this mapping in its appeal briefing, but that argument was never put before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the relevant document describes calculations involving the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2, not their direct multiplication, Finesse's proposed mapping is erroneous because, for instance, S3 must relate to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications since Nokia's documents indicate that they in fact perform ten multiplications, not just seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells specify which seven out of the ten multiplications in the Nokia documentation match the seven claimed in the patent. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they explain how these ten operations are related to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse sought to link to the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 17–18. Based on this record, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications claimed. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because substantial evidence does not support the jury's verdict of infringement.

II. Damages

We review the district court's denial of a new trial motion in accordance with the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denial of a motion for new trial is examined for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury’s verdict, Appellants requested JMOL on damages or, in the alternative, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Since we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we set aside the denial of JMOL regarding noninfringement and annul the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
