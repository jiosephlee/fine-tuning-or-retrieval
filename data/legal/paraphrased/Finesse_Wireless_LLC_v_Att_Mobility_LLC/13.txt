Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas's decision denying their motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s refusal to grant a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons stated below, we overturn the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the award of damages.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which broadly concern techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. Although radios prevent signal interference by utilizing separate frequencies, their transmission signals may interact—especially upon contact with certain objects such as metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors—creating additional frequencies referred to as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs result from non-powered objects, they are specifically called “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated a lawsuit against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent based on AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia-manufactured radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the action. J.A. 389–90. The jury concluded that each asserted claim was both valid and infringed, and awarded a lump sum of $166,303,391 as damages covering the duration remaining for both the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants then filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and for a new trial; the district court denied all these motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants have brought this appeal. Our jurisdiction arises under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL based on the precedent of the applicable regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit evaluates denials of JMOL anew, and will only reverse such decisions “if, after reviewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence so greatly favors one side that no reasonable jury could reach a different result.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). In determining whether a jury’s infringement verdict is supported, our standard is substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). A factual determination is underpinned by substantial evidence if, taking into account the evidence from trial, a reasonable jury could decide in favor of the winning party. Id. (internal citations omitted). We approach claim construction matters without deference, except for any subsidiary factual findings made by the district court in support of its construction, which we review for clear error. See Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM results from mechanical defects within the radio, whereas external PIM originates from sources outside of the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process for minimizing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] over-sampling a passband of received signals at a selected frequency in order to produce a bit stream, where the received signals contain both signals of interest and interference generating signals, and such interference generating signals can create intermodulation products within the same band as the signals of interest; [b] employing one or more decimating filters to extract the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] using one or more decimating filters to identify the source signals responsible for producing one or more inband intermodulation products affecting the signal of interest; [d] calculating an estimate for each of the inband intermodulation products based on the identified source signals; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products by applying the computed estimate; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude adjustments for the estimations of the intermodulation product interference signals in a closed loop fashion, which includes performing sub-sample phase shifts to adjust the phase of the estimations of these intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). Since Finesse was the plaintiff, it had the obligation to demonstrate that the accused radios performed sampling of a passband containing both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants maintain that Finesse failed to establish that the accused radios sampled both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment. During trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, opined that the accused radios infringe the claims of the ’134 patent, basing his analysis on a technical document from Nokia that describes the functionality of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, included below). Finesse Br. 15 (containing annotations to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) functions as a receiver which samples the recited “signals of interest” as well as “interference generating signals” (each shown in the dashed red path), and then produces a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly explained that he equated “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and matched “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path (as illustrated by the dashed red path and described in the red box under the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In his expert report, Dr. Wells similarly concluded, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

When cross-examined, Appellants’ counsel highlighted that the RF ADC was not capable of sampling the modeled PIM path, since this path is produced downstream of the RF ADC after sampling has taken place, and thus signals cannot be sampled before their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. The implication is that the modeled PIM path cannot correspond to the claimed “interference generating signals.” Dr. Wells, in response, shifted his position and asserted, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells never stated that x1 and x2 corresponded to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” and persisted in stating, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). Upon being asked specifically whether he meant that “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he clarified, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Nonetheless, he did not connect x1 or x2 with either “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as the claim language requires.

The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. At JMOL, Appellants argued Dr. Wells equated “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path and infringement could not be found because the receiver in the accused radios does not sample a passband of signals containing “interference generating signals,” given that those signals do not exist until after the sampling has happened. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system comprising a receiver, co-located with a transmitter, the method involves: [a] creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) in real-time, continuously, within the receiver to counteract passive IMPs, utilizing duplicates of the transmitter’s own signals, [b] where these passive IMPs arise from passive elements both in the transmitter (post-high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter) and within receiver components, [c] with the transmitter filter positioned between the HPA and the transmitter’s antenna, [d] and where generating ICSs is predicated on a power series representation of the non-linear process behind IMP creation, further requiring for the n-th order ICS the digital multiplication and filtering of combinations such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3 using three signals S1, S2, and S3, with n as an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original).

At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but did not require them to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record does not mandate “S1, S2 and S3” to be unique input signals and also considered the inventor’s extrinsic testimony indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” are general designations and can represent either identical or different signals. J.A. 109–14.

The principal claim limitation contested on appeal is the necessity that all seven possible third-order products of the three signals be computed to satisfy the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells opined that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document that details the accused radios’ operation. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios perform multiplication of three separately identifiable signals, matching the three multiplication operations outlined in the Nokia document with the seven multiplications required by the patent claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined all challenged claims of the ’775 patent to be infringed. J.A. 169.

On JMOL, the district court found that Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must refer to unique signals was waived, as this issue was not raised at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court ruled that employing two signals and a duplicate of one suffices under the court’s construction of “three separately identifiable signals.” J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court determined that using two distinct signals produces only three unique products, but this was enough to establish infringement because each of those three products aligns with two or three of the seven products specified in the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not conclude that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the radios in question utilize solely two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. They further assert that during prosecution, Finesse narrowed the claim scope from "two or three signals" to specifically "three signals" in response to the Filipovic prior art, which disclosed the generation of cancellation signals using two inputs. References include J.A. 1021–22 (original claim language); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (applicant’s response to rejection). Appellants additionally contend that no rational jury could determine that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications specified in the claims, noting that Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope with respect to two signals. The examiner had rejected a claim that involved “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in view of Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07; however, Finesse successfully countered this rejection by, among other arguments, clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the patented method also targets passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse subsequently amended the claims to specify “three signals S1, S2 and S3” being digitally multiplied in seven multiplications, such an amendment does not constitute the “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope that would exclude products—including the accused radios—that use two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, the amendment mandates the presence of three distinct signals and imposes no requirement for a particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that the evidence would not have allowed a reasonable jury to conclude that the accused radios perform the seven required multiplications as specified. During trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to assert that the accused devices practice the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the referenced document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not demonstrate that the accused radios execute the seven specified multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, the document describes only three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence indicating that any multiplications are repeated such that the seven multiplications required by limitation 1[d] are performed. J.A. 26486 (as reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not describe how the three different multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications asserted in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse endeavors to clarify this correspondence, but such evidence was not introduced to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the referenced document does not disclose multiplying x1 and x2 themselves, but rather their complex conjugates and moduli, Finesse’s mapping is incorrect; for instance, it would necessitate S3 being assigned to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios undertake the seven multiplications recited in the claims because Nokia’s documents reflect that the accused devices actually perform a total of ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referencing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (showing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells ever specify which seven of the ten listed multiplications in the Nokia document allegedly match the seven multiplications required by the claims. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they elucidate a mapping between the ten multiplications in the document and the three unique multiplications that Finesse attempted to align with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Under these circumstances, a reasonable jury could not conclude that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement for the claims at issue in the ’775 patent, as the jury’s verdict of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support. 

II. Damages

We apply the law of the regional circuit when reviewing the denial of a motion for new trial. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of motions for a new trial are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury rendered its verdict, Appellants filed a motion for JMOL concerning damages or, in the alternative, sought a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both of these post-trial motions. J.A. 42–57 (JMOL on damages denied); J.A. 58–65 (motion for new trial on damages denied). Because we have reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we consequently vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
