Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas's refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants additionally contest the district court’s denial of their request for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we overturn the denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) owns the ’134 and ’775 patents, which broadly concern methods to reduce intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. While radios prevent interference by operating on separate frequencies, transmission signals may interact when encountering obstructions, such as metal fencing, improperly connected cables, or corroded connectors, thereby generating new frequencies known as intermodulation products (IMPs). See J.A. 14892, 14920. When IMPs result from passive obstructions, they are termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). See J.A. 14895; ’134 patent, Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent, Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the litigation. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were valid and infringed and granted a lump-sum damages award of $166,303,391, covering the remainder of the ’134 and ’775 patents’ lifespans. J.A. 169–71. Appellants subsequently moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL concerning damages, and requested a new trial; the district court denied all motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the standard of review for a district court’s denial of JMOL as set by the pertinent regional circuit—in this instance, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). According to the Fifth Circuit, the denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo and is reversed "only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit precedent). The determination of infringement by a jury is reviewed for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). As defined in that precedent, "[a] factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." Id. (internal citations omitted). Issues of claim construction are considered de novo, but any subsidiary factual determinations made by the district court are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a technique to mitigate IMP interference by first separating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals," and subsequently eliminating the "interference generating signals" through the use of an estimated computation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, the passband of incoming signals to produce a stream of digital bits, where these incoming signals comprise both the signals targeted for reception and signals that act as sources of interference, with the latter being capable of producing intermodulation artifacts that fall within the frequency range of the desired signals; [b] extracting the desired signals from the generated bit stream through the use of one or more decimating filters; [c] separating, using one or more decimating filters, the source signals responsible for producing the inband intermodulation artifacts within the frequency spectrum of the target signal; [d] generating an estimated value for each of the intermodulation products that originate from the identified source signals; [e] removing one or more of the intermodulation artifacts present in the band of interest by using the calculated estimates for those products; and [f] conducting modifications to both phase and amplitude of the intermodulation product estimation signals in a closed feedback loop, where such modifications include implementing sub-sample phase shifts to effectuate phase corrections on these estimated interfering intermodulation signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases retained). Finesse, as plaintiff, held the responsibility to demonstrate that the accused radios actually sample a signal passband that incorporates both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse failed to establish that the accused radios indeed sample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. At trial, Finesse’s expert in infringement matters, Dr. Jonathan Wells, opined that the accused radios satisfied the claims of the ’134 patent, basing his opinion on a technical document provided by Nokia, which outlines the operational structure of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, depicted below). Finesse Br. 15 (with annotation to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown within a blue box) functions as a receiver that samples both the alleged “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each depicted along the dashed red path) to produce a stream of bits. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently assigned the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, both indicated in the dashed red path and further explained in the red box within the legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report echoed this view, stating “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants highlighted that the RF ADC cannot possibly sample the modeled PIM path since this path is created downstream of the RF ADC, after the sampling process, such that signals cannot be sampled prior to their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This reasoning would mean the modeled PIM path could not constitute the claimed “interference generating signals.” In reply, Dr. Wells shifted position and asserted, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not specifically map x1 and x2 onto the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” as recited in the claims, instead repeating that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When pressed to clarify if “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” his reply was, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. He once again did not explicitly correlate x1 or x2 with either “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” which is what the claims require. The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In the post-trial motion for JMOL, Appellants argued that because Dr. Wells had mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, there could be no infringement, since in the accused radios, the receiver does not sample a received passband including interference generating signals (those signals only arise after sampling). J.A. 19–20. 1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver, where both the transmitter and receiver are co-located, the method includes: [a] continuously and nearly in real-time creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to eliminate passive IMPs present in the receiver, utilizing replicas of the transmitter’s signals; [b] these passive IMPs arise in passive transmitter components of the transmitter and in receiver components of the receiver, subsequent to the high powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter's transmitter filter; [c] the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and an antenna employed by the transmitter; [d] the process for generating the ICSs relies on a power series characterization of the non-linear process responsible for generating the IMPs, and involves producing an n-th order ICS by, for three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n denotes an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). During summary judgment, the district court determined the term “three signals S1, S2 and S3” should be interpreted as “separately identifiable,” though not required to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court held that the intrinsic record does not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals and also relied upon the inventor's testimony, serving as extrinsic evidence, confirming that “S1, S2 and S3” stand for signals generally and can either be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. The central claim limitation on appeal concerns whether seven third order multiplications involving the three signals are performed to satisfy the limitation. At trial, Dr. Wells testified, relying on a Nokia technical document outlining the operation of the accused radios, that the accused radios practice the asserted claims of the ’775 patent. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells stated the accused devices multiply three separately identifiable signals and equated the three unique multiplications identified in the Nokia document with the seven required in the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found that every asserted claim of the ’775 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must consist of unique signals as waived because Appellants did not raise this argument at the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court determined that utilizing two signals plus a duplicate of one of them met its construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court found that only three distinct multiplications result when two unique signals are used, and determined that this sufficed to demonstrate infringement since each of these three distinct multiplications corresponds to either two or three of the seven multiplications specified in the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting instead that the devices in question operate using only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, Appellants maintain that Finesse disclaimed coverage for two-signal systems by amending the claims during prosecution to require "three signals" rather than "two or three signals," in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generation of cancellation signals utilizing two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Furthermore, Appellants argue that there was insufficient evidence for any reasonable jury to conclude the accused radios execute the seven multiplications recited in the claims, as Dr. Wells testified to identifying only three such multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We reject the argument that Finesse relinquished claim coverage regarding two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim encompassing “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious based on Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07. In response, Finesse overcame the rejection by arguing that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the method in the claims further addresses passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse amended the claims to incorporate the step of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplicative operations, this amendment does not constitute the type of “clear and unambiguous” relinquishment of claim scope with respect to devices, such as the accused radios, possessing two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. Finesse’s amendment merely establishes that three individually discernible signals are necessary and does not specify any required number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios conduct the seven multiplicative operations as required by the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells relied upon a Nokia document to purport that the accused radios executed the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, this document references modulus and complex conjugate functions and does not demonstrate that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications identified in the claims. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it identifies only three unique multiplication expressions—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence indicating that any multiplication is repeated such that all seven required by limitation 1[d] are carried out. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony explaining how the three separate multiplications are related to the seven multiplications identified in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse attempts to elucidate this mapping on appeal; however, such evidence was not introduced before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, because the document does not describe multiplication of x1 and x2, but instead discusses their complex conjugate and modulus, the mapping proposed by Finesse is defective. For instance, it requires S3 to correspond to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse argued that the accused radios meet the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation demonstrates that the accused products perform ten multiplications, not merely seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells clarify which seven of the ten multiplications described in the Nokia documentation correspond with the claimed seven multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Moreover, no correspondence is provided between the ten multiplications in the Nokia document and the three different multiplications Finesse assigned to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s infringement finding is not backed by substantial evidence.

II. Damages

We review a denial by the district court of a motion for a new trial in accordance with regional circuit precedent. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit examines the denial of a new trial for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). Subsequent to the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL as to damages, or alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. Both motions were denied by the district court. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Because we overturn the denial of JMOL of noninfringement on all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we therefore vacate the damages judgment.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we reverse the district court's denial of JMOL on noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
