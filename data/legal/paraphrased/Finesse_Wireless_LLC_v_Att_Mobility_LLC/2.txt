Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s decision not to grant a new trial on damages. For the reasons discussed below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the holder of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain generally to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. Radios typically prevent interference by using separate frequencies; however, their transmitted signals can merge when they encounter obstructions such as metal fences, unsecured cable connections, or corroded connectors, thereby producing new frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs result from passive barriers, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, specifically alleging use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality within Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the suit. J.A. 389–90. A jury concluded that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum of $166,303,391 in damages for the remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants subsequently sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement, JMOL concerning damages, and requested a new trial; the district court denied all motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now appeal, and our jurisdiction is established pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the pertinent regional circuit—in this instance, the Fifth Circuit—when evaluating a district court’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit examines JMOL denials using a de novo standard, and will reverse the district court “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Jury determinations regarding infringement are reviewed for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). As stated in that case, “[a] factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We review claim construction issues de novo, and assess any subsidiary factual determinations underlying the district court’s construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process intended to mitigate IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” through use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency, a passband comprising received signals so as to generate a bit stream, where the received signals consist of both signals of interest and interference-generating signals, with the interference-generating signals being capable of producing intermodulation products that fall within the band of the signals of interest; [b] using one or more decimating filters to extract the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] using one or more decimating filters to extract source signals responsible for generating one or more intermodulation products located inband of the signal of interest; [d] determining an estimate for each such intermodulation product based on the extracted source signals; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products by using the estimated intermodulation products; and [f] applying phase and amplitude modifications to the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals in a closed loop fashion, where this phase and amplitude modification includes performing sub-sample phase shifts to achieve a phase adjustment on the estimates of the intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). Because Finesse was the plaintiff, it had the obligation to demonstrate that the accused radios perform sampling of a passband that encompasses both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this argument. During trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, maintained that the accused radios meet the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, relying on a technical document from Nokia that illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), shown within the blue box, acts as a receiver that samples both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each shown following a dashed red path), converting them into a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently mapped the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, with both illustrated along the dashed red path as detailed in the legend’s red box. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). His expert report similarly stated that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants noted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because that path is produced after the RF ADC, meaning sampling happens before these signals are generated; thus, the modeled PIM path cannot serve as the claimed “interference generating signals.” J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Accordingly, the modeled PIM path does not qualify as the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his explanation, testifying, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not identify x1 and x2 as mapping to the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” and maintained his testimony that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When prompted to clarify whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he stated, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, he did not assign x1 or x2 to either “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” as required by the claims. The jury concluded all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In the motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), Appellants contended Dr. Wells mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path and that infringement could not be found, since the receiver of the accused radios does not sample a passband of received signals containing interference generating signals because those signals arise only after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). 1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, in conjunction with a transmitter that is situated in the same location as the receiver, the method involves: [a] creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) for the continuous, near real-time cancellation of passive IMPs in the receiver, by utilizing duplicates of the transmitter signals, [b] where the passive IMPs arise in passive components of the transmitter as well as in the receiver's components, subsequent to the high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter in the transmitter, [c] where the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the transmitter's antenna, [d] where the generation of ICSs is predicated upon a power series representation of the nonlinear process responsible for IMP generation, and entails creating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n representing an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). 

At the summary judgment phase, the district court interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean that they must be “separately identifiable,” but did not require them to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that nothing in the intrinsic record limits “S1, S2 and S3” to only unique input signals, and also relied on the inventor’s testimony, as extrinsic evidence, that “S1, S2 and S3” are meant to generally represent signals, which may either be the same or different signals. J.A. 109–14.

The central claim limitation disputed on appeal concerns whether the claim requires seven third-order multiplications among the three signals. During trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document that describes the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells explained that the accused radios perform multiplication of three separately identifiable signals and equated the three distinct multiplications shown in the Nokia document to the seven required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found all asserted claims of the ’775 patent to be infringed. J.A. 169.

On JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals as having been waived, because Appellants did not raise this argument during the Rule 50(a) motion. J.A. 38, 40. The court held that using two signals plus a duplicate of one of those signals satisfied the requirement for three separately identifiable signals as construed by the court. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court found that, when only two unique signals are involved, only three distinct multiplications occur, but that was adequate to establish infringement since each of those three multiplications corresponded to two or three of the seven multiplications specified in the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no rational jury could have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, as the accused devices only utilize two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse narrowed the scope of its claims to exclude two signals during prosecution by amending the claims from “two or three signals” to specifically recite “three signals” in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art, which discloses producing cancellation signals from two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants assert that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications, given that Dr. Wells identified only three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope relating to two signals. The examiner found a claim that recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” to be obvious over Filipovic combined with additional references, see J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse overcame that objection in part by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the method claimed also covers passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to require the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven separate multiplications, this amendment does not constitute the type of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim coverage for products—such as the accused radios—that utilize two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At best, the amendment requires the presence of three separately identifiable signals, without specifying the total number of unique input signals needed. See J.A. 968–69. However, we concur that a reasonable jury could not have found the accused radios perform the seven multiplications claimed. At trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document in an attempt to demonstrate that the accused radios execute the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Nevertheless, the Nokia document describes operations involving modulus and complex conjugate functions and does not establish that the accused radios undertake the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it identifies only three specific multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and no evidence indicates that any multiplications are repeated to achieve the full seven as required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells failed to specify how the three separate multiplications he identified correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse tries to clarify this mapping during the appeal, such evidence was not introduced before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, the document in question does not reveal multiplication of x1 and x2, but instead discusses their complex conjugate and modulus, rendering Finesse’s mapping defective since it would, for instance, require S3 to be associated with signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation indicates the radios actually execute not just seven but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referring to p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identifies which specific subset of the ten multiplications mentioned in Nokia’s document matches the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). They also fail to establish a mapping between the ten multiplications and the three separate multiplications that Finesse related to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given this, a reasonable jury could not have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s verdict of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

Our review of the district court’s denial of a new trial follows regional circuit precedent. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit evaluates a denial of a new trial for an abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on the issue of damages or, in the alternative, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Since we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED
