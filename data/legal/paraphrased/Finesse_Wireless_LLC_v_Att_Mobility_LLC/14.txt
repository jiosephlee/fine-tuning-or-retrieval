Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants additionally contest the district court’s denial of their motion for a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons explained below, we reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques aimed at reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radio systems. To prevent interference, radios use separate frequencies, but their transmission signals may interact when encountering certain objects—such as metal barriers, unsecured cabling, or corroded connectors—leading to the creation of additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs result from non-electronic obstacles, they are termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought an action against AT&T, asserting that the use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia radios constituted infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent. J.A. 267–87. Nokia joined the suit as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were valid and infringed, and awarded a lump-sum payment of $166,303,391 to cover the duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants submitted motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, damages, and sought a new trial; the district court denied all such requests. J.A. 1–72. Appellants have now taken this appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the standard for reviewing a district court’s denial of JMOL as set by the relevant regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). According to Fifth Circuit precedent, review of a JMOL denial is conducted de novo, with reversal warranted "only if, when all evidence is viewed in the light most favorable to the jury's verdict, the evidence overwhelmingly favors one party to such a degree that reasonable jurors could not conclude otherwise." Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). When evaluating a jury verdict of infringement, we determine whether substantial evidence exists. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A jury’s factual determination is based on substantial evidence where a reasonable juror could, in consideration of the evidence adduced at trial, find in favor of the winning party.” Id. (internal citations omitted). With respect to claim construction, we perform a de novo review, but any subsidiary factual determinations underlying the district court's interpretation are reviewed for clear error. See Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process intended to diminish IMP interference, which operates by separating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals," and subsequently eliminating the "interference generating signals" through use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] performing over-sampling of a received signal passband at a chosen frequency to produce a stream of bits, where these received signals contain both the desired signals and signals that cause interference, with such interference-generating signals being able to produce intermodulation products that fall within the passband of the signals of interest; [b] separating the desired signals from the bit stream through the use of one or more decimating filters; [c] separating the source signals responsible for producing one or more inband intermodulation products affecting the signal of interest using one or more decimating filters; [d] calculating an estimated value for each of the one or more intermodulation products originating from these source signals; [e] removing one or more inband intermodulation products by utilizing these estimations of the intermodulation products; and [f] adjusting the phase and amplitude of the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals through a closed loop process, wherein this phase and amplitude adjustment includes implementing sub-sample phase shifts to achieve the desired phase modification of the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

As the party asserting the claim, Finesse had the obligation to demonstrate that the accused radios performed sampling of a passband that comprised both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants maintain that Finesse did not sufficiently establish that the accused radios sampled both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this position. During the trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, presented testimony that the accused radios practiced the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, relying on a technical document from Nokia that illustrated the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (marking J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown inside the blue box) functions as a receiver that samples both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (both marked by the dashed red line), thereby generating a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently asserted that he associated “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path (each represented within the dashed red line as described in the red box beneath the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In his expert report, Dr. Wells further stated, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel challenged that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path because it is created downstream from the RF ADC, after sampling, and thus the signals cannot be sampled prior to their creation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This means that the modeled PIM path cannot constitute the claimed “interference generating signals.” When confronted, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony, asserting, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not testify that x1 and x2 directly correspond to “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; instead, he continued to insist that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). Upon questioning to clarify if he was stating that “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Once again, he did not equate x1 or x2 to the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as specified by the claims. Ultimately, the jury determined all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. On JMOL, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells associated the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, precluding infringement because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband of received signals that includes “interference generating signals”—those signals are not produced until after sampling is completed. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and noted Dr. Wells had testified the PIM Model is created in the GROOT FPGA. J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation 1

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, where both transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that eliminate passive IMPs in the receiver, in an ongoing and nearly real-time manner, through the use of duplicates of the transmitter signals, [b] with the passive IMPs being created by passive transmitter elements of the transmitter and components of the receiver, after the transmitter's high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter, [c] the transmitter filter being positioned between the HPA and an antenna that the transmitter employs, [d] wherein the process for generating ICSs is guided by a power series that models a nonlinear process which produces the IMPs, including, for generating an n-th order ICS, the steps of—provided with three signals S1, S2, and S3—digitally multiplying and filtering the combinations S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n denotes an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases preserved). Upon summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean signals that are “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined the intrinsic record does not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals and gave weight to inventor testimony—introduced as extrinsic evidence—that “S1, S2 and S3” are representative of signals in general and may be either identical or distinct. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation contested in this appeal is the requirement that seven third order multiplications among the three signals must be performed to satisfy the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the radios accused of infringement do in fact infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document describing the function of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused devices multiply three separately identifiable signals, and he compared the three different multiplications detailed in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications mandated by the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined all asserted claims of the ’775 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. During the JMOL phase, the district court held that Appellants had waived the argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals, as they failed to present this contention at the Rule 50(a) juncture. J.A. 38, 40. The court further found that use of two signals together with a copy of one signal satisfied the construction of “three separately identifiable signals.” J.A. 38. Moreover, the court determined that with two unique signals, only three distinct multiplications result, and this sufficed to demonstrate infringement, because each of these three distinct multiplications maps to two or three of the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that only two signals, x1 and x2, are employed by the accused devices. See Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that, during prosecution, Finesse limited the claim scope to "three signals" instead of "two or three signals" in response to an obviousness rejection based on the Filipovic prior art—which teaches generating cancellation signals using two inputs—thereby surrendering claim coverage for systems utilizing only two signals. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants argue that there was insufficient evidence for a jury to reasonably determine the accused radios conduct the seven claimed multiplications, emphasizing that Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We reject the argument that Finesse relinquished claim scope relating to two signals. The examiner found a claim covering “digitally multiplying two or three signals” to be obvious in view of Filipovic together with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. However, Finesse responded to that rejection partly by stating that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which are also covered by the claimed method. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse amended the claims to require digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, such a modification does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope for devices, such as the accused radios, that have two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The most that Finesse’s amendment mandates is the presence of three distinct signals; it does not demand a particular count of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications. At trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document to assert that the accused radios execute the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Yet, that document discusses modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not demonstrate the performance of all seven claimed multiplications by the accused radios. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, the document enumerates only three different multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence indicating repetition of multiplications sufficient to account for the seven required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (as reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not offer any testimony regarding how the three separate multiplications relate to the seven multiplications identified in the asserted claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to elucidate this connection on appeal, yet the record shows that this evidence was not presented to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, since the document in question describes multiplying the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2 rather than the variables themselves, Finesse’s mapping is defective. For instance, the mapping requires S3 to correspond to signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications since Nokia’s documentation indicates that they, in fact, carry out ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do Finesse or Dr. Wells clarify which selection of the ten multiplications referenced in the Nokia documentation equates to the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they describe how the ten listed multiplications align with the three discrete multiplications Finesse identified as corresponding to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given this, the evidence could not permit a reasonable jury to conclude that the accused radios practice the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent since the jury’s infringement verdict is unsupported by substantial evidence.

II. Damages

The review of a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial follows the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard in evaluating denials of new trials. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury rendered its verdict, Appellants filed for JMOL on the issue of damages or, in the alternative, sought a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both of these requests. J.A. 42–57 (JMOL on damages denied); J.A. 58–65 (new trial on damages denied). In view of our decision to reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
