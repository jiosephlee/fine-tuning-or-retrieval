Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Author: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement of the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s decision denying a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the holder of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference within radio devices. While radios typically prevent signal interference by operating at distinct frequencies, transmitted signals may interact with certain obstacles (such as metal fences, improperly connected cables, or corroded connectors) and give rise to novel frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs are generated by non-active, passive impediments, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought a lawsuit against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through AT&T’s utilization of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia then intervened in the proceedings. J.A. 389–90. Following trial, the jury concluded that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and it awarded Finesse a lump-sum of $166,303,391, reflecting damages for the full remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants subsequently sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) for noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and requested a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now appeal, and our court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the standard for reviewing a district court's denial of JMOL in accordance with the law of the relevant regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit conducts de novo review of such denials, overturning only “if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit authority). Infringement findings by a jury are examined under the substantial evidence standard. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). As explained in Apple, “[a] factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). With respect to claim construction, our review is de novo, while any subsidiary factual determinations underlying the district court’s interpretation are assessed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at decreasing IMP interference, which involves separating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals," followed by the elimination of the "interference generating signals" through the application of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] performing oversampling at a selected frequency upon a passband containing incoming signals to generate a sequence of bits, where these incoming signals comprise both signals of interest and interference-generating signals, and where the interference-generating signals are capable of producing intermodulation products within the band of the signals of interest; [b] employing one or more decimating filters to separate the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] utilizing one or more decimating filters to isolate the source signals responsible for creating inband intermodulation products relative to the signal of interest; [d] determining an estimation for each of the intermodulation products produced by these source signals; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products utilizing the respective estimated values of the intermodulation products; and [f] adjusting both phase and amplitude of the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals iteratively in a closed-loop fashion, wherein such phase and amplitude adjustments involve making sub-sample phase shifts to effectuate the phase correction on the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases added). Because Finesse is the plaintiff, it carried the obligation of demonstrating that the accused radios are capable of sampling a passband of signals including both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The Appellants assert that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sampled both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment. During the trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, Finesse’s infringement expert, stated that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing his conclusions on a technical document from Nokia that illustrated the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, see reproduction below). Finesse Br. 15 (marking J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, represented within the blue box) functions as a receiver that samples both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” (each illustrated by the dashed red path), producing a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells maintained that he associated “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path (with both shown as the dashed red path and described in the legend’s red box). For instance, see J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In his expert report, Dr. Wells likewise concluded, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants pointed out that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path, since the modeled PIM path is produced only after the RF ADC performs its sampling and because signals cannot be sampled before their creation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Consequently, the modeled PIM path could not correspond to the claimed “interference generating signals.” Dr. Wells responded by stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not clarify that x1 and x2 represented the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” and continued to describe “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked specifically whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, he never specifically aligned x1 or x2 with the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as required by the claims. 

The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. Upon JMOL, Appellants argued that, since Dr. Wells equated the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, infringement could not be found because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a signal passband that includes the interference generating signals; such signals are only generated after the sampling step occurs. J.A. 19–20. The court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and acknowledged that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path corresponds to the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation 1[a] is not satisfied, because the accused receivers

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver, where both a transmitter and the receiver are situated together, the method involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) in order to eliminate passive IMPs in the receiver on a continual and near real-time basis, utilizing replicas of the transmitter signals; [b] the passive IMPs are formed within passive components of the transmitter and within the receiver components after the transmitter's high-powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter; [c] the transmitter filter is connected between the HPA and the antenna used by the transmitter; [d] the process of generating the ICSs utilizes a power series representation of the nonlinear process responsible for creating the IMPs, and includes the generation of an n-th order ICS by, when given three signals S1, S2, and S3, performing digital multiplication and filtering on S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). In the summary judgment phase, the district court interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean that the signals are “separately identifiable,” but do not need to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record did not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to unique inputs, and relied on inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence that “S1, S2 and S3” can refer to signals generally, which may be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation in dispute on appeal concerns the requirement that the method perform seven third order multiplications using the three signals. At trial, Dr. Wells provided testimony that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document detailing the operation of the accused devices. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals, equating the three unique multiplications shown in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found for infringement on all asserted claims of the ’775 patent. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court deemed Appellants’ argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must refer to unique signals waived, finding that Appellants had not raised it under Rule 50(a). J.A. 38, 40. The court held that the use of two signals, along with a duplicate of one, sufficed to fulfill the construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court observed that when two unique signals are used, only three distinct multiplications result, but this still demonstrated infringement because each of these three distinct products corresponds to two or three of the seven required by the claim. J.A. 38–39.

The Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, on the basis that the accused radios employ only two signals: x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, the Appellants assert that Finesse limited the scope of its claims with respect to two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to specify “three signals” rather than “two or three signals,” thereby distinguishing over the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generation of cancellation signals using two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Furthermore, the Appellants argue that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications required by the claims because Dr. Wells only pointed to three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We do not concur with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage regarding systems with two signals. The examiner previously rejected a claim reciting the step of "digitally multiplying two or three signals" as being obvious in view of Filipovic and secondary references, see J.A. 1006–07. However, Finesse was able to overcome this rejection, at least in part, by noting that "Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs" and does not address passive IMPs, which are also treated by the method set forth in the claims. See J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse also revised the claims to require the digital multiplication of "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in seven multiplications, such an amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope as it pertains to products—such as the accused radios—that feature two separate input signals. See Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, this amendment requires the presence of three distinct signals without specifying a minimum number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we do agree that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios actually execute the seven multiplications specified by the asserted claims. At trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document as evidence that the accused radios carried out the claimed multiplications. See J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Yet, that document is concerned with modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not establish that the seven claimed multiplications occur in the accused radios. See J.A. 26485–86. Instead, only three separate multiplications are identified—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and the record contains no indication that any multiplication is repeated, which would be necessary to satisfy the seven-multiplication requirement in limitation 1[d]. See J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells failed to provide testimony describing how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse attempts to clarify this correlation in its appeal, such evidence was never submitted before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, the referenced document does not reveal multiplication of x1 and x2 themselves, but instead addresses their complex conjugate and modulus. Accordingly, Finesse’s mapping is defective in that it requires, for instance, S3 to be associated with the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications, referencing Nokia’s documentation showing the accused radios actually perform not only the seven, but a total of ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referring to p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells specified which of the ten multiplications in the Nokia documentation correspond to the seven multiplications at issue in the asserted claims. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor did they elucidate a relationship from those ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications Finesse linked to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. In light of these deficiencies, it would not have been reasonable for a jury to conclude that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications. We therefore reverse the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because there is not substantial evidence to support the jury’s verdict of infringement.

II. Damages

The standard for reviewing a denial of a motion for a new trial follows the applicable regional circuit law. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit assesses such denials for an abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Subsequent to the jury’s verdict, Appellants filed a motion for JMOL on damages or, alternatively, for a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both post-trial requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Because we have reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award must be vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we reverse the denial of judgment as a matter of law on noninfringement and set aside the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
