Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the decision of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, which denied their motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement of the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court's refusal to grant a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons discussed below, we reverse the district court’s JMOL denial and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) holds ownership of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio communications. Typically, radios avoid interference by transmitting on separate frequencies, but transmit signals may mix when meeting impediments—such as metallic barriers, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors—producing additional frequencies identified as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). See J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs are induced by passive elements, they are termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). See J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated a lawsuit against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent based on AT&T’s utilization of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability within Nokia radios. See J.A. 267–87. Nokia entered the suit as an intervenor. See J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that each of the asserted claims was both valid and infringed and awarded a lump sum of $166,303,391 in damages, covering the entire duration remaining on the ’134 and ’775 patents. See J.A. 169–71. The appellants submitted motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL with respect to damages, and a motion for a new trial, all of which were denied by the district court. See J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal, and jurisdiction lies pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the standard of review for a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) as established by the law of the applicable regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit conducts a de novo review of JMOL denials, overturning a verdict “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit authority). Infringement determinations by a jury are reviewed by this court based on whether substantial evidence supports the verdict. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding will be considered supported by substantial evidence so long as a reasonable jury, viewing the trial evidence, could have found for the party prevailing at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Questions of claim construction are reviewed de novo, while any subsidiary factual findings made in support of the district court’s construction are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a technique aimed at mitigating IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently neutralizing the “interference generating signals” through the application of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency above the Nyquist rate, a passband from the received signals so as to produce a bit stream, where these received signals comprise both desired signals and interference-generating signals, with the latter being capable of producing intermodulation products that fall within the same band as the desired signals; [b] employing one or more decimating filters to separate the desired signals from the bit stream; [c] using one or more decimating filters to extract the source signals responsible for generating at least one inband intermodulation product affecting the desired signal; [d] determining an estimation of each such intermodulation product based on the corresponding source signals; [e] removing one or more of the inband intermodulation products by applying the previously computed estimation of such products; and [f] adjusting both phase and amplitude of the intermodulation product interference signal estimations in a closed loop, where such adjustment involves sub-sample phase shifting to effectuate the phase modification on those estimations of the intermodulation product interference signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

As the party bringing the action, Finesse was required to establish that the accused radios carry out sampling of a passband containing both the “signals of interest” as well as the “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not demonstrate that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, provided testimony that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent based on information from a Nokia technical document that illustrates the functionality of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, also shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (citing annotations to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated during his testimony that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) serves as a receiver that samples both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (both illustrated by the dashed red path), producing a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently maintained that he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path (each depicted along the dashed red path and clarified in the red box under the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In Dr. Wells’ expert report, he similarly asserted that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants highlighted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path since the modeled PIM path is only produced downstream of the RF ADC after the sampling occurs; thus, sampling cannot take place before signal generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Therefore, the modeled PIM path cannot serve as the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his position, saying “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, he did not specify that x1 and x2 correspond to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; rather, he persisted in stating that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked directly whether he was asserting “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Again, he did not associate x1 or x2 with either the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as the claims require. The jury rendered a verdict of infringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 patent. J.A. 169. On JMOL, Appellants argued that Dr. Wells identified “interference generating signals” as being the modeled PIM path, and that infringement is impossible since the accused radios’ receiver cannot sample a received signals passband containing interference generating signals, as those signals do not exist until after sampling is performed. J.A. 19–20. The district court conceded that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and further, “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, should the modeled PIM path be considered as the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, where both the transmitter and receiver are situated together, the described method involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that negate passive IMPs in the receiver, on a continuous and nearly real-time basis, by utilizing copies of the transmitter’s signals; [b] the passive IMPs arise within passive components of the transmitter and components of the receiver after the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter; [c] with the transmitter filter positioned between the HPA and the transmitter’s antenna; [d] ICS generation relies on a power series model of the nonlinear process generating the IMPs, specifically requiring the generation of an n-th order ICS. This entails, with three signals S1, S2, and S3, the digital multiplication and filtering of the combinations S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphasis in original). At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as needing to be “separately identifiable,” not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined the intrinsic record does not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, further crediting inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence, that “S1, S2 and S3” can represent general signals, which may be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. On appeal, the primary contested claim limitation requires that all seven specified third order multiplications among the three signals be performed to satisfy the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells opined that the radios accused of infringement meet the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document that describes their operation. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically indicated that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals, and mapped the three distinct multiplications shown in the Nokia document to the seven required by the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found infringement on all asserted claims of the ’775 patent. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court deemed the Appellants’ position that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals to have been waived, noting this argument was not advanced at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court also held that utilizing two signals, along with a repeated version of one, satisfied its interpretation of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Further, the court concluded that only three distinct multiplications occur when two unique signals are used, and considered that adequate for infringement because each of those three multiplications accounts for two or three of the seven claimed ones. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios produce cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, as the accused radios purportedly utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, Appellants assert that Finesse disclaimed coverage of configurations involving two signals when, during prosecution, it amended the claims to specify “three signals” instead of “two or three signals,” in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art, which discloses generating cancellation signals from two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Appellants further argue that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications required by the claims, as Dr. Wells only referenced three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding systems with two signals. The examiner had found a claim that included the language “digitally multiplying two or three signals” to be obvious based on Filipovic and other references, see J.A. 1006–07; however, Finesse overcame this rejection by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which are covered by the claimed method. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did further amend its claims by adding the requirement of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, such amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope that would exclude products like the accused radios, which use two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, Finesse’s amendment requires the presence of three distinct signals but does not impose any mandate regarding the specific count of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios carry out the seven specified multiplications. During the trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to argue that the accused devices execute the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Nevertheless, this document discusses modulus and complex conjugate calculations and fails to demonstrate that the accused radios perform all seven requisite multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, the document shows only three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence indicating that any are repeated so as to achieve the total of seven multiplications as specified in limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

In addition, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony explaining how the three distinct multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse seeks to clarify this correspondence on appeal, such evidence was not presented for the jury's consideration. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, the relevant document fails to disclose multiplication of x1 and x2, but instead discusses their complex conjugate and modulus; therefore, Finesse's attempted correspondence is flawed—for instance, mapping S3 to signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios meet the requirement of seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documents indicate the accused radios perform ten multiplications, not just seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identifies which specific seven among the ten Nokia-documented multiplications correspond to the seven recited in the patent. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). They also fail to explain how the ten documented multiplications are mapped to the three distinct multiplications and subsequently to the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 17–18. On this record, no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios perform the seven multiplications required by the claims. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as substantial evidence does not support the jury's verdict of infringement.

II. Damages

The denial of a motion for a new trial by the district court is reviewed under regional circuit precedent. See Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, such denials are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants filed a JMOL motion regarding damages, or alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both motions. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL as to damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying motion for a new trial on damages). Since we now reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the decision denying JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
