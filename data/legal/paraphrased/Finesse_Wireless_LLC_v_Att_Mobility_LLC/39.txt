Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the decision of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas denying their motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court's refusal to grant a new trial on damages. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) owns the ’134 and ’775 patents, which broadly concern techniques to reduce intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Although radios generally prevent signal interference by operating at separate frequencies, transmitted signals can combine after interacting with certain obstacles, such as metal fences, improperly connected or rusted cables, producing additional frequencies identified as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When passive obstacles cause these IMPs, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated a lawsuit against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent based on AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability within Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia entered the action as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. Following trial, the jury concluded all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and it granted lump-sum damages of $166,303,391 covering the remaining terms of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement and damages, as well as seeking a new trial, but the district court rejected all motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL following the law applicable to the pertinent regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The standard in the Fifth Circuit for reviewing denials of JMOL is de novo; a reversal is appropriate “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit authority). Substantial evidence governs our review of a jury’s infringement determination. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We examine claim construction questions de novo, and review the district court’s subsidiary factual determinations supporting its construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process designed to mitigate IMP interference by separating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals," and subsequently eliminating the "interference generating signals" through the application of a calculated estimation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a passband containing received signals to produce a stream of bits, where these received signals comprise both signals of interest and interference generating signals, with the interference generating signals being capable of creating inband intermodulation products within the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bit stream by utilizing one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting the source signals responsible for generating one or more inband intermodulation products in the signal of interest with the use of one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimate of each intermodulation product generated by the identified source signals; [e] suppressing one or more inband intermodulation products utilizing the calculated estimates; and [f] carrying out phase and amplitude corrections on the estimations of the interfering intermodulation product signals via a closed loop process, such phase and amplitude corrections involving sub-sample phase shifts to adjust the phase of the estimations of the interfering intermodulation product signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases added). 

As plaintiff, Finesse was responsible for establishing that the accused radios sample a passband of signals that contains both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not demonstrate that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this contention. During the trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, maintained that the accused radios practiced the asserted claims of the ’134 patent based on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (showing annotations to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated during his testimony that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC)—represented within the blue box—functions as a receiver that takes samples of what are described in the claims as “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” both of which are shown along the dashed red path, to produce a bit stream. See J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently assigned the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and linked the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, with both identified in the dashed red path according to the legend’s red box explanation. For example, he stated, “The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest,” (J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19), and regarding the modeled PIM path, he described it as “the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest” (J.A. 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1). Further, he affirmed during questioning, “[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.” (J.A. 15009 at 141:10–14). His expert report also maintained that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” while “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal’” (J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343).

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants argued the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path because it is generated after the point of sampling—that is, downstream of the RF ADC—and a signal cannot be sampled before it exists. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; also see J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Therefore, counsel contended, the modeled PIM path cannot satisfy the claim limitation of being “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, he did not equate x1 and x2 with the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; instead, he persisted in assigning “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20. He continued this line: see J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), and J.A. 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), J.A. 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). Upon being pressed as to whether he was asserting “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he clarified, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Notwithstanding this, he never matched x1 or x2 with the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as the patent requires.

Despite this, the jury found all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants asserted that since Dr. Wells identified the “interference generating signals” as the modeled PIM path—yet the accused radios’ receivers do not sample a passband that includes these signals, as they are only created after the sampling process—there could be no infringement. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “The ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals,’” and acknowledged his testimony that the PIM Model is created in the GROOT FPGA. J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, with both the transmitter and receiver being physically located together, the method includes: [a] continuously and in nearly real time, producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to mitigate passive IMPs occurring in the receiver, by employing copies of signals transmitted by the transmitter, [b] with the passive IMPs arising within passive transmitter components belonging to the transmitter, as well as in receiver components of the receiver, after both a high powered amplifier (HPA) and a transmitter filter in the transmitter, [c] where the transmitter filter is connected between the HPA and the antenna that serves the transmitter, [d] and generating ICSs depends upon representing the non-linear process responsible for IMPs with a power series, such that producing an n-th order ICS consists of, for three signals—S1, S2, and S3—digitally multiplying and filtering the following products: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n as an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). 

At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean that these signals must be “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that nothing in the intrinsic evidence confines “S1, S2 and S3” solely to unique input signals, and also gave weight to the inventor’s testimony as extrinsic evidence, which explained that “S1, S2 and S3” are general representations of signals that may either be the same or different. J.A. 109–14. The principal disputed claim limitation on appeal is the requirement that the claim be satisfied only when all seven third-order multiplications involving the three signals are carried out.

During the trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, relying on a technical document from Nokia that details the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells testified that these radios perform multiplications involving three separately identifiable signals and related the three separate multiplications shown in the Nokia document to the seven specified in the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169.

On a JMOL motion, the district court ruled that Appellants had waived the argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals by not advancing it during the Rule 50(a) motion. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that it was adequate under its construction for there to be two signals plus a duplicate of one signal to satisfy the requirement of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court determined that using two unique signals leads to three distinct multiplications, which suffices to demonstrate infringement because each of those three distinct multiplications corresponds to either two or three of the seven multiplications required by the claim language. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that only two signals, x1 and x2, are utilized in the accused radios. Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, they claim that during prosecution, Finesse relinquished claim scope involving two signals when it amended the claims to specify “three signals” rather than “two or three signals,” in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art reference, which disclosed cancellation signals generated using two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants argue that the evidence was insufficient for a reasonable jury to find that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications set forth in the claims, since Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim coverage regarding two signals. The examiner rejected a claim encompassing “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in light of Filipovic and additional secondary references, see J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse overcame this rejection in part by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which are also covered by the claimed method. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Although Finesse amended the claims to incorporate the limitation of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” across seven multiplications, this amendment does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of coverage for devices, such as the accused radios, that have two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, Finesse’s modification requires three separately identifiable signals but does not specify any particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have determined the accused radios perform all seven multiplications as required by the claims. During trial, Dr. Wells referred to a Nokia document to demonstrate that the accused radios engage in the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, this document describes modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not evidence that the accused radios execute the seven specified multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, the document only lists three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no indication that any multiplication is repeated so as to satisfy all seven claimed multiplications as required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not explain the relationship between the three separate multiplications and the seven multiplications asserted in the claim. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse seeks to clarify this correspondence, but this information was never introduced before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, since the referenced document does not describe multiplication of x1 and x2, but rather refers to their complex conjugates and moduli, the mapping proposed by Finesse is problematic as it, for instance, links S3 to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications, citing Nokia’s documentation purportedly showing that the radios in question actually perform not just seven, but ten, multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referencing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (noting p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do Finesse or Dr. Wells specify which group of the ten multiplications described in the Nokia documentation matches the seven multiplications required by the claim. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Similarly, they do not indicate how the ten multiplications correspond to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse equated with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. We therefore reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s finding of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

Our review of the denial of a motion for a new trial is governed by the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard to denials of new trial motions. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). After the verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on the damages issue or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court rejected both motions. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL regarding damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying a new trial on damages). Because we have reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we hereby vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
