Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Author: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s rejection of their motion for a new trial on damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the award of damages.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for addressing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Radios typically prevent signal interference by utilizing distinct frequencies, yet when their transmission signals interact with certain obstacles (such as metal fences, poor cable connections, or corroded connectors), these transmissions can mix and generate additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs arise from non-electronic obstacles, they are termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought an infringement action against AT&T, alleging that claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent were infringed through AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia entered the case as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. The jury found every asserted claim to be both valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum of $166,303,391 to cover damages over the remaining term of both the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants then filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and for a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now seek review in this court, which has jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the relevant regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit, to assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit employs a de novo standard for such review, overturning the denial “only if, considering the evidence most favorably to the verdict, the evidence so clearly and overwhelmingly favors one side that reasonable jurors could not find otherwise.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit precedent). Substantial evidence is the standard for reviewing a jury’s determination of infringement. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). This means a factual finding must be sustained if a reasonable jury could have returned a verdict for the prevailing party based on the trial evidence. Id. (internal citations omitted). Distinctions exist between internal PIM, resulting from mechanical problems inside the radio, and external PIM, which is attributable to sources external to the radio. J.A. 15223–24. We review claim construction de novo, but assess any subsidiary factual determinations that informed the district court’s construction under the clear error standard. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a technique intended to lessen IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” through use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a range of received signals to generate a stream of bits, where these received signals encompass both the desired signals and those signals responsible for creating interference, and such interference-producing signals are capable of generating intermodulation products within the frequency range of the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bit stream through the use of one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting the originating signals that result in one or more intermodulation products within the same band as the signals of interest by means of one or more decimating filters; [d] generating an estimate for each of these intermodulation products originating from their respective source signals; [e] removing one or more inband intermodulation products by employing the estimated values for these products; and [f] conducting adjustments to both phase and amplitude in the estimates of the interfering intermodulation signals within a feedback (closed loop) configuration, where such adjustments include making phase shifts of less than one sample to refine the phase alignment of the intermodulation product estimates. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphasis in original).

As the party bringing suit, Finesse carried the responsibility to establish that the accused radios sample a range of signals that comprises both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants maintain that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both types of signals. Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with that position. During trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, testified that the accused radios infringed the claims at issue in the ’134 patent, basing his analysis on a Nokia technical document which illustrated the operational characteristics of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, as shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) acts as a receiver which samples both the alleged “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” (these are shown following the dashed red path), resulting in the production of a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly asserted that he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path (each depicted within the dashed red path, as indicated by the red box in the legend). For example, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In Dr. Wells’ expert report, he similarly opined, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and stated that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants emphasized that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because it is produced downstream of the ADC, after sampling occurs, such that a signal cannot be sampled before its generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This point suggests that the modeled PIM path cannot be the claimed “interference generating signals.” In reply, Dr. Wells shifted his explanation and testified, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not testify that x1 and x2 mapped to “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals.” Instead, he reiterated that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked directly whether he meant x1 is the modeled PIM path, he replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, he never made explicit that x1 or x2 map to either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as claimed.

A jury determined all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. At JMOL, Appellants argued that since Dr. Wells mapped “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, infringement was impossible because the accused radios' receivers do not sample a passband containing such signals—the modeled PIM path signals do not exist at the time of sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation 1[a] cannot be met, as the

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver where both a transmitter and the receiver are located together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to counteract passive IMPs within the receiver, performed continuously and in near real time, by utilizing copies of the transmitter's signals; [b] these passive IMPs originate from passive elements in the transmitter and receiver following the transmitter’s high power amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter; [c] the transmitter filter itself is positioned between the HPA and the transmitter’s antenna; [d] generating the ICSs involves applying a power series approach describing the nonlinear generation of IMPs, requiring, for the n-th order ICS, that when given three signals S1, S2, and S3, one digitally multiplies and filters the combinations S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as being “separately identifiable,” without a requirement that they be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined intrinsic evidence did not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to solely unique signals, further accepting extrinsic evidence from the inventor that “S1, S2 and S3” generally signifies any signals and could be identical or distinct. J.A. 109–14. The primary claim limitation contested on appeal concerns the necessity for seven third-order multiplications of the three signals to fulfill the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells presented evidence that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the radios in question. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells particularly testified that the accused radios perform multiplication on three separately identifiable signals and mapped the three distinct multiplications indicated in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications specified in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. Upon JMOL, the district court held that Appellants’ assertion that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique was waived since it was not raised during the Rule 50(a) proceedings. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that the use of two signals plus a duplicate of one of these signals satisfied its construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. It further concluded that only three unique multiplications occur when employing two distinct signals, but this was still sufficient to show infringement, as each of the three unique multiplications aligns with two or three of the seven claimed multiplication steps. J.A. 38–39.

The Appellants contend that no rational jury could have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that these radios utilize solely two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. More precisely, the Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for two signals when it amended the claims during prosecution to specify “three signals” in place of “two or three signals,” thereby addressing the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generating cancellation signals through two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, the Appellants assert that no reasonable jury could have concluded the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications required by the claims, pointing out that Dr. Wells only testified to identifying three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope with respect to two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim reciting “digitally multiplying two or three signals” on the grounds of obviousness over Filipovic and additional secondary references, see J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, responded to this rejection by, among other things, clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which are encompassed by the method claimed. See J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Finesse also amended its claims to introduce the limitation of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” via seven multiplications, but this does not constitute the kind of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope concerning products such as the accused radios, which utilize two distinct input signals. See Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The most that can be said of Finesse’s amendment is that it calls for three individually identifiable signals, not a set or specific number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. That said, we concur that no rational jury could have concluded that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications required by the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document as evidence that the accused radios perform the necessary multiplications. See J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not demonstrate the performance of the seven requisite claimed multiplications by the accused radios. See J.A. 26485–86. In fact, the document identifies only three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence indicating any repetition of multiplications such that all seven required multiplications, per limitation 1[d], are carried out. See J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

In addition, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three separate multiplications he discussed align with the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse seeks to clarify this relationship in its appeal, such evidence was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the referenced document details the multiplication of the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2 instead of the variables themselves, Finesse’s proposed mapping is problematic, since it necessitates, for example, associating S3 with x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse argued that the accused radios perform the seven asserted multiplications because Nokia’s documentation indicates that not only seven but a total of ten multiplications are carried out by the accused radios. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do either Finesse or Dr. Wells specify which set of the ten multiplications in Nokia’s document aligns with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they identify any connection between the ten multiplications listed in the document and the three multiplications that Finesse relates to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. On this record, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we overturn the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as substantial evidence does not support the jury’s verdict of infringement. 

II. Damages

Our review of a district court’s refusal to grant a new trial follows the law of the applicable regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit evaluates a denial of a new trial on an abuse-of-discretion standard. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit precedent). After the jury reached its verdict, Appellants sought JMOL as to damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court rejected both requests. J.A. 42–57 (JMOL on damages denied); J.A. 58–65 (motion for new trial on damages denied). Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for every asserted claim of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the district court's refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
