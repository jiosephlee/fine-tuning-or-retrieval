Case Name: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket Number: 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia) (together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court's denial of their motion for a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which are directed to techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMP) in radio systems. Radios use different frequencies to prevent signal interference, but their transmitted signals can interact when they encounter obstacles like metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors, leading to the generation of additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs are generated by passive objects, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated a lawsuit against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent due to AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia entered the case as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and awarded a lump-sum of $166,303,391 as damages for the remaining terms of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Following the verdict, Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, JMOL on damages, and a new trial, but the district court denied each of these motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal. Our court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the pertinent regional circuit—in this instance, the Fifth Circuit—when examining a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL. Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit undertakes a de novo review of JMOL denials, overturning only if “when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit precedent). The standard for reviewing a jury’s infringement determination is whether substantial evidence exists. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (citations omitted). We review claim construction questions anew, with any factual determinations underpinning the district court’s construction evaluated for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process intended to mitigate IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by the elimination of the “interference generating signals” with the aid of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as an illustrative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] acquiring a bit stream by sampling, at a selected frequency above the Nyquist rate, a frequency band that comprises received signals, where these received signals consist of both the target signals and signals that produce interference, and where the interference-generating signals are capable of creating intermodulation products within the frequency range of the signals of interest; [b] using one or more decimation filters to separate the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] further using one or more decimation filters to separate source signals that give rise to one or more intermodulation products occurring within the frequency band of the signals of interest; [d] deriving estimates of each intermodulation product from those source signals identified as generating said products; [e] using these estimates to mitigate or suppress one or more in-band intermodulation products; and [f] applying adjustments for phase and amplitude to the intermodulation product estimation signals within a closed-loop system, which includes performing sub-sample phase adjustments to carry out phase correction of those estimation signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

Finesse, as the plaintiff, had the obligation to demonstrate that the accused radios acquire samples from a frequency band containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused products sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment. During trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios meet the limitations of the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document illustrating the functioning of the accused devices. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (reproduced Nokia document). Finesse Br. 15 (citing annotated J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in a blue box) functions as a receiver that samples both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each shown by the dashed red line) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Dr. Wells repeatedly testified that he associated “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and linked “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path (both shown in the dashed red line and described in the red box in the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report similarly asserted, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel argued that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path since it is created after the sampling point, downstream from the RF ADC, making it impossible to sample signals before their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; also see J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This would mean the modeled PIM path cannot be the “interference generating signals” referenced in the claims. In reply, Dr. Wells shifted and stated, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Nevertheless, Dr. Wells did not assert that x1 and x2 align with either the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” and instead maintained, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; also see J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked to clarify if “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he responded, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Again, he never expressly linked x1 or x2 to the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as required by the claim language. The jury concluded all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants maintained that Dr. Wells mapped “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path and that infringement could not be found because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a set of received signals containing “interference generating signals,” as these signals are produced only after the sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court noted “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path were considered to be the “interference generating signals” identified in the claims, limitation 1[a] would not be satisfied since the

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver system where the transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that offset passive IMPs in the receiver, performed continually and nearly in real time, utilizing replicas of the transmitter’s signals, [b] where the passive IMPs originate in the passive components of the transmitter as well as the receiver’s components, positioned subsequent to both the high powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter filter, [c] where the transmitter filter is installed between the HPA and the antenna employed by the transmitter, [d] where creating the ICSs employs a power series representation of the nonlinear process responsible for generating the IMPs, and entails creating an n-th order ICS by, for three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering combinations such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n being an integer. See Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphasis in original). At summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as being “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record did not restrict “S1, S2, and S3” solely to unique input signals and also relied upon inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence, indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” may generally refer to signals which could be the same or different. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation contested on appeal concerns the requirement for seven third-order multiplications among the three signals to fulfill the claim’s terms. During trial, Dr. Wells stated that the accused radios infringed the asserted ’775 patent claims, relying on a Nokia technical document that describes the operation of these radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). In particular, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals, correlating the three distinct multiplications identified in the Nokia document with the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In addressing JMOL, the district court rejected Appellants’ assertion that “S1, S2 and S3” must each be unique signals, holding that the argument was waived since Appellants did not present it at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The district court concluded that employing two signals along with a duplicate of one constituted sufficient compliance with its construction of “three separately identifiable signals.” J.A. 38. Additionally, it found that only three distinct multiplications result when two unique signals are used, and determined that this sufficed for infringement since each of those three different multiplications equates to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded the accused radios produce cancellation signals by means of multiplying three signals, since, according to Appellants, these radios utilize solely two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. More particularly, Appellants assert that Finesse narrowed the scope of the claims during prosecution by amending them to specify “three signals” in place of “two or three signals,” thereby disclaiming coverage of two-signal systems in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generating cancellation signals with two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Further, Appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could find that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications recited in the claims, as Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding two signals. The examiner had previously rejected a claim referring to "digitally multiplying two or three signals" as being obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse countered this rejection by stating that "Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs," and not the passive IMPs that the claimed method also targets. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse also amended its claims to include the limitation of digitally multiplying "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in seven multiplications, this amendment does not amount to a "clear and unambiguous" disclaimer of claim coverage over products, such as the accused radios, that use two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, Finesse’s modification stipulates three distinct, individually identifiable signals, but does not mandate a specific number of different input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios perform the seven multiplications claimed. During trial, Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document to establish that the accused radios performed the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the document describes modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not demonstrate that the accused radios execute the required seven multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it references only three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence to show that any multiplication operation is repeated to fulfill all seven required by claim limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (shown below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not explain the relationship between those three unique multiplications and the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse seeks to elucidate this mapping; however, this evidence was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, since the document fails to disclose multiplication of x1 and x2—describing instead their complex conjugate and modulus—Finesse’s mapping is flawed, as it necessitates that, for example, S3 be assigned to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation evidences that the accused radios perform not only seven multiplications, but actually ten. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (citing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells at any point identify which subset of the ten multiplications referenced in Nokia’s document aligns with the seven multiplications set forth in the claims. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they offer a correspondence from the ten multiplications to the three discrete multiplications that Finesse had equated to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. In light of these facts, a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios performed the seven multiplications at issue in the claims. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s finding of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant a new trial according to the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, review of a denial of a new trial proceeds under an abuse-of-discretion standard. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants requested JMOL as to damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying motion for new trial on damages). Because we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for every asserted claim of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we hereby vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
