Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s rejection of their request for a new trial on damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which broadly cover techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. Typically, radios prevent interference by operating on separate frequencies; however, when their transmitted signals interact with physical obstacles—such as metal fences, unsecured cable connections, or corroded connectors—they can mix and create additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs result from non-active, physical obstructions, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging that AT&T infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through the use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the lawsuit. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all of the asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and it granted Finesse a lump-sum damages award of $166,303,391 for the remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants requested judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL as to damages, and a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal. Our court exercises jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of a district court's denial of JMOL follows the legal standards of the applicable regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are considered de novo, and reversal is warranted "only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit law). We evaluate a jury’s infringement determination for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). As articulated in Apple, "[a] factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." Id. (internal citations omitted). We also review claim construction de novo, and any factual determinations underpinning the district court’s construction for clear error. See Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM arises due to mechanical issues within the radio, while external PIM results from factors outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process aimed at mitigating IMP interference, wherein “signals of interest” are separated from “interference generating signals,” followed by the elimination of the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated IMP estimate. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a passband from incoming signals so as to generate a stream of bits, where the incoming signals comprise both the desired signals and those signals that act as sources of interference, with the latter being capable of producing intermodulation products within the same frequency band as the signals of interest; [b] extracting the desired signals from the bit stream through the use of one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting source signals responsible for producing one or more inband intermodulation products using one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimated value for each of the one or more intermodulation products from the respective source signals; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products by utilizing the estimated intermodulation products; and [f] carrying out phase and amplitude corrections on the estimated intermodulation product interference signals in a closed loop fashion, where such phase and amplitude corrections include making sub-sample phase shifts to adjust the phase of the estimated intermodulation product interference signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). Finesse, as the plaintiff, was responsible for demonstrating that the accused radios sample a passband of signals encompassing both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment. During trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, provided testimony that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document that illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, displayed below). Finesse Br. 15 (referencing annotations to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), illustrated within the blue box, functions as a receiver that samples both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each indicated by the dashed red path) to generate a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently stated that he mapped the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, both of which appear on the dashed red path as detailed in the red box beneath the legend. For example, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report similarly stated that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants argued that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because this path is generated after the RF ADC, and thus after the sampling event; it follows that signals cannot be sampled before they exist. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Therefore, the modeled PIM path cannot be considered the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Dr. Wells did not testify that x1 and x2 correspond to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; instead, he continued to claim that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned further as to whether he was stating that “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet, again, he did not identify x1 or x2 as the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as the claims require. The jury found that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants asserted that Dr. Wells had mapped “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, and that infringement could not be found because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband of received signals containing interference generating signals—those signals are only produced after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and acknowledged that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Accordingly, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the claimed “interference generating signals,” then

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver system where both the transmitter and receiver are located together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to counteract passive IMPs present in the receiver, performing this process on a continuous and near real-time basis, utilizing copies of signals from the transmitter; [b] where these passive IMPs are formed within passive transmitter components of the transmitter and receiver components of the receiver, and this occurs downstream from a high powered amplifier (HPA) and a transmitter filter within the transmitter; [c] where the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the transmitter’s antenna; [d] where the creation of ICSs relies on a power series model of the non-linear mechanism generating the IMPs, and involves creating an n-th order ICS by, for example, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally computing and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n being an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original).

During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable” but did not require them to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that nothing in the intrinsic record confined “S1, S2 and S3” to only unique input signals and further credited inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence to establish that “S1, S2 and S3” are generic representations for signals, which may either be identical or different. J.A. 109–14.

The principal claim limitation contested on appeal is that the claim requires seven distinct third-order multiplications involving the three signals. At trial, Dr. Wells opined that the accused radios practiced the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document outlining the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused radios perform multiplications of three separately identifiable signals and equated the three different multiplications described in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17).

The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court found Appellants had waived their argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals by not presenting it during the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that employing two signals, together with a duplicate of one of those signals, satisfied its construction requiring three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court determined that when two unique signals are used, only three distinct multiplications result, and that this sufficed to prove infringement since each of these three unique multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 38–39.

The appellants contend that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that these radios utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, appellants maintain that Finesse narrowed the claim scope by amending the claims during prosecution from “two or three signals” to “three signals” in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art, which discloses the generation of cancellation signals with two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). The appellants further assert that no reasonable jury could find the accused radios perform the seven multiplications claimed, as Dr. Wells only identified three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding the use of two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim containing the phrase “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse countered this rejection in part by asserting that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which the claimed method encompasses. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven distinct multiplications, such an amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of claim scope for products, including the accused radios, that utilize only two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The most the amendment requires is that there be three individually identifiable signals, without mandating any particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications set forth in the claims. At trial, Dr. Wells pointed to a Nokia document in an effort to establish that the accused radios engage in the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the document discusses modulus and complex conjugate operations and fails to demonstrate that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it references only three specific multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence indicating that any multiplications are repeated to fulfill the requirement of all seven multiplications as set forth in limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony explaining how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications required by the claims. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse tries to clarify this correspondence in its appeal briefing, such evidence was not presented to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, the referenced document does not describe the multiplication of x1 and x2, but rather their complex conjugate and modulus; thus, Finesse’s mapping is incorrect because, for instance, it would necessitate S3 to map onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios implement the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documents indicate the accused devices actually perform ten multiplications, not just seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referencing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells, however, identify which seven of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia documentation correspond to the claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they explain how the ten multiplications map onto the three distinct multiplications Finesse associates with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these circumstances, a reasonable jury could not have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because the infringement finding lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

We assess a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial according to the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit law, a denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). After the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages, or alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying a new trial on damages). Since we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
