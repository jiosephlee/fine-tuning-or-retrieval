MOORE, Chief Judge.

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the decision of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, which rejected their request for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement of the asserted claims in U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s refusal to grant a new trial concerning the assessment of damages. For the reasons discussed below, we overturn the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the award of damages.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) is the holder of the ’134 and ’775 patents, both of which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) 1 interference in radio systems. Typically, radios minimize interference by transmitting on distinct frequencies; however, when their output signals interact in the presence of obstructions such as metallic fencing, unsecured cable links, or corroded connectors, these signals can mix and produce additional frequencies referred to as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise due to passive objects, they are designated as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. See FINESSE WIRELESS LLC v. AT&T MOBILITY LLC 3; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought a lawsuit against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, asserting that AT&T employed the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia-manufactured radios. J.A. 267–87. Subsequently, Nokia entered the proceedings as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. A jury found each of the asserted claims both valid and infringed, and granted a lump-sum award of $166,303,391 as compensation for the entire duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Afterward, the appellants requested judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) for noninfringement and damages, as well as a new trial, but the district court denied all motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now challenge these decisions on appeal, and we exercise jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL in accordance with the law of the relevant regional circuit, which in this matter is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, a denial of JMOL is subject to de novo review; such denial is reversed only when, after interpreting the evidence most favorably for the verdict, the record compels the conclusion that no reasonable jury could decide otherwise. Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit authority). The determination of infringement by a jury is reviewed for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). A factual finding qualifies as being supported by substantial evidence if, considering the trial record, a reasonable jury could have sided with the prevailing party. Id. (citations omitted). Additionally, we undertake de novo review of claim construction, while any subsidiary factual determinations underpinning the district court’s interpretation are evaluated for clear error. See Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM arises from mechanical factors within the radio apparatus, whereas external PIM results from influences situated outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a technique aimed at mitigating IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” by applying a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as an illustrative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a predetermined frequency, a passband of incoming signals in order to produce a bit stream, where these incoming signals comprise both desired signals and interference-generating signals, and where the interference-generating signals are capable of producing intermodulation products within the band of the desired signals; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bit stream by means of one or more decimating filters; [c] identifying source signals responsible for generating one or more inband intermodulation products via one or more decimating filters; [d] estimating each of these intermodulation products based on the identified source signals; [e] mitigating one or more inband intermodulation products through cancellation utilizing the estimated intermodulation products; and [f] applying phase and amplitude corrections to the estimations of the interfering intermodulation product signals in a closed-loop configuration, where the process of adjusting phase and amplitude involves implementing sub-sample phase shifts to fine-tune the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

As the plaintiff, Finesse was responsible for demonstrating that the accused radios conduct sampling of a signal passband that contains both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios actually sample both types of signals. Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this position. During the trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios meet the limitations of the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, primarily referencing a technical document from Nokia that outlines the operational details of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, reproduced below). Finesse Br. 15 (with annotations to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells explained during his testimony that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, illustrated within the blue box) acts as a receiver that samples both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each indicated along the dashed red line) to produce a digital bit stream. See J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly stated that he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and attributed the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path—both indicated by the dashed red path and clarified in the red box accompanying the legend. For example, Dr. Wells remarked, “The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.” J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19, and stated, “the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest.” J.A. 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1. He confirmed, when asked, “So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct?”—answering, “Yes, I did.” J.A. 15009 at 141:10–14. Consistent with his in-court statements, Dr. Wells also opined in his expert report that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

However, during cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel emphasized that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path, as it is created after the RF ADC samples—meaning signals cannot be sampled prior to their generation. See J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Thus, the modeled PIM path cannot constitute the claimed “interference generating signals.” In light of this, Dr. Wells revised his testimony, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Notably, Dr. Wells did not specifically connect x1 and x2 to the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals”; instead, he maintained, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also FINESSE WIRELESS LLC v. AT&T MOBILITY LLC 7 J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). Upon being pressed about whether he was testifying that “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Again, he never explicitly mapped x1 or x2 to either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as the claims require.

The jury subsequently found all asserted claims of the ’134 patent to be infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells equated “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, and since the modeled PIM path is not present until after sampling, the receiver in the accused radios could not possibly sample the required interference signals. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged, “The ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals,’” and further noted that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver system where the transmitter and receiver are situated in close proximity, the described method includes: [a] creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that suppress passive IMPs in the receiver, on an ongoing and near real-time basis, by utilizing replicas of the transmitter’s signals; [b] noting that these passive IMPs arise in passive components of both the transmitter and receiver, following the high-powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter filter in the transmitter; [c] specifying that the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the transmitter's antenna; [d] stating that the process for generating ICSs relies upon a power series representation of the non-linear process responsible for producing IMPs, and encompasses synthesizing an n-th order ICS by, for three given signals S1, S2, and S3, carrying out digital multiplications and filtering on products including S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n as an integer. The district court, at the summary judgment phase, interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that nothing intrinsic to the patent confines “S1, S2 and S3” to being only unique input signals and, in addition, found persuasive the inventor’s testimony—considered extrinsic evidence—that “S1, S2 and S3” can generally refer to any signals and may in fact be identical or distinct. J.A. 109–14. The primary claim limitation under scrutiny on appeal involves the requirement that seven third-order products of the three signals must be computed. During trial, Dr. Wells stated that the allegedly infringing radios met the contested claims of the ’775 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document that demonstrates the operation of the radios at issue. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the radios perform multiplications of three separately identifiable signals, mapping the three distinct products detailed in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications described in the patent claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury subsequently found all asserted claims of the ’775 patent to be infringed. J.A. 169. In post-verdict JMOL proceedings, the district court rejected the argument from Appellants that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique on the grounds that it was not raised at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court held that employing two distinct signals plus a repeated version of one was sufficient to meet the construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, it found that, with two unique signals, only three different products are generated, and this satisfied the infringement requirement, since each of the three products corresponds to two or three of the seven multiplications set forth in the claims. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios generate cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, given that these radios employ only two signals, specifically x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. They further assert that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for systems utilizing two signals when, during prosecution, the claims were amended to require “three signals” rather than “two or three signals” in order to distinguish the invention from the Filipovic prior art, which discloses producing cancellation signals with two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claim language); J.A. 1006–10 (discussion of obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to Office Action). Additionally, Appellants maintain that the evidence does not support a jury finding that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications recited in the claims, arguing that Dr. Wells only demonstrated three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We reject the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage pertaining to two signals. The examiner initially deemed a claim involving "digitally multiplying two or three signals" as obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references (see J.A. 1006–07). However, Finesse was able to overcome this rejection in part by clarifying that "Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs" and does not address passive IMPs, which are included in the claimed process (J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941, Notice of Allowance). While Finesse subsequently modified the claims to include the step of digitally multiplying "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in seven separate multiplication operations, this does not constitute a "clear and unambiguous" relinquishment of claim scope covering devices such as the accused radios that operate with two unique input signals, consistent with Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The amendment, at best, necessitates the presence of three separately identifiable signals, without imposing a restriction on the precise number of unique input signals (J.A. 968–69). Nonetheless, we concur that the evidence does not permit a reasonable jury to conclude that the accused radios perform all seven of the specified multiplication operations. During trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document in support of the claim that the accused radios carry out the required multiplications (J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86, Nokia document). However, that document details operations involving modulus and complex conjugation and fails to demonstrate execution of all seven multiplication steps in the accused radios (J.A. 26485–86). It instead lists only three discrete multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no indication that any multiplications are duplicated or that all seven required by limitation 1[d] are carried out (J.A. 26486, reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells failed to provide testimony explaining the relationship between the three separate multiplications and the seven multiplications that are the subject of the claim. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse attempts to elucidate this connection on appeal, such clarification was not submitted into evidence before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, the pertinent document omits discussion of multiplying x1 and x2 directly, addressing only their complex conjugates and moduli, rendering Finesse’s proposed mapping inadequate—particularly since, for example, S3 must correspond simultaneously to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios satisfy the seven claimed multiplications because, according to Nokia’s documentation, the accused radios actually perform ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (showing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells specifies which of the ten multiplications documented by Nokia correspond to the seven multiplications required by the claim. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they delineate a mapping from the ten Nokia multiplications to the three discrete multiplications that Finesse associates with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these deficiencies, there is insufficient evidence for a reasonable jury to conclude that the accused radios implement the seven claimed multiplications. Consequently, we overturn the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, finding the jury's infringement determination to lack substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

Our review of the district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial follows regional circuit precedent. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit jurisprudence, such denials are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit standards). Subsequent to the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL as to damages or, in the alternative, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying motion for new trial on damages). Because we have reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is hereby vacated.

The parties also contest the correct interpretation of “co-located.” Appellants Br. 59–61; Finesse Br. 51–55. However, since our reversal of JMOL of noninfringement resolves the appeal, we need not address this claim construction issue.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED
