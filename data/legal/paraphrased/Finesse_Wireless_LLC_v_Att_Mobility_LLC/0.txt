Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court’s rejection of their motion for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the decision denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. While radios typically prevent signal interference by transmitting on separate frequencies, their signals can mix together upon encountering obstacles—such as metal fences, loose or rusted cable connections—resulting in new frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs originate from non-active obstacles, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated a lawsuit against AT&T, claiming infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent due to AT&T’s utilization of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability within Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the case. J.A. 389–90. The jury concluded that each asserted claim was both valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum damages amount of $166,303,391 to cover the remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, for JMOL regarding damages, and for a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now seek review on appeal. Our court has jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL based on the law of the pertinent regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). According to the Fifth Circuit, review of JMOL denials is conducted de novo and will only result in reversal “if, when considering the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence so strongly and overwhelmingly favors one party that the court concludes that reasonable jurors could not reach any different result.” Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit precedent). We examine jury determinations of infringement to determine whether they are supported by substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A finding of fact is supported by substantial evidence when a reasonable jury, in view of the trial record, could have found for the prevailing party.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We review claim construction without deference and review any factual determinations underpinning the district court’s construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a technique for mitigating IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” based on a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a chosen frequency, a range of received signals to generate a sequence of bits, where these received signals comprise both signals of interest and signals that cause interference, with the interference signals being capable of producing intermodulation products within the frequency band of the signals of interest; [b] using one or more decimating filters to separate the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] employing one or more decimating filters to separate the source signals responsible for generating one or more intermodulation products within the band of the signals of interest; [d] calculating an estimated value for each of the one or more intermodulation products based on the source signals that give rise to those products; [e] eliminating one or more intermodulation products present within the band by utilizing the estimated intermodulation products; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude modifications on the estimates of the interfering intermodulation product signals in a closed loop fashion, where this process of adjusting phase and amplitude includes carrying out sub-sample phase adjustments to modify the phase of the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases added). As the party bringing suit, Finesse had the responsibility to demonstrate that the accused radios sample a passband of signals comprising both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants maintain that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. At the trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document that illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, reproduced below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown within the blue box) functions as a receiver that samples the specified “signals of interest” as well as “interference generating signals” (each represented along the dashed red path), in order to generate a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently equated the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path (both indicated by the dashed red path as detailed in the red box within the legend). See, for instance, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Similarly, in his expert report, Dr. Wells concluded that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants highlighted that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path, since the modeled PIM path arises downstream of the RF ADC after the sampling event, and thus, signals cannot be sampled prior to their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This indicates that the modeled PIM path cannot constitute the claimed “interference generating signals.” When confronted, Dr. Wells changed his position, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not assert that x1 and x2 correspond to the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals”; rather, he reiterated, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). Upon being asked for clarification as to whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Once again, he did not match x1 or x2 to either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as the claims require.

The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. During JMOL, Appellants argued that Dr. Wells had mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path and that infringement was not possible because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband of received signals that includes interference generating signals, as these are only created after the sampling process. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and further noted “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (referencing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Thus, if the modeled PIM path corresponds to the

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system comprising a receiver and a transmitter, both situated at the same location, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) in the receiver for the purpose of mitigating passive IMPs in a continuous, near real-time manner, by utilizing duplicates of the transmitter’s own signals; [b] specifying that such passive IMPs are formed within passive transmitter components as well as within receiver components, subsequent to the high-powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter filter in the transmitter chain; [c] clarifying that the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the antenna used by the transmitter; [d] indicating that the process of generating ICSs relies on a power series model characterizing the non-linear generation of IMPs, and involves the creation of an n-th order ICS through digital multiplication and filtering of combinations such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n representing an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original).

During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as needing to be “separately identifiable,” though not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record does not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to being unique input signals, and further considered inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence, which explained that “S1, S2 and S3” are generic signal representations and may refer to the same signal or to different signals. J.A. 109–14.

The central claim limitation on appeal concerns the requirement that seven third-order multiplications among the three signals must be performed to satisfy the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios practice the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document describing the operation of the accused products. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically stated that the accused devices multiply three separately identifiable signals and equated the three distinct multiplications identified in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17).

The jury found that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. When considering judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the district court declined to entertain Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals, deeming the argument forfeited since it was not raised at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court held that employing two signals together with a duplicate of one signal sufficed under its construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court found that, when using two unique signals, only three distinct multiplications result, and that this was sufficient to establish infringement because each of these three unique multiplications aligns with two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, as these radios utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. Appellants further assert that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for two signals during prosecution by amending the claims from "two or three signals" to "three signals" in order to distinguish the invention from the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses cancellation signals generated from two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications because Dr. Wells only identified three such multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding two signals. The examiner had initially rejected a claim that included the step of “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious over Filipovic along with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. However, Finesse responded by stating that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which are also covered by the claimed method. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Finesse did subsequently amend the claims to specifically require digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, but this amendment does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim coverage for products—such as the accused radios—that utilize two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, the amendment imposes a requirement for three individually identifiable signals, but does not mandate a particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications claimed. At trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document in support of the assertion that the accused radios perform the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document describes modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not demonstrate that the accused radios carry out all seven of the claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it identifies only three specific multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence indicating that any of these are performed multiple times so as to satisfy the requirement for seven distinct multiplications as specified by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three separate multiplications align with the seven multiplications specified in the asserted claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse tries to provide clarification regarding this mapping on appeal, this information was not presented before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, since the referenced document does not reveal multiplication of x1 and x2, but rather their complex conjugate and modulus, Finesse’s proposed mapping is incorrect as it necessitates, for instance, mapping S3 to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios satisfy the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation demonstrates the accused radios perform not only those seven but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identify which seven of the ten multiplications described in the Nokia documentation correspond to the claimed seven. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they establish a mapping from the ten described multiplications to the three distinct multiplications Finesse attempted to match to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these circumstances, a reasonable jury could not conclude that the accused radios practice the seven multiplications required by the asserted claims. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s finding of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

We review the district court’s denial of a motion for new trial in accordance with the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit evaluates such denials for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL regarding damages, or alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Since we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we also vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
