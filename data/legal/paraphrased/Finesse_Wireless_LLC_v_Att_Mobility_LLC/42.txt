Caption: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Case No.: 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s refusal to grant a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radio communications. Radios typically prevent interference by utilizing separate frequencies; however, when their transmitted signals encounter certain obstacles—such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or corroded connectors—the signals can interact to generate new frequencies referred to as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs result from passive elements, they are known as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, accusing them of infringing claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent as well as claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through the employment of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia joined the case as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, awarding $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages to cover the entire remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Following the verdict, Appellants requested judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and a new trial; the district court rejected all such motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants have now brought this appeal. Our jurisdiction arises pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL pursuant to the law of the relevant regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The standard of review in the Fifth Circuit for such denials is de novo, and a reversal is appropriate “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (applying Fifth Circuit precedent). Jury determinations of infringement are reviewed for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). Substantial evidence exists if “a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (citations omitted). Claim construction is evaluated de novo, while factual findings underpinning the district court’s claim construction are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM stems from mechanical issues within the radio, whereas external PIM originates from outside sources. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process aimed at minimizing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently negating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling, at a selected frequency, a range of received signals to produce a sequence of bits, where the received signals comprise both signals of interest and signals responsible for creating interference, with these interference generating signals being able to cause intermodulation products within the band of the signals of interest; [b] separating out the signals of interest within the bit sequence via one or more decimating filters; [c] separating out the source signals that result in one or more inband intermodulation products of the signals of interest using one or more decimating filters; [d] generating an estimate for each intermodulation product from the identified source signals that create the intermodulation products; [e] removing one or more inband intermodulation products based on the intermodulation product estimates; and [f] carrying out adjustments to phase and amplitude for the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals in a closed loop process, where such adjustments include making phase changes to the estimations at sub-sample increments in order to alter the phase of the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases original). 

Finesse, as the plaintiff, had the responsibility to demonstrate that the radios accused of infringement perform sampling of a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). On appeal, Appellants assert that Finesse did not sufficiently establish that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this position. During the trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, stated that the accused radios violate the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document that illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, provided below). Finesse Br. 15 (marking J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells explained in his testimony that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), depicted within the blue box, serves as a receiver that samples the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” shown in the dashed red path, in order to generate a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. He consistently reiterated that the “signals of interest” align with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and the “interference generating signals” correspond to the modeled PIM path; both of these are represented along the dashed red path, as identified in the explanatory red box in the legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’s expert report also offered the view that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants emphasized that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because the modeled PIM path arises only downstream of the RF ADC, after the sampling takes place, so the signals cannot be sampled before they exist. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; also see J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Therefore, the modeled PIM path cannot satisfy the requirement for “interference generating signals” in the claims. Dr. Wells responded to this by stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Notably, Dr. Wells did not equate x1 or x2 to either the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” and instead continued his assertion that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When pressed to clarify whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. He again failed to associate x1 or x2 specifically with either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as required by the asserted claims.

The jury ultimately found infringement of all asserted claims of the ’134 patent. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants argued that Dr. Wells’s mapping of “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path was faulty because, in the accused radios, the receiver’s sampled passband does not contain interference generating signals—those are only created after sampling occurs. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and also noted Dr. Wells’s testimony that the PIM Model is produced in the GROOT FPGA. J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Thus, if the modeled PIM path corresponds to the “interference generating signals,” then the sampling limitation of

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver where both a transmitter and the receiver are located together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that neutralize passive IMPs within the receiver in a continuous and nearly real-time manner, making use of transmitter signal copies from the transmitter, [b] with the passive IMPs arising in the transmitter's passive components and in the receiver's components after the transmitter's high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter, [c] the transmitter filter being situated between the HPA and the antenna employed by the transmitter, [d] with the ICSs being generated based on a power series model of the non-linear mechanism responsible for the IMPs, including the production of an n-th order ICS by digitally multiplying and filtering, for three signals S1, S2, and S3, the products S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n represents an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). On summary judgment, the district court interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” rather than requiring them to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record did not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” solely to unique inputs, and relied additionally on the inventor’s testimony—considered as extrinsic evidence—that “S1, S2 and S3” refers generally to signals and that they can be either the same or different. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation under dispute on appeal is whether there must be seven third order multiplications involving the three signals to satisfy the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells opined that the accused radios infringe the asserted ’775 patent claims by relying on a technical document from Nokia that describes the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals and identified three distinct multiplications from the Nokia document as equivalent to the seven required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that every asserted claim of the ’775 patent was infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, the district court ruled that Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals was waived, since they had not raised this argument under Rule 50(a). J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that the use of two signals and a repeat of one of those signals was enough to meet the requirement for three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Further, the court determined that utilizing two unique signals results in only three distinct multiplications, but this suffices to show infringement because each such distinct multiplication aligns with two or three out of the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios create cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting instead that these radios employ only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. They specifically argue that Finesse narrowed the claim scope from covering both "two or three signals" to only "three signals" during prosecution, in response to the Filipovic prior art reference—which disclosed generating cancellation signals using two inputs—in order to overcome an obviousness rejection. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications, as Dr. Wells only identified three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope concerning two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim reciting “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in view of Filipovic along with additional references, J.A. 1006–07. However, Finesse overcame this rejection in part by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the claimed invention also addresses passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Finesse did subsequently amend the claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, but this amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope with respect to products—such as the accused radios—that utilize two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At best, the amendment imposes a requirement for three individually distinguishable signals, without mandating any particular quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios execute the seven specified multiplications in the claims. At trial, Dr. Wells relied upon a Nokia document to argue that the accused radios perform these claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, this document references modulus and complex conjugate operations and fails to establish that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it merely lists three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and no evidence indicates that any multiplications are duplicated to achieve all seven claimed multiplications, as limitation 1[d] requires. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not provide an explanation regarding how the three distinct multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse seeks to clarify this correspondence on appeal, such evidence was never brought before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the relevant document fails to disclose multiplication of x1 and x2, instead referencing their complex conjugate and modulus, the mapping set forth by Finesse is defective as it necessitates, for example, associating S3 with the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications, purportedly because Nokia's documentation demonstrates that the accused radios, in fact, perform a total of ten multiplications, not merely seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referencing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (discussing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identifies which group of the ten Nokia-documented multiplications aligns with the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Likewise, there is no explanation mapping the ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse linked to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Under these conditions, a reasonable jury could not find that the accused radios practice the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court's denial of JMOL of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury's infringement determination lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

The denial of a motion for a new trial by the district court is reviewed in accordance with regional circuit precedent. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard to such decisions. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit precedent). Following the jury's verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court rejected both requests. J.A. 42–57 (JMOL on damages denied); J.A. 58–65 (new trial on damages denied). Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement on all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we set aside the denial of JMOL on noninfringement and nullify the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
