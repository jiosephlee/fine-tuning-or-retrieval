Case Name: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date of Decision: September 24, 2025  
Docket Number: 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, the Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court’s denial of their request for a new trial on damages. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the award of damages.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Radios typically prevent signal interference by utilizing separate frequencies, yet transmission signals can interact when they encounter physical barriers (such as metal fences, degraded cable connections, or corroded connectors), generating novel frequencies referred to as "intermodulation products" (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs are produced by non-active barriers, they are known as "passive intermodulation products" (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the action. J.A. 389–90. A jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum of $166,303,391 for the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants filed post-trial motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, for JMOL on damages, and for a new trial; the district court denied all motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now seek appellate review. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We evaluate a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL according to the legal standards of the regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo, and reversal is appropriate “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit law). We determine whether the jury’s infringement determination is supported by substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). The standard is satisfied “[a] factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Our review of claim construction is de novo, while any factual findings underlying the district court’s claim construction are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM is attributable to mechanical issues within the radio, whereas external PIM arises from external sources outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process designed to mitigate IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by the cancellation of the “interference generating signals” based on a calculated estimation of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] Sampling, at a chosen frequency, the passband of incoming signals at a rate above the Nyquist rate to produce a bit stream, where these incoming signals encompass both desired signals and interference-producing signals, with the interference-producing signals being capable of creating intermodulation products that fall within the band of the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bit stream by employing one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting the source signals responsible for generating one or more inband intermodulation products within the signal of interest by using one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimated value for each intermodulation product, based on the extracted source signals; [e] using the estimates of the intermodulation products to eliminate one or more inband intermodulation products; and [f] adjusting the phase and amplitude of estimations of the interfering intermodulation signals in a closed loop, wherein the adjustment of phase and amplitude for these estimations includes carrying out phase shifts smaller than a single sample to refine phase on the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As plaintiff, Finesse had the obligation to demonstrate that the accused radios sample a passband of signals that comprises both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios perform sampling on both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s expert witness on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing his testimony on a technical document from Nokia which illustrates the functioning of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, as shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (highlighting J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), indicated within a blue box, operates as a receiver that samples both the asserted “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” (each shown by a dashed red path) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently indicated he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, both shown along the dashed red path as explained in the red box under the legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Likewise, Dr. Wells’s expert report stated, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. On cross-examination, counsel for Appellants argued the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because this path is formed downstream of the RF ADC, that is, after sampling, so signals cannot be sampled prior to their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. The implication is that the modeled PIM path cannot be the “interference generating signals” as claimed. In reply, Dr. Wells adjusted his testimony, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Nevertheless, Dr. Wells did not state that x1 and x2 correspond to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; rather, he continued, asserting, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When pressed to clarify whether he was asserting “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, Dr. Wells did not attribute x1 or x2 specifically to either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as required by the claims. The jury ultimately found all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants contended Dr. Wells identified the “interference generating signals” as the modeled PIM path, and so there could not be infringement since the receiver in the accused radios does not sample a set of received signals that includes the interference generating signals, given those signals only come into existence after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court observed that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and further, “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). 1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, alongside a co-located transmitter, the method encompasses: [a] the creation of intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that mitigate passive IMPs in the receiver, performed on a continual, near-real-time basis by utilizing copies of the transmitter’s signals; [b] the passive IMPs arise from passive transmitter elements within the transmitter and from receiver components within the receiver, specifically after the transmitter’s high power amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter; [c] with the transmitter filter arranged between the HPA and the antenna serving the transmitter; [d] ICS generation proceeds from a power series representation describing the non-linear process that yields the IMPs, and the process includes producing an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, performing digital multiplications and filtering on combinations S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n stands for an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original).

On summary judgment, the district court interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as meaning that the signals are “separately identifiable” but need not be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court held that the intrinsic evidence did not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, and it gave weight to inventor testimony (as extrinsic evidence) supporting that “S1, S2 and S3” may be any signals, whether identical or distinct. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation on appeal concerns whether all seven third-order multiplications using the three signals must occur to satisfy the claim.

During trial, Dr. Wells opined that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused devices. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells stated the accused radios perform multiplication involving three separately identifiable signals and correlated the three separate multiplications described in the Nokia document with the seven multiplications identified in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found infringement as to all asserted claims of the ’775 patent. J.A. 169.

When deciding JMOL, the district court determined that Appellants’ argument requiring “S1, S2 and S3” to be unique signals was forfeited because Appellants failed to make this point at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that using two signals and an additional copy of one of those signals sufficed for the construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court observed that when only two unique signals are used, there are three distinct multiplications performed, and this was adequate for infringement because each of these three unique multiplications corresponded to two or three of the seven claimed combinations. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the radios utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for two signals by amending its claims during prosecution to specify “three signals” instead of the prior language, “two or three signals,” in order to distinguish from the Filipovic prior art, which teaches cancellation signal generation using two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Further, Appellants assert that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications recited in the claims, as Dr. Wells only identified three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim coverage relating to the use of two signals. The examiner had rejected a claim that included “digitally multiplying two or three signals” on the grounds of obviousness in light of Filipovic along with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse overcame this rejection partly by arguing that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address the passive IMPs that the claimed process also covers. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to require digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” via seven multiplications, such an amendment does not constitute the “clear and unambiguous” surrender of claim scope that would exclude devices, like the accused radios, which involve two distinct input signals. See Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The most that can be inferred from Finesse’s amendment is a requirement for three individually distinguishable signals, without specifying any particular count of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications as claimed. Dr. Wells, during the trial, relied upon a Nokia document to demonstrate that the accused radios performed the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document describes operations involving modulus and complex conjugate, but does not establish that the accused radios perform the seven specific multiplications claimed. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it enumerates only three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no evidence that any multiplications are duplicated so as to achieve all seven of the claimed operations required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not describe how the three different multiplications relate to the seven multiplications asserted in the claim. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse attempts to clarify this relationship, but the jury was not presented with such evidence. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, as the document fails to disclose multiplication of x1 and x2, instead referencing their complex conjugates and moduli, Finesse’s proposed mapping is incorrect because it entails, for example, mapping S3 to signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse stated that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications described in the claims, asserting that Nokia’s documentation indicates that the accused radios in fact perform ten multiplications, not just seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (discussing p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do Finesse or Dr. Wells specify which seven of the ten multiplications identified in the Nokia documentation correspond to the multiplications required by the claim. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they delineate a mapping between the ten described multiplications and the three distinct multiplications Finesse had previously mapped to the seven claim multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. In light of these facts, no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused products execute the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court's denial of judgment as a matter of law of noninfringement on the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, since substantial evidence does not support the jury’s infringement finding.

II. Damages

We evaluate a district court’s refusal to grant a new trial following the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denial of a new trial is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury returned its verdict, Appellants filed for JMOL on damages or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the district court's denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the denial of judgment as a matter of law regarding noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
