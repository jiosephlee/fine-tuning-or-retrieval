Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s rejection of their request for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the decision denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which broadly concern techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. To prevent interference, radios typically use separate frequencies; however, their transmitted signals may mix after encountering physical obstructions—such as metallic fences, loose cables, or corroded connectors—resulting in the generation of additional frequencies, termed “intermodulation products” (IMPs). See J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs are generated by non-active obstacles, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse filed suit against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent by AT&T’s utilization of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature incorporated in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the action. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that every asserted claim was both valid and infringed, awarding lump-sum damages in the amount of $166,303,391 to cover the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Following this verdict, Appellants filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and requested a new trial; all such motions were denied by the district court. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal. Jurisdiction is proper pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the appropriate regional circuit—in this instance, the Fifth Circuit—when reviewing a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). According to the Fifth Circuit, denials of JMOL are subject to de novo review, and reversal is warranted “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). The determination by a jury regarding infringement is assessed under the substantial evidence standard. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). As set forth in Apple, “[a] factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Internal PIM arises from mechanical faults within the radio, whereas external PIM results from factors located outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24. Issues of claim construction are reviewed de novo, with any factual findings underpinning the district court’s construction reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a method aimed at mitigating IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by neutralizing the “interference generating signals” by employing a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a frequency band of incoming signals to generate a digital bit stream, with these incoming signals containing both desired signals and signals causing interference, where such interference-causing signals can produce intermodulation products that fall within the frequency band of the desired signals; [b] separating the desired signals within the bit stream through the use of one or more decimating filters; [c] separating the original signals responsible for creating one or more inband intermodulation products that overlap with the signals of interest, also by using one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimated value for each of the inband intermodulation products based on the identified source signals responsible for them; [e] removing one or more of the inband intermodulation products by applying the computed estimates for those products; and [f] adjusting the phase and amplitude of the estimations for the interfering intermodulation products using a closed loop technique, where such adjustment includes applying sub-sample phase shifts to fine-tune the phase of the interference estimations. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

Finesse, as the plaintiff, had the obligation to demonstrate that the radios accused of infringement actually sample a frequency band that contains both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both categories of signals—the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s expert witness on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, claimed the accused radios meet the requirements of the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing his testimony on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, which appears below). Finesse Br. 15 (with annotations to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown within a blue box) serves as a receiver that samples what the patent terms “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (both illustrated along a dashed red path), thereby producing a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly maintained that the “signals of interest” correspond to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” correspond to the modeled PIM path—both represented on the same dashed red path, as outlined in the legend’s red box. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be ... the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In his expert report, Dr. Wells likewise opined that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal ... meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. On cross-examination, counsel for Appellants highlighted that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path since it is produced only after the sampling process, downstream of the RF ADC, such that a signal cannot be sampled before it exists. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. As a result, the modeled PIM path could not represent the “interference generating signals” recited in the claims. In reply, Dr. Wells shifted his explanation, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not testify that x1 and x2 represented the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals.” Instead, he persisted in claiming “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. ... the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked for further clarification if he was “testifying x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he stated, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, at no point did Dr. Wells explicitly correlate either x1 or x2 with the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as required by the claims.

The jury determined all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. On a motion for JMOL, Appellants argued Dr. Wells had mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, making infringement impossible because the accused radio’s receiver does not sample a passband containing interference generating signals, since those signals arise only after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged that “the ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals,’” and further, “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path is the claimed “interference generating signals,” element

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver that is co-located with a transmitter, the method involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to counteract passive IMPs in the receiver, continuously and nearly in real time, through the use of copies of signals from the transmitter; [b] the passive IMPs originate in passive components of the transmitter and components of the receiver after both the high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter of the transmitter; [c] the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the antenna utilized by the transmitter; [d] generation of the ICSs is based on representing a non-linear process that forms IMPs by a power series, which includes forming an n-th order ICS by, for three signals S1, S2, and S3, performing digital multiplication and filtering of S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n being an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). At summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as meaning “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that intrinsic evidence did not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, further relying on inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence, that “S1, S2 and S3” generally indicate signals that can either be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. The main disputed claim limitation on appeal concerns whether seven third order multiplications involving the three signals are required to satisfy the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells provided testimony that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically asserted that the accused radios perform multiplication of three separately identifiable signals, drawing an equivalence between the three separate multiplications listed in the Nokia document and the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. Upon JMOL, the district court found Appellants’ argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals to be waived, as they did not present it at the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court held that utilizing two signals and an additional copy of one met the requirement of having three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court determined that when two unique signals are used, only three distinct multiplications are produced, but this suffices to prove infringement since each of those three distinct multiplications is matched to two or three of the seven required multiplications under the claims. J.A. 38–39.

The appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios create cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the devices in question only utilize two signals—x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. More particularly, they assert that Finesse narrowed the claim's coverage during prosecution by amending the language from “two or three signals” to “three signals” in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art, which discloses generating cancellation signals with only two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Furthermore, appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could have determined the accused products perform the seven claimed multiplications, noting that Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope as to two signals. The examiner had deemed a claim mentioning “digitally multiplying two or three signals” to be obvious over Filipovic along with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, overcame this rejection in part by pointing out that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” not the passive IMPs that the claimed method addresses as well. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to require digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” via seven multiplications, such amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim coverage as to devices, such as the accused radios, that involve two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The amendment, at most, demands three separately identifiable signals but does not specify any minimum or maximum number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios perform the seven specified multiplications. At trial, Dr. Wells referred to a Nokia document in an effort to establish that the accused radios execute the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). This document, however, describes operations involving modulus and complex conjugate calculations and does not indicate that the accused radios perform all seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it details only three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence presented that any of these multiplications are duplicated so as to satisfy the requirement of seven total multiplications specified by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells failed to explain how the three separate multiplications are correlated with the seven multiplications required by the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse seeks to clarify this mapping, yet such evidence was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, the document in question does not describe multiplication of x1 and x2, but instead discusses their complex conjugates and moduli. This renders Finesse’s mapping deficient since, for instance, it necessitates S3 being mapped to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', as well as its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios undertake the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation demonstrates that the accused radios actually perform ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). Finesse and Dr. Wells never specify which group of the ten multiplications identified in the Nokia document matches the seven multiplications in the claims. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they delineate a mapping from those ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse earlier associated with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Under these facts, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. We thus reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, because the infringement verdict lacks substantial supporting evidence. II. Damages We review the district court’s denial of a motion for new trial in accordance with the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, an order denying a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (applying Fifth Circuit precedent). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (JMOL on damages denied); J.A. 58–65 (motion for new trial on damages denied). Given our reversal of the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the damages judgment.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
