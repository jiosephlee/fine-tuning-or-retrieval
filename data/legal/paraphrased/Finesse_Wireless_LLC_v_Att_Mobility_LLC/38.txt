Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, the Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court's decision to deny their request for a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons detailed below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which broadly cover techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. While radios prevent interference by using distinct frequencies, their transmission signals can interact in the presence of certain impediments (such as metallic fences, unsecured cable connections, or corroded connectors), resulting in the creation of new frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs originate from passive sources, they are termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through the use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature incorporated in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the litigation. J.A. 389–90. The jury concluded that all asserted claims were valid and infringed, awarding $166,303,391 as a lump-sum payment for the remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and a new trial, but the district court denied these requests. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now appeal, and we possess jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the relevant regional circuit, in this instance the Fifth Circuit, when reviewing a district court’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are examined de novo and will be overturned only “if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit law). Our review of a jury’s infringement determination is for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). A jury’s factual determination meets the substantial evidence standard if “a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Internal Passive Intermodulation (PIM) arises from mechanical problems within the radio itself, whereas external PIM is attributable to sources located outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24. We consider claim construction issues de novo and review any factual underpinnings of the district court’s interpretation for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at lessening IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” utilizing an estimated calculation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a specified frequency, a frequency band of received signals that forms a bit stream, wherein these received signals comprise both signals of interest and signals generating interference, with the interference generating signals being able to cause intermodulation products that fall within the band of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bit stream using one or more filters that decimate; [c] extracting originating signals that produce one or more intermodulation products within the band of interest, also using one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimate of each such intermodulation product based on the identified source signals; [e] using this estimate to cancel one or more intermodulation products that are present within the band; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude corrections on the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals in a feedback loop, which entails performing sub-sample phase shifts to adjust the phase of these interference estimates. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As the party bringing suit, Finesse bore the responsibility of establishing that the accused radios in fact sample a passband of signals containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants assert that Finesse did not demonstrate that the accused radios actually sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s expert witness on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, gave testimony that the accused radios infringed the claims in the ’134 patent, relying on a technical document from Nokia that outlines the manner in which the accused radios function. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (with annotation to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells explained that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), shown in the blue box, functions as a receiver that samples both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” (each indicated by a dashed red path), producing a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, both represented by the dashed red path as further described in the red box within the legend. For example, he testified, “The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.” J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19; and stated, “the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest.” J.A. 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1. When asked, “So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct?” Dr. Wells answered, “Yes, I did.” J.A. 15009 at 141:10–14. Dr. Wells’ expert report likewise asserted that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

However, during cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel pointed out that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path, because this path is produced after the RF ADC performs sampling, making it impossible to sample a signal prior to its generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Thus, under this framework, the modeled PIM path cannot be the “interference generating signals” described in the claim. In response, Dr. Wells adjusted his position, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Yet, he did not state that x1 or x2 correspond to “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals”; rather, he persisted in characterizing the “two signals” as the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When Dr. Wells was specifically asked whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Nonetheless, at no point did he specify x1 or x2 as the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as required by the claims.

The jury found all asserted claims of the ’134 patent to be infringed. J.A. 169. Upon JMOL, Appellants maintained that Dr. Wells mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path and, accordingly, no infringement could be found because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample any passband of received signals including interference generating signals—since such signals are not present until after the sampling occurs. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “the ‘modeled PIM path’ is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and acknowledged Dr. Wells’ testimony that “the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore,

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver, wherein the transmitter and receiver are positioned together, the method includes: [a] creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) for the purpose of canceling passive IMPs within the receiver, on a continuous and near real-time basis, by employing copies of the transmitter’s own signals, [b] where such passive IMPs are produced in the transmitter's passive components and in the receiver’s components after both the high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter within the transmitter, [c] with the transmitter filter installed between the HPA and the antenna utilized by the transmitter, [d] and where ICS generation is predicated upon a power series model of a non-linear mechanism responsible for producing the IMPs, which comprises generating an nth order ICS by, given signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, wherein n denotes an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original).

At the summary judgment phase, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean signals that are “separately identifiable,” rather than “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined from the intrinsic record that “S1, S2 and S3” is not restricted to unique input signals, and also relied on inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” are general representations which may refer to the same or different signals. J.A. 109–14.

The principal claim limitation contested on appeal concerns the requirement that seven third order multiplications involving the three signals must occur to satisfy the limitation. At trial, Dr. Wells stated that the accused radios practiced the asserted claims of the ’775 patent based on a Nokia technical document illustrating their operation. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically indicated the accused radios perform multiplications involving three separately identifiable signals and equated the three distinct multiplications presented in the Nokia document with the seven multiplications stated in the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17).

The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In considering JMOL, the district court found that the Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals was waived, since it had not been presented at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court determined that utilizing two signals, along with a duplicate of one of them, was sufficient to meet its construction of “three separately identifiable signals.” J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court found that, when only two unique signals are used, only three distinct multiplications result, and that was enough to establish infringement, since each such multiplication correlated to two or three of the seven required multiplications as claimed. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that these radios in fact utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, Appellants maintain that during prosecution, Finesse relinquished any claim to coverage of systems with two signals by amending the claims to specify “three signals” rather than “two or three signals,” aiming to distinguish the claims over the Filipovic prior art reference, which describes generating cancellation signals with two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Furthermore, Appellants argue that the jury could not reasonably have determined that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications required by the claims, given that Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim coverage pertaining to two signals. The examiner had rejected a claim containing the language “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious based on Filipovic and secondary references, see J.A. 1006–07; however, Finesse responded to the rejection by asserting that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” and does not address passive IMPs, which are also within the scope of the claimed method, see J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to be digitally multiplied in seven multiplication steps, this amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope concerning products—such as the accused radios—with only two unique input signals. See Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At the very most, the amendment mandates the presence of three separate identifiable signals, but does not specify a minimum count of distinct input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplication operations. During trial, Dr. Wells referred to a Nokia document as evidence that the accused radios carry out the claimed multiplications, see J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document refers to modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not support a finding that the accused radios engage in the seven specific multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, the document identifies only three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence suggesting that any multiplications are duplicated so as to account for all seven multiplications as required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (shown below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony detailing how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications asserted in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to clarify this mapping during appeal; however, this information was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, because the referenced document describes multiplication of the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2 rather than multiplying x1 and x2 themselves, Finesse’s mapping is deficient. For instance, the mapping would necessitate associating S3 with x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. At oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios perform the seven asserted multiplications because Nokia’s documentation indicates that the radios, in fact, carry out not only seven but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (describing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identifies which seven of the ten multiplications detailed in the Nokia document equate to the asserted seven multiplications in the claims. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). They also fail to relate the ten described multiplications to the three distinct multiplications Finesse suggested correspond with the claimed seven multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. In light of these facts, a reasonable jury could not conclude that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the infringement verdict rendered by the jury lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

Our review of a district court’s refusal to grant a new trial is governed by the law of the applicable regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, the standard of review for denial of a new trial is abuse of discretion. Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit precedent). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on the issue of damages, or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court rejected both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Because we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
