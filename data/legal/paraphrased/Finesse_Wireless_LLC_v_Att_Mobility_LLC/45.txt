Case Name: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Decision Date: September 24, 2025  
Case Number: No. 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court’s denial of their motion for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Typically, radios minimize interference by operating on separate frequencies; however, when their transmitted signals are reflected or encounter obstructions such as metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors, they may generate additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). See J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs result from non-electronic, passive obstructions, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). See J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T, accusing it of infringing claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, specifically through the use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability included in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the litigation. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted patent claims were both valid and infringed, and it granted $166,303,391 as a lump-sum award covering the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants subsequently requested judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, on damages, as well as a new trial, but the district court denied all motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal. This court’s jurisdiction is based on 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We evaluate a district court's refusal to grant JMOL according to the law of the applicable regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit applies a de novo standard of review to denials of JMOL, overturning such decisions “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Our standard for reviewing a jury’s determination on infringement is whether there is substantial evidence to support the finding. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We examine claim construction matters anew, or de novo, and review any subsidiary factual determinations underlying the district court's construction for clear error. See Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at lessening IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through an estimated calculation of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] Oversampling a received signal passband at a specified frequency to generate a bit stream, wherein the received signals comprise both the signals of interest and signals responsible for causing interference, the latter capable of producing intermodulation products within the band of the signals of interest; [b] utilizing one or more decimating filters to separate out the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] utilizing one or more decimating filters to extract the source signals responsible for producing one or more intermodulation products that fall within the signal of interest's band; [d] determining an estimate of each of those intermodulation products based on the source signals that produce them; [e] using the estimates of the intermodulation products to eliminate one or more inband intermodulation products; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude corrections on estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals in a closed-loop fashion, where such corrections include making sub-sample phase shifts to adjust the phase of the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

Finesse, as plaintiff, had the responsibility to demonstrate that the accused radios sample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The Appellants contend that Finesse did not prove the accused radios actually sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this view. During trial, Finesse presented Dr. Jonathan Wells, their expert in infringement, who stated that the accused radios practiced the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing his conclusion on a Nokia technical document that illustrated how the accused radios function. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (highlighting J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) functions as a receiver that samples both the alleged “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (depicted along the dashed red path), producing a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently equated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and linked the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, both presented in the dashed red path and explained in the red box under the legend. For example, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be ... the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report similarly stated, “The ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal ... meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and the “‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. On cross-examination, counsel for Appellants highlighted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because this path is created after the RF ADC samples, making it impossible to sample a signal prior to its generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Accordingly, the modeled PIM path cannot represent the “interference generating signals” claimed. In reply, Dr. Wells shifted his position, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Nevertheless, Dr. Wells did not assert that x1 and x2 correspond to the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals”; rather, he reiterated, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned directly if “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, he did not align x1 or x2 with the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as the claims require. The jury nevertheless found that every asserted claim of the ’134 patent was infringed. J.A. 169. Upon JMOL, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells associated “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, and there could be no infringement because the accused radios’ receiver is not sampling a received signal passband that contains interference generating signals, as these only arise after sampling is complete. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “the ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and further acknowledged Dr. Wells’ testimony that the PIM Model is produced in the GROOT FPGA. J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Thus, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the “interference generating signals” required by the claim, then limitation 1[a] is not satisfied since the accused receivers are unable to sample a passband including the interference signals, which are

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system comprising both a transmitter and a receiver situated together, the method includes: [a] generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) intended to suppress passive IMPs within the receiver, in an ongoing and near-real-time fashion, employing replicas of the transmitter’s signals; [b] specifying that the passive IMPs arise within passive components of the transmitter as well as components of the receiver, subsequent to the high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter in the transmitter; [c] wherein the transmitter filter is connected between the HPA and an antenna utilized by the transmitter; [d] wherein the ICSs are generated based on a power series representation describing a nonlinear mechanism for IMP generation, and specifically include producing an n-th order ICS by digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, where n denotes an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean signals that are “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record does not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, and it also gave weight to the inventor’s extrinsic testimony asserting “S1, S2 and S3” can generally signify signals that may or may not be the same. J.A. 109–14. The central limitation at issue on appeal is that the claim requires seven distinct third-order multiplications involving the three signals. During trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios satisfied the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, drawing upon a Nokia technical document illustrating the functionality of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells stated that the accused radios perform multiplication on three separately identifiable signals, relating the three separate multiplication operations outlined in the Nokia document to the seven multiplication steps specified by the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that every asserted claim of the ’775 patent was infringed. J.A. 169. In the context of JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” should be distinct signals, deeming the argument waived for lack of preservation at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that using two signals, together with a duplicate of one, met its interpretation of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. It further determined that using two unique signals yields just three distinct multiplication operations, which suffices to establish infringement because each multiplication matches two or three of the seven claim-specified multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios produce cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, since the radios in question utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants assert that during prosecution, Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding two signals by amending the claims to specify “three signals,” as opposed to “two or three signals,” for the purpose of distinguishing over the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generation of cancellation signals using two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants argue that a reasonable jury could not have determined the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications because Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage concerning two signals. The examiner rejected a claim that recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” on the ground of obviousness in light of Filipovic and secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse countered this rejection by partly noting that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the method in question also addresses passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse subsequently amended the claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, this does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” waiver of claim scope that excludes products—such as the accused radios—that employ two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At best, the amendment requires the presence of three individually distinguishable signals but does not dictate a minimum number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we agree that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios implement the seven specified multiplications. During the trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to assert that the accused radios execute the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate processes and does not demonstrate that the accused radios perform the seven required multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it only enumerates three specific multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence suggesting that any multiplications are duplicated to satisfy all seven as required under limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three distinct multiplications he identified correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the asserted claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse seeks to elucidate the mapping, yet this information was not presented to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, because the referenced document specifies performing multiplication on the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2, rather than x1 and x2 themselves, the mapping proposed by Finesse is deficient, as it requires, for example, the element S3 to align with x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse argued that the accused radios satisfy the seven claimed multiplications since Nokia’s documentation reveals that the accused products conduct not only seven, but a total of ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (noting p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells indicated which specific seven out of the ten multiplications disclosed in the Nokia documents are the ones purportedly corresponding to the asserted claims. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Likewise, they did not delineate how the ten Nokia-documented multiplications could be related to the three separate multiplications Finesse previously mapped onto the seven claimed functions. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these gaps, no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications. We accordingly reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL of noninfringement on the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because the verdict lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

Our review of a district court’s denial of a motion for new trial proceeds under the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, such denials are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury's verdict, Appellants requested JMOL as to damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (order denying JMOL as to damages); J.A. 58–65 (order denying new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award must be vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we set aside the judgment denying JMOL of noninfringement and nullify the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
