Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Author: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s rejection of their motion for a new trial on damages. For the reasons explained below, we reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) is the holder of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for addressing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. Radios operate on separate frequencies to prevent interference; however, their transmission signals may interact in the presence of barriers—such as metallic fencing, defective cables, or corroded connectors—to produce additional frequencies, termed “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs arise from passive obstacles, they are known as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent due to AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia joined the suit as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and granted a lump-sum damages award of $166,303,391 covering the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement, challenged the damages, and requested a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now bring this appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) follows the precedent of the pertinent regional circuit, in this instance the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit applies de novo review to JMOL denials and will reverse “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). When considering a jury's infringement verdict, we assess whether substantial evidence supports it. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). A factual determination meets the substantial evidence threshold if a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party in view of the trial evidence. Id. (internal citations omitted). Internal PIM results from mechanical problems within the radio, while external PIM originates from outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24. We examine claim construction de novo and review for clear error any underlying factual findings that inform the district court’s interpretation. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a technique for diminishing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” based on a calculated approximation of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a chosen frequency, a passband from the received signals in excess of the Nyquist rate so as to produce a bit stream, with the received signals comprising both signals of interest and signals that generate interference, where such interference-generating signals are capable of producing inband intermodulation products within the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bit stream through the use of one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting source signals responsible for causing one or more inband intermodulation products within the signal of interest via one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimate for each of the one or more intermodulation products using the source signals that generate them; [e] removing one or more inband intermodulation products utilizing the generated estimates; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude adjustments on estimates of the interfering intermodulation product signals in a closed loop fashion, where this involves making phase and amplitude corrections by performing sub-sample phase shifts in the estimates of the intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As plaintiff, Finesse was required to establish that the accused radios perform sampling of a passband which contains both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not demonstrate the accused radios actually sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, stated the accused radios practiced the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by relying upon a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, excerpted below). Finesse Br. 15 (with annotation to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells explained that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in a blue box) operates as a receiver sampling the asserted "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" (each represented along a dashed red path), producing a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently stated that he assigned the "signals of interest" to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the "interference generating signals" to the modeled PIM path (both shown in the dashed red path, as indicated in the red explanatory box within the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report similarly concluded that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel highlighted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path, as the latter is created after the RF ADC samples—therefore, signals cannot be sampled before their creation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This would mean that the modeled PIM path cannot constitute the claimed "interference generating signals." In response, Dr. Wells shifted, stating, "there's two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2." J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, he never specifically asserted that x1 and x2 represented the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," and instead continued to refer to "the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path." J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned to clarify if "x1 is the modeled PIM path," he replied, "I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path." J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, Dr. Wells never matched x1 or x2 to either the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” as required by the claims. The jury ultimately found infringement of all asserted claims of the ’134 patent. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells assigned the "interference generating signals" to the modeled PIM path, and, since the receivers in the accused radios do not sample a passband containing the interference generating signals—because these signals only arise after sampling—there is no infringement. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged, “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and noted, “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path is the "interference generating signals" claimed, limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied because, in the accused products, the

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver, where both the transmitter and receiver are located together, the method consists of: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that eliminate passive IMPs in the receiver, performed continuously and in near real time, by utilizing replicas of transmitter signals from the transmitter; [b] the passive IMPs are formed within passive transmitter elements of the transmitter and receiver elements of the receiver after the high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter in the transmitter; [c] the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and an antenna that the transmitter employs; [d] the ICSs are created based on a power series model describing the non-linear process behind IMP generation, and involve the production of an n-th order ICS by, for three signals S1, S2, and S3, carrying out digital multiplication and filtering of combinations including S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n being an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases original). At the summary judgment phase, the district court determined that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” means that the signals must be “separately identifiable,” but they do not have to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court found that the intrinsic record does not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, and relied also on testimony from the inventor—used as extrinsic evidence—that “S1, S2 and S3” are broad representations of signals and may be the same signal or different ones. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim requirement under dispute on appeal concerns whether the claim necessitates seven third-order multiplications among the three signals. At trial, Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios infringe the asserted ’775 patent claims by referencing a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused products. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). In particular, Dr. Wells stated the accused devices multiply three separately identifiable signals and matched the three separate multiplications in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications described in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all the asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court found Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals was waived since it had not been asserted at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that using two distinct signals, plus a duplicate of one of those, satisfied the requirement for three separately identifiable signals under its construction. J.A. 38. The court further determined that, when two unique signals are used, only three unique multiplications are generated, and this was sufficient to establish infringement since each of those three unique multiplications aligns with either two or three out of the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios generate cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, since, according to Appellants, the accused devices utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, Appellants maintain that Finesse effectively relinquished any claim coverage involving two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to specify “three signals” rather than “two or three signals,” in response to the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generating cancellation signals using two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants assert that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios conduct the seven multiplications required by the claims, since Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage regarding two signals. The examiner had deemed a claim referring to “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious over Filipovic and additional references. See J.A. 1006–07. However, Finesse addressed this rejection by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the patented method further addresses passive IMPs as well. See J.A. 985; also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse amended the claims to expressly require the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” across seven multiplications, this does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim coverage for devices such as the accused radios that possess only two distinct input signals. See Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The amendment, at best, calls for three individually recognizable signals, not a particular minimum count of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no rational jury could have determined the accused radios carry out all seven of the required multiplications specified in the claims. During trial, Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document to attempt to show the accused radios performed the claimed multiplications. See J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document describes modulus and complex conjugate processes, and does not show the accused radios conducting the seven distinct multiplications at issue. See J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it only itemizes three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no proof that any are repeated so as to satisfy the requirement in limitation 1[d] for all seven multiplications. See J.A. 26486 (quoted below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells failed to explain the relationship between the three distinct multiplications and the seven multiplications asserted in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse makes an attempt on appeal to clarify this relationship, but that evidence was not before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the document in question does not disclose multiplication of x1 and x2 themselves, but rather their complex conjugate and modulus, Finesse’s method of mapping is deficient. For example, it incorrectly requires S3 to correspond to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications on the basis that Nokia’s documents indicated the accused radios carry out not only seven, but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referring to p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells has specified which group of the ten multiplications described in the Nokia documents matches the seven multiplications claimed. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Similarly, they have not identified how these ten multiplications correspond to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse mapped to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, no reasonable jury could find that the accused radios practice the seven claimed multiplications. We therefore reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because the evidence is insufficient to support the jury’s verdict on infringement. 

II. Damages

Our review of the district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial follows the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, the denial of a new trial is evaluated for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Subsequent to the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on the damages issue, or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both motions. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Since we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we set aside the denial of JMOL concerning noninfringement and nullify the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED
