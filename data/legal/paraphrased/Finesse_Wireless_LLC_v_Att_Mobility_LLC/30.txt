Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Author: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court’s denial of their request for a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the decision denying JMOL and set aside the award of damages.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. While radios typically minimize interference by transmitting on separate frequencies, their outgoing signals may mix when impeded by objects such as metal fences, loose cabling, or corroded connectors, resulting in the creation of additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs are generated due to non-active, physical obstacles, they are specifically referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse filed suit against AT&T, accusing it of infringing claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through the use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability found in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the action. J.A. 389–90. Following trial, the jury determined that all asserted patent claims were both valid and infringed, and it awarded Finesse a lump-sum of $166,303,391 to compensate for the expected remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) for noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and also requested a new trial, but the district court denied all such post-trial motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now challenge those outcomes on appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We evaluate a district court's refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) pursuant to the law of the relevant regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are examined de novo and may be overturned “only if, when the evidence is considered in the light most favorable to the verdict, it so strongly and overwhelmingly favors one party that the court concludes that reasonable jurors could not reach a different decision.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit authority). Our review of a jury's infringement determination is for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “Substantial evidence supports a factual determination if, based on the trial record, a reasonable jury could have rendered a verdict in favor of the prevailing party.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We conduct de novo review of claim construction matters, while we evaluate any factual determinations underpinning the district court's construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process aimed at minimizing IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” through a calculated estimation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] Oversampling, at a chosen frequency, a passband of received signals to produce a bit stream, where the received signals are comprised of both signals of interest and signals generating interference, the latter of which may create intermodulation products within the band of the signals of interest; [b] utilizing one or more decimating filters to isolate the signals of interest within the bit stream; [c] utilizing one or more decimating filters to isolate source signals responsible for generating one or more intermodulation products that fall within the band of the signal of interest; [d] calculating an estimate of each intermodulation product generated from these source signals; [e] using the estimate(s) of the intermodulation products to cancel out one or more inband intermodulation products; and [f] adjusting phase and amplitude for estimated intermodulation product interfering signals via a closed loop process, wherein the phase and amplitude adjustment includes sub-sample phase shifts applied to the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals for phase adjustment. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As plaintiff, Finesse was required to establish that the accused radios perform sampling of a passband containing both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not prove the accused radios actually sample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. At trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, testified regarding infringement by the accused radios with respect to the asserted ’134 patent claims by relying on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown within the blue box) serves as a receiver, sampling the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” specified in the claims (both marked as dashed red paths) and producing a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly indicated that he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path (each represented by the dashed red path as clarified in the red box in the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report similarly concluded “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants highlighted that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path because it is produced after the ADC sampling occurs, i.e., downstream of the RF ADC, and thus such signals cannot be sampled prior to their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Accordingly, the modeled PIM path cannot represent the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his answer, testifying that “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not state that x1 and x2 directly correspond to “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” instead continuing to testify that the “two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked to specify whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he responded, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Again, Dr. Wells never assigned x1 or x2 to either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as required by the claims.

The jury ultimately found every asserted claim of the ’134 patent infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants argued that Dr. Wells mapped “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path and that infringement could not be shown because the receivers of the accused radios do not sample a passband of received signals that contains interference generating signals, since those signals only come into existence after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and further that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, should the modeled PIM path constitute the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, alongside a transmitter where both devices are positioned together, the described method includes: [a] generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that serve to continuously, and in near real-time, counteract passive IMPs within the receiver by utilizing duplicates of transmitter signals from the transmitter itself, [b] with the passive IMPs arising in passive transmitter parts of the transmitter and components of the receiver after both a high powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter filter within the transmitter, [c] with the transmitter filter placed in the signal path between the HPA and a shared antenna used by the transmitter, [d] and with the creation of the ICSs relying on a power series representation of a nonlinear phenomenon producing the IMPs, specifically requiring for the generation of an n-th order ICS, given three signals, S1, S2, and S3, the digital multiplication and filtering of S1×S1×S2; S1×S2×S2; S1×S2×S3; S1×S1×S3; S2×S2×S3; S1×S3×S3; and S2×S3×S3, with n being an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases original). At the summary judgment phase, the district court construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" as "separately identifiable," but not necessarily "unique." J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record does not confine "S1, S2 and S3" to distinct input signals and also relied on the inventor’s testimony as extrinsic evidence, indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” represent signals generally, which may either be the same or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The pivotal claim limitation addressed on appeal is the requirement that the claim be met by performing seven third-order multiplications involving these three signals. At trial, Dr. Wells presented evidence that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operational details of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals, equating the three distinct multiplications itemized in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In its JMOL ruling, the district court dismissed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must represent unique signals, treating the argument as waived because it was not raised during the Rule 50(a) motion. J.A. 38, 40. The court further held that the use of two signals, together with a duplicate of one of those signals, sufficed under its interpretation of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. The court also noted that only three distinct multiplications result when using two unique signals, yet found this adequate for a showing of infringement, since each of these three unique multiplications maps onto two or three of the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that these radios operate with only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse narrowed the scope of the claims to exclude two-signal embodiments during prosecution when it amended the claims from “two or three signals” to “three signals” to overcome the Filipovic prior art, which discloses the generation of cancellation signals using two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants argue that the evidence does not support a finding that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications recited in the claims, as Dr. Wells only identified three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We reject the contention that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for systems utilizing two signals. The examiner found a claim encompassing “digitally multiplying two or three signals” to be obvious over Filipovic combined with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse addressed this rejection in part by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” whereas the invention addresses passive IMPs as well, as claimed. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Although Finesse further modified the claims to include the requirement of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” across seven multiplications, this modification does not meet the threshold of an “unequivocal and unmistakable” waiver of claim scope with respect to devices, including the accused radios, that process only two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. Finesse’s amendment, at most, establishes the need for three individually identifiable signals, but does not mandate a specific quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. We concur, nevertheless, that the evidence could not support a jury finding that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications set forth in the claims. During trial, Dr. Wells referred to a Nokia document to contend that the accused radios carry out the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the referenced document describes operations involving modulus and complex conjugate functions and does not demonstrate performance of the seven required multiplications by the accused radios. J.A. 26485–86. It specifies only three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no indication that any multiplications are repeated in such a way as to achieve the seven multiplications specified by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

In addition, Dr. Wells did not offer testimony connecting the three separate multiplications to the seven multiplications required by the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse seeks to elucidate this mapping on appeal, such evidence was not introduced to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the document discloses multiplication of the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2 rather than x1 and x2 themselves, the mapping Finesse proposes is deficient; for instance, it entails that S3 corresponds to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. At oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications because, according to Nokia’s documentation, they in fact carry out ten multiplications rather than only seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells delineate which specific seven of these ten Nokia multiplications allegedly correspond to the claimed seven. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they articulate any mapping between the ten multiplications and the three distinct multiplications Finesse sought to equate to the claimed seven. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios practice the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL of noninfringement on the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because there is not substantial evidence to support the infringement verdict.

II. Damages

We assess a district court’s denial of a new trial motion following the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury returned its verdict, Appellants moved for JMOL as to damages, or, in the alternative, for a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we also vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the decision denying JMOL on noninfringement and set aside the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
