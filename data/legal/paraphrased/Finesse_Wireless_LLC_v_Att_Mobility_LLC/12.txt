Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. In addition, Appellants contest the district court’s decision denying their request for a new trial on damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the decision denying JMOL and set aside the award of damages.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) holds ownership of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which primarily pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (“IMP”) interference encountered in radios. Typically, radios use distinct frequencies to prevent signal interference; however, when their transmitted signals are obstructed by objects such as metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors, these signals may combine and create new frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (“IMPs”). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise due to non-powered obstacles, they are termed “passive intermodulation products” (“PIM”). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, claiming infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, based on AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (“PIM-C”) function included in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the case. J.A. 389–90. A jury determined that every asserted claim was both valid and infringed and awarded Finesse a lump-sum payment of $166,303,391 as damages covering the remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants requested judgment as a matter of law (“JMOL”) regarding noninfringement, damages, and sought a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants have filed an appeal. This court possesses jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We evaluate a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL pursuant to the legal standards of the relevant regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). According to the Fifth Circuit, de novo review applies to JMOL denials, with reversals warranted only “if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit precedent). Infringement findings by a jury are reviewed to determine if substantial evidence supports them. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). That is, substantial evidence exists if a rational jury could have ruled for the prevailing party, taking into account the trial evidence. Id. (internal citations omitted). Our review of claim construction is conducted de novo, while any factual findings underlying the district court’s construction are examined for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM results from mechanical problems within the radio, while external PIM arises from factors external to the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at mitigating IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently neutralizing the “interference generating signals” through a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling a passband of incoming signals at a specified frequency with a higher-than-normal sampling rate to produce a stream of bits, where these incoming signals comprise both signals of interest and interference-generating signals, the latter being capable of producing intermodulation products that appear within the frequency band of the signals of interest; [b] utilizing one or more decimating filters to extract the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] applying one or more decimating filters to separate source signals that produce one or more intermodulation products that fall within the same frequency band as the signal of interest; [d] generating an estimate for each identified intermodulation product based on the separated source signals; [e] removing one or more inband intermodulation products from the signal using the generated estimate of those products; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude modifications on the interference estimates of the intermodulation product signals within a feedback system, where these phase and amplitude modifications on the interference estimates involve implementing phase shifts at a sub-sample level to achieve the desired phase adjustment. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases added). As the party seeking relief, Finesse bore the responsibility to demonstrate that the accused radios sample a signal passband comprising both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this position. At trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios met the asserted claim requirements of the ’134 patent, basing his analysis on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, reproduced below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) serves as a receiver, sampling both the alleged “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each illustrated by the dashed red path) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently indicated that the “signals of interest” correspond to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and that the “interference generating signals” relate to the modeled PIM path, both depicted in the dashed red path and as explained in the red box accompanying the legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report likewise stated that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

On cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel contended that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path since it is generated downstream of the RF ADC, after sampling occurs; as a result, signals cannot be sampled before their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Therefore, the modeled PIM path could not be the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Dr. Wells did not state that x1 and x2 represent the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” and instead maintained that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When further questioned about whether he was asserting “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” his response was, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Again, Dr. Wells did not link x1 or x2 with either “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as specified by the claims.

The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In their motion for JMOL, Appellants argued that Dr. Wells identified “interference generating signals” as the modeled PIM path, and thus infringement was impossible, since the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a received signal passband that contains interference generating signals; those signals are generated only after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Consequently, if the modeled PIM path is indeed the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation 1[a] cannot

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system where the transmitter and receiver are situated together, the claimed method involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to mitigate passive IMPs within the receiver, on a continuous and near real-time basis, by utilizing duplicates of the transmitter's own signals; [b] with the passive IMPs arising from passive components within the transmitter and from the receiver's components, specifically after the transmitter's high powered amplifier (HPA) and filter; [c] the transmitter filter being connected between the HPA and the transmitter's antenna; [d] with ICS generation relying on a power series representation of a nonlinear process that creates the IMPs, which entails producing an n-th order ICS by, when given three signals, S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering the following products: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n denotes an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record did not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, and also gave weight to the inventor’s testimony, presented as extrinsic evidence, that “S1, S2 and S3” are general representations of signals that may be the same or different. J.A. 109–14. The primary claim limitation under review on appeal is that the seven enumerated third-order multiplications involving the three signals must be carried out to satisfy the claim requirement. During trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios met the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document which illustrates the functioning of the accused products. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells stated that the accused devices perform multiplication on three separately identifiable signals and equated the three discrete multiplications shown in the Nokia documentation to the seven multiplications called for in the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found that each asserted claim of the ’775 patent was infringed. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals as being forfeited, since it was not raised during the Rule 50(a) motion. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that utilizing two signals together with a duplicate of one of them satisfied the construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. The court further decided that only three unique multiplications occur when two distinct signals are employed, but this suffices to establish infringement, as each of those three multiplications matches up with two or three of the seven multiplications enumerated in the claims. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios create cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, since, according to Appellants, the accused devices utilize only two signals: x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants assert that Finesse relinquished claim coverage encompassing two signals when, during prosecution, it amended the claims to specify “three signals,” replacing the original “two or three signals,” in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art, which discloses generating cancellation signals from two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios engage in the seven multiplications claimed, arguing that Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim coverage regarding two signals. The examiner rejected a claim involving “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious over Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, overcame this rejection in part by noting that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” not passive IMPs, which are addressed by the claimed method. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did modify the claims to include the feature of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplication steps, this amendment does not constitute the type of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope concerning products that utilize two distinct input signals, such as the accused radios. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The amendment, at most, requires the presence of three individually distinguishable signals, without specifying any particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. However, we concur that a reasonable jury could not have found that the accused radios carry out the seven specific multiplications as claimed. During trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document in support of the assertion that the accused devices conduct the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the referenced document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate calculations and fails to establish that the accused products perform all seven of the required multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it only identifies three unique multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no proof showing any repetitions of these operations so as to satisfy the performance of all seven claimed multiplications stipulated by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three unique multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse tries to clarify how this mapping works, but this evidence was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, since the referenced document does not teach multiplying x1 and x2 themselves, but rather their complex conjugate and modulus, Finesse’s mapping is faulty. It impermissibly requires mapping, for example, S3 onto the signal x2, as well as its complex conjugate x2' and modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. At oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios conduct the seven claimed multiplications based on Nokia’s documentation, which allegedly shows the accused radios actually perform ten multiplications, not just seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referring to p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identify which selection of ten multiplications from the Nokia document should be matched to the seven required by the claims. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they provide any mapping from these ten multiplications back to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse previously mapped to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Under these circumstances, a reasonable jury could not have concluded the accused radios meet the seven claimed multiplication steps. We accordingly reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, because substantial evidence does not support the jury's verdict of infringement. 

II. Damages

The standard for reviewing a district court's denial of a new trial motion is set by regional circuit law. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard to such denials. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants requested JMOL on damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of motion for new trial on damages). Because we have reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED
