Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket No.: 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s decision to deny a motion for a new trial on damages. For the reasons explained below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the holder of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which broadly concern techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radio systems. Radios prevent signal interference by utilizing different frequencies; however, their transmitted signals may combine upon encountering obstructions (such as metal fences, improperly connected cables, or corroded connectors), generating additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs arise from non-active obstacles, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, contending such infringement resulted from AT&T’s implementation of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature within Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined all challenged claims were both valid and infringed, granting $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages for the remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants then sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) for noninfringement, for damages, and requested a new trial; the district court denied each motion. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now seek appellate review. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the applicable regional circuit when assessing a district court’s denial of JMOL, which in this matter is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The standard employed by the Fifth Circuit for reviewing such denials is de novo, and reversal is warranted “only if, after viewing the evidence in a light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence is so overwhelmingly in favor of one party that reasonable jurors could not have reached a different verdict.” Id. (citing precedent from the Fifth Circuit). The jury’s determination regarding infringement is examined for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). A finding is sustained by substantial evidence where a reasonable jury could reasonably decide for the prevailing party based on the trial record. Id. (internal citations omitted). Internal PIM arises from mechanical issues within the radio, while external PIM results from factors external to the radio. J.A. 15223–24. Claim construction is subject to de novo review, and any related factual determinations made by the district court in support of its construction are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at decreasing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 exemplifies this invention.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling, at a selected frequency, a passband from incoming signals in order to generate a sequence of bits, where these incoming signals comprise both desired signals and interference-generating signals, with the latter being capable of producing inband intermodulation products that fall within the range of the desired signals; [b] separating out the desired signals within the bit sequence by applying one or more decimating filters; [c] isolating the original signals responsible for producing one or more inband intermodulation products within the desired signal using one or more decimating filters; [d] generating an estimate for each inband intermodulation product from the original signals that cause those products; [e] removing one or more inband intermodulation products with the aid of the estimates created; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude corrections on the estimates of the interfering intermodulation product signals using a closed-loop process, where such phase and amplitude corrections entail implementing phase adjustments at sub-sample intervals on the estimates of the interfering intermodulation product signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As plaintiff, Finesse had the responsibility to demonstrate that the accused radios perform sampling of a passband containing both “signals of interest” as well as “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants maintain that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, opined that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by relying on a Nokia technical manual showing the operation of the accused devices. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, cited below). Finesse Br. 15 (referencing annotation to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown within a blue box) functions as a receiver which samples the asserted “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” (both shown along a dashed red path) in order to create a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly maintained that he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, as shown along the dashed red path and detailed in the red box under the diagram legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Consistently, Dr. Wells’ expert report stated that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants argued that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path, as the modeled PIM path only arises downstream of the RF ADC following sampling, so it is not possible for a signal to be sampled before it exists. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Consequently, this would mean that the modeled PIM path could not be the “interference generating signals” as required by the claims. In reply, Dr. Wells asserted, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells never stated that x1 and x2 correspond to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; instead, he continued to identify “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When further pressed to clarify whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells stated, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Even then, he did not associate x1 or x2 with the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as the claims require.

The jury found infringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 patent. J.A. 169. On JMOL, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells had attributed the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, but the accused radios’ receiver cannot sample a passband containing “interference generating signals” because those signals arise only after sampling has taken place. J.A. 19–20. The court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and also that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path is the asserted “interference generating signals

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, where the transmitter and receiver are positioned together, the method includes: [a] the continuous and near real-time production of intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) designed to counter passive IMPs in the receiver, utilizing duplicates of the transmitter's own signals, [b] with passive IMPs originating from passive components in both the transmitter and the receiver, specifically those after the high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter of the transmitter, [c] where the transmitter filter is installed between the HPA and the transmitter's antenna, [d] where the formation of ICSs relies on a power series model describing the non-linear process that generates IMPs, and incorporates the creation of an n-th order ICS by taking three signals, S1, S2, and S3, and digitally multiplying and filtering the combinations S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n represents an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added). During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require that the signals be “separately identifiable,” though not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that neither the claim language nor the specification confines “S1, S2 and S3” to distinct inputs, and the court also accepted the inventor’s extrinsic testimony that these labels could stand for either identical or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation addressed on appeal concerns whether seven third order products involving the three signals must be performed to satisfy the limitation. At trial, Dr. Wells gave testimony that the radios accused of infringement practice the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical reference which describes the radios’ operation. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells explained that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals, and he equated the three distinct multiplications disclosed in the Nokia document to the seven combinations required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury concluded that all challenged claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court rejected Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique, holding that this argument was waived since it was not presented at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court held that using two different signals, along with a duplicate of one signal, was enough to satisfy its claim construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court determined that the use of only two unique signals would yield three distinct products, which sufficiently demonstrated infringement, as each of the three products maps to two or three of the seven listed in the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios create cancellation signals by means of multiplying three signals, as the devices in question utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse disclaimed claim coverage for systems using two signals when, during prosecution, it amended the claims to specify “three signals,” rather than “two or three signals,” in response to the Filipovic prior art, which discloses the use of two inputs to produce cancellation signals. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Appellants further assert that the jury lacked a reasonable basis to conclude the accused radios performed the seven multiplications claimed in the patent, given that Dr. Wells only identified three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope involving two signals. The examiner found a claim reciting “digitally multiplying two or three signals” obvious in light of Filipovic combined with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse overcame the rejection by arguing that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which are also tackled by the claimed invention. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Although Finesse further modified its claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in a series of seven multiplications, such amendment falls short of constituting a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of claim coverage for products—such as the accused radios—that have only two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At best, Finesse’s amendment imposes a requirement for three separate, identifiable signals, without mandating a minimum number of distinct input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that the evidence would not permit a reasonable jury to conclude that the accused radios perform the seven specified multiplications set out in the claims. At trial, Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document to support the assertion that the accused radios implement the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, this document discusses modulus and complex conjugate operations and fails to demonstrate that the accused radios execute all seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. In reality, it only identifies three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and no evidence establishes that any multiplications are repeated so that all seven as required by limitation 1[d] are performed. J.A. 26486 (see reproduction below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony describing how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications stated in the claims. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to clarify this correspondence on appeal, but such evidence was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, because the relevant document fails to disclose multiplication of x1 and x2 themselves, instead discussing their complex conjugate and modulus, Finesse’s proposed correspondence is deficient since it, for example, requires S3 to be matched with signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications based on Nokia documentation, which, according to Finesse, indicates the accused radios execute not just seven, but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referring to p(n) = a0 through a9). Finesse and Dr. Wells do not specify which seven of the ten multiplications identified in the Nokia document align with the claimed seven multiplications. See Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they explain how those ten multiplications are related to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse associated with the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 17–18. Accordingly, under these facts, no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications required by the claims. Therefore, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because there is not substantial evidence to support the jury’s verdict on infringement.

II. Damages

We examine a district court’s denial of a new trial motion pursuant to the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit law). After the jury rendered its verdict, Appellants filed for JMOL concerning damages, or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court rejected both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL regarding damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying motion for new trial on damages). Since we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement regarding all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we also vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the decision denying JMOL on noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
