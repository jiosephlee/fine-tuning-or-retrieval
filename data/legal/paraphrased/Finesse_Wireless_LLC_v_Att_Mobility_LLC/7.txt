Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) in their favor regarding noninfringement of the asserted claims in U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s refusal to order a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL and set aside the award of damages.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radio devices. Although radios typically avoid interfering with each other by operating on separate frequencies, their transmitted signals can interact in the presence of obstacles such as metal fences, loose cables, or corroded connectors, resulting in the generation of new frequencies, referred to as “intermodulation products” or IMPs. J.A. 14892, 14920. When IMPs are generated by non-active obstacles, they are specifically termed “passive intermodulation products” or PIM. J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, claiming infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, based on AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability in Nokia-manufactured radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the action. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and awarded Finesse a lump-sum payment of $166,303,391 covering the remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Following the verdict, Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, damages, and requested a new trial, but the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. The Appellants now bring this appeal. Jurisdiction is proper pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court's refusal to grant JMOL according to the legal standards of the regional circuit—in this case, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are subject to de novo review and will only be overturned “if, when the evidence is considered in the light most favorable to the verdict, it so heavily and conclusively favors one party that the court is convinced reasonable jurors could not reach a different outcome.” Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit law). The determination of infringement by a jury is reviewed for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). A finding of fact has substantial evidentiary support if the record at trial would allow a reasonable jury to find in favor of the prevailing party. Id. (internal citations omitted). Claim construction is examined de novo, while any subsidiary factual determinations underpinning the district court's interpretation are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a technique to diminish IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently neutralizing the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a range of received signals to generate a stream of bits, where the received signals comprise both desired signals and signals that cause interference, with such interference signals being capable of producing inband intermodulation products within the range of the desired signals; [b] using one or more decimating filters to extract the desired signals from the bit stream; [c] employing one or more decimating filters to isolate source signals responsible for generating one or more inband intermodulation products within the range of the desired signals; [d] calculating an estimated value of each of the one or more inband intermodulation products from the source signals that generate them; [e] removing one or more inband intermodulation products by utilizing the estimated values; and [f] applying adjustments to phase and amplitude of the estimations of the interfering intermodulation product signals through a closed loop process, where such adjustment includes sub-sample phase shifting to achieve phase modification on the estimated interfering intermodulation product signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases added). As plaintiff, Finesse had the obligation to demonstrate that the accused radios perform sampling on a passband that contains both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, stated that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document illustrating the functioning of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, set forth below). Finesse Br. 15 (with annotation to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown within the blue box) functions as a receiver, sampling both the designated “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” (each illustrated in the dashed red path), to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly matched the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path (both indicated in the dashed red path, with explanations in the red box under the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Similarly, Dr. Wells’ expert report concluded, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants pointed out that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because it is created downstream of the RF ADC after sampling, and thus, signals cannot be sampled prior to their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This suggests that the modeled PIM path cannot serve as the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Dr. Wells did not claim that x1 and x2 represent the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals”; instead, he reiterated that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When prompted to clarify whether he meant “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Again, he did not specifically connect x1 or x2 to either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as the claims require.

The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In the motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), Appellants contended that Dr. Wells equated “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path and argued that there could be no infringement because the receiver in the accused radios does not sample a passband that includes interference generating signals, as those signals do not exist until after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and acknowledged, “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Thus, if the modeled PIM path is equated with the claimed “

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system where both a transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to counteract passive IMPs in the receiver, continuously and in near real time, by employing copies of the transmitter’s signals; [b] specifying that these passive IMPs are formed within passive components of both the transmitter and receiver, subsequent to the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter; [c] with the transmitter filter positioned between the HPA and an antenna used by the transmitter; [d] specifying that ICSs are generated by modeling the nonlinear process for IMP generation with a power series, which includes creating an n-th order ICS by, for three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record does not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to distinct input signals and gave weight to inventor testimony—as extrinsic evidence—that “S1, S2 and S3” refer to signals in general and may be the same or different. J.A. 109–14. The principal disputed claim limitation on appeal concerns whether seven third-order multiplications of these three signals are required for the limitation to be satisfied. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios practiced the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells stated that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals and equated the three distinct multiplications presented in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications required by the claim. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. When considering JMOL, the district court rejected Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals as waived, since Appellants did not raise this argument at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court further found that using two signals plus a duplicate of one sufficed to satisfy its interpretation of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court concluded that employing two unique signals results in only three distinct multiplications, and that this sufficed for infringement because each of these three unique multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven multiplications specified in the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the devices in question utilize only two signals: x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished any claim coverage extending to two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to require “three signals” rather than “two or three signals,” in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generation of cancellation signals using two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Further, Appellants assert that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications recited in the claims, since Dr. Wells only identified three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope relating to two signals. The examiner found the claim which recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” to be obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse countered this rejection by stating that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the claimed method also addresses passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse subsequently amended the claims to specify digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” across seven multiplications, such an amendment does not constitute the type of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope that would exclude products like the accused radios, which utilize two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At best, the amendment demands the presence of three individually distinguishable signals but does not mandate a particular quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios execute the seven specified multiplications as claimed. During trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document to demonstrate that the accused radios performed the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the referenced document describes modulus and complex conjugate operations and fails to show that the accused radios perform the required seven multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it identifies only three different multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and no evidence indicates any of the multiplications are duplicated to collectively fulfill the seven required multiplications under limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse attempts to provide clarification regarding this mapping on appeal, that evidence was not presented before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Moreover, since the referenced document does not disclose multiplication of x1 and x2, but rather their complex conjugates and moduli, Finesse's mapping is deficient because it requires, for instance, mapping S3 to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications, pointing to Nokia’s documentation that shows the accused radios actually carry out not just seven, but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do Finesse or Dr. Wells clarify which group of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document aligns with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they delineate a mapping from those ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse associated with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these circumstances, a reasonable jury could not have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. We therefore reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s verdict of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

We assess a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial according to the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of motions for a new trial are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages, or alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the award of damages.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
