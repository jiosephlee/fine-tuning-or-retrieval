Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s denial of their motion for a new trial on damages. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) holds the rights to the ’134 and ’775 patents, which concern techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radio devices. Typically, radios minimize interference by transmitting on separate frequencies; however, these transmission signals can merge upon interacting with certain obstacles (such as metal fences, loose or rusted cable connections), resulting in the creation of new frequencies termed “intermodulation products” (IMPs). See J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise from passive obstacles, they are known as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse filed suit against AT&T, claiming that AT&T infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through its use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature in Nokia-manufactured radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the case. J.A. 389–90. The jury concluded that each asserted claim was valid and infringed, awarding Finesse a lump sum of $166,303,391 as compensation for the remainder of the terms of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants submitted motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, damages, and requested a new trial, all of which were denied by the district court. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now bring this appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the regional circuit, in this case the Fifth Circuit, when reviewing a district court's denial of JMOL. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, the denial of JMOL is considered de novo; the court will reverse “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Our review of a jury’s infringement finding is for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We review claim construction without deference, but review any subsidiary factual determinations underlying the district court’s construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a technique aimed at decreasing IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by neutralizing the “interference generating signals” based on a calculated estimation of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a chosen frequency, a range of received signals to produce a sequence of bits, where these received signals comprise both the signals of interest and signals that generate interference, with the latter capable of causing intermodulation products that appear within the band of the signals of interest; [b] separating the signals of interest from the bit sequence by utilizing one or more decimating filters; [c] separating source signals that produce one or more intermodulation products within the band of the signals of interest by using one or more decimating filters; [d] generating an estimate for each of the intermodulation products from the source signals responsible for generating them; [e] eliminating one or more intermodulation products within the band by using the estimates of these intermodulation products; and [f] carrying out phase and amplitude corrections on the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals in a closed-loop process, where making phase and amplitude corrections to the estimates involves implementing sub-sample phase shifts to adjust the phase of the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases added).

As the party bringing suit, Finesse was responsible for demonstrating that the radios in question sample a range of signals containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants maintain that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios actually sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this argument. During the trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, serving as Finesse’s infringement expert, asserted that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by relying on a Nokia technical document that describes the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, reproduced below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), shown within a blue box, functions as a receiver that samples both the alleged “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” represented by a dashed red path, in order to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently asserted that he associated “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, both of which are indicated by the dashed red path and described in the legend’s red box. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report also stated, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants argued that the RF ADC is incapable of sampling the modeled PIM path because the modeled PIM path is produced after the RF ADC has performed sampling, meaning that it cannot sample signals that have not yet been created. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Consequently, the modeled PIM path cannot serve as the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells changed course, asserting, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not clarify that x1 and x2 directly correspond to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; instead, he reiterated, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When pressed further as to whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells stated, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, Dr. Wells did not specify that x1 or x2 represent the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as claimed. The jury ultimately determined all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. During the JMOL stage, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells linked the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, making infringement impossible because the receivers in the accused radios do not sample a passband of signals that includes “interference generating signals,” since those signals do not exist until after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized, “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation 1[a

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system where a receiver is present and both the transmitter and receiver are situated together, the process involves: [a] creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) for suppressing passive IMPs in the receiver, on a continuous and nearly real-time basis, utilizing copies of signals from the transmitter; [b] where the passive IMPs arise in passive transmitter components as well as receiver components, following a high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter within the transmitter; [c] with the transmitter filter positioned between the HPA and the transmitter's shared antenna; [d] and the ICSs being generated based on a power series model that describes the nonlinear process responsible for the IMPs, which includes, for generating an n-th order ICS with three signals S1, S2, and S3, the digital multiplication and filtering of the terms S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). On summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as meaning they are “separately identifiable,” without requiring them to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined the intrinsic record did not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to distinct input signals and also relied on the inventor’s testimony, offered as extrinsic evidence, that “S1, S2 and S3” generally refer to signals and could represent the same or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The central claim limitation under review in this appeal is the requirement that all seven third-order products derived from the three signals are to be formed in order to satisfy the limitation. During the trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing this on a Nokia technical document outlining the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells explained that the accused radios carry out multiplication of three separately identifiable signals, and he correlated the three specific products identified in the Nokia document to the seven multiplication products recited in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. When considering JMOL, the district court determined that Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals was waived because it had not been raised at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that using two signals and a duplicate of one of them was adequate to meet the requirement for three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court held that when only two unique signals are used, this results in three distinct multiplication products, and this was sufficient to establish infringement because each of these three products aligns with two or three of the seven multiplication products claimed. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, as these radios allegedly utilize only two signals: x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants assert that Finesse relinquished any claim to two-signal scope during prosecution when it amended the claims to require “three signals,” replacing the earlier “two or three signals” language, in order to distinguish the claims over the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generating cancellation signals from two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Appellants further argue that no reasonable jury could have concluded the accused radios perform the seven multiplications claimed, given that Dr. Wells identified only three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim that recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious over Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse overcame this rejection in part by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” and not passive IMPs, which the claimed method also addresses. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to include the requirement of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, this amendment does not constitute the “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope with respect to products—such as the accused radios—that have two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, Finesse’s amendment specifies that there must be three independently identifiable signals, but it does not dictate a precise number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. We do find, however, that no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications required by the claims. During trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document to assert that the accused radios performed the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document involves modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not demonstrate that the accused radios carry out all seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it merely identifies three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no evidence showing that any multiplications are repeated so that all seven required multiplications under limitation 1[d] are performed. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three distinct multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse tries to clarify this correlation on appeal, such evidence was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, because the referenced document does not describe multiplying x1 and x2 directly, but rather addresses their complex conjugates and moduli, the mapping provided by Finesse is deficient; for example, it attempts to map S3 to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation indicates that the radios actually perform ten multiplications, not merely seven. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identify which subset of these ten multiplications aligns with the seven claimed multiplications. See Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they provide an explanation linking the ten multiplications from the Nokia document to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse associated with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. In view of this, no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios practice the seven claimed multiplications. Therefore, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s finding of infringement is not substantiated by substantial evidence.

II. Damages

We evaluate a district court’s denial of a new trial motion under the applicable regional circuit’s law. See Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit examines such denials for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants requested JMOL on damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (JMOL on damages denied); J.A. 58–65 (new trial on damages denied). Since we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
