Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia), referred to together as Appellants, challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s decision denying a new trial on damages. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, both of which pertain broadly to techniques aimed at reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. In radio communications, interference between signals is typically avoided by employing different frequency bands; however, when these transmit signals encounter physical obstacles, such as metal fencing, unsecured cable connections, or corroded connectors, they may combine to generate new frequencies, referred to as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs result from passive physical obstacles, they are known as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging that it infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent by employing the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the lawsuit. J.A. 389–90. The jury returned a verdict finding all asserted claims both valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum amount of $166,303,391 in damages for the remaining terms of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants subsequently moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL with respect to damages, and for a new trial, each of which the district court denied. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal. This court has jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

The denial of a motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) by the district court is examined in accordance with the law of the regional circuit, in this case, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are subject to de novo review and will only be reversed if, considering the evidence in the light most favorable to the jury’s verdict, the evidence so strongly and overwhelmingly favors one party that no reasonable jury could reach a different conclusion. Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit precedent). We assess jury determinations of infringement under the substantial evidence standard. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence when a reasonable jury, based on the trial record, could have ruled in favor of the party that prevailed.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Claim construction is reviewed de novo, while underlying factual determinations by the district court that inform the construction are evaluated for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM results from mechanical conditions within the radio itself, whereas external PIM arises from sources external to the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process for minimizing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated IMP estimate. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling, at a selected frequency, a band of received signals to produce a bitstream, where the received signals comprise both signals of interest and interference-generating signals, the latter being capable of producing inband intermodulation products within the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bitstream by means of one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting the source signals responsible for creating one or more inband intermodulation products using one or more decimating filters; [d] calculating an estimate for each such intermodulation product based on the source signals that generate them; [e] using these estimates to cancel one or more of the inband intermodulation products; and [f] executing phase and amplitude modifications on the estimates of the interfering intermodulation product signals in a closed-loop fashion, where the process of making phase and amplitude modifications to the estimates includes applying sub-sample phase shifts to adjust the phase of the estimated intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As the plaintiff, Finesse had the obligation to demonstrate that the accused radios oversample a band of signals encompassing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios oversample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment. At trial, Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios met the claims asserted in the ’134 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, depicted below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, marked by the blue box) functions as a receiver which samples both the identified “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each indicated along the dashed red path) in order to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently indicated that he matched the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and assigned the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, both of which are marked along the dashed red path and as explained in the red box under the legend. See, for example, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be... the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’s expert report similarly concluded that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal... meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants pointed out that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path because this path is created downstream of the RF ADC after the sampling step; it is not possible to sample signals prior to their creation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Therefore, the modeled PIM path cannot be the “interference generating signals” specified in the claims. In reply, Dr. Wells shifted his explanation, testifying that “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Nevertheless, Dr. Wells did not state that x1 and x2 equate to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Instead, he reiterated that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked directly if “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells responded, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Once more, he did not assign x1 or x2 to the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as stipulated by the claims.

The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. Upon JMOL, Appellants argued that Dr. Wells associated the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, and accordingly infringement could not be found because the receiver of the accused radios does not sample a passband of incoming signals that contains interference generating signals, as those signals are only created after the sampling event. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and acknowledged “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). 1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, where the transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to eliminate passive IMPs in the receiver, in a continuous and nearly real-time manner, by utilizing duplicates of the transmitter's output signals, [b] the passive IMPs arise in the transmitter’s passive components and the receiver’s components after the high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter in the transmitter, [c] with the transmitter filter positioned between the HPA and the transmitter’s antenna, [d] the creation of the ICSs relies on a power series model describing the non-linear IMP-generating process, including the generation of an n-th order ICS by taking three signals, S1, S2, and S3, and digitally performing multiplication and filtering operations such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n representing an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added). At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as requiring that the signals be “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record does not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to unique inputs, and also considered the inventor’s extrinsic testimony stating that “S1, S2 and S3” serve as general representations and may refer to identical or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The central limitation contested on appeal concerns whether seven third-order multiplications among the three signals must be performed to satisfy the claim requirement. During the trial, Dr. Wells testified, using a Nokia technical document outlining the operation of the accused radios, that these radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells opined that the accused radios conduct multiplications involving three separately identifiable signals, and equated the three unique multiplications from the Nokia document to the seven required by the patent. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that the asserted claims of the ’775 patent were all infringed. J.A. 169. When addressing JMOL, the district court treated Appellants’ assertion that “S1, S2 and S3” must refer to unique signals as waived, because they failed to raise this at the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court held that employing two signals, along with a repeat of one, sufficed to meet its interpretation of “three separately identifiable signals.” J.A. 38. Further, the court concluded that when only two unique signals are used, just three distinct multiplications result, yet this suffices for infringement since each such multiplication corresponds to two or three of the seven enumerated in the claim. J.A. 38–39.

The Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the radios in question utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, they maintain that, during prosecution, Finesse relinquished claim coverage involving two signals by amending the claims from “two or three signals” to just “three signals” in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art, which discloses creating cancellation signals with two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). The Appellants further assert that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications, because Dr. Wells only identified three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished any claim to coverage over two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim that recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse overcame that rejection partly by stating that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the claimed method addresses passive IMPs as well. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to incorporate the requirement of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” a total of seven times, this action does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” relinquishment of claim scope that would exclude products with two unique input signals, such as the accused radios. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, the amendment necessitates the presence of three distinct signals and does not impose a limitation on the precise number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. However, we concur that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications recited in the claims. During trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document to support the position that the accused radios implement the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not demonstrate that the accused radios conduct the seven specified multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. The document merely references three particular multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence indicating any of these multiplications are repeated so as to constitute the seven distinct operations mandated by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells failed to describe how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications set forth in the claims. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to clarify this correspondence on appeal; however, such evidence was never made available to the jury. See Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the document references multiplying the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2—not x1 and x2 themselves—Finesse’s suggested correspondence is incorrect, as it would require, for instance, that S3 be tied to x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios accomplish the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s materials indicate the radios in fact carry out ten multiplications, not just seven. See Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referring to J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (discussing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells ever clarify which particular subset of these ten multiplications in the Nokia document represents the seven multiplications recited in the claims. See Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they show a mapping from the ten documented multiplications to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse asserts correspond to the seven in the claims. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these gaps in the evidence, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios perform all seven claimed multiplications. Therefore, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, since the jury’s finding of infringement is not supported by substantial evidence.

II. Damages

Our review of a district court's denial of a motion for a new trial is guided by regional circuit law. See Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit reviews a denial of a new trial for an abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL as to damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both motions. See J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the denial of judgment as a matter of law on noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
