Case Name: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket Number: 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the decision of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, which denied their motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) concerning noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court's refusal to grant a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court's denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the rights to the ’134 and ’775 patents, both of which generally concern techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. While radios typically prevent signal interference by operating on distinct frequencies, their transmitted signals may mix when they encounter obstacles such as metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors, resulting in new frequencies referred to as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When passive obstacles are responsible for producing IMPs, these are termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, alleging that the use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia radios constituted infringement. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the litigation. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all challenged claims were both valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum of $166,303,391 in damages for the remainder of the lifespan of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and a new trial; the district court denied each of these motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now appeal. Our jurisdiction is conferred by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of a district court's denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) is governed by the law of the appropriate regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit applies de novo review to JMOL denials and will reverse only “if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit precedent). We examine a jury’s finding of infringement to determine if it is supported by substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We analyze claim construction anew, without deference, and review any subsidiary factual determinations made by the district court in connection with its construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at minimizing IMP interference, wherein “signals of interest” are separated from “interference generating signals,” followed by neutralizing the “interference generating signals” with a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a portion of received signals in order to generate a sequence of bits, where the received signals encompass both the desired signals and signals that cause interference, the latter being capable of producing inband intermodulation products within the desired signals; [b] employing one or more decimating filters to extract the desired signals from the generated bit stream; [c] utilizing one or more decimating filters to separate source signals responsible for causing one or more intermodulation products inband of the desired signal; [d] determining an approximation of each of the one or more inband intermodulation products by using the identified source signals responsible for those intermodulation products; [e] using the generated approximations to eliminate one or more inband intermodulation products; and [f] carrying out phase and amplitude modifications on the approximated intermodulation product interfering signals within a closed loop system, wherein these phase and amplitude modifications include implementing sub-sample phase changes to effectuate phase corrections to the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As the party bringing suit, Finesse was responsible for establishing that the accused radios sampled a passband containing both “signals of interest” as well as “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The Appellants contend that Finesse did not provide evidence showing the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During the trial, Finesse’s expert witness on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, opined that the accused radios violated the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document that illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (containing the Nokia document, presented below). Finesse Br. 15 (featuring an annotation of J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, represented by the blue box) operates as a receiver, sampling the alleged “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (both illustrated along the dashed red path), resulting in a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly stated that he correlated the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, both shown along the dashed red path, as explained in the legend’s red box. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). His expert report similarly opined that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel emphasized that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path because it is created downstream of the RF ADC and therefore does not exist at the time of sampling; as such, a signal cannot be sampled prior to its generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Consequently, the modeled PIM path cannot satisfy the “interference generating signals” limitation in the claims. When confronted, Dr. Wells shifted his explanation, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Dr. Wells, however, did not state that x1 and x2 are the same as the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Instead, he maintained his position that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked specifically whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Once again, he did not assign x1 or x2 to either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as the claims require. The jury ultimately found all asserted claims of the ’134 patent infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, and therefore, infringement was impossible since the accused radios’ receivers do not sample a passband that includes the “interference generating signals,” as these signals arise only after sampling takes place. J.A. 19–20. The district court noted, “The ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Thus, if the modeled PIM path qualifies as the claimed “

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system comprising a receiver and a transmitter positioned together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that counteract passive IMPs within the receiver in a continuous, nearly real-time manner by using replicas of the transmitter’s signals, [b] where the passive IMPs are created within passive components of both the transmitter and receiver, specifically after the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter, [c] where the transmitter filter is located between the HPA and the antenna employed by the transmitter, and [d] where ICS generation is predicated on a power series description of a nonlinear process that produces the IMPs, such that generating an nth-order ICS entails, for three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases original). At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable,” but not required to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record does not confine “S1, S2 and S3” solely to unique input signals, and the court gave weight to inventor testimony, serving as extrinsic evidence, that “S1, S2 and S3” could indicate either the same or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The central limitation disputed on appeal concerns whether seven third-order products, made from multiplying the three signals, are required by the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells provided testimony that the allegedly infringing radios meet the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document that illustrates the operational method of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). In his testimony, Dr. Wells asserted the radios under accusation multiply three separately identifiable signals, matching the three different multiplications mentioned in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications prescribed by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referring to J.A. 28416–17). The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In its JMOL opinion, the district court declined Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must necessarily be unique signals, holding the argument was forfeited by not being presented at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court found it sufficient that two signals along with a copy of one signal constituted “three separately identifiable signals” under the court’s claim construction. J.A. 38. It further determined that employing two unique signals results in only three distinct multiplications, and that this was enough to establish infringement, because each of the three distinct multiplications corresponds with two or three of the seven required multiplications listed in the claims. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by performing multiplications of three signals, since, according to appellants, the accused devices utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, appellants assert that Finesse disclaimed claim coverage for two-signal embodiments when it amended its claims during prosecution, changing the language from “two or three signals” to explicitly require “three signals” in order to distinguish the invention from the Filipovic prior art, which teaches generating cancellation signals with two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claim language); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to office action). Additionally, appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications required by the claims, since Dr. Wells only identified three such multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We reject the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage concerning two signals. The examiner had initially rejected a claim that included “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious over Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, succeeded in overcoming this rejection in part by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the invention in question addresses passive IMPs as well, J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did later revise the claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven separate multiplications, such an amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” relinquishment of claim coverage with respect to devices—such as the accused radios—that use two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The most that Finesse’s amendment requires is that there be three separately recognizable signals; it does not mandate a set number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios execute the seven specified multiplications in the claims. During trial, Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document to attempt to show that the accused radios performed those multiplications, J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document describes operations involving modulus and complex conjugates and fails to indicate that the accused radios complete the seven claimed multiplications, J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it identifies only three distinct multiplication operations—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no evidence presented that these or other operations are repeated to yield the seven multiplications required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells failed to explain how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications specified in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse seeks to clarify this mapping on appeal, that information was not presented to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the document does not reveal a multiplication of x1 and x2, but rather the complex conjugate and modulus of those values, Finesse’s proposed mapping is incorrect, as it would, for instance, require S3 to correspond to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications on the grounds that Nokia’s records indicate the accused radios perform not just seven but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (noting p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do Finesse or Dr. Wells identify which group of the ten multiplications noted in the Nokia document correspond to the seven claimed in the patent. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they set forth a mapping from these ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications which Finesse had previously linked to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given this lack of evidence, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. We therefore reverse the district court’s decision to deny JMOL of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as there is not substantial evidence supporting the jury’s verdict of infringement.

II. Damages

Our review of a district court’s refusal to grant a motion for new trial proceeds according to the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit standards, denials of a motion for new trial are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury verdict, Appellants moved for JMOL as to damages or, alternatively, sought a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Since we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the decision denying JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
