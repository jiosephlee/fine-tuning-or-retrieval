Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants additionally contest the district court’s refusal to order a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the decision denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which concern techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Radios seek to minimize signal interference by functioning on separate frequencies; however, when transmitted signals encounter physical barriers such as metal fencing, improperly connected cables, or corroded connectors, they may combine to create additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. If these IMPs are generated by passive impediments, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent as well as claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent by AT&T’s utilization of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability in Nokia-manufactured radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia joined the proceedings as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. The jury concluded that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and awarded a lump-sum payment of $166,303,391 in damages, covering the full term remaining on the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants then requested judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement, JMOL concerning damages, as well as a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now seek review. Our jurisdiction is pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL follows the standards of the applicable regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). In the Fifth Circuit, denials of JMOL are assessed de novo and will be overturned “only if, viewing the evidence most favorably to the verdict, the evidence is so decisively and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that no reasonable juror could find otherwise.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit precedent). The jury’s verdict on infringement is examined under the substantial evidence standard. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). As stated in that case, “[a] factual finding has substantial evidence support if a reasonable jury, considering the trial record, could have decided in favor of the prevailing party.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Claim construction is reviewed de novo, whereas any factual determinations underpinning the district court’s construction are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a method designed to mitigate IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] over-sampling, at a chosen frequency, a received signal passband to generate a sequence of bits, where the received signals comprise both desired signals and signals causing interference, with these interfering signals being capable of producing intermodulation products that appear within the bandwidth of the signals of interest; [b] using one or more decimating filters to extract signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] utilizing one or more decimating filters to extract the source signals responsible for generating intermodulation products located in the same band as the signals of interest; [d] calculating an estimate for each of these intermodulation products based on the extracted source signals; [e] eliminating one or more intermodulation products found inband by using the calculated estimate of the intermodulation products; and [f] carrying out phase and amplitude adjustments on the estimates of the intermodulation product interfering signals within a closed loop, where the phase and amplitude adjustments include applying sub-sample phase shifts for the purpose of adjusting the phase of the intermodulation product interfering signal estimates. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphasis in original). As the party bringing the claim, Finesse was required to demonstrate that the accused radios perform sampling on a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing this conclusion on a Nokia technical document that explains the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, set out below). Finesse Br. 15 (referencing annotations to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated in his testimony that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (“RF ADC,” shown within a blue box) functions as a receiver that samples both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” referenced in the claims (each illustrated along the dashed red path), thereby producing a bit stream. See J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently asserted that he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (“DL(TX)”) reference and mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, both of which are illustrated on the dashed red path, as set forth in the legend’s red box explanation. See, for example, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), and 15009 at 141:10–14 ([Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells likewise explained in his expert report, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest,’” and, “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel highlighted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because the modeled PIM path is created only after the RF ADC samples the signal—meaning a signal cannot be sampled before it comes into existence. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; also see J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Consequently, the modeled PIM path cannot fall within the meaning of the claimed “interference generating signals.” When confronted with this, Dr. Wells responded by asserting, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not indicate that x1 and x2 corresponded to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Instead, he continued to claim, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When further questioned whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, he failed to equate x1 or x2 with either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as required by the claims.

The jury determined all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, and therefore, no infringement could be found because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband containing interference generating signals, given those signals are not present prior to sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and further noted, “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system with both a transmitter and receiver located together, the method includes: [a] generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that suppress passive IMPs within the receiver, in a continuous and nearly real-time manner, utilizing replicas of the transmitter’s own signals, [b] where the passive IMPs form within passive components of both the transmitter and the receiver after the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter, [c] with the transmitter filter positioned between the HPA and the transmitter’s antenna, [d] and generating ICSs utilizes a power series model to describe the nonlinear process of IMP generation, including the generation of an n-th order ICS by, for any three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering combinations such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n represents an integer. See Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added). On summary judgment, the district court interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as requiring the signals to be “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court held that the patent’s intrinsic evidence does not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to unique inputs, and considered inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence, to determine that “S1, S2 and S3” may signify signals that are either identical or distinct. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation contested on appeal centers on whether seven third-order multiplications involving the three signals must be performed to satisfy the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells presented testimony that the accused radios infringed the claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document outlining the operation of the accused devices. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios perform multiplication of three separately identifiable signals and equated the three specific multiplications detailed in the Nokia document to the seven required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. In ruling on JMOL, the district court deemed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals as waived due to their failure to advance the argument during the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court found that employing two signals along with a copy of one signal fulfilled the requirement for three separately identifiable signals per its construction. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court concluded that, when two unique signals are used, only three distinct multiplications result, and this was sufficient to establish infringement because each of the three multiplications aligns with two or three of the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios produce cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, asserting that the accused devices utilize only two signals—x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. They further assert that Finesse disclaimed claim coverage for systems using two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to specify “three signals,” rather than “two or three signals,” to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generating cancellation signals from two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants maintain that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications claimed, pointing to Dr. Wells’ testimony in which he only identified three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage pertaining to two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim encompassing "digitally multiplying two or three signals," deeming it obvious in light of Filipovic and various secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse managed to overcome this rejection, in part, by clarifying that "Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs," unlike the claimed method which additionally addresses passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse subsequently amended the claims to recite the digital multiplication of "three signals S1, S2 and S3" across seven multiplications, this does not constitute the sort of "clear and unambiguous" disclaimer of claim scope regarding products, such as the accused radios, that use two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, the amendment specifies the requirement of three distinct signals, without mandating any precise quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have concluded the accused radios conduct the seven specified multiplications claimed. During trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to support the argument that the accused radios carry out the asserted multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the referenced document addresses modulus and complex conjugate calculations and does not substantiate that the accused radios carry out all seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it only enumerates three specific multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is a lack of evidence indicating any multiplications are duplicated to achieve the seven multiplications required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (as reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three distinct multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications described in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse attempts to elucidate this mapping during the appeal, but such evidence was not before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, as the document describes operations involving the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2, rather than simply multiplying x1 and x2, Finesse’s mapping is defective; for instance, it improperly maps S3 onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios implement the seven multiplications specified in the claims because Nokia’s documentation reveals the accused radios execute not just seven, but ten, multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identify which particular group of the ten multiplications noted in the Nokia document align with the seven multiplications claimed. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Similarly, they do not delineate how the ten multiplications relate to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse asserts correspond to the seven in the claims. Finesse Br. 17–18. In light of these facts, it would not be reasonable for a jury to determine that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications recited in the claims. Therefore, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement on the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as substantial evidence does not support the jury’s verdict of infringement.

II. Damages

We apply the law of the regional circuit when considering a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse-of-discretion standard to such denials. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on the damages issue or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (order denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (order denying new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for every asserted claim of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we also vacate the award of damages.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED
