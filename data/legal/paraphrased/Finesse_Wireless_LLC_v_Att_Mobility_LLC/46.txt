Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court's refusal to grant a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) possesses the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to methods designed to reduce interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radio systems. To prevent signal interference, radios operate on separate frequencies; nevertheless, transmit signals may combine, especially when they encounter obstacles such as metal fences, poorly connected cables, or corroded connectors, leading to the generation of new frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise due to passive obstacles, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated a lawsuit against AT&T, asserting that the use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality within Nokia radios infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent as well as claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the action. J.A. 389–90. The jury concluded that every asserted claim was both valid and infringed, and awarded a lump-sum amount of $166,303,391 as damages covering the remaining duration of both patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants requested judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, damages, and sought a new trial; the district court rejected all motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now bring this appeal. Our jurisdiction is conferred by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court's refusal to grant JMOL in accordance with the law governing the regional circuit—in this case, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit employs a de novo standard in reviewing denials of JMOL and will overturn such a denial “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (applying Fifth Circuit precedent). We evaluate a jury’s infringement determination based on whether substantial evidence supports the verdict. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (citations omitted). As to claim construction, our review is de novo, while any factual determinations underlying the district court’s construction are examined for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a technique for minimizing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” with a calculated approximation of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling, at a selected frequency, a received signal passband in order to produce a bitstream, where the received signals comprise both desired signals and signals that generate interference, with the latter being capable of producing intermodulation products that fall within the passband of the signals of interest; [b] employing one or more decimating filters to extract the signals of interest from the bitstream; [c] using one or more decimating filters to extract source signals that are responsible for generating one or more inband intermodulation products affecting the signal of interest; [d] calculating an estimation of each of the intermodulation products based on the identified source signals; [e] mitigating the one or more inband intermodulation products by using the calculated estimate of those products; and [f] conducting both phase and amplitude adjustments on the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals within a closed-loop system, where such phase adjustment on the estimations is performed by making sub-sample phase shifts to the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As the plaintiff, Finesse had the obligation to demonstrate that the accused radios oversample a passband of signals that consists of both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document which illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (the Nokia document, as shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (with annotation at J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, illustrated in the blue box) serves as a receiver that samples the asserted “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each represented with dashed red lines) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently maintained that he associated “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path (each shown in the dashed red pathway and explained in the red box in the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Likewise, Dr. Wells’ expert report stated, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants highlighted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path, as that modeled path is generated after the RF ADC performs its sampling, such that signals cannot be sampled prior to their creation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Thus, the modeled PIM path cannot represent the “interference generating signals” as recited in the claims. In reply, Dr. Wells shifted his position and stated, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not expressly map x1 and x2 to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; rather, he maintained his previous assertion that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). Upon being asked for clarification about whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he responded, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Despite this, he did not associate x1 or x2 with either the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as the claims require.

The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells equated “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, precluding infringement since the accused radios’ receivers do not sample a passband that contains such interference generating signals, as those signals arise only after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The court acknowledged that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Accordingly, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation 1[a] is not met, since the accused

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver system where both the transmitter and the receiver are located together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that are utilized to eliminate passive IMPs from the receiver in a continuous and near real-time fashion, employing copies of the transmitter’s signals, [b] where the passive IMPs arise in passive components of both the transmitter and the receiver after both the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and its transmitter filter, [c] where the transmitter filter is situated between the HPA and the antenna used by the transmitter, [d] where ICS generation relies on a power series model to describe the nonlinear generation of the IMPs and involves synthesizing an nth order ICS by, given three signals labeled S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering the sets S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n being an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added). At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” but not “unique.” J.A. 115. The district court concluded that intrinsic evidence does not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to only unique input signals, and also considered as extrinsic evidence the inventor’s testimony, which stated “S1, S2 and S3” could generally denote signals that are either the same or different. J.A. 109–14. The principal limitation in dispute on appeal is that the claim requires performing seven distinct third-order multiplications among the three signals. At trial, Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document outlining the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). More specifically, Dr. Wells stated that the accused devices multiply three individually identifiable signals and linked the three different multiplications found in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications recited in the patent claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. When ruling on JMOL, the district court found that Appellants’ argument—that “S1, S2 and S3” must refer to unique signals—was waived, since they did not raise it at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that utilizing two signals along with a duplicate of one counted as three separately identifiable signals in accordance with its claim construction. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court determined that only three distinct multiplications result if just two unique signals are involved, but that this sufficed to establish infringement because each distinct multiplication matches two or three of the seven multiplications required by the patent claims. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios generate cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, asserting that the devices at issue utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse narrowed the claim scope with respect to signal number during prosecution by amending the claims to specify "three signals," replacing the original language of "two or three signals," thereby disavowing coverage of two signals in order to distinguish from the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses the use of two inputs to create cancellation signals. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants claim that no reasonable jury could find the accused radios conduct the seven multiplications claimed, since Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope concerning two signals. The examiner had initially rejected a claim that included the phrase “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious over Filipovic in combination with other references, as noted at J.A. 1006–07. Finesse overcame this rejection in part by stating that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which the claimed method covers, referencing J.A. 985 and the Notice of Allowance at J.A. 941. While Finesse did amend the claims to specifically recite digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” performed in seven multiplication steps, such an amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope with respect to devices—including the accused radios—that utilize two distinct input signals, citing Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019) and J.A. 110–11. The amendment merely necessitates the presence of three separately identifiable signals, without mandating any set number of unique input signals, as supported at J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications specified in the claims. During trial, Dr. Wells based his testimony on a Nokia document to assert the accused devices perform these claimed multiplications, as indicated at J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12 and J.A. 26485–86. However, this document references modulus and complex conjugate operations and fails to show that the accused radios actually implement all seven multiplications described in the claims, as indicated at J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it describes three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no indication in the evidence that these multiplications are repeated to achieve the sevenfold multiplication required by claim limitation 1[d], see J.A. 26486 (shown below).

In addition, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony detailing how the three separate multiplications he identified correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referring to J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse attempts to elucidate this correspondence on appeal, the underlying evidence was not introduced before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Further, since the referenced document describes operations involving the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2, rather than multiplying x1 and x2 themselves, the mapping put forth by Finesse is erroneous. For instance, it necessitates that S3 be associated simultaneously with x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications, citing Nokia's records as proof that the devices actually perform not only seven, but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (regarding p(n) = a0 through a9). Finesse, nor Dr. Wells, at any point, specify which subset of these ten listed multiplications in the Nokia document are equivalent to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they present any mapping that connects the ten multiplications described in Nokia's documentation to the three distinct multiplications Finesse equated to the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these deficiencies, it would not be reasonable for a jury to determine that the accused radios practice the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s infringement finding lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

Our review of a district court’s decision to deny a motion for a new trial is governed by the law of the regional circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, such denials are reviewed for an abuse of discretion. Id. (citing relevant Fifth Circuit authority). After the jury delivered its verdict, Appellants sought JMOL with respect to damages, or alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both applications. J.A. 42–57 (JMOL on damages denied); J.A. 58–65 (new trial on damages denied). Because we have reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the denial of the JMOL regarding noninfringement and set aside the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
