Case Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Case Number: No. 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement for the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. In addition, Appellants contest the district court’s decision denying their request for a new trial on damages. For the reasons set forth below, we overturn the order denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds ownership of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Typically, radios prevent signal interference by operating at separate frequencies; however, when transmitted signals interact with obstacles—such as metal fences, degraded cable connections, or corroded connectors—they may mix to generate additional frequencies referred to as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs arise due to passive obstructions, they are designated as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through AT&T’s implementation of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality within Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia joined the case as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. A jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and granted a lump-sum damages award of $166,303,391 for the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants requested judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement and on damages, as well as a new trial; the district court rejected all such motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal. Our jurisdiction is invoked pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We evaluate a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL according to the governing law of the applicable regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). In the Fifth Circuit, such denials are assessed de novo and may be reversed "only if, when considering the evidence most favorably to the prevailing verdict, it so compellingly and overwhelmingly favors one party that the court concludes no reasonable jury could reach a different result." Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit precedent). Our review of a jury’s infringement determination is for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). A factual finding meets the substantial evidence standard if, in view of the trial evidence, a reasonable jury could have reached the winning party’s conclusion. Id. (internal citations omitted). We review claim construction afresh, without deference, while we evaluate any factual determinations that underpin the district court's claim construction for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM arises from mechanical problems within the radio, whereas external PIM results from influences located outside of the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at minimizing IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling a selected frequency passband of incoming signals at a higher-than-necessary rate to generate a digital bit stream, where these incoming signals consist of both desired “signals of interest” and undesired “interference generating signals,” and the interference generating signals are capable of producing intermodulation products that fall within the same band as the signals of interest; [b] using one or more decimating filters to extract the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] using one or more decimating filters to isolate source signals responsible for producing intermodulation products within the passband of the signals of interest; [d] determining an estimated value of each such intermodulation product from the isolated source signals; [e] employing these intermodulation product estimates to remove one or more inband intermodulation products; and [f] adjusting the phase and amplitude of the intermodulation product estimate signals iteratively through a closed-loop method, where this process includes applying sub-sample phase adjustments to the estimations of the intermodulation product interference signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases retained). As the party bringing suit, Finesse had the obligation to establish that the radios accused of infringement actually sample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not demonstrate that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document explaining the operation of the accused radios to support Finesse’s claim that these radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells explained that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), indicated in a blue box, acts as a receiver which samples both the specified “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” (both shown in a dashed red path), resulting in a bit stream. See J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently mapped the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, both located on the dashed red path as described in the red box below the legend. Refer, for example, to J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”); J.A. 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”); and J.A. 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In his expert report, Dr. Wells similarly opined: “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest,’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel noted that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path because this path is created only after the RF ADC performs its sampling, meaning the signals cannot be sampled before they exist. See J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This would mean the modeled PIM path does not qualify as the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells altered his testimony to say, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not specify that x1 and x2 represented the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” but instead reiterated that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”); J.A. 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned to confirm whether his testimony meant “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells stated, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, he did not equate x1 or x2 to the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as delineated in the claims.

The jury determined all asserted claims of the ’134 patent to be infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants asserted that Dr. Wells assigned the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path and argued that there could not be infringement since the receiver in the accused radios cannot sample a received signal passband containing interference generating signals, as such signals are generated only after the sampling operation. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized, “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Thus, if the modeled

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver that is co-located with a transmitter, the described method includes: [a] creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) for ongoing, near real-time elimination of passive IMPs in the receiver, employing duplicates of the transmitter’s own signals; [b] where the passive IMPs arise within passive components of the transmitter and receiver subsequent to the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter; [c] where the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the antenna used by the transmitter; [d] where the generation of ICSs relies on a power series framework describing the non-linear creation of IMPs, and the process for generating an n-th order ICS involves, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, the digital multiplication and filtering of combinations S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n denotes an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). On summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable,” not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that the intrinsic record does not confine “S1, S2 and S3” to unique inputs, and also accepted extrinsic testimony from the inventor indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” are generic representations of signals and may be the same or different. J.A. 109–14. The main claim limitation at issue on appeal concerns the requirement that seven third order multiplications involving the three signals must be performed to fulfill the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios infringe the claims of the ’775 patent, basing his opinion on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of these radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells stated that the accused devices multiply three separately identifiable signals, correlating the three distinct multiplications identified in the Nokia document with the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, the district court dismissed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals on the ground of waiver, since this argument was not presented during Rule 50(a) proceedings. J.A. 38, 40. The court also held that use of two signals plus a duplicate of one satisfied its construction requiring three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Moreover, the court found that only three distinct multiplications result when two unique signals are used, but deemed this sufficient for infringement because each multiplication corresponds to two or three of the seven that are claimed. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the radios at issue actually utilize only two signals: x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished any claim to two-signal embodiments during prosecution when it amended the claims to specify “three signals” instead of “two or three signals” in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art reference, which describes the creation of cancellation signals based on two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Furthermore, Appellants claim that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications recited in the claims because Dr. Wells only identified three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding two signals. The examiner had previously rejected a claim that included “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references, see J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, was able to overcome this rejection partly by arguing that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which the asserted method claims also to address. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Although Finesse subsequently amended the claims to introduce a limitation requiring the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplication steps, such an amendment does not constitute the kind of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope that would exclude products—such as the accused radios—that use two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, Finesse’s claim amendment necessitates the presence of three individually identifiable signals, but it does not dictate a particular quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that the evidence could not support a finding by a reasonable jury that the accused radios execute all seven multiplications claimed. During trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to suggest the accused radios performed the necessary multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, this document addresses operations involving modulus and complex conjugate functions, rather than the seven specific multiplications claimed. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it lists only three separate multiplication operations—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no indication that any of these multiplications are repeated in such a way that all seven required multiplications in limitation 1[d] are actually performed. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three distinct multiplications relate to the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse attempts on appeal to clarify this correspondence, that explanation was not presented to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, the referenced document does not describe multiplying x1 and x2, but rather discusses their complex conjugate and modulus, making Finesse’s mapping problematic because it assumes, for example, that S3 is associated with the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse argued that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation demonstrates the radios actually perform ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referencing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (noting p(n) = a0 through a9). Finesse and Dr. Wells never clarify which specific set of the ten multiplications in Nokia’s document corresponds with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they establish a connection from the ten documented multiplications to the three unique multiplications that Finesse alleges relate to the seven at issue. Finesse Br. 17–18. On these facts, no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications as claimed. We therefore reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, finding the jury’s infringement determination lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

A district court’s decision to deny a motion for a new trial is reviewed under the regional circuit's standard. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, denials of motions for a new trial are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (applying Fifth Circuit precedent). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement regarding all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we set aside the order denying JMOL of noninfringement and annul the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
