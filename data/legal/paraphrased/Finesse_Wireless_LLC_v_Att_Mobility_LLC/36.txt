Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Author: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) in their favor on noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court's rejection of their request for a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court's denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Radios prevent interference by utilizing separate frequencies; however, their transmitted signals may mix when they encounter impediments—such as metallic fences, improperly connected cables, or corroded connectors—which results in the creation of additional frequencies termed “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise from passive obstructions, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse filed suit against AT&T, asserting that AT&T’s utilization of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality within Nokia radios infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the proceedings. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted patent claims were both valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum of $166,303,391 in damages for the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants subsequently filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and for a new trial; the district court denied all these motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal. This court has jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL by applying the legal standard of the relevant regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, a denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo, and reversal is appropriate “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit authority). For jury findings of infringement, we apply the substantial evidence standard. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (quoting internal citations omitted). Internal PIM arises from mechanical problems within the radio, whereas external PIM is attributed to sources outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24. When reviewing claim construction, we do so de novo, and examine any subsidiary factual determinations made by the district court for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a technique aimed at diminishing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently negating the “interference generating signals” through use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling, at a selected frequency, the passband of incoming signals in order to produce a bitstream, where these incoming signals comprise both signals of interest and signals that cause interference, the latter being capable of generating intermodulation products that fall within the bandwidth of the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bitstream through the use of one or more decimating filters; [c] isolating the original signals responsible for producing intermodulation products within the passband of the signals of interest by applying one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimate for each of these intermodulation products based on the respective source signals that generate them; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products by using their estimated values; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude modifications on the estimates of the intermodulation product interference signals in a closed-loop process, which includes implementing sub-sample phase changes for phase adjustment on those estimations. See id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As plaintiff, Finesse carried the responsibility of demonstrating that the radios in question sample a passband comprising both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). The Appellants contend that Finesse did not sufficiently establish that the accused radios capture both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this position. At trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, the infringement expert presented by Finesse, asserted that the accused radios infringed the relevant claims of the ’134 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document which outlines the functioning of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, set forth below). Finesse Br. 15 (providing annotated J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, highlighted in the blue box) operates as a receiver which samples both the alleged “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each illustrated along the dashed red line), in order to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells maintained that the “signals of interest” correspond to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, while the “interference generating signals” map to the modeled PIM path; both are depicted along the dashed red path, as detailed in the legend within the red box. See, for example, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Similarly, in Dr. Wells’ expert report, he stated that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel noted that the RF ADC is incapable of sampling the modeled PIM path, as that signal is created downstream from the RF ADC, following the sampling stage, and, consequently, signals cannot be sampled before their creation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This would mean that the modeled PIM path could not constitute the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his stance, claiming, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Dr. Wells did not actually state that x1 and x2 aligned with either the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals”; rather, he continued asserting that the “two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned as to whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells responded, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. However, he did not equate x1 or x2 with the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” contrary to the claim requirements. 

The jury concluded that every asserted claim of the ’134 patent was infringed. J.A. 169. At the judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) stage, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells equated the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, thus precluding a finding of infringement because the receiver in the accused radios cannot sample a passband of received signals that contains “interference generating signals” when those signals are only produced subsequent to sampling. J.A. 19–20. The court noted that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and also that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver system where both the transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to suppress passive IMPs within the receiver, continually and almost in real time, by employing replicas of the transmitter's signals, [b] where the passive IMPs arise from passive components of the transmitter as well as from components of the receiver, specifically downstream of a high power amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter in the transmitter, [c] with the transmitter filter positioned between the HPA and an antenna utilized by the transmitter, [d] and where the generation of ICSs follows a power series characterization of a nonlinear process resulting in the IMPs, and involves the production of an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering combinations such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases preserved). 

During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable,” without requiring them to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that intrinsic evidence does not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to only unique input signals and gave weight to inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence, that these designations can reference signals that may be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. The primary claim limitation contested on appeal concerns the requirement that seven third-order multiplications among the three signals are necessary to satisfy the claim. 

At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document detailing the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). In particular, Dr. Wells explained that the accused radios perform multiplications involving three separately identifiable signals and equated the three distinct multiplications from the Nokia documentation with the seven multiplications enumerated in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. In ruling on JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals as waived, noting the argument was not raised at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that employing two signals in addition to a duplicate of one of them was adequate to fulfill the definition of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court held that the use of two unique signals results in only three different multiplications, and this suffices to establish infringement because each of the three distinct multiplications is equivalent to two or three of the seven multiplications set forth in the claims. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, on the grounds that only two signals, x1 and x2, are utilized in these radios. Appellants Br. 49–55. They further assert that Finesse disclaimed claim scope relating to two signals when, during prosecution, it amended the claims to require “three signals” rather than “two or three signals” in order to distinguish from the Filipovic prior art reference, which disclosed the creation of cancellation signals using two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants maintain that there was insufficient evidence for a reasonable jury to conclude that the accused radios conduct the seven claimed multiplications, as Dr. Wells only identified three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We reject the contention that Finesse gave up claim scope covering two signals. The examiner had deemed a claim that recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious in light of Filipovic and other references, J.A. 1006–07; nevertheless, Finesse overcame that rejection in part by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not extend to passive IMPs addressed by the method as claimed. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Finesse further amended the claims to include the requirement of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, but this does not amount to the kind of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of scope over devices—such as the accused radios—utilizing only two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. Finesse’s amendment, at most, imposes a requirement for three individually distinct signals without specifying an exact number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios perform the seven required multiplications. During trial, Dr. Wells presented a Nokia document to support that the accused radios carried out the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document addresses modulus and complex conjugate operations, and fails to establish that the accused radios conduct the seven specified multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it only identifies three discrete multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is an absence of evidence demonstrating that any multiplications are repeated so as to total the seven recited in limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (as reproduced below).

In addition, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications set out in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse attempts to clarify this correlation on appeal, this material was never provided to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the referenced document does not describe multiplication of x1 and x2, but rather refers to their complex conjugates and modulus values, Finesse's proposed mapping is incorrect because, for instance, it would require S3 to be associated with the signal x2, the complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications claimed because Nokia’s documents indicate that the accused radios actually perform ten multiplications in total. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells clarify which subset of these ten multiplications in the Nokia documentation correspond to the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Likewise, no mapping is provided from the ten identified multiplications to the three specific multiplications that Finesse equated with the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, a reasonable jury could not conclude that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the verdict of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

Our review of the district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial follows the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit evaluates such denials for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury rendered its verdict, Appellants sought JMOL regarding damages, or alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we also set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law regarding noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
