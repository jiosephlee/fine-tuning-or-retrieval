Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court’s rejection of their request for a new trial on the issue of damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. While radios typically prevent signal interference by transmitting on separate frequencies, their signals may interact in the presence of obstacles—such as metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors—producing new frequencies identified as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs are generated due to non-electronic obstacles, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought a lawsuit against AT&T, alleging that AT&T infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent by implementing the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the case. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, awarding $166,303,391 as a lump-sum for the remainder of the patents’ term. J.A. 169–71. Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement and damages, as well as a new trial; the district court denied these motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now bring this appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess the district court's refusal to grant JMOL in accordance with the precedent of the pertinent regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit conducts a de novo review of JMOL denials, and will only overturn the decision "if, considering the evidence in the manner most favorable to the verdict, the evidence so strongly and conclusively favors one party that the court determines reasonable jurors could not reach any opposing conclusion." Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). We consider a jury's infringement verdict under the substantial evidence standard. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). "A factual determination meets the substantial evidence threshold if, in view of the trial evidence, a reasonable jury could find in favor of the party who prevailed." Id. (internal citations omitted). Internal PIM arises from mechanical problems within the radio itself, whereas external PIM results from factors outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24. We review claim construction without deference, but evaluate any factual underpinnings of the district court’s interpretation for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process intended to diminish IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” through a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] Sampling, at a selected frequency higher than the Nyquist rate, a frequency band of received signals so as to produce a stream of digital bits, where these received signals comprise both the signals of interest and those signals that cause interference, with the latter being capable of producing intermodulation products that fall within the frequency band of the signals of interest; [b] using one or more decimating filters to separate the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] employing one or more decimating filters to separate the source signals responsible for producing one or more intermodulation products that occur within the frequency band of the signal of interest; [d] determining an estimated value for each of the intermodulation products generated from the source signals that produce those products; [e] eliminating one or more intermodulation products that fall within the frequency band using the estimated values of the intermodulation products; and [f] conducting both phase and amplitude modifications to the estimated signals that interfere due to intermodulation products in a feedback (closed loop) process, where such phase and amplitude adjustments entail making sub-sample level phase changes to the estimations of the interfering signals from the intermodulation products. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). As the plaintiff, Finesse had the responsibility to demonstrate that the accused radios perform sampling of a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. At trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios infringed the claims at issue in the ’134 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document that illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (citing J.A. 26421 with annotations).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), shown in the blue box, functions as a receiver that samples both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each represented along a dashed red path), thereby producing a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently identified the “signals of interest” as corresponding to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and the “interference generating signals” as relating to the modeled PIM path—both illustrated on the dashed red path as described in the red box of the legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). His expert report likewise stated that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel argued that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path, since this modeled PIM path is generated after the RF ADC performs sampling, meaning that a signal cannot be sampled before it exists. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Accordingly, the modeled PIM path could not be the “interference generating signals” as claimed. In response, Dr. Wells changed course, testifying, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, he did not testify that x1 and x2 represented the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” and instead maintained that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked for clarification about whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells responded, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, he did not equate x1 or x2 with the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” as the claims require.

The jury found all asserted claims of the ’134 patent infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells assigned the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path and that infringement could not occur, since the accused radios’ receivers do not sample a passband of received signals containing the interference generating signals, because such signals only arise after the sampling step. J.A. 19–20. The district court noted that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Thus, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the “interference generating signals,” then limitation 1[a] is not satisfied, as the accused

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver setup where both a transmitter and receiver are located together, the described method includes: [a] creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) in order to eliminate passive IMPs present in the receiver, doing so in a continuous and nearly real-time manner by using copies of the transmitter’s own signals; [b] the passive IMPs are produced in passive components of the transmitter as well as in components of the receiver, with these occurring downstream from a high-powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter in the transmitter; [c] the transmitter filter is situated between the HPA and an antenna utilized by the transmitter; [d] generating the ICSs relies on employing a power series to characterize the non-linear process responsible for IMP formation, which entails producing an n-th order ICS. For three signals, S1, S2, and S3, this includes the digital multiplication and filtering of the products S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n as an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added).

At summary judgment, the district court interpreted the term “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” without requiring them to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that nothing in the intrinsic evidence restricted “S1, S2 and S3” to distinct input signals, and further relied on inventor testimony (as extrinsic evidence) asserting that “S1, S2 and S3” could represent signals that are the same or different. J.A. 109–14.

The pivotal claim limitation contested on appeal concerns the requirement that seven third-order multiplications among the three signals must occur to satisfy the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells opined that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document that illustrated the operation of the accused devices. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios perform multiplications among three separately identifiable signals, and correlated the three distinct multiplications mentioned in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications required by the patent claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17).

The jury ultimately determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court found Appellants had forfeited their argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals, as it was not raised during the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that using two signals along with a copy of one sufficed under its interpretation requiring three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court determined that using two unique signals yields three distinct multiplications, which was adequate to establish infringement, because each of these three multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

The Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals through multiplication of three signals, as the radios in question only utilize two signals, specifically x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. They further assert that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding the use of two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to specify "three signals" rather than "two or three signals," in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art, which discloses generating cancellation signals with two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, the Appellants maintain that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications, as Dr. Wells only identified three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope with respect to two signals. The examiner had initially rejected a claim that included the language “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious over Filipovic in combination with other secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, successfully addressed this rejection in part by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the claimed method also covers passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did further amend the claims to require digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven separate multiplications, this amendment does not constitute the kind of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope necessary to exclude products such as the accused radios, which have two distinct input signals. See Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, Finesse’s amendment mandates that there be three independently identifiable signals, without imposing a requirement for a particular quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have determined that the accused radios carry out the seven specific multiplications recited in the claims. At trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to support the position that the accused radios execute the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the cited document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate operations, and it does not demonstrate that the accused radios actually perform all seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it identifies only three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and the record contains no evidence that any of the multiplications are repeated in a manner that would satisfy the performance of all seven multiplications required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony detailing how the three unique multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications identified in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). On appeal, Finesse endeavors to explain this correspondence, but that information was not introduced to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, since the referenced document does not reveal the multiplication of x1 and x2 but instead describes operations on their complex conjugates and moduli, the mapping provided by Finesse is deficient. For instance, it necessitates mapping S3 to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications based on Nokia’s documentation indicating that not only are seven multiplications carried out, but ten in total. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (noting p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do Finesse or Dr. Wells clarify which particular subset of these ten multiplications identified in Nokia’s documentation match up with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Similarly, they do not delineate a correspondence between these ten multiplications and the three specific multiplications that Finesse related to the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 17–18. In light of this record, no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. We therefore reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because substantial evidence does not support the jury’s infringement finding.

II. Damages

We apply the law of the regional circuit in reviewing the district court’s denial of a new trial. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit assesses a denial of a new trial for an abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants filed for JMOL on the issue of damages, or alternatively, sought a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court rejected both requests. J.A. 42–57 (JMOL on damages denied); J.A. 58–65 (new trial on damages denied). As we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
