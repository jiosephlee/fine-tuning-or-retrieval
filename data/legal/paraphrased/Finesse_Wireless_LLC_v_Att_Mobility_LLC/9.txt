Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s denial of their motion for a new trial on damages. For the reasons below, we reverse the order denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to technologies aimed at reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. Radios typically prevent signal interference by utilizing distinct frequencies; however, when their transmission signals are impeded by physical obstructions such as metal fences, loose cabling, or corroded connectors, these signals may merge to generate additional frequencies known as "intermodulation products" (IMPs). See J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise due to non-active obstructions, they are referred to as "passive intermodulation products" (PIM). See J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, based on AT&T’s utilization of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature present in Nokia radios. See J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the case. See J.A. 389–90. The jury rendered a verdict that all contested claims were valid and infringed, and assessed $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages covering the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. See J.A. 169–71. Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and moved for a new trial; the district court denied all such post-trial motions. See J.A. 1–72. Appellants now pursue this appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the relevant regional circuit—here, the Fifth Circuit—when reviewing a district court's refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, the denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo and may be overturned "only if, considering the evidence in the light most favorable to the jury’s verdict, the proof is so strong and one-sided in favor of a party that reasonable jurors could not find otherwise." Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit law). Jury determinations of infringement are examined for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). "Substantial evidence exists when a reasonable jury could have returned a verdict for the prevailing party based on the trial record." Id. (internal citations omitted). We conduct de novo review of claim construction and assess for clear error any subsidiary factual findings that underlie the district court's construction. See Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM results from mechanical faults within the radio, while external PIM arises from external sources. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at minimizing IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of an estimated calculation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] Sampling, at a frequency higher than necessary, a specific passband from the received signals in order to produce a bit stream, where these received signals consist of both the signals of interest as well as signals that generate interference, with the latter being capable of producing intermodulation products within the same band as the signals of interest; [b] employing one or more decimating filters to extract the signals of interest from this bit stream; [c] using one or more decimating filters to isolate the original signals responsible for generating one or more inband intermodulation products that overlap with the signal of interest; [d] determining an estimate for each of these intermodulation products based on the identified source signals; [e] removing one or more inband intermodulation products utilizing the calculated estimates; and [f] executing phase and amplitude corrections on the estimated intermodulation product interference signals through a closed loop process, wherein such corrections involve applying sub-sample phase shifts as part of adjusting the estimates of the interfering intermodulation product signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). Finesse, as plaintiff, bore the responsibility of establishing that the accused radios indeed sample a passband of signals comprising both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants maintain that Finesse failed to demonstrate the accused radios actually sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment. During the trial, Finesse’s expert witness on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios practice the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document describing the operational features of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, included below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), illustrated within a blue box, functions as a receiver that samples both the asserted “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each represented by dashed red paths) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly aligned the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path; both are represented in the dashed red path and explained within the red box legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Similarly, Dr. Wells’ expert report indicated that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants noted that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path, as this path is created downstream from the RF ADC post-sampling, thereby preventing signals from being sampled before their creation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Consequently, the modeled PIM path cannot qualify as the claimed “interference generating signals.” In reply, Dr. Wells shifted his position, asserting that “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not attest that x1 and x2 represent the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals”; instead, he reaffirmed his earlier position that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When pressed to clarify whether he was testifying that “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells responded, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Once again, he did not equate x1 or x2 with either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as called for by the claims. The jury subsequently determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. 

During JMOL, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells associated “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, precluding infringement because the receiver in the accused radios does not sample a received passband that contains interference generating signals, given that such signals are only produced after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and noted that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path corresponds to the claimed “interference generating signals,” then

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system comprising a receiver, along with a transmitter situated at the same location as the receiver, the process includes: [a] creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to neutralize passive IMPs within the receiver, on a continuous basis and in near real time, by utilizing duplicates of the transmitter’s signals, [b] with the passive IMPs originating in passive components of the transmitter and elements of the receiver that follow a high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter of the transmitter, [c] where the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and an antenna that serves the transmitter, [d] wherein generation of the ICSs uses a power series characterization of a nonlinear process responsible for generating the IMPs, and this step involves creating an n-th order ICS by, for any three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2; S1×S2×S2; S1×S2×S3; S1×S1×S3; S2×S2×S3; S1×S3×S3; and S2×S3×S3, with n being an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases retained). During summary judgment, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable,” and not “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that nothing in the intrinsic record confines “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, and it accepted, as extrinsic evidence, inventor testimony indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” generally designate signals that may either be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation disputed on appeal is the requirement that the three signals must undergo seven third order multiplications to satisfy the claim limitation. At trial, Dr. Wells stated that the radios accused of infringement satisfied the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his conclusions on a Nokia technical document that illustrates how these accused devices function. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells maintained that the radios in question multiply three separately identifiable signals, and he compared the three distinct multiplications from the Nokia document to the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found infringement of all the asserted claims of the ’775 patent. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals, deeming it waived since Appellants did not present this argument during the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court determined that employing two signals plus a duplicate of one such signal was adequate to fulfill the requirement for three separately identifiable signals under its construction. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court determined that only three distinct multiplications occur when using two unique signals, and found this sufficed to prove infringement, as each of those three distinct multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, since the accused devices allegedly utilize only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, Appellants assert that, during prosecution, Finesse limited the claim scope from encompassing “two or three signals” to only “three signals,” thereby disclaiming coverage of systems that employ two signals. This amendment was made to overcome the Filipovic prior art, which describes cancellation signals generated from two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). In addition, Appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could determine the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications, as Dr. Wells purportedly identified only three such multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim coverage as to two signals. The examiner originally rejected a claim that included the limitation “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in view of Filipovic in combination with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07; however, Finesse overcame this rejection partially by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the claimed invention further addresses passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Although Finesse later amended the claims to specify “digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2 and S3” with seven multiplications, this does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim coverage for products, such as the accused radios, that utilize two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, Finesse’s amendment imposes a requirement for three individually distinguishable signals, but does not dictate any particular quantity of distinct input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios carry out the seven specified multiplications as claimed. Dr. Wells, at trial, referenced a Nokia document as support that the accused radios implement the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the referenced document details modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not indicate the accused radios execute the seven required multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it only enumerates three unique multiplication operations—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no evidence of any of these multiplications being repeated so as to total the seven multiplications as required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

In addition, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony establishing how the three separate multiplications are matched to the seven multiplications identified in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse seeks to clarify this mapping on appeal, such evidence was not introduced at trial before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referring to J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, because the document fails to describe multiplying x1 and x2—referring instead to their complex conjugate and modulus—Finesse’s proposed mapping is flawed; for instance, it attempts to assign S3 to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios conduct the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation indicates the radios perform not only seven, but ten, multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (mentioning p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells provides an explanation matching any subset of these ten multiplications in the Nokia document to the seven claimed in the patent. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Additionally, there is no mapping provided connecting the ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse equated with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. In light of this, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. We therefore reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s infringement determination lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

We assess a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial according to the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of motions for a new trial are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants moved for JMOL on the issue of damages or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both the JMOL and the motion for new trial on damages. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Given our reversal of the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we set aside the award of damages.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

COSTS

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
