Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Opinion by: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, the Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas's refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. In addition, Appellants contest the district court's rejection of their request for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons outlined below, we reverse the order denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the holder of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. While radios prevent interference by utilizing separate frequencies, their transmission signals may mix when they encounter certain barriers, such as metal fences, improperly secured cables, or corroded connectors, resulting in the creation of additional frequencies called “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When IMPs are generated due to passive barriers, they are identified as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, alleging such infringement arose through use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia radio products. J.A. 267–87. Nokia entered the case as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and it awarded a lump-sum payment of $166,303,391 as compensation for the future duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants then submitted motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, JMOL on damages, as well as a motion for a new trial; however, the district court denied each of these requests. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal, and we exercise jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We evaluate a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL based on the law of the regional circuit—in this instance, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The standard in the Fifth Circuit requires de novo review of denials of JMOL, with reversal appropriate “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit precedent). When reviewing a jury’s infringement determination, we consider whether substantial evidence supports it. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Claim construction is reviewed without deference, though we review any factual findings underlying the district court’s construction only for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at lessening IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated approximation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as an illustrative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] oversampling, at a selected frequency, the passband of incoming signals to produce a stream of bits, where these incoming signals comprise both the signals of interest and interference generating signals; the interference generating signals are capable of producing intermodulation products that fall within the band of the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bit stream by means of one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting the source signals responsible for producing one or more intermodulation products within the same band as the signal of interest, using one or more decimating filters; [d] calculating an estimated value for each of the one or more intermodulation products based on the source signals which generate those intermodulation products; [e] removing one or more intermodulation products present in band, by utilizing the estimated values of those products; and [f] making adjustments to phase and amplitude of the estimated interfering intermodulation signals in a closed-loop fashion, with such adjustment specifically involving sub-sample phase shifts for refining the phase of the estimated intermodulation interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

As the plaintiff, Finesse was required to prove that the accused radios oversample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse failed to demonstrate that the accused radios in fact sample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We agree with Appellants. At trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, Finesse’s expert on infringement, testified that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document describing the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, set forth below). Finesse Br. 15 (citing annotations to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), illustrated within the blue box, functions as a receiver that samples what are referred to as “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (both depicted by the dashed red path), in order to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells maintained that he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and linked the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, both illustrated along the dashed red path as further explained in the red box accompanying the legend. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In his expert report, Dr. Wells similarly stated that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that the “‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants noted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path since this path is not created until after the RF ADC completes sampling; thus, sampling cannot occur before the signals are generated. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Accordingly, it follows that the modeled PIM path cannot represent the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells altered his position, stating that, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not affirmatively state that x1 and x2 correspond to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; instead, he reiterated that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned for further clarification on whether he was asserting “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet, he did not equate x1 or x2 to the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as specified by the claims.

The jury found that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In post-trial motions, Appellants asserted that Dr. Wells equated “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, and that infringement could not be found because the receiver of the accused radios does not sample a received signal passband that includes interference generating signals, since those signals are not present until after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, where both a transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method consists of: [a] creating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) for the continuous and nearly real-time cancellation of passive IMPs in the receiver, achieved by utilizing copies of the transmitter’s own signals, [b] wherein these passive IMPs arise in the transmitter’s passive components and the receiver’s components following the high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter of the transmitter, [c] wherein the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the transmitter’s antenna, [d] wherein the ICSs are generated according to a power series representation of the non-linear process that produces the IMPs, and include generating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally performing multiplications and filtering such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n being an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added).

At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as being “separately identifiable,” though not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court held that intrinsic evidence did not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to being distinct input signals, and also credited, as extrinsic evidence, the inventor’s testimony that “S1, S2 and S3” could generally refer to signals that may or may not be the same. J.A. 109–14. The principal disputed claim limitation on appeal is the requirement for seven third-order multiplications among the three signals to fulfill the claim limitation.

At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios practice the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his testimony on a Nokia technical document that describes the operation of these radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells stated the accused devices multiply three separately identifiable signals and compared the three distinct multiplications listed in the Nokia documentation to the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found all asserted claims of the ’775 patent to be infringed. J.A. 169.

On JMOL, the district court found that Appellants had waived the argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals by failing to present it at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that using two signals, plus a duplicate of one of them, satisfied the construction requiring three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court determined that when only two unique signals are used, this results in three distinct multiplications, and this was sufficient to demonstrate infringement because each of those three distinct multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven claimed ones. J.A. 38–39.

The Appellants contend that no rational jury could conclude that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the devices in question employ only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. More specifically, the Appellants claim that Finesse relinquished any claim coverage encompassing two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to specify “three signals” rather than “two or three signals,” a change made to distinguish their invention over the Filipovic prior art, which disclosed generating cancellation signals from two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). The Appellants further assert that a reasonable jury could not have found that the accused radios execute the seven multiplications required by the asserted claims, since Dr. Wells’s testimony only supported the existence of three such multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We do not concur that Finesse relinquished claim scope concerning two signals. The examiner had rejected a claim that included "digitally multiplying two or three signals" as being obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse overcame this rejection in part by stating that "Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs," which does not address passive IMPs covered by the claimed method. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Although Finesse also modified the claims to specify digitally multiplying "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in seven multiplications, this change does not amount to the “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope with respect to products—such as the accused radios—that have two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At best, Finesse’s amendment indicates a requirement for three individually recognizable signals but does not mandate any particular number of different input signals. See J.A. 968–69. However, we do agree that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios perform all seven claimed multiplications. During trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to suggest the accused radios executed the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Nevertheless, this document discusses modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not establish that the accused radios carry out the seven required multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it specifies only three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence showing any repeated multiplications so that all seven as required by limitation 1[d] are performed. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

In addition, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three specific multiplications he identified relate to the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to clarify this relationship on appeal; however, that evidence was never presented before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (referring to J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the relevant document does not describe the multiplication of x1 and x2 themselves, but rather their complex conjugate and modulus, Finesse’s attempt to map the claimed multiplications is faulty; it would require, for example, mapping S3 onto x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation indicates the accused radios actually perform ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). Nowhere do either Finesse or Dr. Wells indicate which of the ten multiplications described in the Nokia documentation align with the seven that are claimed. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they demonstrate a connection between the ten multiplications and the three distinct multiplications that Finesse attempts to correlate with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. Thus, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as there is not substantial evidence to support the jury’s finding of infringement.

II. Damages

We review a district court’s denial of a motion for new trial in accordance with the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit examines the denial of a new trial for any abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit precedent). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants requested JMOL regarding damages or, alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (order denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (order denying new trial on damages). Because we have reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is accordingly vacated.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the decision denying JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
