Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. In addition, Appellants contest the district court’s rejection of their request for a new trial on damages. For the reasons that follow, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) holds the rights to the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain generally to techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radios. Radios prevent interference by operating at separate frequencies; however, when their transmitted signals encounter certain obstacles (such as metal fences, poorly connected cables, or corroded connectors), the signals can combine to generate new frequencies known as intermodulation products (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise due to passive obstacles, they are referred to as passive intermodulation products (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent, Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent, Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T for allegedly infringing claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the matter. J.A. 389–90. The jury concluded that all of the asserted claims were valid and infringed, awarding a lump-sum of $166,303,391 as damages covering the remaining term of both patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL on damages, and requested a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal. Our jurisdiction is based on 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of a district court’s denial of JMOL follows the law of the applicable regional circuit, in this case, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are examined de novo, and a reversal occurs “only if, after considering the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence so heavily and conclusively favors one side that the court concludes reasonable jurors could not decide otherwise.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit precedent). The standard for reviewing a jury’s finding of infringement is whether substantial evidence supports that finding. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual determination is underpinned by substantial evidence when a reasonable jury, considering the trial record, could have resolved the matter in favor of the prevailing party.” Id. (internal citations omitted). For claim construction, we conduct de novo review, while any factual determinations the district court made in support of its construction are evaluated for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process for mitigating IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling a passband of incoming signals at a rate higher than the Nyquist frequency in order to produce a bit stream, where these incoming signals consist of both the signals of interest and signals that generate interference, and the interference-generating signals are capable of creating intermodulation products within the frequency band of the signals of interest; [b] employing one or more decimating filters to separate the signals of interest from the bit stream; [c] using one or more decimating filters to isolate the originating signals responsible for producing one or more inband intermodulation products; [d] determining an estimated value for each intermodulation product arising from the isolated source signals; [e] using these estimates to eliminate one or more inband intermodulation products; and [f] implementing phase and amplitude adjustments to the interference signals that are estimations of the intermodulation products in a closed loop, where such adjustments involve applying sub-sample phase shifts to alter the phase of these estimated interference signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original).

Finesse, as the plaintiff, was required to prove that the radios accused of infringement perform sampling of a passband that contains both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend Finesse did not establish that the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this argument. During the trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted the accused radios practice the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document illustrating the accused radios’ operation. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (reproducing the Nokia document below). Finesse Br. 15 (citing J.A. 26421 with annotations).

Dr. Wells stated in his testimony that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), shown within a blue box, functions as a receiver by sampling both the asserted "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" (both marked along a dashed red route) to generate a bit stream. See J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently assigned "signals of interest" to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and "interference generating signals" to the modeled PIM path, with both illustrated in the dashed red pathway, as further noted in the red box under the legend. Refer, for instance, to J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), and 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). In his expert report, Dr. Wells similarly concluded that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and designated “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ [as] the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants noted that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path because that signal is produced downstream of the RF ADC and, therefore, after sampling takes place; thus, signals cannot be sampled prior to being generated. See J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Thus, the modeled PIM path cannot correspond to the asserted “interference generating signals.” In reaction, Dr. Wells changed course and testified, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not testify that x1 and x2 mapped to the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals”; instead, he continued to assert that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned to clarify whether his testimony was that "x1 is the modeled PIM path," Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Nevertheless, he still did not establish x1 or x2 as the claimed “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” per the claim requirements.

The jury ultimately determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. At the JMOL stage, Appellants argued that because Dr. Wells mapped “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path and that path is not present until after the receiver samples, there could be no infringement: the receiver in the accused devices does not sample a received signal passband that includes interference generating signals since those signals are not generated until post-sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court conceded that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver system with both transmitter and receiver located together, the described method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that counteract passive IMPs present in the receiver, doing so on an ongoing basis and in near real time, by utilizing duplicates of the transmitter’s signals; [b] specifying that the passive IMPs arise within passive components of both the transmitter and the receiver, subsequent to the high powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter filter in the transmitter; [c] the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the antenna used by the transmitter; [d] generation of ICSs relies on representing the non-linear generation of the IMPs through a power series model and involves forming an n-th order ICS, where, for three signals S1, S2, and S3, digital multiplication and filtering of terms such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3 are performed, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added).

During summary judgment, the district court interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require that they are “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that intrinsic evidence does not restrict “S1, S2 and S3” to different input signals, and it accepted the inventor's testimony (as extrinsic evidence) indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” simply refer to signals in general, and they could be identical or distinct. J.A. 109–14. The central limitation on appeal is that the claim mandates seven third-order multiplications involving the three signals.

At trial, Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent by referencing a Nokia technical document that illustrates the functioning of the radios in question. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused radios conduct multiplications of three separately identifiable signals and associated the three separate multiplications outlined in the Nokia document with the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17).

The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. When addressing JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ argument that “S1, S2 and S3” should be unique signals as being waived, since Appellants did not raise this issue at the Rule 50(a) phase. J.A. 38, 40. The court further held that the use of two signals, along with a duplicate of one of them, was adequate to satisfy its interpretation of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court concluded that only three distinct multiplications result when using two unique signals, and this sufficed to establish infringement, since each distinct multiplication corresponds to two or three of the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios create cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, as these radios employ only two signals—x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished claim coverage involving two signals when it amended its claims during prosecution from stating “two or three signals” to “three signals” in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art, which discloses generating cancellation signals with just two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (initial claim language); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Appellants further assert that it would not be reasonable for a jury to find that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications claimed, as Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope concerning two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim that recited “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious based on Filipovic combined with secondary references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse responded to this rejection by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the claimed method also addresses passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to incorporate the limitation of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, this does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope covering products, such as the accused radios, that use two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The most that can be inferred from Finesse’s amendment is that three distinguishable signals are required, without specifying the exact quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios execute the seven multiplications claimed. During trial, Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document to support the assertion that the accused radios perform the required multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the referenced document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not demonstrate that the accused radios carry out all seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. The document only specifies three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence that any multiplications are performed more than once to account for the seven multiplications as required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications required by the claims. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse offers an explanation of the mapping on appeal, this was not put before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the referenced document describes multiplication of the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2 instead of x1 and x2 themselves, Finesse’s mapping is inadequate because it relies, for instance, on mapping S3 to x2, x2', and |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios carry out the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation indicates the accused products actually perform ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (noting p(n) = a0 to a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells detail which ten of the listed multiplications in the Nokia documentation are meant to represent the seven claimed ones. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they identify how the ten Nokia multiplications correspond either to the seven claimed ones or to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse purported to relate to the seven at issue. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. Therefore, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent because substantial evidence does not support the jury’s verdict of infringement.

II. Damages

We review the district court’s denial of a motion for new trial under the relevant regional circuit’s law. See Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse-of-discretion standard to the denial of a new trial. Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit precedent). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. Both motions were denied by the district court. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying request for a new trial on damages). Because we are reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award must be vacated.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED
