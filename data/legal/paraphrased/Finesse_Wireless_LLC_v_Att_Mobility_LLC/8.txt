Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement concerning the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. In addition, Appellants contest the district court’s decision to deny a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain generally to techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMP) in radio devices. While radios typically minimize interference by transmitting on separate frequencies, their signals can mix upon encountering certain impediments (such as metal fences, improperly connected cables, or corroded connectors) to generate additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). See J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs arise due to passive impediments, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). See J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, asserting that AT&T infringed claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through its use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature in Nokia-manufactured radios. See J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the suit. J.A. 389–90. A jury determined that all asserted claims were valid and infringed, and awarded $166,303,391 as a lump-sum for the remaining term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and requested a new trial; the district court denied all such motions. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal. Our court has jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

Our review of a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL is guided by the law of the applicable regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). According to the Fifth Circuit, denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo and may be overturned “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit precedent). A jury’s determination of infringement is examined for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). With respect to claim construction, our review is de novo, while any factual determinations underlying the district court’s claim construction are examined for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM is attributable to mechanical problems within the radio itself, whereas external PIM arises from sources located outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at mitigating IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently eliminating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as the representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a chosen frequency above the Nyquist rate, a passband of incoming signals to produce a sequence of bits, where the incoming signals consist of both signals of interest and signals that cause interference, the latter being capable of producing intermodulation products within the same band as the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bit stream by utilizing one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting source signals responsible for generating one or more intermodulation products that fall within the band of the signal of interest using one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimation for each of the intermodulation products from the source signals responsible for their creation; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products by applying the previously determined estimations; and [f] carrying out phase and amplitude adjustments on the estimations of the interfering intermodulation product signals in a feedback (closed loop) fashion, with such phase and amplitude adjustments including performing phase shifts at sub-sample intervals to fine-tune the phase adjustment of the interfering intermodulation product signal estimations. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). Because Finesse is the plaintiff, it bore the responsibility to prove that the accused radios perform sampling on a passband comprising both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants maintain that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios actually sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During the trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted that the accused radios infringe the claims at issue of the ’134 patent by referring to a Nokia technical document that illustrates the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, provided below). Finesse Br. 15 (providing annotation to J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated in his testimony that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, shown in the blue box) functions as a receiver, sampling both the asserted “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (each represented by the dashed red path) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently mapped the “signals of interest” to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path (both shown on the dashed red path as clarified in the red box within the legend). For example, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’ expert report also concluded that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel pointed out that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path, as this path is produced downstream of the RF ADC—i.e., after sampling has already taken place—thus, signals cannot be sampled before their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Consequently, the modeled PIM path cannot serve as the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells changed course and testified, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not assert that x1 and x2 correspond to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” instead continuing to maintain that “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When pressed as to whether he was equating “x1” with the modeled PIM path, he replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Once again, Dr. Wells did not establish that x1 or x2 represent either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals,” as required by the patent claims. The jury ultimately found infringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 patent. J.A. 169. In post-trial motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), Appellants contended Dr. Wells mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, making infringement impossible because the accused radios’ receivers cannot sample a received signal passband that includes “interference generating signals,” since those signals only come into existence after the sampling has already taken place. J.A. 19–20. The district court acknowledged, “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and also recognized, “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver, where both a transmitter and the receiver are situated together, the method consists of: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) for the purpose of continuously and in near real time eliminating passive IMPs within the receiver, this being accomplished through the use of copies of the transmitter’s signals; [b] wherein the passive IMPs arise in passive components of both the transmitter and receiver, subsequent to the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter; [c] wherein the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the antenna utilized by the transmitter; [d] wherein the process of generating the ICSs is predicated on a power series representation of the nonlinear process responsible for creating the IMPs, and includes the generation of an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering combinations such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added).

At the summary judgment phase, the district court interpreted the term “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as meaning the signals must be “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined that nothing in the intrinsic record confines “S1, S2 and S3” to only unique input signals, and it gave weight to inventor testimony offered as extrinsic evidence, stating that “S1, S2 and S3” are to be interpreted as signals in general and can represent the same or different signals. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim limitation in dispute on appeal is that the claim requires performing seven distinct third-order multiplications of the three signals. 

During trial, Dr. Wells provided testimony that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document that outlines the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically asserted that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals and equated the three unique multiplications described in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found infringement for all asserted claims of the ’775 patent. J.A. 169.

On JMOL, the district court deemed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals as waived, on the grounds that Appellants did not present the argument at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that using two signals along with a duplicate of one sufficed to satisfy its construction of three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court found that only three distinct multiplications result when using two unique signals, and considered this adequate to prove infringement, since each of those three unique multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven multiplications required by the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios generate cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the devices in question employ only two signals—namely, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. Appellants specifically claim that Finesse disclaimed claim coverage for two signals when it amended the claims during prosecution to specify "three signals" in place of "two or three signals," in order to distinguish from the Filipovic prior art, which discloses generation of cancellation signals using two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Furthermore, Appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios conduct the seven multiplications as claimed, since Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse disclaimed claim coverage for two signals. The examiner had previously rejected a claim containing the language “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in light of Filipovic and additional references, J.A. 1006–07. However, Finesse addressed this rejection partly by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which the claimed method does cover. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did further amend the claims to specify the digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” through seven multiplication operations, such an amendment does not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of coverage for devices, such as the accused radios, which use two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, the amendment imposes a requirement for three distinctly identifiable signals, without specifying a particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications specified in the claims. During trial, Dr. Wells pointed to a Nokia document as evidence that the accused radios implement the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the document describes modulus and complex conjugate operations, and does not establish that the accused radios execute the seven required multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it identifies only three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no supporting evidence that any of these multiplications are repeated so as to reach the total of seven specified in limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony describing how the three different multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to elucidate this mapping in its appeal, yet that evidence was not introduced before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, since the referenced document does not reveal multiplication of x1 and x2, but instead their complex conjugate and modulus, the mapping Finesse proposes is incorrect as it necessitates, for example, mapping S3 to x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documentation shows the radios carry out not only seven, but ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (citing p(n) = a0 through a9). Finesse and Dr. Wells never clarify which of the ten multiplications from the Nokia document align with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they identify how these ten multiplications relate to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse connected to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios perform the seven multiplications specified in the claims. We therefore reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as substantial evidence does not support the jury’s finding of infringement.

II. Damages

We review a district court’s decision to deny a motion for a new trial by applying the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit evaluates the denial of a new trial under an abuse of discretion standard. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit precedent). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages, or alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Since we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we also set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
