Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 24, 2025  
Docket: No. 2024-1039  
Author: Chief Judge Moore

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the decision of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas denying their motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. In addition, Appellants contest the district court's refusal to grant a new trial regarding damages. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the district court's denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the ’134 and ’775 patents, both of which are directed generally to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio systems. Radio devices prevent signal interference by tuning to separate frequencies; however, transmitted signals may interact upon encountering various obstacles—such as metallic fencing, improperly secured cables, or corroded connectors—leading to the generation of additional frequencies referred to as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs are produced as a result of non-active, physical obstacles, they are specifically termed “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, accusing it of infringing claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through implementation of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability in Nokia radio equipment. J.A. 267–87. Nokia intervened in the proceedings. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and it awarded $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages for the remainder of the enforceable term of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants submitted motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) asserting noninfringement, challenging the damages award, and seeking a new trial, all of which the district court rejected. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the law of the regional circuit—in this case, the Fifth Circuit—when reviewing a district court’s refusal to grant JMOL. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit evaluates denials of JMOL de novo and will reverse such a decision “only if, when reviewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the jury’s verdict, the evidence weighs so heavily and indisputably in one party’s favor that the court determines reasonable jurors could not have reached a different verdict.” Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit precedent). The question of whether a jury properly found infringement is examined under a substantial evidence standard. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual determination is deemed to rest on substantial evidence when a rational jury, in light of the trial record, could have reasonably decided for the prevailing party.” Id. (internal citations omitted). For claim construction, we utilize de novo review and scrutinize any factual findings made by the district court underlying its construction for clear error. See Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM results from issues within the radio, while external PIM is attributed to factors outside of the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at diminishing IMP interference through separation of “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by cancellation of the “interference generating signals” using a calculated estimation of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling a passband of incoming signals at a selected frequency at a rate higher than the Nyquist rate to produce a stream of bits, where the incoming signals consist of both desired signals and signals responsible for generating interference, the latter being capable of producing intermodulation artifacts within the frequency band occupied by the desired signals; [b] employing one or more decimating filters on the bit stream to separate out the desired signals; [c] using one or more decimating filters to isolate the original signals that are responsible for generating one or more inband intermodulation products overlapping with the desired signal; [d] generating an estimate for each of those inband intermodulation products from the corresponding source signals; [e] using these computed estimates to suppress or remove one or more of the inband intermodulation products; and [f] adjusting both phase and amplitude of the intermodulation product estimations through a closed loop process, which includes effecting sub-sample phase shifts as a part of the phase correction to those estimations. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases original). Finesse, as the plaintiff, was required to establish that the accused radios perform sampling on a passband containing both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals." Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse failed to demonstrate the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur with this assessment. During trial, Finesse’s expert on infringement, Dr. Jonathan Wells, asserted the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by referring to a Nokia technical document explaining the function of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, depicted below). Finesse Br. 15 (providing annotated J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), shown within a blue box, operates as a receiver that samples both the claimed “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals,” each indicated by a dashed red line, to generate a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly matched the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path—each shown by the dashed red line, and explained in the red box under the legend. For example, see J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells’s expert report similarly concluded: “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. 

During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel argued that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path because it is generated downstream of the RF ADC, i.e., after sampling, and thus, signals cannot be sampled prior to their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This would mean that the modeled PIM path cannot constitute the claimed “interference generating signals.” In reply, Dr. Wells shifted, stating that “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not testify that x1 and x2 correspond to “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Instead, he maintained that the two signals were the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When Dr. Wells was asked whether he was testifying “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he answered: “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, Dr. Wells failed to associate x1 or x2 directly with either the “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as recited in the claims. 

The jury returned a verdict finding all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In their motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), Appellants contended that Dr. Wells had mapped “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path and, since in the accused radios the receiver does not sample a passband containing such signals (because they are created post-sampling), there could be no infringement. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and noted that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Accordingly, if the modeled PIM path corresponds to the “

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver system, where a transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) designed to neutralize passive IMPs present in the receiver, on a continuous and nearly real-time basis, utilizing copies of the transmitter’s output signals; [b] the passive IMPs originate within passive components of the transmitter and receiver, specifically after the transmitter’s high power amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter; [c] the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the transmitter's antenna; [d] the generation of ICSs utilizes a power series model describing a nonlinear process to form the IMPs and includes forming an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, performing digital multiplication and filtering for S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n represents an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases retained). During summary judgment, the district court determined that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” need only be “separately identifiable,” without necessarily being “unique.” J.A. 115. The court held that nothing in the intrinsic evidence restricts “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, and also gave weight to inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence asserting that “S1, S2 and S3” are to be understood generally as signals, which may be the same or different. J.A. 109–14. The central claim limitation in dispute on appeal is the requirement for seven third-order multiplications involving the three signals to satisfy the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells presented testimony that the accused radios infringe the ’775 patent's asserted claims based on a Nokia technical document showing the functionality of those radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells stated that the accused devices multiply three separately identifiable signals and interpreted the three distinct multiplications described in the Nokia document as aligning with the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found infringement of all asserted claims of the ’775 patent. J.A. 169. On JMOL, the district court found Appellants had waived their argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals because they had not presented it at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court concluded that two signals plus a duplicate of one was adequate to satisfy its construction requiring three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Further, the court observed that with two unique signals, only three distinct multiplications result, but held this was enough for infringement, as each distinct multiplication corresponded to two or three of the seven listed in the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no rational jury could have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, as the accused devices allegedly utilize only two signals: x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants assert that Finesse relinquished any claim coverage for systems employing two signals when, during prosecution, it amended the claims to specifically require “three signals” rather than the original “two or three signals,” in order to distinguish over the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generating cancellation signals with only two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claim language); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (applicant's response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants maintain that no reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications required by the claims because Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding the use of two signals. The examiner had rejected a claim that included “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in light of Filipovic and other secondary sources, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse successfully addressed this rejection by arguing that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the patented method also covers passive IMPs. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Finesse did subsequently revise the claims to expressly recite digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications; however, this revision does not amount to the “clear and unambiguous” surrender of claim scope regarding devices—such as the accused radios—that utilize two distinct input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, Finesse’s claim amendment specifies that three distinct signals must be identifiable, but it does not require any particular count of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios carry out the seven specified multiplications as claimed. At trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document in support of his position that the accused radios perform the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the referenced document only involves modulus and complex conjugate calculations and does not establish that the accused products perform all seven specified multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. The document in question enumerates only three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no supporting evidence to suggest any multiplication is repeated such that all seven of the claimed multiplications are carried out as mandated by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony explaining how the three distinct multiplications relate to or correspond with the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse attempts to elucidate the mapping on appeal, the related evidence was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, because the document does not identify multiplication of x1 and x2 but instead addresses their complex conjugate and modulus, the mapping proposed by Finesse is defective, as it would necessitate, for instance, mapping S3 onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse stated that the accused radios complete the seven claimed multiplications based on Nokia’s documentation indicating not only seven but a total of ten multiplications are performed by the accused radios. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (citing p(n) = a0 through a9). At no point do Finesse or Dr. Wells articulate which specific group of ten multiplications described in the Nokia document match up with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they identify a mapping that relates the ten multiplications back to the three distinct multiplications which Finesse had previously aligned with the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these facts, there is insufficient evidence for a reasonable jury to have determined that the accused radios practice the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent since the jury’s finding of infringement lacks substantial evidentiary support. II. Damages The denial of a motion for a new trial by the district court is reviewed under the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. In the Fifth Circuit, such denials are considered under an abuse of discretion standard. Id. (applying Fifth Circuit precedent). After the jury returned its verdict, Appellants filed a motion for JMOL regarding damages or, alternatively, sought a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both post-verdict motions. J.A. 42–57 (denial of JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denial of new trial on damages). Because we have reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we also vacate the award of damages.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we set aside the denial of JMOL regarding noninfringement and nullify the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
