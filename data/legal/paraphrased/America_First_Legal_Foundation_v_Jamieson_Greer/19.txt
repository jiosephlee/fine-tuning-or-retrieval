Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-5168  
Author: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to investigate if a federal agency unlawfully denied AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court adjudicated the case and ruled against AFL on substantive grounds. We determine, however, that AFL does not have standing under Article III to bring these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC), created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service,” operates as both an investigative and prosecutorial entity. Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Pursuant to the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, where appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board seeking either corrective measures as outlined in 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary actions in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the procedures for addressing corrective action in cases of prohibited personnel practices, including requirements for updating the party who submitted the complaint regarding the status of the investigation. See, for example, 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for certain additional types of government employee misconduct. Pertinent here, this section directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," referring to withholdings that contravene the Freedom of Information Act. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases of such alleged FOIA violations, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes an independent section addressing OSC enforcement. According to this provision, where a court (1) directs the release of agency records that were wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorney’s fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

AFL, in its pursuit to examine judicial recusals, sought access from the Department of Justice to specific emails exchanged between an external lawyer and DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division rejected its request, citing as justification—questionably—that employee emails are not searched for relevant records unless the employee agrees. Subsequently, AFL initiated litigation against DOJ under FOIA. In the course of that action, DOJ has provided, and remains in the process of providing, documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ's document production is not presently before the Court.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee emails without their approval. It cited section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC responded that it lacked the authority to pursue an investigation because the three conditions necessary to initiate an inquiry under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. OSC maintained that the alleged FOIA policy did not trigger its investigatory powers, contending that section 1216 did not require an investigation if FOIA itself did not.

The district court partially found in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action for failure to state a claim. It determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, according to the district court, OSC’s refusal to investigate rested on a legal mistake. Id. However, the court declined to compel OSC to undertake an investigation into any FOIA policy, holding that such a determination falls within OSC’s discretion under section 1216(c). Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for further action. Id. Following OSC’s renewed refusal to investigate, AFL requested entry of final judgment, resulting in the district court’s dismissal of its remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and we review the case de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary for us to determine whether AFL met the criteria for Article III standing, as this issue affects the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). The parties did not originally discuss standing; therefore, we directed them to provide additional briefing on the matter.

Article III of the Constitution confines the exercise of federal “judicial Power” to the resolution of specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” See U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises out of this case-or-controversy limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to establish standing under Article III, it must demonstrate that it has sustained an injury attributable to the defendant, which is likely to be remedied by a favorable judicial outcome. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The injury must represent “an invasion of a legally protected interest,” one that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent,” rather than “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the stage of considering a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must assert factual allegations that plausibly support every component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL contends that OSC breached what it asserts is a procedural right to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish standing under Article III. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific informational injuries: First, the Department of Justice failed to provide AFL with records it had sought through FOIA; second, OSC did not furnish the notices and other information mandated for an investigation under section 1214.

AFL attempts to take advantage of the more lenient standing standards that apply in instances involving claimed procedural injuries by presenting its argument in this way. According to Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Therefore, in these circumstances, plaintiffs must demonstrate that the breach of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet both these prerequisites as well as a relaxed approach to redressability. Since AFL does not satisfy even the eased requirements for procedural-injury standing, it is unnecessary to resolve whether the alleged injuries should be characterized as procedural or substantive.

AFL’s primary standing argument asserts that OSC’s alleged failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 deprived AFL of records sought through FOIA. However, this assertion is undermined by insurmountable issues with causation and redressability. First, any informational harm arising from DOJ’s refusal to release records under FOIA can only be directly attributed to DOJ, not OSC. Furthermore, any purported link between OSC’s decision not to initiate a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is too tenuous and conjectural to support Article III standing. The proposed chain of causation runs as follows: Had OSC undertaken the supposed obligatory investigation under section 1216, it might have opted to pursue either corrective action pursuant to section 1214, or disciplinary action under section 1215, against DOJ employees responsible. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Afterward, the MSPB might have issued sanctions against such employees for unjustified FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Subsequently, DOJ could have elected to provide the records at issue. This chain relies on suppositions about actions of two governmental entities not joined in this litigation—the MSPB and DOJ—yet, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Moreover, it involves guessing how OSC would wield its enforcement discretion, something generally not subject to judicial compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Due to this speculative sequence, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable court ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the asserted informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also neglects the fact that redressability typically turns on the extent to which a judgment binds the parties actually before the court, rather than its potential to influence external actors. See Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL is unable to demonstrate standing based on its asserted interest in obtaining the DOJ records requested under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—premised on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fares no better. For a plaintiff asserting informational standing to demonstrate a concrete injury, he must have been denied access to information that “a statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, in this case, even supposing section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to look into FOIA violations, it does not confer a right to obtain information. AFL argues that, if OSC undertakes the purportedly required section 1216 investigation, it might elect to provide AFL with notifications and information similar to what is mandated for OSC when probing an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Yet, AFL does not assert that section 1214’s notice requirements must be applied to OSC's investigations under section 1216. In fact, AFL admits that precedent establishes OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during a section 1216 inquiry is a matter of “discretion.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without alleging an entitlement to information pursuant to section 1214, AFL does not suffer a concrete injury supporting its second basis for standing. Similarly, because OSC is not obligated to give section 1214 notice in the context of a section 1216 investigation, requiring OSC to conduct such an investigation by court order would not be likely to address AFL’s purported second injury.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
