Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to look into whether a federal agency unreasonably refused AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court resolved AFL’s claims against it on their merits. We determine that AFL does not have Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency with investigative and prosecutorial roles, created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC holds authority to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, where appropriate, may initiate proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board either for corrective relief in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or for disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures set out in § 1214 regulate the implementation of corrective action for such practices, and include requirements for updating the complainant regarding the status of any investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) authority to investigate and pursue remedial measures regarding particular types of employee misconduct within the government. Pertinent to this case, the section mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” meaning withholdings disallowed by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When such alleged violations of FOIA occur, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA itself contains an additional, distinct provision addressing OSC enforcement. Under this provision, if a court (1) mandates the release of agency records that were improperly withheld in contravention of FOIA, (2) orders the agency to pay attorneys’ fees, and (3) expressly raises doubt as to whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice for specific emails exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contended that the Civil Division rejected the request based on what it viewed as an unconvincing rationale: that employee emails are not searched for responsive documents absent the employee's consent. Subsequently, AFL initiated a FOIA action against DOJ. During that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to the request. Whether this production is adequate is not the subject of the current matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA policy of declining to search employee emails without their consent. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary refusals to release FOIA materials. OSC, however, asserted it lacked authority to undertake an investigation because the three necessary preconditions for an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met regarding the alleged FOIA policy. AFL maintained that section 1216 required such an investigation regardless of whether FOIA independently did so.

The district court partially sided with AFL, but ultimately dismissed the action on the grounds that the complaint failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to conduct investigations into alleged FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to initiate an investigation was premised upon an error of law. Id. However, the court refused to require OSC to pursue an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) conferred that choice to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for additional consideration. Id. Upon OSC’s renewed refusal to investigate, AFL sought final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss its remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and we review the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine if AFL has met the prerequisites for Article III standing, which is essential to the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because the parties did not initially brief the issue of standing, we requested supplemental briefing to address it.

Under Article III of the Constitution, the exercise of federal “judicial Power” is limited to certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing derives from this case-or-controversy limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury that was caused by the defendant and which is likely to be redressed by a favorable ruling. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The injury in question must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” and must be both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When confronted with a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff bears the burden of alleging facts that plausibly support each aspect of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL argues that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, a “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). Attempting to address this deficiency, AFL asserts that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, that DOJ failed to supply records requested by AFL under FOIA; and second, that OSC did not furnish the notices and information mandated for an investigation by section 1214.

By characterizing its claim in this manner, AFL attempts to take advantage of the more lenient standing standards applicable to claims based on alleged procedural injuries. According to established precedent, “[w]hen plaintiffs assert harm from the infringement of a procedural right conferred by statute for the protection of their threatened concrete interest, courts loosen—though do not completely dispense with—the requirements concerning imminence and redressability, yet maintain the injury in fact and causation elements.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs are obligated to demonstrate that the deprivation of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet these requirements, and additionally cannot satisfy even a reduced redressability threshold. Because AFL does not fulfill the more permissive procedural injury standing criteria, we are not required to determine whether the asserted injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial basis for standing asserts that OSC’s lack of investigation into DOJ under section 1216 has kept AFL from obtaining the documents it sought through FOIA. This argument faces insurmountable issues regarding both causation and redressability. Any alleged informational harm resulting from DOJ’s refusal to provide agency records is directly attributable to DOJ itself, not OSC. Furthermore, the purported link between OSC’s failure to initiate a section 1216 inquiry and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is excessively tenuous and conjectural to satisfy Article III standing requirements. The supposed sequence is as follows: Had OSC conducted the allegedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it might have opted to pursue corrective action under section 1214, or disciplinary action under section 1215, against DOJ personnel who violated the law. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have imposed sanctions on those DOJ employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Only then might DOJ have elected to release the requested information. This chain depends on assumptions about the independent choices of two agencies not parties to the case (namely, the MSPB and DOJ), even though “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, this theory requires conjecture about OSC’s use of enforcement discretion, an area in which courts typically will not intervene. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because of this speculative nature, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable ruling in this litigation “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also ignores that redressability depends largely on the effect of a judgment upon the litigants, not whether the reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL is unable to base its standing on the informational interest underlying its FOIA request to DOJ.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing, predicated on its claimed informational interest in the outcome of the OSC investigation, is unavailing. Informational standing requires that a plaintiff has suffered a concrete injury by being denied information that “a statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, assuming section 1216(a)(3) obliges OSC to conduct investigations into FOIA violations, the provision does not provide any statutory entitlement to the information generated by those investigations. AFL argues that, should OSC carry out the purportedly mandatory section 1216 investigation, it might elect to furnish AFL with notifications and information of the sort mandated if OSC were pursuing an investigation of an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not maintain that OSC is required to apply section 1214’s notice obligations to section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes existing precedent which holds that OSC’s decision to employ section 1214 procedures in the context of a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL does not claim any statutory right to receive information under section 1214, its claimed injury is insufficiently concrete to sustain its second standing theory. Similarly, because OSC is not obligated to apply section 1214 notice requirements in its section 1216 inquiries, there is no likelihood that an order compelling such an investigation would remedy the injury AFL alleges in its second standing argument.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
