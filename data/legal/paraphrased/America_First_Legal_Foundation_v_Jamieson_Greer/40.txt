Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to determine whether a federal agency unjustifiably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court ruled against AFL on substantive grounds and dismissed the claims. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) operates as an investigative and prosecutorial body formed to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). The Civil Service Reform Act authorizes the OSC to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board, either seeking corrective relief under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or pursuing disciplinary measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines procedures for taking corrective actions in response to prohibited personnel practices, including requirements for informing the party who raised the complaint about the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to examine and pursue corrective remedies for specific types of misconduct committed by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, it stipulates that the OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—which refers to withholdings forbidden by the Freedom of Information Act. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases of such purported FOIA violations, the OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA establishes a distinct mechanism for enforcement by OSC. According to its terms, when a court: (1) mandates the release of agency records that have been wrongfully withheld under FOIA; (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency; and (3) directly questions whether the agency’s withholding of records was “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In seeking to examine judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice for specific email communications between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected this request based on a questionable rationale, claiming that it refrains from searching employee email accounts for relevant records unless the employee agrees. AFL subsequently filed a FOIA action against the DOJ. In the ensuing litigation, the DOJ has provided, and continues to provide, records responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of these disclosures is not currently under consideration in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee emails absent their approval. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), AFL contended that OSC is required to “conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary withholdings under FOIA. However, OSC asserted it lacked the authority to proceed with an investigation because the conditions necessary to activate an inquiry under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met concerning the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that even though section 1216 required such an investigation, FOIA itself did not impose a similar obligation.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to examine alleged FOIA infractions under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court stated that OSC had based its refusal to investigate on a legal mistake. Id. However, the court declined to compel OSC to conduct an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) assigns that judgment to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for further proceedings. Id. When OSC once more declined to investigate, AFL sought entry of a final judgment, after which the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and our review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine if AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, which directly relates to the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). As neither side originally discussed standing, we required additional briefing specifically on that issue.

The federal “judicial Power” is limited by Article III of the Constitution to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The standing doctrine emerges from this case-or-controversy requirement. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To satisfy Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury that the defendant caused and that is likely to be remedied by a favorable court ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must amount to “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent,” as opposed to being speculative or hypothetical. Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When opposing a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff needs to set forth allegations that plausibly support each required component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation as outlined in section 1216. However, as the Supreme Court held in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” for Article III standing; see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In response to this obstacle, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific, informational injuries: first, the Department of Justice did not release the records AFL sought under FOIA; and second, OSC failed to furnish the notices and information mandated for an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

By framing its claims in this manner, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards applicable in cases asserting a procedural injury. As articulated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these situations, plaintiffs must still demonstrate that breaching a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet either this requirement or the less stringent redressability threshold. As AFL cannot fulfill even the relaxed procedural injury standing criteria, it is unnecessary for us to resolve whether the claimed injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s primary standing argument asserts that OSC’s omission in investigating DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has denied AFL access to records sought under FOIA. However, this theory faces insurmountable issues regarding causation and redressability. Any injury stemming from the lack of disclosure of agency records by DOJ is, by its nature, attributable to DOJ and not to OSC. Moreover, the proposed causal link connecting OSC’s failure to investigate under section 1216 with DOJ’s noncompliance with AFL’s FOIA request is overly tenuous and conjectural for Article III standing. The purported sequence is as follows: Had OSC undertaken the supposedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it could have opted to initiate corrective measures under section 1214, or pursue disciplinary proceedings under section 1215, against DOJ employees who violated standards. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have imposed sanctions on those employees for unjustified FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). This, in turn, could have led DOJ to release the requested information. Such an outcome, however, demands supposition regarding independent decisions by MSPB and DOJ, both of which are non-parties to this case, while “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, this theory presumes speculation over OSC’s discretionary enforcement decisions, a subject courts are typically not empowered to direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given the speculative chain of events required, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Furthermore, AFL neglects the established principle that redressability ordinarily depends on how a judgment binds the actual parties to the lawsuit, rather than on whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot satisfy standing based on its desire to obtain information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s second asserted basis for standing, which rests on its claimed right to obtain information produced during the OSC’s investigation, is equally unsuccessful. For informational standing, a plaintiff demonstrates a concrete injury when deprived of information that a statutory provision entitles him to access. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, here, even granting that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to look into FOIA violations, that section does not grant anyone a right to obtain information. AFL contends that if OSC undertakes the supposedly obligatory inquiry under section 1216, it might opt to furnish AFL with notices and data that section 1214 would require for investigations into alleged “prohibited personnel practice[s].” Still, AFL does not argue that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notice obligations to inquiries under section 1216. In fact, AFL concedes precedent holding that whether OSC follows section 1214’s procedures during a section 1216 inquiry is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without any asserted entitlement to information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury to support this alternative standing theory. Similarly, since OSC is not obligated to deliver section 1214 notices during a section 1216 proceeding, a judicial directive requiring such an investigation would not likely redress the second purported injury to AFL.

III

Since AFL does not have standing under Article III, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to a lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
