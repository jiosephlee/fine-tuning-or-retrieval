Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency had improperly denied AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court ruled against AFL on the substantive issues, resulting in dismissal of its claims. We hold that AFL does not possess Article III standing to assert these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, if necessary, initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board to obtain either corrective relief in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures as provided under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the procedures applicable to corrective actions related to prohibited personnel practices and includes requirements for keeping the individual who filed the complaint informed about the status of any ongoing investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes OSC to investigate and pursue remedial measures regarding certain other types of misconduct committed by federal employees. Pertinently, this section mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” referring to withholdings disallowed by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases involving such alleged violations of FOIA, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were at issue.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA itself has an independent provision for OSC enforcement. According to this provision, should a court (1) issue an order requiring the release of agency records wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grant attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly question whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its efforts to probe judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice access to specific emails exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL alleges that the Civil Division rejected this request, asserting, questionably, that it does not examine employee email accounts for records relevant to a FOIA request unless it has the consent of the employee involved. Subsequently, AFL initiated litigation against DOJ pursuant to FOIA. During the course of that case, DOJ has produced—and continues to produce—records responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s document production, however, is not at issue in the present matter.

AFL further petitioned the OSC to look into DOJ’s purported FOIA policy of refraining from searching employee email accounts without consent. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” concerning arbitrary withholdings under FOIA, AFL urged OSC to act. However, OSC declined to assert investigatory authority, reasoning that the three necessary conditions to initiate an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. AFL argued that even if FOIA did not mandate an investigation, section 1216 nonetheless required OSC to proceed.

The district court issued a partial ruling in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action on the grounds that it failed to state a valid claim. The court found that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to examine purported FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court determined that OSC's refusal to open an investigation rested on an incorrect legal interpretation. Id. Even so, the court opted not to compel OSC to undertake an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) assigns that determination to OSC's judgment. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for additional proceedings. Id. When OSC once more declined to investigate, AFL sought the entry of a final judgment, after which the district court dismissed the remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and our review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substance of the case, we are required to determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, an issue that goes to whether the district court possessed subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). As neither side initially briefed standing, we requested further submissions specifically on that matter.

The Constitution’s Article III confines the federal judiciary’s authority to adjudicating only those matters falling within “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing emerges from this case-or-controversy limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury traceable to the defendant's actions that a favorable court ruling will likely redress. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The asserted injury must involve “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent,” rather than being “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the motion-to-dismiss stage, plaintiffs are required to plead facts that, taken as true, plausibly support each standing component. See Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation as set forth in section 1216. However, as noted in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and reiterated in Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Attempting to address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to pursue an investigation resulted in two distinct informational harms: first, the Department of Justice failed to provide AFL with records requested pursuant to FOIA; and second, OSC did not furnish the notices and information mandated in the course of an investigation under section 1214.

By framing its claim in this manner, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing requirements applicable in cases of claimed procedural injury. As explained in “Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005)”: When plaintiffs claim harm due to a statutory procedural right meant to safeguard a concrete threatened interest, the courts ease—though do not remove—the requirements of imminence and redressability, but not those of injury in fact or causation; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs are nonetheless required to demonstrate that the procedural violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet these obligations, and even falls short of the relaxed redressability standard. Because AFL cannot fulfill the standing criteria even under this relaxed procedural injury standard, it is unnecessary for us to determine whether the alleged harms are properly categorized as procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing asserts that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has deprived AFL of records sought under FOIA. However, this position encounters insurmountable difficulties with respect to causation and redressability. Primarily, any informational harm resulting from DOJ’s denial of access to agency records stems from DOJ’s actions, not OSC’s conduct. Additionally, any purported link between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with AFL’s FOIA request is excessively indirect and conjectural to satisfy Article III’s standing requirements. The hypothesized sequence of events is as follows: OSC’s performance of the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation could have resulted in a decision to initiate either a corrective action pursuant to section 1214 or a disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Following that, the MSPB might have chosen to impose sanctions on those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Subsequently, DOJ might have opted to release the sought-after records. This scenario necessitates conjecture regarding how two agencies that are not parties in this litigation—the MSPB and DOJ—might act, yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, it also depends on speculating how OSC would choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, a decision courts typically do not have authority to direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given the tenuous nature of this causative link, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its claimed informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also ignores the tenet that redressability is ordinarily determined by the effect of a judgment on the litigants, rather than whether its reasoning might influence “actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL is unable to ground standing in its interest in the information it sought from DOJ through FOIA.

AFL's alternative theory of standing, which is premised on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation, is equally unavailing. In order for a plaintiff to establish informational standing, he must suffer a concrete injury by being denied information that a statute affirmatively entitles him to receive. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). Here, however, even assuming that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, the provision does not grant any right to obtain information. AFL argues that, if OSC proceeds with the allegedly required investigation pursuant to section 1216, it might elect to provide AFL with the notices and information it would have to provide in connection with investigating a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Yet, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notice provisions in its section 1216 proceedings. On the contrary, AFL concedes precedent holding that OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without any claimed right to receive information pursuant to section 1214, AFL cannot establish a concrete injury to support its second standing theory. Similarly, because OSC is not obligated to adhere to section 1214’s notice requirements during a section 1216 investigation, a judicial order compelling such an investigation would not likely redress AFL’s alleged informational injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
