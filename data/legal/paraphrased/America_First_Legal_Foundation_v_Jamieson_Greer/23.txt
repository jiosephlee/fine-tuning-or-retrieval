Case Name: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date Decided: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: 24-5168  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency had improperly refused AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court rejected AFL’s claims based on their substance. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body, created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Pursuant to the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board. Such actions may include seeking corrective relief under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or pursuing disciplinary measures in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the process for obtaining corrective action in response to prohibited personnel practices and includes requirements for keeping the complainant informed about the status of an OSC investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes OSC to look into and pursue remedies for certain additional types of improper conduct by government employees. Pertinent to this matter, the statute states that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—meaning withholdings that the Freedom of Information Act forbids. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases involving alleged FOIA violations, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides an independent mechanism for OSC enforcement. Under its terms, where a court (1) directs the release of records unlawfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees to the prevailing party, and (3) expressly raises doubt as to whether the agency acted "arbitrarily or capriciously" in withholding the records, OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific emails exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected the request, citing the questionable rationale that employee emails are not searched for responsive documents unless the employee consents. AFL subsequently brought a FOIA action against DOJ. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has released—and continues to release—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of those productions is not contested in this matter.

AFL additionally requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported policy under FOIA of declining to search employee emails absent their consent. In support, AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which obligates OSC to “conduct an investigation” into arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC asserted it lacked jurisdiction to pursue such an investigation since the three necessary conditions outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) to initiate an investigation had not been met. AFL maintained, however, that section 1216 imposed a requirement for OSC to investigate regardless of whether FOIA independently did so.

The district court partially ruled in AFL’s favor but ultimately dismissed the action on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court found that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to look into suspected FOIA infractions under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court concluded that OSC's refusal to investigate was premised on an error of law. Id. However, the court refused to require OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) confers discretion over such decisions to OSC. Id. at *6. The court therefore remanded the matter to OSC for additional proceedings. Id. Once OSC declined to pursue an investigation again, AFL sought the entry of final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss AFL's outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and this Court’s review proceeds de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are obligated to determine if AFL has met the criteria for Article III standing, as this issue implicates the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither party initially briefed the standing issue, we directed them to submit additional briefing on the matter.

The federal judicial power is limited by Article III of the Constitution to the adjudication of specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing derives from this fundamental case-or-controversy limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to demonstrate standing under Article III, the plaintiff must show that they sustained an injury that is attributable to the defendant and that is likely to be remedied by a favorable outcome. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent,” rather than being merely speculative or hypothetical. Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When addressing a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to plead facts that, if true, would plausibly establish each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon what it characterizes as a procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as the Supreme Court held in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to establish Article III standing; see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC's decision not to conduct an investigation has resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, that DOJ has failed to disclose the records that AFL requested under FOIA; and second, that OSC has not supplied the notifications and information stipulated by section 1214 as part of an investigation.

By presenting the issue in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards that are applicable in matters involving asserted procedural injuries. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under these circumstances, the plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither this requirement nor even the relaxed standard for redressability. Therefore, since AFL fails to fulfill even the less stringent standing criteria for procedural injury, it is unnecessary to determine whether these alleged injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial argument for standing contends that OSC’s omission in investigating DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has blocked AFL’s access to documents sought under FOIA. However, this rationale faces insurmountable issues regarding causation and redressability. Specifically, any informational harm from DOJ’s refusal to disclose its records is directly attributable to DOJ itself—not to OSC. Moreover, any purported link between OSC’s lack of a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with FOIA is too conjectural and remote to satisfy Article III standing requirements. The asserted causation chain unfolds as follows: if OSC had undertaken the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it could have opted to initiate a corrective action under section 1214, or a disciplinary action pursuant to section 1215, against DOJ employees alleged to have erred. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have imposed sanctions on such employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following this, DOJ might have released the records requested. Yet this scenario requires conjecture as to what two agencies outside this litigation—the MSPB and DOJ—would do, but “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). In addition, it assumes speculation about OSC’s use of its enforcement discretion, which is generally outside the courts’ mandate to direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). This highly contingent chain demonstrates that AFL cannot establish that a favorable outcome here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards the doctrine that redressability usually hinges on whether a judgment binds the litigants, not whether its logic could “persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). For all these reasons, AFL’s claimed interest in obtaining records from DOJ through FOIA fails to confer standing.

AFL’s alternative basis for standing—which relies on its claimed right to obtain information arising from the OSC investigation—also does not succeed. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff experiences a concrete injury if he is denied information that a statute grants him the right to obtain. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, in this matter, even if section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, that provision does not confer any right to receive information. AFL contends that if OSC conducts the investigation purportedly required by section 1216, it might choose to furnish AFL with notices and information that would be mandated if the investigation concerned a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not argue that OSC is required to apply section 1214’s notice requirements to investigations conducted under section 1216. In fact, AFL concedes that there is precedent establishing that OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during a section 1216 investigation remains “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without alleging any entitlement to receive information pursuant to section 1214, AFL cannot show the requisite concrete injury for this second standing theory. Similarly, because OSC is not required to supply the notices outlined in section 1214 during a section 1216 investigation, an order compelling such an investigation would not be likely to remedy AFL’s asserted injury under this theory.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s decision and return the matter with directions to dismiss due to the absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
