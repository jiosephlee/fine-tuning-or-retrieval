Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency unjustifiably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court disposed of AFL’s claims on their substantive grounds. We determine, however, that AFL does not have Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) operates as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is granted the authority to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board to secure corrective relief pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures related to corrective action for prohibited personnel practices are described in § 1214, which includes requirements for updating the complainant regarding the status of the OSC’s investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to look into and pursue remedies for specific types of misconduct committed by federal employees. In pertinent part, the statute requires OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, information that must not be withheld according to the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When FOIA violations of this kind are alleged, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were at issue." Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes an independent mechanism for OSC enforcement. According to the statute, if a court (1) directs the release of agency records that were unlawfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees to the plaintiff, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific emails exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division from the Department of Justice. AFL maintains that the Civil Division rejected this request based on the questionable reasoning that it does not conduct searches of employee email accounts for relevant records without obtaining consent from those employees. Subsequently, AFL filed a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ. In response to that lawsuit, DOJ has produced—and is still in the process of producing—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s production is not a matter presently before the Court.

AFL further requested that OSC examine the DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refraining from searching employee email accounts unless they obtained consent. In support, AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary withholdings under FOIA. OSC, however, denied that it possessed the authority to proceed with an investigation, explaining that the three prerequisites outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) that would necessitate such an inquiry had not been met in connection with the alleged FOIA policy. AFL contended that section 1216 required the investigation independently, regardless of FOIA’s specific provisions.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was predicated on a misinterpretation of the law. Id. Despite this, the court refused to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) vests such decisions in OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The case was thus remanded to OSC for further evaluation. Id. When OSC again opted not to investigate, AFL requested entry of final judgment, after which the district court dismissed AFL’s outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and our standard of review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine whether AFL met the criteria for Article III standing, as this is relevant to whether the district court possessed subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since the parties did not originally discuss standing, we directed them to submit additional briefing on this matter.

Article III of the Constitution confines the federal judiciary's authority to adjudicating particular “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. This limitation forms the foundation for the doctrine of standing. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To demonstrate standing under Article III, a plaintiff must show an injury that was caused by the defendant and that would likely be addressed by a favorable judicial outcome. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The harm alleged must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized,” as well as “actual or imminent,” rather than “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When facing a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to set forth plausible factual allegations for each standing element. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL contends that OSC infringed upon its asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation under section 1216. However, as held in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and reiterated in Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two distinct, concrete informational harms: First, that DOJ has failed to release the records AFL sought under FOIA; and second, that OSC has not furnished the notifications and information mandated for an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards applicable to claims of procedural injury by presenting its case in this way. As explained by the D.C. Circuit, when plaintiffs contend that they have suffered harm due to the violation of a statutory procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest at risk, courts may ease, though not do away with, the requirements of imminence and redressability; the injury in fact and causation elements, however, remain unchanged. See Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under this framework, a plaintiff must still demonstrate that the breach of a procedural protection caused harm to a concrete and particularized interest. Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet either this burden or even a less demanding standard regarding redressability. Because AFL fails to clear even the relaxed standing threshold available for procedural injuries, it is unnecessary to address whether its asserted injuries should be characterized as procedural or substantive.

AFL's initial theory of standing asserts that OSC's omission in investigating DOJ under section 1216 resulted in AFL being unable to access records it sought via FOIA. However, this argument fails due to insurmountable issues concerning causation and redressability. Primarily, any injury AFL claims to have suffered by being denied access to DOJ records through FOIA arises directly from DOJ’s actions, not OSC’s. Furthermore, the connection between OSC not performing a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s alleged failure to fulfill AFL’s FOIA request is too conjectural and remote to satisfy Article III’s requirements for standing. According to AFL’s asserted sequence: had OSC undertaken the supposedly obligatory investigation under section 1216, OSC could potentially have pursued corrective measures per section 1214 or disciplinary proceedings under section 1215 against DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have imposed sanctions on those individuals for improperly withholding records under FOIA. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following such sanctions, DOJ might have released the requested documents. This theory presumes hypothetical choices by the MSPB and DOJ—entities that are not parties in this case—and “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it rests upon conjecture regarding OSC’s discretionary enforcement decisions, an area which the judiciary is typically prohibited from controlling. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because this chain of events is speculative, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable ruling in this suit “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” any purported informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards the principle that redressability is generally assessed based on whether a judgment has binding force on the parties to the case, and not whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot establish standing grounded on its claimed informational interest in the documents requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing, premised on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation, is similarly unpersuasive. In order for a plaintiff to establish informational standing, they must suffer a concrete injury stemming from the denial of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this situation, however, even assuming that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not grant any right to receive information. AFL asserts that, should OSC proceed with a mandatory investigation under section 1216, it might opt to give AFL the notices and information required under section 1214 if it were investigating an alleged “prohibited personnel practice.” Yet, AFL does not maintain that OSC is compelled to apply the section 1214 notice protocols to its section 1216 inquiries. In fact, AFL concedes, in line with existing precedent, that whether OSC implements section 1214’s procedural measures in a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Given the absence of any claim to a statutory right to information under section 1214, AFL does not have a sufficient concrete injury to support its second basis for standing. Similarly, because OSC is not required to supply section 1214 notices in a section 1216 investigation, a judicial directive to perform such an investigation would not be likely to remedy AFL’s second alleged injury.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
