Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency capriciously rejected AFL's Freedom of Information Act request. The district court resolved AFL's claims on their merits and dismissed them. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Pursuant to the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the authority to investigate allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board for either corrective measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary proceedings as set forth in 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the processes applicable to corrective action in cases of prohibited personnel practices, and includes requirements for providing updates to the complainant about the investigation’s status. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to look into and pursue remedies for additional types of government employee misconduct. Pertinent to this matter, the statute mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—which pertains to information unlawfully withheld under the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases of alleged FOIA infractions, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides a distinct mechanism for enforcement by the OSC. According to its terms, when a court (1) directs the release of agency documents that were wrongly withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then the OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email correspondence exchanged between an external attorney and the Civil Division of the DOJ. AFL alleges that the Civil Division rejected this request, citing as justification its policy of not searching employee email accounts for responsive materials without the respective employee's consent—a rationale AFL characterizes as questionable. Following this denial, AFL brought suit against DOJ pursuant to FOIA. Within the context of that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—records that are responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s document production is not a matter under consideration in this proceeding.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employees’ emails without their approval. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” when FOIA withholdings are arbitrary. OSC responded that it lacked the jurisdiction to investigate because the three prerequisites outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met in connection with the alleged FOIA policy. OSC contended that an investigation was not required by section 1216 if FOIA’s own investigatory requirements were not triggered.

The district court partially found in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it did not state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court held that OSC’s refusal to investigate resulted from an error of law. Id. However, the court refused to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, believing that section 1216(c) granted OSC discretion over such a decision. Id. at *6. The court remanded the matter to OSC for reconsideration. Id. Once OSC again declined to investigate, AFL requested entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed AFL’s outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and our review is conducted de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine if AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue relates to the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since neither side initially discussed standing, we required additional briefing specifically on that issue.

The federal judiciary’s authority under Article III of the Constitution is confined to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The concept of standing derives from this case-or-controversy limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to possess Article III standing, they must demonstrate an injury caused by the defendant that is likely to be remedied through a favorable judgment. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” and must be both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When faced with a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to allege facts that plausibly establish each standing element. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC breached what it asserts is a procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as established in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and reaffirmed in Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), the mere denial of a procedural right, absent an impact on a specific, tangible interest, is insufficient to satisfy Article III standing; a procedural right in isolation does not suffice. Attempting to address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, the Department of Justice failed to release the records that AFL sought under FOIA; and second, OSC did not furnish the notices and information required for an investigation in accordance with section 1214.

By presenting the issue in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards that apply to claims asserting procedural injury. As explained in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Plaintiffs making such claims, therefore, must demonstrate that the infringement of a procedural right has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither of these standards, nor the eased redressability standard. Since AFL fails to fulfill even the reduced standing prerequisites applicable to procedural injuries, we do not need to reach the issue of whether these claimed injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial argument for standing asserts that OSC’s lack of investigation of DOJ under section 1216 has prevented AFL from obtaining records it sought through FOIA. This argument is undermined by critical issues of causation and redressability. Any informational harm caused by DOJ’s decision not to disclose agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, rather than to OSC. Moreover, any link between OSC’s omission to initiate a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s failure to comply with the FOIA request is overly conjectural and insufficiently direct to establish standing under Article III. The posited chain of causation proceeds as follows: had OSC performed the alleged mandatory section 1216 investigation, it potentially could have initiated corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have chosen to impose sanctions on those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). After that, DOJ might have then decided to release the requested documents. This scenario depends on conjecture regarding decisions by two separate government agencies not before the court—the MSPB and DOJ—and, as stated in FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024), “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” Furthermore, it relies on hypothesizing how OSC would exercise its discretionary enforcement powers, which courts generally cannot mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Due to this chain of speculation, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its claimed informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL ignores that redressability generally hinges on how a court's judgment binds the actual parties before it, rather than on the possibility that the decision's rationale “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). For all these reasons, AFL cannot ground standing on its desire for the information it requested from DOJ via FOIA.

AFL's alternative basis for standing, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the outcome of the OSC investigation, is equally unpersuasive. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, there must be a concrete injury arising from the denial of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, regardless of whether section 1216(a)(3) imposes on OSC the duty to investigate FOIA violations, this provision does not grant a right to obtain information. AFL argues that should OSC carry out the purportedly obligatory investigation pursuant to section 1216, OSC could opt to supply AFL with notices and information consistent with the requirements applicable when OSC investigates a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. However, AFL does not assert that OSC is obligated to import section 1214's notice requirements into section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes existing case law establishing that OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in connection with a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Since there is no asserted right to receive information under section 1214, AFL fails to allege a concrete injury to support its informational standing argument. Furthermore, because OSC is not required to extend section 1214 notice requirements to investigations under section 1216, a judicial directive compelling such an investigation would not likely remedy AFL’s purported second injury.

III

As AFL does not possess standing under Article III, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
