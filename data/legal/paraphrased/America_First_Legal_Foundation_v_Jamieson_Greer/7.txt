Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to investigate whether a federal agency unlawfully rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the case against AFL on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigatory and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). The Civil Service Reform Act authorizes OSC to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board to obtain corrective relief under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or to seek disciplinary measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures governing corrective actions for prohibited personnel practices are outlined in Section 1214, which includes requirements to keep the complainant informed of the investigation’s status. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate and pursue remedies for certain additional types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, the statute mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, refusals to disclose information in violation of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). For allegations of such FOIA violations, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

The FOIA statute itself also provides an independent enforcement mechanism involving OSC. According to its terms, if a court (1) directs the release of agency records that were unlawfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the question whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email communications between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL claims that the Civil Division rejected this request, citing as justification—questionably, in AFL’s view—that it does not examine employees’ email accounts for relevant records unless those employees provide consent. AFL subsequently initiated litigation against DOJ pursuant to FOIA. In the course of that case, DOJ has released, and continues to release, records responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s ongoing document production is not the matter before the Court.

Additionally, AFL petitioned OSC to examine DOJ’s purported FOIA policy of refraining from searching employee email accounts without employee approval. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary FOIA withholdings, AFL urged such action. However, OSC declined to proceed, asserting that the three preconditions necessary to trigger an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met regarding the alleged FOIA policy. AFL contended that section 1216 obligated OSC to investigate regardless of whether FOIA independently required it.

The district court partially sided with AFL but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court found that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to examine alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court held that OSC’s refusal to conduct an investigation was premised on an error of law. Id. Nevertheless, the court declined to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, determining that section 1216(c) leaves such a decision to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC again opted not to investigate, AFL sought entry of final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss the remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and we consider the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue concerns the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither side initially discussed standing, we directed the parties to provide supplemental briefs on this point.

Under Article III of the Constitution, federal courts are limited to exercising judicial authority only in specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing derives from this case-or-controversy limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For Article III standing to exist, a plaintiff must demonstrate that they suffered an injury attributable to the defendant’s conduct, and that this injury is likely to be remedied by a favorable judicial ruling. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The alleged harm must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the motion to dismiss stage, the plaintiff is required to assert facts that plausibly establish each aspect of standing. See Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC breached its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as established by Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and further supported by Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), the denial of a procedural right alone, without an associated tangible interest being impacted, is inadequate to establish Article III standing. To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two distinct and tangible informational harms: First, DOJ has failed to supply the records that AFL sought through its FOIA request. Second, OSC has withheld the notifications and information that section 1214 requires in connection with an investigation.

By characterizing its claim this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing criteria that are applicable in matters alleging procedural harm. According to Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In circumstances of this nature, plaintiffs are therefore required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet either these requirements or the even more forgiving redressability standard. Since AFL is unable to establish standing under the less stringent standard for procedural injuries, we do not address the question of whether the alleged injuries should be deemed procedural or substantive.

AFL’s primary argument for standing asserts that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has resulted in AFL not obtaining the documents it sought through FOIA. This argument is fundamentally flawed with respect to both causation and redressability. To begin with, any injury AFL claims due to DOJ’s withholding of records is directly attributable to DOJ itself, not to OSC. Additionally, any purported link between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s refusal to release records in response to the FOIA request is overly tenuous and conjectural, falling short of the requirements for Article III standing. AFL’s alleged chain of causation is as follows: had OSC performed the allegedly required section 1216 investigation, it could have opted to initiate either a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ employees who violated the law. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have sanctioned those employees for making arbitrary FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Then, DOJ might have chosen to disclose the sought-after records. This scenario involves conjecture regarding the decisions of two separate government bodies not involved in this case—the MSPB and DOJ—and, as “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts,” this does not satisfy standing. FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, it presumes how OSC might use its enforcement discretion, something courts ordinarily do not compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of this speculative sequence, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable decision would be likely to prompt the further actions required to remedy its informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards the rule that redressability usually depends on how a judgment constrains the litigants before the court, not on whether it could potentially influence non-parties. Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL is unable to base standing on its asserted entitlement to information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative standing argument, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the outcome of the OSC’s investigation, is equally unavailing. A plaintiff who alleges informational standing suffers a concrete injury only if they are denied access to information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this instance, even if section 1216(a)(3) imposes an obligation on OSC to look into FOIA violations, it does not confer any right to obtain information. AFL counters by arguing that, if OSC carries out the supposedly required investigation under section 1216, it might elect to provide AFL with notices and information similar to those required in cases involving alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. However, AFL does not argue that OSC is compelled to apply the notification provisions of section 1214 to its section 1216 inquiries. On the contrary, it concedes that existing case law establishes that whether OSC opts to use section 1214’s procedural mechanisms in section 1216 investigations is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no asserted right to information under section 1214, AFL fails to demonstrate the concrete injury necessary to establish its second theory of standing. Similarly, since OSC is not required to adhere to section 1214 notice requirements when acting under section 1216, compelling OSC by court order to conduct an investigation would not be likely to remedy the second alleged harm identified by AFL.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
