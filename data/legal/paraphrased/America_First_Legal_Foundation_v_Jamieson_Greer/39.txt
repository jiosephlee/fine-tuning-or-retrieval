Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-5168  
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency unjustifiably refused AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved AFL’s claims on their merits and dismissed them. We determine, however, that AFL does not have Article III standing to assert these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency tasked with both investigating and prosecuting matters to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to examine alleged “prohibited personnel practices” and, where justified, initiate proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board seeking either corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates procedures governing the pursuit of corrective actions in response to prohibited personnel practices and mandates, among other requirements, that the complainant receive updates on the status of the investigation. See, for example, 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedies regarding certain other types of misconduct committed by government employees. In this context, the statute specifies that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—meaning withholdings forbidden by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When such FOIA violations are alleged, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes a distinct section governing OSC enforcement. Under this provision, when a court (1) issues an order compelling release of agency records that were unlawfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees to the prevailing party against the agency, and (3) expressly questions whether the agency’s withholding conduct was “arbitrary or capricious,” OSC is directed to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific emails exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL alleges that the Civil Division refused the request on questionable grounds, namely that it does not search employees' emails for relevant records unless the employee provides consent. AFL subsequently filed a FOIA suit against the DOJ. During that litigation, DOJ has already provided—and continues to provide—records that are responsive to AFL’s request. Whether these productions are adequate is not a matter currently before the Court.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA policy of declining to search employee emails absent the employee’s consent. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC denied having the authority to investigate, maintaining that the three conditions necessary under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) to trigger an investigation were unmet with respect to the alleged FOIA policy. OSC asserted that section 1216 did not obligate an investigation where FOIA itself did not.

The district court partially found in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC's authority to look into alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, the court stated that OSC's refusal to conduct an investigation was predicated on a mistake of law. Id. Nevertheless, the court chose not to mandate that OSC investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) grants such discretion to OSC. Id. at *6. The court sent the matter back to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC once more declined to investigate, AFL sought entry of final judgment, prompting the district court to dismiss its outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then filed an appeal, which is reviewed de novo by this Court. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine whether AFL has met the criteria necessary for Article III standing, as this directly concerns the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since neither party raised the issue of standing in their initial briefs, we requested additional briefing on this matter.

The federal judiciary’s authority is confined by Article III of the Constitution to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The standing doctrine arises from this limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish standing under Article III, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury attributable to the defendant’s actions, which a favorable ruling is likely to remedy. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The alleged injury must amount to “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When opposing a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff is required to allege facts that plausibly support every element necessary to standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation as set forth in section 1216. However, a mere denial of a procedural right, absent an associated concrete interest adversely impacted by that denial—a procedural right in isolation—does not satisfy Article III standing requirements. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two specific, tangible informational injuries: first, the Department of Justice has failed to provide the records that AFL sought pursuant to FOIA, and second, OSC has not furnished the notices and information mandated for an investigation under section 1214.

By framing the case this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the less stringent standing standards that apply when a procedural injury is alleged. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), when plaintiffs claim harm from a violation of a statutory procedural right that was intended to protect a threatened, concrete interest, courts tend to ease—but not eliminate—the requirements regarding imminence and redressability; however, the injury in fact and causation elements remain unchanged. See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs still must demonstrate that the procedural right’s violation "resulted in injury" to a "concrete, particularized interest." Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet these requirements, and does not even satisfy the more lenient redressability standard. Given that AFL cannot meet even the relaxed standards for standing in procedural injury cases, there is no need for us to determine whether the injuries at issue are procedural or substantive.

AFL’s primary argument for standing asserts that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has prevented it from obtaining records requested under FOIA. However, this theory is undermined by insurmountable issues concerning causation and redressability. Specifically, any informational harm resulting from DOJ’s withholding of agency records can be directly traced to DOJ, not OSC. Moreover, any link between OSC’s alleged failure to investigate pursuant to section 1216 and DOJ’s purported noncompliance with the FOIA request is overly tenuous and speculative for Article III standing. The posited chain of causation unfolds as follows: If OSC had carried out what AFL claims was a mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC might then have opted to initiate a corrective action under section 1214 or institute a disciplinary proceeding under section 1215 against DOJ employees alleged to have committed violations. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Thereafter, the MSPB might have sanctioned those employees for improperly withholding information under FOIA. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following these hypothetical actions, DOJ could have decided to release the requested records. However, such a series of events would rely on assumptions about how two governmental bodies not parties to this litigation—the MSPB and DOJ—might act, and “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, this scenario also presumes how OSC would exercise its discretion to enforce, an area where judicial compulsion is generally inappropriate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because this causal chain is speculative, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable outcome here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). In addition, AFL fails to recognize that redressability typically depends on the effect a judgment has on the parties bound by it, not on the possibility that its rationale “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL cannot rely on its interest in obtaining information sought from DOJ through FOIA as a basis for standing.

AFL’s alternative basis for standing—premised on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation—proves equally unavailing. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, he must suffer a concrete injury by being denied information to which “a statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this instance, though section 1216(a)(3) might obligate the OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not provide any right to receive information. AFL argues that if OSC were to undertake the supposedly required investigation pursuant to section 1216, it might choose to furnish AFL with notices and information, as would be mandatory if OSC were probing a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. However, AFL does not argue that section 1214’s notice requirements are mandatory for OSC during section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes, citing case law, that OSC retains “discretion” regarding “whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in such investigations. Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Since AFL has not established an entitlement to information under section 1214, it fails to allege the sort of concrete injury necessary to support its second theory of standing. Similarly, because OSC is not required to provide section 1214 notices as part of a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation would not likely redress AFL’s second purported injury.

III

As AFL does not have standing under Article III, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
