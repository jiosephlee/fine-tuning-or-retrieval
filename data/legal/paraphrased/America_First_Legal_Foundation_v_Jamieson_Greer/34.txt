Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency unjustifiably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the claims in favor of the defendants on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to assert these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to ensure “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the authority to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board in pursuit of corrective relief pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines the procedures applicable to corrective actions addressing prohibited personnel practices, and contains requirements for updating the complainant regarding the status of an ongoing investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to investigate and pursue corrective measures for particular types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, the statute mandates that the OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” referring to withholdings that violate the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases of alleged FOIA violations, the OSC is empowered to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA has a distinct section that addresses OSC enforcement. According to this provision, if a court (1) mandates the release of agency records that were wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency's withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific emails from the Department of Justice involving communications between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division refused the request on questionable grounds, contending that it will not search employees’ emails for responsive documents absent the employees’ consent. Consequently, AFL initiated litigation against DOJ under FOIA. In that case, DOJ has released—and continues to release—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of these disclosures is not before the Court in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employees’ emails unless they gave approval. To support this, AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC, however, asserted it lacked the jurisdiction to investigate because the prerequisites set forth in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) necessary to initiate an investigation had not been met. Nonetheless, AFL maintained that section 1216 required OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy regardless of FOIA’s provisions.

The district court partially sided with AFL but ultimately dismissed the action on the basis that the complaint failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). As such, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate rested on a mistaken interpretation of law. Id. Nevertheless, the court refrained from directing OSC to pursue any FOIA policy investigation, holding that under section 1216(c), the discretion to make such a determination resides with OSC. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for further review. Id. OSC subsequently declined once more to investigate, prompting AFL to seek a final judgment; the district court then dismissed AFL’s outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and our standard of review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue affects the district court's subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since neither party raised the issue of standing at the outset, we directed them to provide supplemental briefing to address it.

The federal judicial authority under Article III of the Constitution is limited to adjudicating particular “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this case-or-controversy mandate. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to satisfy the requirements of Article III standing, they must demonstrate that they have suffered an injury which is attributable to the defendant’s actions and which can probably be remedied by a favorable court ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must involve “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the stage of a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to plead facts that plausibly support each standing element. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its supposed procedural entitlement to an investigation as set forth in section 1216. However, the Supreme Court has held that losing a procedural right without any accompanying concrete interest impacted by that loss—a so-called “procedural right in vacuo”—does not satisfy the requirements for Article III standing. See Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In an attempt to address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to conduct an investigation resulted in two specific informational harms: first, that the DOJ failed to produce records AFL sought under FOIA; and second, that OSC did not supply the notices and information that section 1214 requires in the context of an investigation.

By presenting its claim in this manner, AFL attempts to utilize the more lenient standing requirements applicable to claims of procedural injury. As articulated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs must nevertheless establish that the breach of a procedural entitlement “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL is unable to meet any of these requirements, nor does it demonstrate even the less stringent standard for redressability. Since AFL does not satisfy even the relaxed criteria for procedural standing, there is no need for us to address whether these alleged injuries are in fact procedural as opposed to substantive.

AFL’s primary argument for standing is that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has obstructed AFL from obtaining records sought under FOIA. This argument is fundamentally flawed due to problems with causation and redressability. Any informational harm from DOJ’s non-disclosure of agency records is directly attributable to DOJ rather than OSC. Further, any link between OSC’s omission of a section 1216 inquiry and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is too conjectural and remote to satisfy Article III standing. The proposed chain of causation assumes that, had OSC undertaken the allegedly required section 1216 investigation, it could have chosen to initiate corrective or disciplinary proceedings under sections 1214 or 1215 against DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have elected to penalize those individuals for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Only then might DOJ have opted to release the requested documents. This scenario depends on uncertain decisions from two governmental entities outside this litigation (the MSPB and DOJ), yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also necessitates speculation about OSC’s exercise of its prosecutorial discretion, a matter the courts typically do not command. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given these tenuous steps, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its alleged informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL disregards the standard that redressability is typically determined by the binding force of a judgment on the parties, not by whether the reasoning might “persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL cannot ground its standing on the interest in DOJ records sought via FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—premised on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fares no better. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff must show a concrete injury resulting from the denial of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In the present situation, however, even if section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA infractions, it does not confer any statutory right to receive information. AFL argues that if OSC undertakes the claimed obligatory investigation under section 1216, it could elect to provide AFL with notices and information that would be mandatory if OSC were instead investigating an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not assert that OSC is required to apply section 1214’s notice obligations to investigations initiated pursuant to section 1216. Indeed, AFL concedes authority establishing that whether OSC chooses to follow section 1214’s procedures in the context of a section 1216 investigation remains “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Lacking any claimed statutory entitlement to information under section 1214, AFL’s alleged injury does not meet the threshold for a concrete injury to support its second standing theory. For similar reasons, since OSC is not obligated to provide section 1214 notice requirements in section 1216 proceedings, judicially compelling such an investigation would not be likely to redress AFL’s asserted informational injury.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
