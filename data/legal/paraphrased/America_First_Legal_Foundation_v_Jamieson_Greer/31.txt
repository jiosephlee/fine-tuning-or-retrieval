Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation filed this action seeking to force the Office of Special Counsel to determine whether a federal agency improperly denied AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court resolved AFL’s claims against them on the merits. We determine, however, that AFL does not possess Article III standing to bring these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to ensure “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, when justified, initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board for either corrective measures in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or for disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates the protocols for pursuing corrective action in cases involving prohibited personnel practices and contains requirements for keeping the complainant informed about the status of an investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to investigate and pursue remedies for specific additional types of government employee misconduct. In this context, the section states that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," referring to improper withholdings under the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). For such purported FOIA infractions, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA has an independent provision addressing OSC enforcement. Under this section, when a court (1) directs that records improperly withheld under FOIA be released, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) explicitly questions whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In seeking to investigate instances of judicial recusal, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice for specific email correspondence between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected this request on the questionable basis that it does not review employee email accounts for responsive documents unless the employee agrees. Consequently, AFL brought a FOIA action against DOJ. During the course of that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of these productions is not a matter for consideration in the present context.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refusing to search employee emails without their consent. Citing section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary withholdings under FOIA, AFL sought OSC’s intervention. OSC, however, asserted it lacked jurisdiction to investigate because the three conditions necessary to trigger an inquiry under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met with respect to the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that, notwithstanding the requirements of FOIA itself, section 1216 did not independently mandate such an investigation.

The district court issued a partial ruling in AFL’s favor but ultimately dismissed the suit for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict the OSC's authority to examine alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate stemmed from a legal mistake. Id. Nevertheless, the court refused to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) assigns that determination to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court remanded the matter to OSC for additional consideration. Id. Once OSC again opted not to pursue an investigation, AFL sought entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed its outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and this Court reviews the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substance of the case, we are obligated to determine whether AFL met the criteria for Article III standing, an issue that affects the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). At the outset, neither side discussed standing, prompting us to request additional briefs specifically on that matter.

Article III of the Constitution limits the federal judiciary’s authority to adjudicating designated “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this requirement for a genuine case or controversy. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order to satisfy Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury that was caused by the defendant and that would likely be remedied by a court ruling in the plaintiff’s favor. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The harm suffered must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When faced with a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff bears the burden of pleading facts that make each standing requirement plausible. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its claimed procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, the Supreme Court has made clear that “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish standing under Article III. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific, informational harms: namely, DOJ has withheld records that AFL sought under FOIA, and OSC failed to furnish the notices and information that section 1214 requires as part of an investigation.

AFL, by presenting the issue in this manner, attempts to take advantage of the more lenient standing standards applicable to alleged procedural injuries. As noted by the court in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under this doctrine, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural protection “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet these criteria, including the eased burden with regard to redressability. Since AFL cannot establish standing even under the more forgiving procedural injury requirements, the Court does not reach the question of whether these injuries are procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial standing argument asserts that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 prevented AFL from obtaining the FOIA records it sought. However, this position is undermined by insurmountable issues with both causation and redressability. Any informational harm arising from DOJ’s denial of agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, not OSC. Furthermore, any purported linkage between OSC’s lack of a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is too conjectural and remote to satisfy Article III standing requirements. The alleged chain of causation suggests that had OSC carried out the supposedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it could have pursued either corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against responsible DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). The MSPB could then have imposed sanctions on those employees for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Subsequently, DOJ might have chosen to release the requested records. This narrative depends on speculative predictions about the discretionary decisions of two agencies not joined in this case—the MSPB and DOJ—and “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also presupposes speculation regarding OSC’s enforcement discretion, which the courts are typically not authorized to compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of this tenuous causal sequence, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable outcome here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL disregards the standard that redressability usually hinges on a judgment’s binding effect on the parties, not on the possibility that the court’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL fails to establish standing based on its claimed interest in receiving the requested DOJ information through FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—predicated on its purported informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fares no better. Informational standing requires a plaintiff to demonstrate a tangible harm caused by being denied information “that a statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) imposes an obligation on OSC to investigate FOIA violations, this provision does not grant any right to obtain information. AFL argues that, if OSC carries out the allegedly mandatory investigation under section 1216, it might voluntarily provide AFL with notices and information, akin to those required when OSC investigates alleged “prohibited personnel practice[s]” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not assert that section 1214's notice requirements are mandatory for section 1216 proceedings. On the contrary, AFL concedes precedent establishing that OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during section 1216 investigations is discretionary. Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL does not claim any entitlement to receive information pursuant to section 1214, its second theory of standing fails to establish a concrete injury. Similarly, since OSC is not obligated to provide section 1214 notices in a section 1216 investigation, an order compelling OSC to initiate such an investigation would not be likely to remedy the second alleged injury suffered by AFL.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to the absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
