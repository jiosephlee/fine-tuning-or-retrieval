Case Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 24-5168  
Opinion by Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action, seeking to force the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency improperly rejected AFL's request made under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court entered judgment against AFL’s claims on substantive grounds. We find that AFL does not possess Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service,” as articulated in Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Through the authority granted by the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the power to investigate “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board in order to obtain corrective relief pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or to pursue disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates procedures relating to corrective actions in response to prohibited personnel practices, which include requirements that the OSC inform the complainant of the status of its investigation. See, for instance, 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue corrective measures for particular types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinently, this section mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—which refers to withholdings forbidden by the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When dealing with alleged FOIA violations of this nature, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

The FOIA itself contains a distinct mechanism for OSC enforcement. According to FOIA, when a court (1) orders an agency to disclose records wrongfully withheld, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises a question as to whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice seeking specific emails exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division rejected the request on the questionable basis that it does not review employee emails for responsive materials unless the employee agrees. AFL subsequently initiated litigation against DOJ pursuant to FOIA. In that case, DOJ has already provided—and continues to provide—documents that are responsive to the request. The adequacy of DOJ’s ongoing production is not presently before the Court.

Additionally, AFL petitioned OSC to examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee email accounts without the employee’s permission. Citing section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” when FOIA withholdings are arbitrary, AFL argued for an investigation. However, OSC denied that it had authority to proceed because, in its view, the three prerequisites for initiating an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were unmet. AFL maintained that an investigation was obligatory under section 1216, regardless of whether FOIA itself required it.

The district court partially found in AFL's favor but ultimately dismissed the lawsuit for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was predicated on an error of law. Id. Even so, the court chose not to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that under section 1216(c), such a decision was committed to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for additional consideration. Id. After OSC once more declined to pursue an investigation, AFL sought the entry of final judgment, prompting the district court to dismiss its remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and our review proceeds de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, we are required to examine whether AFL has met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this determines the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither party discussed standing at the outset, we directed the parties to provide additional briefing on this issue.

Under Article III of the Constitution, federal courts' “judicial Power” is limited to certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The standing doctrine derives from this case-or-controversy mandate. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For Article III standing, the plaintiff must demonstrate that it has sustained an injury attributable to the defendant's actions and that a favorable ruling is likely to redress that harm. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” which is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When considering a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must allege facts that plausibly establish every component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL argues that OSC infringed upon its asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, a “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In an attempt to address this issue, AFL maintains that OSC’s decision not to investigate has led to two specific, informational injuries: first, that DOJ has failed to produce the documents AFL sought via FOIA; and second, that OSC has withheld the notices and information which section 1214 mandates for an investigation.

By characterizing its claim in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the less stringent standing standards applicable to cases asserting procedural injuries. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the procedural violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL is unable to meet these obligations, nor can it fulfill even the more lenient redressability criteria. Since AFL cannot clear even the threshold of the procedural injury standing test, we are not required to determine whether the asserted injuries are procedural rather than substantive in character.

AFL’s initial argument for standing is premised on the assertion that OSC’s lack of investigation into DOJ under section 1216 has blocked AFL from obtaining the documents it sought through FOIA. This argument is fatally flawed with respect to both causation and redressability. Specifically, any harm related to information denied by DOJ is directly attributable to DOJ, not OSC. Furthermore, the supposed link between OSC’s failure to initiate a section 1216 probe and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is overly speculative and indirect to meet Article III standing requirements. The hypothesized causal progression is as follows: If OSC had undertaken the allegedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it might have chosen to initiate either a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ employees who failed to comply. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have imposed sanctions on those employees for unjustified withholding of records under FOIA. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). After that, DOJ might then have released the sought-after records. This hypothetical chain requires conjecture regarding decisions by two government entities—the MSPB and DOJ—that are not parties to this case, yet, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also necessitates supposition as to how OSC might utilize its enforcement discretion, a judgment that courts are typically not permitted to compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). With such a conjectural sequence of events, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable decision here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its claimed informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also ignores the settled principle that redressability generally depends on whether a judgment is binding on the parties before the court, rather than on whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL cannot establish standing based upon its interest in obtaining information it sought from DOJ through FOIA.

AFL's alternative standing argument—centered on an alleged informational interest in the results of the OSC's investigation—also fails. A plaintiff asserting informational standing only suffers a concrete injury when deprived of information that a statute guarantees access to. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this instance, however, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to probe FOIA violations, that provision does not establish any right to access information. AFL argues that, should OSC carry out the purportedly mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC might opt to furnish AFL with notices and information similar to those required in an investigation into a "prohibited personnel practice" pursuant to section 1214. Still, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to incorporate section 1214's notice provisions into its section 1216 investigative process. Indeed, AFL concedes decisions holding that whether OSC chooses to follow section 1214’s procedural steps during a section 1216 inquiry is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL has alleged no legal entitlement to information under section 1214 in this context, it cannot demonstrate the necessary concrete injury for its second standing theory. Similarly, since OSC is not required to supply notices under section 1214 as part of a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling OSC to conduct such an investigation would not likely remedy AFL’s second asserted injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
