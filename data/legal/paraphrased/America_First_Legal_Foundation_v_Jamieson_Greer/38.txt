Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency capriciously rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court ruled against AFL on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency responsible for investigation and prosecution, with the aim of promoting "a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Pursuant to the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the authority to examine alleged "prohibited personnel practices" and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board to seek corrective relief under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or to pursue disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures for addressing corrective actions in cases of prohibited personnel practices are outlined in Section 1214, which also contains requirements for notifying the complainant about the status and developments of an investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for certain additional types of government employee misconduct. Pertinent to this matter, it specifies that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," referring to improper withholdings under the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When such FOIA violations are alleged, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides a distinct enforcement mechanism for the OSC. According to the statute, where a court (1) directs an agency to release records found to be wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice for specific emails exchanged between an external attorney and the Civil Division of the DOJ. AFL claims that the Civil Division rejected this request, citing the questionable rationale that employee email accounts would not be searched for relevant documents without the employees' approval. AFL subsequently brought a FOIA action against DOJ. In that proceeding, DOJ has released—and continues to release—documents that are responsive to the request. The adequacy of these productions is not before the Court in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refusing to search employee email accounts absent their permission. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholdings, AFL asserted such an investigation was required. OSC, however, asserted it lacked authority to proceed because the prerequisites outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) to initiate an investigation had not been met regarding the claimed FOIA policy. OSC maintained that it was not compelled to investigate since the conditions set by FOIA were not present, despite AFL’s argument that section 1216 independently mandated an investigation.

The district court partially found in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case for failure to state a claim. It determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to examine alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court held that OSC’s refusal to investigate resulted from an error of law. Id. However, the court chose not to compel OSC to conduct an investigation into any FOIA policy because, according to the court, section 1216(c) grants OSC discretion in making that determination. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for further proceedings. Id. Upon OSC again electing not to initiate an investigation, AFL sought entry of final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss its outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and this court reviews the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine whether AFL fulfilled the criteria for Article III standing, as this impacts the district court's subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither party originally discussed standing, we directed them to submit additional briefing on this matter.

The federal judiciary’s authority under Article III is confined to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises from this foundational limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order to satisfy Article III standing, a plaintiff is required to demonstrate that they have sustained an injury that was caused by the defendant and that a favorable court ruling will likely remedy that harm. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such injury must represent “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When faced with a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must allege facts that, if true, would sufficiently establish each component necessary for standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural right to an investigation as provided by section 1216. However, as established in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and reaffirmed in Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. In an attempt to address this obstacle, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific informational injuries: first, AFL claims that DOJ failed to furnish the records it sought via FOIA; and second, AFL alleges that OSC did not deliver the notices and information mandated for an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

By presenting its claim this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards that are applied to claims of procedural injury. According to the court, “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Accordingly, in these scenarios, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that breaching a procedural right has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither this threshold nor the looser standard for redressability. Since AFL cannot meet even the less stringent requirements associated with procedural injury standing, it is unnecessary for us to determine whether these asserted harms are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial argument for standing is that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has deprived AFL of records sought under FOIA. This position is fatally deficient with respect to both causation and redressability. First, any informational harm arising from DOJ’s denial of access to agency records can be directly linked to DOJ itself, not OSC. Furthermore, any link between OSC’s failure to carry out a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is excessively conjectural and remote, rendering it inadequate to satisfy Article III standing requirements. The purported chain of causation is as follows: Had OSC performed the allegedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it could have opted to initiate corrective proceedings under section 1214 or disciplinary proceedings under section 1215 against culpable DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Thereafter, MSPB could have potentially sanctioned those individuals for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). In turn, DOJ might have then decided to release the requested records. This sequence requires surmise about potential actions by two federal agencies—MSPB and DOJ—that are not parties to this case, yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). The theory also invites speculation as to how OSC might exercise its prosecutorial discretion, a matter typically not subject to judicial compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because the proposed chain of events is speculative, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable decision here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL ignores the established rule that redressability generally depends on the binding effect a judgment has on the parties before the court, rather than on whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot assert standing based on its claimed entitlement to the information requested from DOJ pursuant to FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—predicated on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation—fares no better than its first. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate a tangible injury resulting from the denial of information to which a statute grants entitlement. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). Here, however, assuming arguendo that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, the provision does not confer any right to receive information. AFL contends that, should OSC carry out the assertedly compulsory investigation under section 1216, it might elect to provide AFL with the notifications and information otherwise required under section 1214 for “prohibited personnel practice” investigations. However, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notice obligations to its section 1216 inquiries. On the contrary, AFL concedes that precedents have found OSC’s decision whether to invoke section 1214’s procedures during section 1216 investigations to be a matter of discretion. Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL has not alleged a statutory right to receive information under section 1214, it has not sustained the requisite concrete injury to establish this standing theory. Similarly, as there is no requirement for OSC to provide section 1214 notices in the context of a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation is unlikely to remedy the informational harm alleged by AFL.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
