Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to determine if a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court resolved AFL’s claims against it on substantive grounds. We hold that AFL does not possess Article III standing to bring these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigatory and prosecutorial entity created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Through the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board either for corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or for disciplinary sanctions according to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures guiding the correction of prohibited personnel practices, as outlined in Section 1214, include requirements for updating the complainant on the investigation's status. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to investigate and pursue remedies for certain additional types of employee misconduct within the government. Pertinent to this discussion, the statute mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, withholdings not permitted under the Freedom of Information Act. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). With respect to these asserted FOIA violations, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes a distinct section addressing OSC enforcement. According to this provision, when a court (1) directs the release of agency records wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email communications exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected the request on what it considers questionable grounds, namely, that it refrains from searching employee email accounts for relevant records unless the employee agrees. Subsequently, AFL filed a FOIA action against DOJ. During that litigation, DOJ has already provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to AFL's request. The adequacy of DOJ’s document production is not presently before the Court.

AFL further requested that OSC examine the DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refraining from searching employee emails without their consent. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” when there are arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC asserted that it lacked authority to proceed with an investigation because the three conditions specified in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) that would necessitate such an action were unmet. Nevertheless, AFL maintained that section 1216 required OSC to investigate the alleged FOIA policy, regardless of whether FOIA itself imposed such a requirement.

The district court partially ruled in AFL's favor, but ultimately dismissed the action on the basis that AFL failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, the court found that OSC’s refusal to conduct an investigation was predicated on a mistake of law. Id. Despite this, the court refused to mandate that OSC investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) places that determination within OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court then remanded the matter to OSC for additional review. Id. After OSC once more declined to investigate, AFL requested entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed the rest of AFL’s claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and our review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are obligated to determine if AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, an issue that affects the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). As neither party originally discussed standing, we directed the submission of additional briefing regarding that issue.

Article III of the Constitution limits the exercise of federal “judicial Power” to resolving specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” See U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing derives from this case-or-controversy requirement. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order to establish standing under Article III, a plaintiff is required to demonstrate that they have incurred an injury that is attributable to the defendant and is likely to be remedied by a favorable court decision. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). This injury must involve “an invasion of a legally protected interest,” must be “concrete and particularized,” as well as “actual or imminent,” not “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When addressing a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff bears the burden of alleging facts that plausibly support each aspect of standing. See Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural right to an investigation under section 1216. However, as established in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. See also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s failure to investigate resulted in two specific informational harms: first, that DOJ has not produced the records requested by AFL via FOIA; and second, that OSC has failed to furnish the notices and information mandated by section 1214 for an investigation.

By presenting its claim in this manner, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing requirements associated with alleged procedural injuries. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In situations involving procedural injury, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the procedural violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet those criteria or even the less stringent requirements regarding redressability. Given that AFL fails to fulfill even the relaxed standing criteria for procedural injury, we do not reach the question of whether these asserted injuries should be considered procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing is premised on OSC’s alleged failure to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216, which, according to AFL, resulted in its inability to obtain documents sought under FOIA. This position, however, encounters insurmountable issues with respect to causation and redressability. Any injury AFL claims from the withholding of agency records under FOIA can be attributed directly to DOJ’s actions—not to OSC. Moreover, any asserted link between OSC’s omission to initiate a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is far too remote and conjectural to fulfill Article III standing requirements. The hypothetical sequence posited by AFL suggests that, had OSC undertaken the mandatory investigation under section 1216, it might subsequently have opted to initiate corrective proceedings under section 1214 or disciplinary actions under section 1215 against the relevant DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Thereafter, the MSPB might have elected to penalize those individuals for unwarranted FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Ultimately, DOJ might then have chosen to release the sought-after records. This entire causal narrative demands conjecture concerning the independent decisions of MSPB and DOJ—neither of which is a party to this litigation—yet, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it necessitates presuming how OSC would exercise enforcement discretion, a matter courts ordinarily are not empowered to dictate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of this tenuous chain of hypotheticals, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable ruling in this action “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Further, AFL disregards that redressability is typically concerned with whether a judgment would bind the parties before the court, as opposed to whether the decision’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL fails to demonstrate standing based on its interest in obtaining information from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing, which is grounded in its claimed right to access information resulting from the OSC investigation, is also unsuccessful. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, the individual must have suffered a tangible harm from being denied information that a statute specifically entitles them to obtain. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, in this case, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) obligates the OSC to investigate FOIA violations, the provision does not provide a right to access any information as a result. AFL argues that, should OSC undertake the investigation allegedly required by section 1216, the agency might opt to provide AFL with notices and information similar to that mandated in cases involving “prohibited personnel practices” under section 1214. Nonetheless, AFL does not argue that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notice obligations to its investigations under section 1216. On the contrary, AFL concedes that the application of section 1214’s procedural standards in the context of a section 1216 investigation rests within OSC’s discretion. See Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no asserted statutory right under section 1214 entitling AFL to information, AFL does not demonstrate a concrete injury to uphold its second standing argument. Furthermore, since OSC is not required to implement section 1214’s notice procedures in investigations under section 1216, a court order compelling such an investigation would not be likely to remedy the second harm AFL alleges.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to the absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
