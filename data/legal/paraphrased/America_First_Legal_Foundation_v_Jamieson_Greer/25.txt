Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-5168  
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly denied AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the matter by dismissing AFL’s claims on substantive grounds. We determine, however, that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency tasked with investigation and prosecution, created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, if appropriate, initiate proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board either for corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or for disciplinary measures according to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the procedures for addressing corrective actions arising from prohibited personnel practices, and includes requirements for updating the complainant regarding the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) the authority to investigate and pursue corrective measures regarding particular types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinently, the statute mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—meaning information improperly withheld in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When confronted with alleged FOIA violations, the OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides a distinct mechanism authorizing OSC to pursue enforcement. According to this provision, if a court (1) directs that agency records be produced after finding they were improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees to the requester against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific emails from the Department of Justice, namely correspondence between an external lawyer and the DOJ's Civil Division. AFL alleges that the Civil Division refused the request, contending—without justification, in AFL’s view—that it does not examine employee emails for responsive materials absent the employee's consent. Consequently, AFL filed a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ. In response to that litigation, DOJ has been providing—and continues to provide—records pertinent to the request. The adequacy of DOJ’s ongoing production is not subject to dispute in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of not searching employee emails unless consent is given. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC asserted it lacked jurisdiction to investigate because the three conditions necessary to trigger an inquiry under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. AFL maintained that section 1216 nonetheless obliged OSC to conduct an investigation, irrespective of FOIA’s own provisions.

The district court partially ruled in AFL's favor but ultimately dismissed the action for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to examine purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate rested on a legal mistake. Id. However, the court declined to compel OSC to conduct an investigation of any FOIA policy because, in its opinion, section 1216(c) grants that determination to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court thus sent the matter back to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC once more refused to open an investigation, AFL sought entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed the remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and we review the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine if AFL met the prerequisites for standing under Article III, as this issue affects the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Standing was not raised by either party at the outset; accordingly, we requested additional briefing regarding this matter.

The federal “judicial Power” is confined by Article III of the Constitution to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this case-or-controversy limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To demonstrate standing under Article III, a plaintiff must show that they have sustained an injury attributed to the defendant’s actions and that this injury is likely to be remedied by a decision in their favor. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” and must be both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the pleading stage for a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff is required to assert facts that make each element of standing plausible. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as stated in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and referenced in Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to conduct an investigation has resulted in two specific informational injuries: (1) DOJ has failed to supply the records that AFL sought via FOIA; and (2) OSC has withheld the notices and information that section 1214 mandates in connection with an investigation.

By framing its claim in this manner, AFL attempts to take advantage of the less stringent standing criteria applicable in cases asserting procedural injury. As explained in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In such instances, plaintiffs are still required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet these criteria, as well as a relaxed form of the redressability standard. Given AFL’s inability to satisfy the diminished standing threshold for procedural injury, it is unnecessary to determine whether the claimed harms are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL's primary theory for standing asserts that OSC's omission in investigating DOJ under section 1216 has hindered AFL's access to records it sought pursuant to FOIA. However, this theory is fatally flawed in terms of both causation and redressability. Specifically, any informational harm resulting from DOJ's denial of access to agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, not to OSC. Moreover, any connection between OSC's nonperformance of a section 1216 investigation and DOJ's alleged failure to comply with the FOIA request is too tenuous and conjectural to meet Article III standing requirements. The purported causal sequence is as follows: Had OSC conducted the allegedly compulsory section 1216 investigation, it might have opted to initiate either corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against the implicated DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). The Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), then, might have chosen to penalize those employees for wrongful withholding under FOIA. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Subsequently, DOJ could have been prompted to release the requested materials. This chain of causation depends on conjecture regarding decisions made by two government entities not currently parties to this proceeding—the MSPB and DOJ—where, as the Supreme Court explained, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, it involves uncertainty concerning how OSC would exercise its enforcement discretion, which courts typically lack authority to mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of such speculative links, AFL cannot demonstrate that a judgment in its favor “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the informational harm suffered. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL disregards the settled principle that redressability usually depends on the effect of the judgment upon the litigants themselves, rather than whether the court's reasoning might influence nonparties. See Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). For these reasons, AFL is unable to establish standing based on its claimed informational interest in the DOJ records it sought under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation, is equally unavailing. For a plaintiff to have informational standing, the denial of information must constitute a concrete injury if a “statute entitled him to receive” that information. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, assuming arguendo that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, this provision does not grant any right to receive information. AFL argues that if OSC were to perform the claimed mandatory investigation pursuant to section 1216, OSC could, at its discretion, provide AFL with the notifications and information required during investigations of a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not maintain that OSC is required to import section 1214’s notice provisions into section 1216 proceedings. In fact, AFL concedes existing case law establishing that OSC has discretion over “whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” when conducting investigations under section 1216. See Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without alleging a statutory right to information under section 1214, AFL’s second theory fails to identify a concrete injury adequate for standing. Similarly, because OSC is not obligated to provide section 1214 notices during section 1216 investigations, a court order requiring such an investigation would not likely remedy AFL’s purported informational injury.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and remand the case, directing dismissal due to the absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
