Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court ruled against AFL on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency tasked with both investigating and prosecuting in order to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Through the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC is granted authority to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board—seeking either corrective remedies under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures for addressing corrective actions in such cases are delineated in section 1214, which also includes requirements for keeping the complainant informed about the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to look into and pursue remedial measures regarding particular types of misconduct committed by government employees. Pertinent to the present case, this section states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, information the Freedom of Information Act forbids agencies from withholding. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). For these alleged breaches of FOIA, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes a distinct provision addressing enforcement by OSC. Under this provision, if a court (1) directs that agency records unlawfully withheld under FOIA be produced, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific email communications from the Department of Justice that occurred between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division rejected its request on questionable grounds, specifically stating that it does not examine employee email accounts for relevant documents without obtaining the respective employee’s consent. AFL subsequently brought a FOIA action against DOJ. During the course of this litigation, DOJ has produced, and continues to produce, documents responsive to the FOIA request. The adequacy of DOJ’s ongoing production is not the subject of this proceeding.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported practice under FOIA of refusing to search employee emails absent their consent. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC asserted it lacked authority to investigate in this instance, citing the fact that the conditions necessary to initiate an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were unmet in connection with the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that section 1216 did not require it to investigate if FOIA itself did not mandate one.

The district court partially sided with AFL, but still dismissed the action on the grounds that no claim was stated. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not curtail OSC’s authority to pursue investigations into purported FOIA violations as authorized by section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found OSC’s refusal to investigate rested on a legal mistake. Id. Nevertheless, the court declined to compel OSC to probe any FOIA policy, reasoning that the choice to investigate is, under section 1216(c), a matter left to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The case was then remanded to OSC for further evaluation. Id. After OSC again decided not to investigate, AFL requested final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss the outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and the standard of review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine if AFL has met the prerequisites for standing under Article III, as this issue affects the district court's subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither side initially discussed standing, we directed the parties to provide additional briefing on this matter.

The federal judiciary’s authority under Article III of the Constitution is confined to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this requirement for cases or controversies. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order to meet Article III standing requirements, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury attributable to the defendant that a court decision in their favor is likely to remedy. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When evaluating a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff is required to plead factual allegations that make each element of standing plausible. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation as outlined in section 1216. However, as stated in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and reiterated in Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), the mere loss of a procedural right, without a tangible interest being impacted by such loss—that is, a “procedural right in vacuo”—does not suffice to establish Article III standing. To address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, that DOJ has withheld the records AFL requested under FOIA; and second, that OSC has not furnished the notices and information that are mandated in the course of an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

By characterizing its claim this way, AFL attempts to take advantage of the more lenient standing rules applicable in cases of asserted procedural injury. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs must still establish that a procedural right’s violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL, however, is unable to meet these prerequisites, even under the relaxed redressability standard. Since AFL falls short of even the procedural injury threshold for standing, it is unnecessary to determine whether these injuries should be considered procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial standing argument asserts that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has blocked AFL’s access to the records it sought under FOIA. However, this argument encounters insurmountable issues regarding causation and redressability. The informational harm caused by DOJ’s refusal to release agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, not to OSC. Moreover, the purported link between OSC’s noncompliance with section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is too tenuous and conjectural to fulfill Article III’s requirements. The hypothesized chain of causation proceeds as follows: If OSC had initiated the mandatory section 1216 investigation, it might have elected to bring a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ employees who committed violations. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Thereafter, MSPB might have opted to penalize those employees for wrongful withholding of records under FOIA. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Subsequently, DOJ might have determined to release the sought-after documents. This sequence depends on conjecture regarding the independent decisions of two non-party governmental agencies—the MSPB and DOJ—but “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, it hinges upon assumptions about OSC’s discretionary enforcement decisions, which courts are usually not empowered to direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given the highly speculative nature of this causal chain, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable decision in this action “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its claimed informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also ignores the fact that redressability largely depends on how a judgment constrains the actual parties, not whether the court’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot ground its standing in an asserted interest in obtaining the information it requested from DOJ via FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—which rests on its claimed informational interest in the outcomes of the OSC investigation—fares no better. For informational standing to exist, a plaintiff must have suffered a concrete injury as a result of being denied information that a statute grants them a right to obtain. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this matter, even if section 1216(a)(3) does oblige OSC to probe FOIA violations, the statute does not grant any right to information. AFL suggests that, should OSC undertake the purportedly obligatory investigation pursuant to section 1216, it might opt to provide AFL with notices and information such as those that would be compulsory if OSC were examining an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. However, AFL does not assert that OSC is required to apply section 1214’s notification obligations to section 1216 proceedings. On the contrary, AFL admits that precedent holds OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during a section 1216 inquiry is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). With no claimed statutory entitlement to the information governed by section 1214, AFL does not have a concrete injury sufficient for this second theory of standing. Similarly, because OSC is not required to issue section 1214 notices in the context of a section 1216 inquiry, any court order compelling such an investigation would not likely remedy the injury AFL alleges under this theory.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss it for want of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
