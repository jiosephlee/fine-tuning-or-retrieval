Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-5168  
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly refused AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the claims in AFL’s favor on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board seeking corrective relief pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines the procedures that apply to corrective actions addressing prohibited personnel practices, and includes requirements to inform the complaining individual about the status of an ongoing investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants authority to OSC to examine and pursue corrective measures regarding particular types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, the statute mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” which refers to withholdings barred by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When facing such alleged violations of FOIA, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides an independent mechanism for enforcement by the OSC. According to this provision, if a court (1) directs the release of agency records that were wrongly withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice seeking specific email communications between an external attorney and the Civil Division of the DOJ. AFL alleges that the Civil Division refused to fulfill the request, purportedly citing the questionable justification that it does not examine employees’ emails for responsive documents without first obtaining their consent. Subsequently, AFL initiated a lawsuit against DOJ under the FOIA. In response to that litigation, DOJ has already provided—and continues to provide—documents that are responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s response is not subject to dispute in the present matter.

AFL additionally requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search staff emails unless those employees consent. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” into arbitrary FOIA withholdings, AFL made this request. OSC, however, asserted it lacked the authority to investigate because the conditions necessary to compel an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met. AFL maintained, in relation to the alleged FOIA policy, that section 1216 obligated OSC to investigate regardless of whether FOIA imposed such a duty.

The district court partially sided with AFL but ultimately dismissed the lawsuit on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC’s authority to look into alleged FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Consequently, the court concluded that OSC’s refusal to investigate rested on a legal mistake. Id. Despite this, the court refused to compel OSC to examine any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) grants discretion over that decision to OSC. Id. at *6. The case was sent back to OSC for additional review. Id. After OSC once more chose not to investigate, AFL sought the entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed AFL’s outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and this Court reviews the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substance of the case, we are required to determine if AFL met the prerequisites for standing under Article III, as this affects the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since the parties did not originally discuss standing, we directed them to provide additional briefing on this issue.

Article III of the Constitution confines the authority of the federal judiciary to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises out of this requirement. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish standing under Article III, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury attributed to the defendant that a favorable court decision is likely to remedy. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). This injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the motion to dismiss phase, plaintiffs are required to allege facts that plausibly indicate the presence of each standing element. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached an asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to pursue an investigation has inflicted upon it two specific, informational injuries: first, that DOJ has withheld records sought by AFL under FOIA; second, that OSC has failed to supply the notifications and information mandated as part of an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

By characterizing its claim this way, AFL aims to benefit from the less stringent standing criteria applicable to procedural injury cases. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Accordingly, in such instances, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the procedural right’s breach “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither these prerequisites nor the lowered threshold for redressability. Since AFL cannot even fulfill the requirements for standing under the more forgiving procedural injury standard, it is unnecessary for us to determine whether the claimed injuries should be considered procedural or substantive.

AFL initially argues that its standing arises from OSC’s alleged failure to investigate the DOJ under section 1216, claiming this failure has blocked AFL’s access to records requested via FOIA. However, this argument is fatally deficient with respect to causation and redressability. To begin with, any injury AFL claims to have suffered by not receiving agency records under FOIA stems directly from the DOJ’s actions, not OSC’s. Further, the supposed link between OSC not initiating an investigation under section 1216 and the DOJ’s refusal to honor the FOIA request is speculative and insufficient to confer standing under Article III. The theorized causal sequence is as follows: If OSC had carried out the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, OSC might have decided to proceed with corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Following that, the MSPB could have imposed sanctions on those employees for unjustified FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). As a result, DOJ might have been prompted to release the requested information. Yet, this scenario necessitates conjecture about decisions to be made by two other federal agencies not involved as parties here—the MSPB and DOJ—and “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it would require guessing how OSC might deploy its enforcement discretion, something courts usually cannot compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of this uncertain chain, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable outcome “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” any informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also ignores the settled rule that redressability is typically determined by how a court’s judgment affects the litigants directly, and not by whether the reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot show standing on the basis of its asserted right to information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL's second argument for standing—which is premised on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fares no better than its first. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff must show a concrete injury resulting from being denied information to which “a statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even accepting that section 1216(a)(3) obligates the OSC to investigate FOIA violations, that provision does not confer a right to receive information. AFL counters that if the OSC carries out the mandatory investigation under section 1216, it might provide AFL with notices and information, akin to what would be required if OSC were carrying out a “prohibited personnel practice” investigation under section 1214. Nonetheless, AFL does not argue that the OSC is obligated to apply section 1214’s notice procedures during section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes that precedent establishes the OSC’s determination whether to “follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in the context of a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL claims no statutory entitlement to such information under section 1214, its alleged injury does not meet the standard for concreteness needed to ground its informational standing theory. Similarly, given that the OSC is not required to provide notice under section 1214 during a section 1216 investigation, any court order compelling such an investigation would not likely remedy AFL’s asserted second injury.

III

As AFL does not have standing under Article III, we set aside the district court's decision and send the case back with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
