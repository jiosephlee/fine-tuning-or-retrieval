Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation filed this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to determine if a federal agency capriciously rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court disposed of AFL’s claims on their substantive merits. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to pursue “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, if appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board to seek corrective relief pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214, or disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines the procedures applicable to corrective action proceedings addressing prohibited personnel practices and includes requirements for keeping the party who filed the complaint informed of the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to conduct investigations and pursue corrective measures regarding particular types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, the statute requires that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” referring to instances where the Freedom of Information Act forbids such withholding. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When such alleged FOIA violations occur, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

The FOIA includes an independent enforcement provision involving OSC. According to this provision, when a court (1) orders the release of agency records wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) imposes attorneys’ fees on the agency, and (3) explicitly raises the issue of whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," OSC must "promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding." U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice seeking specific email communications between an external attorney and the Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division refused the request on questionable grounds, specifically asserting that it does not search its employees' email accounts for relevant records absent those employees' consent. Subsequently, AFL initiated litigation against DOJ pursuant to FOIA. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has already released—and is still in the process of releasing—documents that are responsive to AFL's request. The adequacy of these productions is not before the Court in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employees’ emails absent their permission. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary withholding under FOIA. However, OSC declined to proceed, asserting that the prerequisites outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) necessary for initiating such an investigation were unmet in relation to the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that, contrary to AFL’s position, section 1216 did not independently require an investigation in the absence of one warranted under FOIA.

The district court issued a partial ruling in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action, finding that the complaint failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict the authority of OSC to examine purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). As a result, the court found that OSC's refusal to initiate an investigation stemmed from an erroneous interpretation of the law. Id. However, the court refused to compel OSC to conduct an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) places such determinations within OSC's discretionary power. Id. at *6. The court therefore remanded the matter to OSC for additional proceedings. Id. When OSC declined a second time to investigate, AFL sought entry of final judgment, after which the district court dismissed the outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and our standard of review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are obligated to determine if AFL met the criteria for Article III standing, as this issue concerns the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because standing was not initially discussed by either party, we requested additional briefing on this matter.

The federal “judicial Power” is limited under Article III of the Constitution to the adjudication of designated “Cases” and “Controversies.” See U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises out of this constitutional limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to meet the requirements of Article III standing, they must show that they experienced an injury traceable to the defendant that a favorable court decision would likely remedy. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The harm must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest,” and it must be both “concrete and particularized,” as well as “actual or imminent” rather than “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When opposing a motion to dismiss, plaintiffs must present factual allegations that, if true, establish each component of standing as plausible. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC breached its asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, "the loss of a procedural right, absent an accompanying concrete interest impacted by such loss—a procedural right in vacuo—does not suffice to establish Article III standing." Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, the DOJ has not released the documents AFL sought under FOIA; and second, OSC has withheld the notices and information mandated as part of an investigation under section 1214.

By presenting its claims in this manner, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards that govern claims of procedural injury. “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under this doctrine, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the infringement of a procedural right has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. Here, AFL meets neither this threshold nor the less demanding requirement for redressability in procedural injury cases. Since AFL does not fulfill even the relaxed procedural injury standing prerequisites, we are not compelled to determine whether the injuries at issue are procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing is predicated on the assertion that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 prevented AFL from obtaining documents sought under FOIA. However, this rationale suffers from incurable defects regarding causation and redressability. Primarily, any informational harm resulting from DOJ’s refusal to produce agency records is directly attributable to DOJ itself, not OSC. The supposed linkage between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is overly tenuous and speculative to support Article III standing. The proposed chain of causation is as follows: Had OSC commenced the claimed mandatory investigation under section 1216, it potentially could have initiated a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary proceeding under section 1215 against culpable DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have imposed penalties on those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). In turn, DOJ could then have chosen to release the requested documents. This argument hinges on conjecture about decisions by two independent agencies not party to this suit—the MSPB and DOJ—yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Moreover, it rests on uncertain predictions about how OSC might employ its enforcement discretion, a domain over which courts typically exercise no compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Owing to this conjectural sequence, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable outcome here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Further, AFL disregards the rule that redressability is generally determined by how a judgment binds the actual parties, not on whether the court’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot satisfy standing requirements based upon its claimed interest in information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing, which is premised on its claimed informational interest in the outcomes of the OSC’s investigation, is similarly unpersuasive. A plaintiff establishes informational standing by showing the denial of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this case, however, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) obliges OSC to investigate violations of FOIA, the provision does not confer any right to obtain information. AFL argues that, if OSC undertakes the allegedly compulsory investigation under section 1216, OSC might elect to supply AFL with notices and information as would be required during an investigation into a “prohibited personnel practice” pursuant to section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not argue that OSC is compelled to apply the section 1214 notification requirements to section 1216 proceedings. Indeed, AFL concedes that courts have determined OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no asserted statutory right to information under section 1214 in this context, AFL fails to demonstrate a concrete injury for purposes of this standing theory. Likewise, since OSC is not obligated to afford section 1214 notices in a section 1216 proceeding, a court order compelling such an investigation would not likely address AFL’s purported injury.

III

Since AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to the absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
