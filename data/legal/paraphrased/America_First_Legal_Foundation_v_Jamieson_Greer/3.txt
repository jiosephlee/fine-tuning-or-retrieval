Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

Case captioned America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer came before the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, with opinion issued on October 3, 2025, under docket number 24-5168, authored by Circuit Judge Katsas.

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to investigate whether a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court ruled against AFL on the merits and dismissed the claims. We hold that AFL does not possess Article III standing to assert these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body designed to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC has the authority to look into allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board, either to obtain corrective measures as authorized by 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or to seek disciplinary action pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 establishes the processes for taking corrective action in cases involving prohibited personnel practices, and it includes requirements for updating the complainant regarding the status of an ongoing investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) authority to investigate and pursue remedial measures regarding certain types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinently, the statute mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, withholdings not permitted by the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases involving alleged FOIA violations, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were at issue.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA itself includes a distinct mechanism for OSC enforcement. Under this provision, when a court (1) directs disclosure of agency records that were unlawfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought certain email communications between an external lawyer and the Department of Justice’s Civil Division. AFL claims that the Civil Division refused this request on the questionable basis that employee emails are not searched for responsive documents unless the employee consents. Consequently, AFL initiated litigation against DOJ under FOIA. In the course of that action, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—records responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s production is not a matter before the Court in this instance.

AFL additionally requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee email accounts without their consent. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to “conduct an investigation” into arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC, however, denied it had the authority to conduct such an investigation, citing the absence of the three conditions necessary to trigger an inquiry under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i). AFL maintained that section 1216 imposed a mandatory investigative duty on OSC, regardless of whether FOIA itself would require such an investigation.

The district court partially ruled in AFL's favor but ultimately dismissed the case for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC’s authority to examine supposed FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court held that OSC’s refusal to investigate stemmed from a mistake of law. Id. However, the court declined to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) assigns such decisions to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The case was remanded to OSC for further action. Id. When OSC again refused to investigate, AFL sought entry of final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss its remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and the standard of review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, we must first determine whether AFL has met the Article III standing requirements, as this issue implicates the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Initially, neither party discussed the issue of standing; accordingly, we directed them to submit additional briefing on this matter.

Under Article III of the Constitution, the federal judiciary’s authority is limited to the adjudication of certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is rooted in this case-or-controversy limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to establish Article III standing, it must demonstrate an injury that is traceable to the defendant’s conduct and is likely to be remedied by a favorable court ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The alleged injury must involve “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When a motion to dismiss is pending, the plaintiff is required to plead facts that plausibly establish each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC infringed its purported procedural right to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as the Supreme Court explained, “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this standing issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific informational injuries: first, the Department of Justice has not furnished AFL with the records it requested under FOIA; and second, OSC has failed to provide the notices and information required in connection with an investigation under section 1214.

By characterizing its claim in this manner, AFL attempts to benefit from the less stringent standing criteria that are applicable to cases asserting procedural injury. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under this approach, plaintiffs are still required to establish that a procedural right’s violation has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither these elements nor the less stringent redressability requirement. Since AFL cannot even fulfill the standing prerequisites for procedural injuries, we need not address whether these alleged injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s primary argument for standing contends that OSC’s omission in investigating DOJ under section 1216 has denied AFL access to records it sought through FOIA. However, this argument fails due to insurmountable issues with causation and redressability. Specifically, any injury related to DOJ’s withholding of agency records is directly attributable to DOJ itself, not OSC. Moreover, the asserted link between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is too conjectural and remote to fulfill Article III’s standing requirements. The hypothesized causal sequence is as follows: Had OSC conducted the purportedly obligatory section 1216 inquiry, it might have opted to initiate either corrective measures under section 1214 or disciplinary proceedings under section 1215 against DOJ employees implicated in wrongdoing. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have chosen to penalize those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Only then, DOJ may have chosen to disclose the sought-after records. This scenario requires speculation regarding actions by two governmental entities not present in this litigation—the MSPB and DOJ—and “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also involves speculation about how OSC would exercise its prosecutorial discretion, a matter typically insulated from judicial compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of this speculative series of events, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable ruling “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also ignores the rule that redressability usually depends on how a judgment affects the parties to the case, rather than whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot show standing premised on its claimed entitlement to the information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL's alternative standing argument—premised on its claimed informational interest in the outcomes of the OSC investigation—fares no better. In order for a plaintiff to establish informational standing, there must be a concrete injury arising from the denial of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even assuming that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not confer a right to obtain information. AFL argues that, should OSC carry out the allegedly mandatory investigation under section 1216, it might opt to provide AFL with notices and information that would be required if OSC were investigating a “prohibited personnel practice” pursuant to section 1214. Yet AFL does not assert that OSC is required to apply the notice requirements of section 1214 to investigations conducted under section 1216. Indeed, AFL concedes the existence of precedent holding that OSC’s choice to follow section 1214’s procedures during a section 1216 investigation remains “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Since there is no claimed right to information under section 1214, AFL does not demonstrate a concrete injury adequate to establish its second standing theory. Similarly, because OSC is not required to provide the notice requirements of section 1214 in a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation would not be likely to remedy AFL’s second asserted injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
