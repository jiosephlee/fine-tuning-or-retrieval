Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-5168  
Author: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation filed this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency unlawfully rejected AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court ruled against AFL on the substance of its claims. We determine, however, that AFL does not possess Article III standing to assert these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial entity created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to probe into “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board, either seeking corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the procedures that apply to obtaining corrective relief for prohibited personnel practices, and includes requirements that the agency inform the complainant on the status of an ongoing investigation. See, for instance, 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedial measures for specific types of misconduct committed by government employees. Pertinent to this case, it stipulates that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," referring to withholdings not permitted under the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In circumstances involving such purported FOIA violations, OSC is authorized to "investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes a distinct section related to enforcement by the OSC. According to this provision, should a court (1) require the disclosure of agency records that were wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grant attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) explicitly raise the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then the OSC is mandated to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific emails from the Department of Justice that had been exchanged between an external attorney and the Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division rejected its request on what it describes as the questionable basis that the Division does not search employee email accounts for records that may be responsive unless the employees themselves consent. As a result, AFL filed a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ. In that litigation, DOJ has already released—and is in the process of continuing to release—documents that are responsive to the request. The adequacy of these disclosures is not a subject of dispute in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported practice under FOIA of declining to search employees’ emails unless they gave permission. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC, however, asserted it lacked authority to pursue an investigation because the necessary criteria set forth in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. Nevertheless, AFL maintained that section 1216 required an investigation regardless of FOIA’s requirements.

The district court issued a partial ruling in AFL’s favor but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict the authority of OSC to investigate purported FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate rested on an incorrect interpretation of the law. Id. Despite this, the court refused to compel OSC to examine any FOIA policy because it found that section 1216(c) granted such decision-making to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court remanded the matter to OSC for additional proceedings. Id. When OSC chose not to investigate once more, AFL requested the entry of final judgment, after which the district court dismissed the outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and our review proceeds de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substance of the dispute, it is necessary to determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for standing under Article III, as this issue impacts the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither side initially discussed standing, we directed the parties to provide supplemental briefing on this matter.

The Constitution’s Article III confines federal judicial authority to the resolution of specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing emerges from this case-or-controversy limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). A plaintiff seeking to invoke Article III jurisdiction must demonstrate an injury attributed to the defendant that a favorable ruling would likely remedy. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). This injury must involve “an invasion of a legally protected interest,” be “concrete and particularized,” and be “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When opposing a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must set forth plausible factual allegations supporting each requisite element of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its claimed procedural entitlement to an investigation as set forth in section 1216. However, the “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s refusal to investigate has resulted in two specific, informational injuries: first, that DOJ has failed to provide records AFL requested under FOIA; and second, that OSC has not supplied the notices and information mandated for an investigation under section 1214.

By presenting the case in this way, AFL attempts to take advantage of the more lenient standing rules applicable to claims of procedural injury. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005): “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In matters of this type, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that infringement of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet any of those prerequisites, nor can it meet even the relaxed standard for redressability. Since AFL fails to establish standing under even the procedural injury framework, we need not address whether the alleged injuries are in fact procedural rather than substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing asserts that OSC’s lack of investigation of DOJ under section 1216 has denied AFL access to records sought under FOIA. However, this theory is fatally flawed regarding causation and redressability. Primarily, any informational harm from DOJ’s withholding of agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, rather than OSC. Additionally, the supposed link between OSC’s omission of a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is overly tenuous and speculative to support Article III standing. The purported causal sequence is as follows: If OSC had carried out the allegedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, OSC may have opted to initiate a corrective action pursuant to section 1214, or pursue a disciplinary proceeding under section 1215, against DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Following this, MSPB could have chosen to penalize those employees for improperly withholding FOIA materials. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Subsequently, DOJ might then have elected to release the records AFL requested. Yet, this chain of events demands conjecture concerning the independent decisions of two government agencies—MSPB and DOJ—that are not defendants in this suit; “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, it depends on speculation about how OSC would exercise its discretionary enforcement powers, which courts generally lack authority to mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given the speculative nature of this chain, AFL has not demonstrated that a judgment in its favor “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the informational harm it alleges. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards that redressability ordinarily depends on whether a judgment is binding on the parties, not on whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot claim standing on the basis of its interest in records it sought from DOJ via FOIA.

AFL’s alternative standing argument—premised on its claimed right to obtain information resulting from the OSC investigation—fares no better. For a plaintiff asserting informational standing, a tangible harm arises only when the individual is denied access to information that a statute explicitly provides. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) imposes a duty on OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not confer a right to receive such information. AFL argues that, upon OSC initiating a mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC could opt to provide AFL with notices and information of the kind required during investigations of “prohibited personnel practices” pursuant to section 1214. Yet, AFL does not assert that OSC is obligated to apply section 1214’s notice requirements in the context of section 1216 investigations. Indeed, AFL concedes, referencing Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011), that whether OSC applies the procedural requirements of section 1214 to a section 1216 investigation remains within OSC’s discretion. Without any claim of a right to obtain information under section 1214, AFL does not have a sufficient concrete injury to establish this ground for standing. Similarly, since OSC is not required to follow section 1214’s notice procedures in a section 1216 investigation, any court order compelling such an investigation would not likely redress the second type of injury that AFL asserts.

III

Since AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s decision and return the case with directions to dismiss due to a lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
