Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation filed this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly refused AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court ruled against AFL on the substance of its claims. We determine, however, that AFL does not have Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to ensure the maintenance of “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the authority granted by the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, where appropriate, pursue enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board for corrective relief as provided in 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or to initiate disciplinary proceedings pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures for handling corrective actions involving prohibited personnel practices, as set forth in § 1214, include specific requirements for informing the complainant regarding the status of the investigation. See, for example, 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue corrective measures for specific additional forms of misconduct by government personnel. Pertinent to this matter, the provision states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, actions forbidden by the Freedom of Information Act. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). With respect to these alleged FOIA infractions, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides a distinct mechanism for OSC enforcement. According to the statute, if a court (1) mandates the release of agency records that were wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) orders the agency to pay attorneys' fees, and (3) expressly questions whether the agency's withholding was "arbitrary or capricious," then OSC is required to "promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding." U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific emails from the Department of Justice, namely, communications between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division refused the request, citing as justification the questionable policy of not searching employee email accounts for relevant records unless the individual employee gives consent. AFL subsequently brought suit against DOJ under FOIA. In that action, DOJ has released, and continues to release, records that respond to AFL’s request. The adequacy of this ongoing production is not the matter presently before the Court.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee emails unless the employee consented. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary denials under FOIA. OSC asserted it lacked authority to investigate because the three requirements necessary to initiate an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met regarding the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that section 1216 did not require an investigation in the absence of such triggering conditions, regardless of FOIA’s requirements.

The district court partially ruled in AFL's favor but ultimately dismissed the lawsuit for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to examine purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, according to the court, OSC’s refusal to initiate an investigation was predicated on an error of law. Id. Despite this, the court refused to require OSC to commence an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) places that choice within OSC’s discretionary authority. Id. at *6. The court sent the matter back to OSC for further consideration. Id. After OSC once again declined to conduct an investigation, AFL requested entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed its remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and our review of the matter proceeds de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine whether AFL has met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue impacts the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because the parties did not initially brief the matter of standing, we required them to provide additional briefing specifically on that issue.

The federal "judicial Power" is limited under Article III of the Constitution to the adjudication of specified "Cases" and "Controversies." U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this case-or-controversy limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order to establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate that they have incurred an injury attributable to the defendant that is likely to be remedied by a favorable court decision. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” and be “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent,” rather than speculative or abstract. Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the motion-to-dismiss phase, it is incumbent upon the plaintiff to allege sufficient facts that, if true, would plausibly satisfy each element necessary for standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL argues that OSC infringed upon its asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as established in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), the loss of a procedural right alone, absent the impairment of a tangible interest, “a procedural right in vacuo,” does not establish standing under Article III; see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In an effort to address this deficiency, AFL maintains that OSC’s failure to investigate has resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, DOJ has withheld records that AFL sought through FOIA; second, OSC failed to supply the notifications and information mandated in connection with an investigation under section 1214.

By characterizing its claim in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the less stringent standing standards applicable to claims of procedural injury. As articulated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these situations, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the infringement of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL is unable to meet these prerequisites or the comparatively lenient redressability criterion. Since AFL fails to establish standing under even the relaxed approach for procedural injuries, it is unnecessary for us to determine whether the injuries in question are procedural or substantive in character.

AFL’s initial standing argument asserts that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 deprived AFL of access to the documents it sought through FOIA. However, this theory is fundamentally flawed in terms of causation and redressability. Any informational harm arising from DOJ’s denial of access to agency records is directly attributable to DOJ itself, not to OSC. Furthermore, any link between OSC’s omission of a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is too conjectural and remote to establish Article III standing. The purported causal sequence proceeds as follows: If OSC had undertaken the allegedly mandatory section 1216 investigation, OSC might have opted to initiate a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary proceeding under section 1215 against DOJ personnel responsible. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have decided to penalize those employees for arbitrary denial of FOIA requests. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Ultimately, DOJ might have then chosen to release the sought-after records. This scenario necessitates speculation concerning decisions by two government agencies (MSPB and DOJ) that are not parties to this litigation; yet, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also depends on speculative assumptions about OSC’s use of its enforcement discretion, a matter that courts ordinarily cannot mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of this highly conjectural progression, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a judgment in its favor “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards the principle that redressability generally is assessed by the extent to which a judgment binds the litigants, rather than whether the judgment’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot demonstrate standing based on its interest in the materials requested from DOJ through FOIA.

AFL's second argument for standing, which is grounded in its claimed right to obtain information from the OSC’s investigation, is equally unavailing. To have informational standing, a plaintiff must experience a tangible harm due to the denial of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, assuming section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not grant any right to obtain information. AFL contends that, should OSC proceed with the mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC might choose to furnish AFL with notices and other information that section 1214 would require if investigating a “prohibited personnel practice.” Nevertheless, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214 notice procedures to section 1216 investigations. On the contrary, AFL concedes that courts have found the “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in section 1216 proceedings to be a matter of “discretion.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without any claim of a statutory right to information under section 1214, AFL fails to allege a concrete injury to support this alternative standing theory. Furthermore, since OSC is not required to apply section 1214’s notice provisions in a section 1216 investigation, an order compelling OSC to conduct such an investigation would not be likely to remedy the second injury AFL claims.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss it due to a lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
