Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking a mandate that the Office of Special Counsel examine if a federal agency unjustifiably refused AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the matter by rejecting AFL’s claims on their substance. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial entity created with the goal of promoting a “fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC holds authority to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, where appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board to pursue corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary remedies pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures for implementing corrective actions for such practices are detailed in Section 1214, which includes, among other requirements, the obligation to keep the complainant informed about the status of the OSC’s investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC authority to examine and pursue remedies for certain additional types of wrongdoing committed by federal employees. Pertinent here, the statute directs OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, instances of withholding information in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). With respect to such alleged FOIA breaches, OSC is authorized to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

The FOIA provides a distinct mechanism for OSC enforcement. According to the statute, if a court (1) directs that agency records improperly withheld under FOIA be produced, (2) orders the agency to pay attorneys' fees, and (3) explicitly raises the issue of whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," OSC is required to "promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding." U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific emails from the Department of Justice involving communication between an external lawyer and the DOJ's Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected the request, claiming—questionably—that it would not search employee email accounts for responsive documents unless the employee agreed to such a search. Subsequently, AFL filed a lawsuit against DOJ pursuant to FOIA. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has provided—and remains in the process of providing—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s ongoing production is not a matter before the Court in this proceeding.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refraining from searching employee emails unless permission was granted. In support, AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC responded that it lacked authority to investigate since the three prerequisites for initiating an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were unmet with respect to the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that section 1216 did not independently require an investigation if FOIA’s requirements were not otherwise triggered.

The district court issued a partial ruling in AFL’s favor but ultimately dismissed the action on the grounds that AFL failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to look into supposed FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). As a result, the court held that OSC’s refusal to investigate arose from a mistake of law. Id. Nevertheless, the court chose not to compel OSC to launch an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) grants that decision to the discretion of OSC. Id. at *6. The case was sent back to OSC for additional consideration. Id. After OSC declined once more to conduct an investigation, AFL sought a final judgment, and the district court dismissed the outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then filed this appeal, which we review de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are obligated to determine whether AFL met the criteria for Article III standing, as this issue pertains to the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since standing was not initially raised by either side, we directed the parties to submit additional briefing on this point.

The federal “judicial Power” under Article III of the Constitution is limited to the adjudication of specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” See U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. This limitation forms the basis for the doctrine of standing. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To satisfy the requirements for Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury attributable to the defendant that is likely to be remedied by a favorable ruling from the court. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). This injury must be an “invasion of a legally protected interest” that is “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When facing a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to allege facts that, taken as true, make it plausible that each element of standing is met. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC infringed its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as established in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. See also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In an attempt to address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s failure to investigate has produced two distinct, tangible informational harms: first, that DOJ has failed to release the records that AFL sought through FOIA; and second, that OSC has not supplied the notifications and information required for an investigation under section 1214.

AFL attempts to use this characterization to benefit from the more lenient standing rules that govern claims of procedural injury. According to established precedent, “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Consequently, plaintiffs in these types of cases must still establish that the breach of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL has not met these prerequisites, nor has it even satisfied the more forgiving redressability requirement. Since AFL cannot fulfill even the relaxed standard applicable to procedural injuries, we do not need to address whether the purported injuries in this matter are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial theory of standing asserts that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has impeded AFL from obtaining the documents it sought via FOIA. This theory is fundamentally flawed due to insurmountable issues of causation and redressability. Any harm arising from DOJ’s nondisclosure of agency records is clearly attributable to DOJ itself, rather than to OSC. Furthermore, the link between OSC’s decision not to initiate a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s failure to satisfy the FOIA request is too uncertain and indirect to fulfill the requirements of Article III standing. According to AFL’s argument, had OSC conducted the ostensibly compulsory section 1216 inquiry, it might have elected to undertake corrective measures under section 1214, or to initiate disciplinary action pursuant to section 1215, targeting responsible DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, MSPB could have chosen to discipline those individuals for arbitrary FOIA denials. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following that, DOJ might have opted to release the requested documents. This sequence relies on conjecture regarding the decisions of two government bodies that are not parties to this litigation (MSPB and DOJ), yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it depends on hypothesizing about OSC’s discretionary enforcement actions, which courts typically cannot mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given this speculative progression, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable ruling “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the asserted informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Furthermore, AFL ignores the doctrine that redressability generally depends on how a ruling is binding upon the parties involved, not on whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Consequently, AFL cannot assert standing based upon its claim to the information it sought from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—premised on its claimed right to information resulting from the OSC investigation—does not succeed either. For informational standing, a plaintiff demonstrates a tangible injury when denied access to information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even presuming that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to conduct investigations of FOIA violations, it does not provide any statutory right to obtain information. AFL argues that, should OSC undertake such a mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC might elect to issue AFL notices and disclosures that would be obligatory in cases involving “prohibited personnel practices” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not argue that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notice provisions to section 1216 investigations. Indeed, it concedes precedent establishing that OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during a section 1216 inquiry is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no asserted entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL cannot demonstrate the concrete injury necessary for standing under its second theory. Similarly, if OSC is not obligated to apply section 1214 notice requirements in section 1216 proceedings, then an order requiring OSC to investigate would not likely remedy AFL’s second alleged harm.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
