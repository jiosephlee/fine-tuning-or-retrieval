Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-5168  
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation filed this action seeking an order directing the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court ruled against AFL on substantive grounds and dismissed the claims. We determine that AFL does not have Article III standing to proceed with these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the authority to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, if appropriate, initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board to obtain corrective relief pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or to seek disciplinary sanctions under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates the procedural requirements for obtaining corrective remedies for prohibited personnel practices, including mandates to update the complainant on the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to conduct investigations and pursue remedial action regarding certain other types of government employee misconduct. Specifically, as applicable here, the statute mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—meaning information that may not be withheld under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In response to such alleged FOIA violations, OSC is permitted to "investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were at issue." Id. § 1216(c).

In addition, FOIA includes a distinct provision addressing OSC enforcement. This provision provides that where a court (1) orders an agency to produce records unlawfully withheld under FOIA, (2) assesses attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly questions whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In pursuit of its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice a set of emails exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division denied this request on the questionable basis that it does not conduct searches of employee email accounts for responsive materials unless the employee provides consent. AFL subsequently filed a lawsuit against DOJ under FOIA. During this litigation, DOJ has produced—and continues to produce—documents responsive to the FOIA request. Whether this production is adequate is not a matter before the Court in this instance.

Separately, AFL requested that OSC investigate DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee email accounts absent consent. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which requires that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” when there are arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC, however, maintained that it lacked authority to conduct such an investigation because the three conditions necessary to trigger an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met. AFL asserted that, regardless of the FOIA provision, section 1216 itself obligated OSC to pursue such an investigation into the alleged FOIA policy.

The district court partially ruled in AFL’s favor but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that a claim had not been stated. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC’s authority to investigate supposed FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to conduct an investigation stemmed from a mistake of law. Id. However, the court declined to mandate that OSC conduct an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) granted that determination to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court therefore remanded the matter to OSC for additional review. Id. After OSC once again chose not to initiate an investigation, AFL requested the entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed its remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and we consider the matter anew. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before we address the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine whether AFL has established Article III standing, as this is a prerequisite for the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since standing was not initially discussed by either party, we requested additional briefing to address this issue.

The judicial authority conferred by Article III of the Constitution extends only to certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” See U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this constitutional limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). Article III requires that a plaintiff demonstrates an injury attributable to the defendant that is likely to be remedied by a favorable outcome. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must affect a “legally protected interest” in a way that is “concrete and particularized” and must be “actual or imminent,” as opposed to merely “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the stage of a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff is obligated to allege sufficient facts that, if true, would plausibly satisfy each requirement of standing. See Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached an asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, a “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish standing under Article III. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to initiate an investigation has resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, that DOJ failed to provide documents AFL sought under FOIA; and second, that OSC has not furnished the notices and other information allegedly mandated for a section 1214 investigation.

By characterizing its claim in this manner, AFL attempts to benefit from the less stringent standing criteria applicable to matters involving claimed procedural injuries. “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under this framework, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that a procedural rights violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL is unable to meet these demands, including even a more lenient test for redressability. Since AFL fails to clear the threshold even under the relaxed procedural injury standing doctrine, it is unnecessary to determine whether the alleged injuries are procedural or substantive in character.

AFL’s initial argument for standing contends that OSC’s neglect to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has obstructed AFL’s access to documents it sought via FOIA. This position is fatally deficient with respect to both causation and redressability. Any informational harm arising from DOJ’s denial of access to agency records is, without question, attributable to DOJ rather than OSC. Further, the link between OSC’s alleged omission to carry out a section 1216 inquiry and DOJ’s purported noncompliance with AFL’s FOIA request is excessively conjectural and remote to satisfy Article III standing requirements. The proposed sequence is: had OSC undertaken the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it might have elected to initiate a corrective proceeding under section 1214 or a disciplinary case under section 1215 against responsible DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have decided to penalize these individuals for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). As a result, DOJ could then have opted to release the requested documents. This hypothesis depends on assumptions regarding the independent decisions of both MSPB and DOJ—entities not named as parties in this action. However, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Moreover, this scenario necessitates conjecture concerning OSC’s exercise of its enforcement discretion, a domain courts ordinarily lack authority to mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because this hypothetical causal chain is so tenuous, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable decision in this case “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL disregards the doctrine that redressability generally depends on how a judgment compels the parties to act, not whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Thus, AFL is unable to base standing on its claimed entitlement to information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL's alternative standing argument—grounded in its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fails as well. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate a concrete injury from being denied information to which a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In the present matter, regardless of whether section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not confer a right to access any information. AFL contends that, upon conducting what it describes as a mandatory section 1216 investigation, OSC may, at its discretion, provide AFL with notices and information otherwise required under section 1214 for investigations into a “prohibited personnel practice.” Yet AFL does not maintain that OSC is compelled to impose section 1214 notice requirements upon a section 1216 investigation. In fact, AFL concedes—citing decisions such as Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)—that OSC’s choice to apply section 1214 procedural requirements in a section 1216 context is “discretionary.” As AFL does not allege any statutory right to information under section 1214, its asserted informational injury is insufficient to establish standing under its second theory. Similarly, since OSC is not required to apply section 1214’s notice provisions in section 1216 investigations, a court order compelling such an investigation would not likely remedy the alleged injury AFL describes.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
