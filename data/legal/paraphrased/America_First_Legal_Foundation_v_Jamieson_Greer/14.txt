Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

In the matter of America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer before the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, decided on October 3, 2025 (No. 24-5168), Circuit Judge Katsas writing, America First Legal Foundation initiated litigation seeking an order requiring the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency unreasonably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the action by dismissing AFL’s claims based on their substantive merits. Upon review, we determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency with investigative and prosecutorial responsibilities, created to pursue “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board, seeking corrective remedies pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures outlined in § 1214 address the mechanisms for taking corrective action against prohibited personnel practices and require, among other things, that the party who filed the complaint be kept informed about the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) the authority to investigate and pursue corrective measures regarding particular types of misconduct committed by federal employees. Pertinently, this section mandates that OSC “shall ... conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, the type of withholding not allowed by the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In situations involving such claimed FOIA violations, OSC is permitted to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA itself establishes a distinct provision addressing OSC enforcement. According to FOIA, when a court (1) orders the disclosure of agency records that were improperly withheld, (2) requires the agency to pay attorneys’ fees, and (3) explicitly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email communications between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected the request, offering the questionable rationale that employee email accounts are not searched for relevant records without the employees’ consent. Subsequently, AFL brought a FOIA action against DOJ. In that proceeding, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents in response to the request. Whether this production is adequate is not presently before this Court.

Additionally, AFL petitioned OSC to examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee email accounts absent consent. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” concerning arbitrary FOIA withholdings, AFL requested such an investigation. OSC declined, taking the position that it lacked authority because the three requirements necessary to initiate an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met. AFL, however, maintained that section 1216 independently required OSC to conduct an investigation even if FOIA did not.

The district court partially found in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action on the grounds that the complaint failed to state a claim. The court held that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not curtail the authority of OSC to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court determined that OSC’s refusal to initiate an investigation was predicated upon an erroneous interpretation of law. Id. Nevertheless, the court declined to compel OSC to commence an investigation into any FOIA-related policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) grants such discretion exclusively to OSC. Id. at *6. The court therefore remanded the matter to OSC for additional proceedings. Id. Once OSC reaffirmed its refusal to investigate, AFL sought entry of final judgment, resulting in the district court’s dismissal of its remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL filed an appeal, over which our review is plenary. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before turning to the substantive issues, we must first determine whether AFL has fulfilled the Article III standing prerequisites, as this issue concerns whether the district court possessed subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). The parties did not initially raise the standing question, prompting us to request additional briefing on the matter.

Article III of the Constitution confines federal “judicial Power” to certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises from this limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury that was caused by the defendant and is likely to be remedied by a favorable court ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must be an “invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the motion-to-dismiss phase, a plaintiff’s complaint must plausibly allege facts supporting each standing element. See Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL argues that OSC infringed upon its asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation as specified in section 1216. However, “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In response to this standing deficiency, AFL asserts that OSC’s failure to investigate has resulted in two specific informational harms: first, DOJ has not produced the documents that AFL sought under FOIA; and second, OSC has withheld the notices and information mandated in connection with a section 1214 investigation.

By presenting its argument in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing criteria applicable to claims of procedural injury. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these instances, plaintiffs are still required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural right has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet either this threshold or the more flexible redressability requirement. Since AFL does not fulfill even the relaxed standing rules for procedural harm, we do not need to resolve whether the claimed injuries are in fact procedural or substantive.

AFL advances an initial standing argument contending that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has deprived AFL of access to records sought under FOIA. This argument fails due to insurmountable problems regarding causation and redressability. To begin with, any harm relating to a lack of information caused by DOJ’s non-disclosure of agency records is directly attributable to DOJ itself, not to OSC. The link between OSC’s alleged failure to pursue a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with AFL’s FOIA request is overly tenuous and rests on speculation, and thus is inadequate to meet Article III’s standing requirements. The hypothesized chain of causation proceeds as follows: had OSC initiated the supposedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it could have opted to proceed with a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary action under section 1215 against the DOJ personnel implicated. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Afterward, MSPB could have elected to penalize those DOJ employees for arbitrary FOIA withholding. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Subsequently, DOJ could have chosen to release the requested documents. This line of reasoning relies upon conjecture concerning the discretionary decisions of two government bodies not parties to this case (MSPB and DOJ), but “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it requires hypothesizing about how OSC might decide to use its enforcement powers—an area courts usually cannot mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In view of this speculative sequence, AFL has not demonstrated that obtaining a favorable decision here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Further, AFL fails to recognize that redressability generally depends on the legal effect of a judgment upon the parties to the suit, not whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot secure standing based on its claimed interest in obtaining information from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative basis for standing, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation, is similarly unsuccessful. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, the injury must be concrete and arise from the denial of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) imposes a duty on OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not create an entitlement to the release of information. AFL argues that, should OSC carry out the allegedly obligatory investigation under section 1216, OSC might opt to provide AFL with notices and information akin to what section 1214 would require for an investigation of a supposed “prohibited personnel practice.” Nonetheless, AFL does not assert that OSC is obligated to apply section 1214’s notice provisions to section 1216 investigations. Rather, AFL concedes the existence of precedents confirming that the application of section 1214’s procedural obligations in the context of section 1216 investigations is within OSC’s discretion. See Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). In the absence of any alleged statutory right to information under section 1214, AFL cannot show a concrete injury sufficient to establish standing on this basis. Similarly, because OSC is not required to provide section 1214 notices during a section 1216 inquiry, a court order compelling such an investigation would not be likely to remedy AFL’s claimed injury under this second theory.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
