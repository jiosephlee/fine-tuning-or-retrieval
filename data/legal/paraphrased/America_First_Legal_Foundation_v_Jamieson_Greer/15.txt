Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking an order requiring the Office of Special Counsel to investigate if a federal agency wrongfully rejected AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court resolved AFL’s claims against it on substantive grounds. We find, however, that AFL does not have Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Pursuant to the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board in pursuit of corrective measures as per 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary action according to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures for taking corrective action in cases of prohibited personnel practices are detailed in Section 1214, which also outlines requirements for providing progress updates to the complainant during the course of an investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes OSC to inquire into and request remedies for certain additional types of wrongdoing by federal employees. Pertinent to this matter, Section 1216 directs that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," meaning withholdings forbidden by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases of these asserted FOIA violations, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Id. § 1216(c).

The Freedom of Information Act includes a distinct provision addressing OSC enforcement. According to this provision, if a court (1) requires disclosure of agency records that were unlawfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys' fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the question of whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," then OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding." U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice seeking specific emails exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division denied this request on the questionable basis that it refrains from searching employees’ emails for relevant documents unless the individual employee consents. Following this, AFL initiated litigation against DOJ under FOIA. In the course of that action, DOJ has already produced—and continues to provide—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of this production is not under consideration in this context.

AFL further petitioned OSC to look into DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of not searching employees’ email accounts without their consent. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC declined to investigate, asserting that the three prerequisites for a mandatory investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met regarding the alleged FOIA practice. AFL countered that section 1216 imposed an investigative obligation irrespective of FOIA’s requirements.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action on the grounds that the complaint failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate supposed FOIA infractions pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Consequently, the court found that OSC’s refusal to pursue an investigation rested on a mistake of law. Id. However, the court declined to direct OSC to investigate any FOIA-related policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) entrusts such determinations to OSC’s judgment. Id. at *6. The court then remanded the matter to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC once more refused to open an investigation, AFL requested that a final judgment be entered, leading the district court to dismiss the claims that remained. J.A. 68. AFL then took an appeal, which is now subject to our de novo review. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before turning to the substance of the case, we are first obliged to examine whether AFL has met the prerequisites for Article III standing—a matter that determines if the district court possessed subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Initially, the parties did not raise the issue of standing; consequently, we directed them to submit supplemental briefs addressing that point.

Pursuant to Article III, the Constitution limits the federal judicial power to the adjudication of certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing emerges from this case-or-controversy clause. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to have Article III standing, it must demonstrate that it has sustained an injury traceable to the defendant, which is likely to be remedied by a favorable judicial outcome. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must amount to “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent,” rather than speculative or hypothetical. Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the stage of a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to plead facts that plausibly establish every component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL contends that OSC infringed its asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation as provided by section 1216. However, merely being deprived of a procedural right, without a corresponding concrete interest being impacted—a “procedural right in vacuo”—does not suffice to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In an attempt to address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific informational harms: First, the Department of Justice has withheld records AFL sought via FOIA; second, OSC failed to furnish the notifications and information mandated for an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

By presenting its argument in this way, AFL aims to benefit from the more lenient standing standards applicable in cases of asserted procedural injury. According to the law, “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In this context, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet these prerequisites, nor does it satisfy even the relaxed standard concerning redressability. Given that AFL fails to fulfill even these mitigated standing criteria related to procedural harms, we do not address whether these injuries should properly be categorized as procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial standing argument posits that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has deprived AFL of access to records requested under FOIA. This position is undermined by fundamental flaws relating to causation and redressability. Any claimed informational harm arising from DOJ’s refusal to provide agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, not OSC. Further, the causal relationship between OSC’s failure to pursue a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is excessively tenuous and speculative to fulfill Article III standing requirements. The purported chain of causation proceeds as follows: Had OSC undertaken the allegedly obligatory investigation under section 1216, it could have opted to initiate either a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary action under section 1215 against responsible DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have decided to penalize those individuals for unjustified FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Afterwards, DOJ potentially could have released the records sought. This sequence depends on conjecture about decisions made by two non-party governmental bodies—the MSPB and DOJ—while “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also presumes how OSC would exercise its enforcement discretion, which courts are generally not empowered to direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because this hypothetical sequence is speculative, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable decision here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). In addition, AFL ignores that redressability typically depends on how a court’s ruling obligates the parties, not whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot rely on its desire for the records requested from DOJ under FOIA to demonstrate standing.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing, premised on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation, is equally unavailing. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, they must demonstrate a tangible injury resulting from the denial of information that a statute guarantees them. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). Here, however, even if section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, the provision does not confer a right to obtain information. AFL argues that, should OSC carry out a mandatory investigation under section 1216, the agency might opt to provide it with notifications and information akin to those required for investigations of a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Yet, AFL does not assert that OSC is required to apply the notification provisions from section 1214 to investigations conducted under section 1216. In fact, AFL concedes the existence of precedent indicating that OSC’s application of section 1214’s procedures to section 1216 matters remains a “discretionary” decision. See Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Since there is no claimed right to receive information under section 1214, AFL cannot demonstrate the concrete injury needed for this theory of standing. Similarly, because OSC is not obligated to furnish section 1214 notices in the context of a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation would be unlikely to redress the purported injury associated with AFL’s second argument for standing.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
