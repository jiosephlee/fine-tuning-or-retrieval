America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to determine whether a federal agency wrongfully rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court entered judgment on the merits in favor of the defendants, dismissing AFL’s claims. We hold, however, that AFL does not possess Article III standing to bring these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). The Civil Service Reform Act authorizes the OSC to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, where appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board, seeking either corrective relief under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines the process for addressing corrective actions involving prohibited personnel practices and includes requirements for keeping the complainant apprised of the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes OSC to examine and pursue remedies for certain additional types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, the statute mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, withholdings not allowed by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In instances of alleged FOIA violations, OSC is permitted to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

The Freedom of Information Act itself has a distinct mechanism for OSC enforcement. Specifically, FOIA provides that when a court (1) orders that an agency must disclose records that were improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly questions whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In pursuit of its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice certain email communications between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division rejected this request on the questionable basis that it refrains from searching employee emails for relevant records unless it obtains the employee’s consent. Following this, AFL brought a FOIA action against DOJ. In the context of that litigation, DOJ has already produced—and continues to produce—documents that are responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s production is not the subject of this matter.

Additionally, AFL requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA policy of declining to search employee emails without their consent. AFL relied upon section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary withholdings under FOIA. OSC asserted it lacked authority to proceed with an investigation because the three prerequisites required to initiate such an inquiry under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were unmet. AFL, for its part, maintained that section 1216 independently obligated OSC to conduct an investigation regardless of FOIA’s provisions.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to examine alleged FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was premised on a legal mistake. Id. Despite this finding, the court refused to mandate that OSC investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) entrusted such determinations to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The case was remanded to OSC for additional review. Id. When OSC once again opted not to investigate, AFL sought entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and our review proceeds de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue affects the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since neither side initially briefed standing, we requested additional submissions specifically on this point.

Article III of the Constitution limits the federal “judicial Power” to the adjudication of certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing derives from this case-or-controversy limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish standing under Article III, a plaintiff must show that it has sustained an injury that was caused by the defendant and that is likely to be remedied by a favorable court decision. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The asserted injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the motion to dismiss stage, the plaintiff is required to allege facts that plausibly support each component of standing. See Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC breached what it contends is its procedural right to an investigation as provided by section 1216. However, as established in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish standing under Article III; see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this obstacle, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two particularized, informational harms: first, DOJ has failed to disclose the documents AFL sought under FOIA; second, OSC has withheld the notifications and information that section 1214 requires to be provided during an investigation.

By presenting the case in this manner, AFL attempts to rely on the more lenient standing standards that are applied when procedural injuries are claimed. According to Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Therefore, plaintiffs in these situations are required to demonstrate that a procedural rights violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither these criteria nor even the less stringent standard regarding redressability. As AFL cannot satisfy the even more permissive procedural injury standing requirements, we do not need to resolve whether the injuries asserted are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial argument for standing asserts that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has hindered AFL from obtaining the documents it sought under FOIA. This position, however, is undermined by insurmountable problems relating to causation and redressability. Primarily, any harm AFL may claim from DOJ’s denial of agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, not OSC. Moreover, any alleged link between OSC’s lack of a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s failure to fulfill the FOIA request is too remote and conjectural to meet the requirements of Article III standing. The supposed chain of causation proceeds as follows: Had OSC initiated the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it potentially could have pursued corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ personnel implicated in the matter. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have opted to impose sanctions on those employees for arbitrary FOIA denials. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Then, DOJ might have decided to release the requested documents. This scenario necessitates conjecture regarding decisions by two independent government agencies that are not parties to this case (MSPB and DOJ), yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, it entails speculation about how OSC would exercise its enforcement discretion, which courts are typically not empowered to direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Due to this speculative series of events, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable court ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL disregards the general principle that redressability focuses on the binding effect of a judgment on the parties, not whether the reasoning behind the judgment “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). For all these reasons, AFL is unable to establish standing premised on its asserted informational interest in the records it sought from DOJ through FOIA.

AFL's alternative standing argument, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC's investigation, is equally unavailing. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff must show a concrete injury resulting from the denial of information that a statute expressly grants the right to receive. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this case, however, even assuming that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not confer a right to receive information. AFL argues that, if OSC performs the purportedly mandatory investigation under section 1216, it might elect to provide AFL with the notices and information that section 1214 would require if OSC were investigating a “prohibited personnel practice.” Nonetheless, AFL does not argue that OSC is required to apply section 1214 notice provisions in section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes that precedent holds OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without a claim of entitlement to information under section 1214, AFL does not demonstrate a concrete injury adequate to establish its second standing theory. Similarly, since OSC is not obligated to provide section 1214 notices in section 1216 investigations, a court order compelling such an investigation would not be likely to remedy AFL’s asserted injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
