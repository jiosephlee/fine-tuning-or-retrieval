Case: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Case No.: 24-5168  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation filed this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to determine if a federal agency unreasonably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the matter by dismissing AFL’s claims on substantive grounds. We find that AFL does not possess Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as both an investigative and prosecutorial body, created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the authority to examine claims of “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board seeking either corrective action pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Procedures applicable to corrective actions for prohibited personnel practices are outlined in section 1214, which also includes requirements that the OSC notify the complainant of developments in the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to investigate and pursue remedial measures for certain additional types of employee misconduct in the government. Pertinent to the present matter, it stipulates that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," which refers to withholdings in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases involving alleged FOIA violations, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes a distinct mechanism authorizing OSC to take enforcement action. According to the statute, when a court (1) mandates the release of agency records found to have been improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC is then required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific emails exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division refused this request, claiming—improperly, in AFL’s view—that it does not conduct searches of employee email accounts for responsive documents without obtaining the employee’s consent. As a result, AFL filed a FOIA action against DOJ. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has already provided, and continues to provide, documents that respond to the request. The adequacy of DOJ’s production is not presently before the Court.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee emails absent their approval. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), asserting that this provision requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” regarding any arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC asserted it lacked jurisdiction to pursue an investigation because the three conditions specified in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been fulfilled. Nonetheless, AFL maintained that section 1216 imposes an independent investigatory obligation, regardless of FOIA’s requirements.

The district court partially found in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate stemmed from a legal mistake. Id. However, the court refused to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy because, as it saw it, section 1216(c) places that decision within OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court sent the matter back to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC once more chose not to investigate, AFL requested entry of final judgment, after which the district court dismissed its outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL filed an appeal, which we review without deference. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine if AFL has met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue relates to the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because the parties did not initially discuss standing, we directed them to submit additional briefing on this topic.

Article III of the Constitution confines the authority of the federal judiciary to adjudicating only those “Cases” and “Controversies” specified therein. U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The principle of standing is derived from this limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order to establish standing under Article III, a plaintiff must demonstrate that they have suffered an injury attributable to the defendant and that this injury is likely to be remedied through a favorable court ruling. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The injury in question must constitute an “invasion of a legally protected interest,” be “concrete and particularized,” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When facing a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff is required to present allegations of fact that sufficiently support each element necessary for standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL contends that OSC breached what it claims is a procedural entitlement to an investigation as outlined in section 1216. However, mere denial of a procedural right, without an accompanying concrete interest impacted by such denial—what the Supreme Court termed a “procedural right in vacuo”—does not establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In order to address this deficiency, AFL asserts that OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two distinct, tangible informational injuries: First, the Department of Justice has not supplied AFL with the documents it sought under FOIA. Second, OSC has failed to furnish the notifications and information mandated during an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

AFL, by framing its argument in this way, attempts to take advantage of the more lenient standing standards applicable to claims alleging procedural injury. According to the court in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—though do not entirely dispense with—the requirements of imminence and redressability, but not those of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In scenarios such as these, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the infringement of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither this standard nor a more permissive standard with respect to redressability. Given AFL’s inability to fulfill even the relaxed procedural injury standing requirements, there is no need for us to determine whether the asserted injuries qualify as procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s primary argument for standing asserts that OSC’s omission in investigating DOJ under section 1216 has deprived AFL of the FOIA records it sought. This line of reasoning fails due to critical defects in causation and redressability. Primarily, any informational harm arising from DOJ’s denial to release agency records can only be directly attributed to DOJ, not OSC. Furthermore, the purported causal link between OSC’s failure to initiate a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s refusal to honor the FOIA request is excessively conjectural and remote to satisfy the requirements of Article III standing. The theorized sequence is as follows: If OSC had carried out what AFL alleges is a compulsory section 1216 investigation, OSC might have chosen to commence corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary proceedings under section 1215 against DOJ personnel deemed to have acted improperly. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Following this, the MSPB might have elected to impose sanctions on those DOJ employees for unjustified FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Afterward, DOJ could then conceivably decide to release the sought-after documents. However, this sequence demands speculation about decisions by two nonparty government entities—the MSPB and DOJ—which is impermissible, as “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also involves conjecture about how OSC might use its enforcement discretion, a matter courts are typically prohibited from compelling. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of these speculative steps, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable ruling would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL disregards the rule that redressability generally concerns how a judgment impacts the parties involved in the case, rather than whether it “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL is unable to ground its standing on the interest in records it sought from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL's alternative argument for standing, premised on its purported informational interest in the outcome of the OSC's investigation, is similarly unavailing. For a plaintiff to have informational standing, they must experience a tangible injury from being deprived of information to which a "statute entitled him to receive." Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this case, however, even accepting that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to look into FOIA violations, the statute does not confer any right to obtain information. AFL argues that, should OSC undertake the investigation allegedly required under section 1216, it may elect to provide AFL with notices and information similar to what section 1214 necessitates for inquiries into "prohibited personnel practice." However, AFL does not maintain that OSC is required to impose section 1214 notification provisions onto section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes existing precedent which finds that OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during a section 1216 inquiry remains “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no asserted right to information under section 1214, AFL cannot claim a particularized injury sufficient to support this standing theory. Similarly, because OSC is not required to meet section 1214 notification standards in the context of a section 1216 investigation, court-ordered investigation would not plausibly redress AFL’s second alleged harm.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to the absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
