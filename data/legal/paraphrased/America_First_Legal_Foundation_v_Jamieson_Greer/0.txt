Case Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 24-5168  
Opinion by Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency unreasonably refused AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court resolved AFL’s claims against it on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not have Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency responsible for investigating and prosecuting matters in pursuit of “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board seeking either corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines the process for implementing corrective actions in cases of prohibited personnel practices, including requirements to update the complainant regarding the status of any ongoing investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for certain types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this matter, the section directs OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” meaning instances where information is withheld in violation of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases involving such claimed violations of FOIA, OSC has the authority to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were at issue.” Id. § 1216(c).

Additionally, FOIA itself establishes a distinct procedure for OSC enforcement. Specifically, it provides that if a court (1) orders the release of agency records that were improperly withheld in violation of FOIA, (2) imposes attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) explicitly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice a set of emails exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contended that the Civil Division denied this request on the questionable basis that employee emails are not searched for relevant records unless the particular employee consents. AFL responded by filing a lawsuit against DOJ under FOIA. In that action, DOJ has already provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s production is not contested in this matter.

Additionally, AFL petitioned OSC to look into DOJ’s purported FOIA policy of refraining from searching employee emails without their approval. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary withholdings under FOIA, AFL argued for OSC’s involvement. However, OSC declined to assert jurisdiction, concluding that the prerequisites for a mandatory investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not present with respect to the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that section 1216 did not compel an investigation absent the conditions set forth in FOIA.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed its case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate rested on a misinterpretation of the law. Id. Nevertheless, the court declined to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) leaves that determination to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court therefore remanded the matter to OSC for additional review. Id. After OSC again chose not to conduct an investigation, AFL sought entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed the rest of its claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and our review proceeds de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, we must first determine whether AFL met the criteria for Article III standing, as this issue impacts the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Initially, neither party raised the issue of standing, prompting us to request additional briefing on that matter.

The Constitution’s Article III confines the federal judiciary’s authority to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises from this case-or-controversy limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must show an injury caused by the defendant that a favorable court decision would likely remedy. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such injury must amount to “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized,” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the motion-to-dismiss stage, plaintiffs are required to assert facts that plausibly establish each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, that the DOJ has withheld the records AFL sought via FOIA; and second, that OSC failed to furnish the notices and information mandated by section 1214 as part of an investigation.

By presenting its argument in this way, AFL aims to benefit from the more lenient standing standards that pertain to claims of procedural injury. According to precedent, “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs are still required to demonstrate that breaching a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither this standard nor even the more permissive requirement of redressability. Since AFL does not satisfy the less stringent standing criteria applicable to procedural injuries, we do not reach the question of whether the alleged injuries are procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial standing argument asserts that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has deprived AFL of access to records it sought via FOIA. This contention is flawed due to insurmountable issues regarding both causation and redressability. To begin with, any harm to AFL arising from DOJ’s refusal to disclose records under FOIA is directly attributable to DOJ itself, not OSC. Further, the supposed link between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with AFL’s FOIA request is overly tenuous and conjectural to satisfy Article III’s requirements. The hypothesized sequence proceeds as follows: Had OSC carried out the allegedly required section 1216 inquiry, OSC might then have opted to initiate corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ personnel deemed culpable. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Thereafter, the MSPB might have imposed penalties on those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Ultimately, DOJ could have elected to release the records in question. However, this sequence necessitates conjecture about decisions by two governmental bodies not present in this suit (namely, MSPB and DOJ), yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it assumes OSC’s discretionary enforcement choices, which are generally beyond judicial compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because of this speculative causal progression, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable ruling would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards that redressability typically depends on the extent to which a judgment binds the litigants, not whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL fails to show standing on the basis of its claimed entitlement to information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL's alternative argument for standing, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation, is equally unavailing. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff must show a tangible injury resulting from being deprived of information that a statute grants them a right to obtain. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this instance, however, even if section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not confer a right to obtain information. AFL argues that, should OSC undertake the purportedly obligatory investigation under section 1216, it might opt to provide AFL with notices and details that would be mandated if OSC were pursuing an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” pursuant to section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not assert that OSC is required to incorporate section 1214’s notification requirements into section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes precedent holding that OSC’s choice to apply section 1214’s procedural standards in a section 1216 investigation remains “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL asserts no right to information under section 1214, it cannot demonstrate the concrete injury necessary to sustain its second theory of standing. Similarly, because OSC is not compelled to apply section 1214 notification requirements in section 1216 investigations, a court order requiring such an investigation would not likely redress the second alleged injury claimed by AFL.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
