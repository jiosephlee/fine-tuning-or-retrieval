GINSBURG, Senior Circuit Judge.

Opinion for the Court filed by Circuit Judge KATSAS.

KATSAS, Circuit Judge: America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency unjustifiably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court ruled against AFL’s claims on substantive grounds. We determine, however, that AFL does not have Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to ensure “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the authority granted by the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC is tasked with investigating “prohibited personnel practices.” If its investigation determines further action is necessary, OSC may initiate enforcement cases before the Merit Systems Protection Board to secure corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or pursue disciplinary sanctions in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates the procedural framework for addressing prohibited personnel practices, which includes requirements for keeping complainants apprised of the investigation’s status. See, for instance, 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for specific additional categories of employee misconduct within the government. Pertinently, it mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, instances of information being withheld in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When such alleged breaches of FOIA occur, OSC is authorized to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were implicated.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes a distinct section dedicated to OSC enforcement. According to this provision, when a court (1) directs the release of agency records that were wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) imposes attorneys’ fees on the agency, and (3) expressly raises concerns regarding whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice seeking specific email correspondence exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division rejected this request on questionable grounds, namely, that it refrains from searching employee email accounts for relevant documents without first obtaining the consent of those employees. Following this denial, AFL filed a lawsuit against DOJ pursuant to FOIA. In response to the litigation, DOJ has already provided—and continues to provide—documents that are responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of this ongoing production is not a matter for consideration in this context.

AFL further requested that OSC examine the DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refusing to search employee emails without their permission. Citing section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” into arbitrary FOIA denials, AFL emphasized this statutory duty. However, OSC declined to initiate an investigation, asserting that the prerequisites outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) necessary to commence such an inquiry had not been met in relation to the alleged FOIA policy. OSC also maintained that even though AFL referenced section 1216, it did not obligate OSC to investigate if the conditions prescribed by FOIA itself were lacking.

The district court partially ruled in AFL’s favor but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a viable claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not curtail OSC’s authority to pursue investigations of purported FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate stemmed from an erroneous interpretation of the law. Id. However, the court declined to mandate that OSC investigate any FOIA-related policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) vests such discretion with OSC. Id. at *6. The matter was sent back to OSC for additional review. Id. When OSC again opted not to investigate, AFL requested entry of a final judgment, after which the district court dismissed the remainder of its claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and we now conduct a de novo review. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine if AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue is central to the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since neither side originally discussed standing, we directed the parties to submit additional briefs specifically on this matter.

Article III of the Constitution confines the exercise of federal “judicial Power” to the resolution of particular “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises from this constitutional case-or-controversy limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to meet the requirements of Article III standing, there must be a demonstration of an injury that is traceable to the conduct of the defendant and capable of being remedied by a favorable court ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” and must be both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the stage where a motion to dismiss is considered, the plaintiff is required to allege facts that plausibly establish each element necessary for standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation as provided by section 1216. However, mere deprivation of a procedural right, absent any concrete interest adversely affected by such deprivation—a procedural right in isolation—does not satisfy the requirements for Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In an effort to address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to pursue an investigation has resulted in two specific, tangible informational injuries: namely, that DOJ has failed to disclose the records requested by AFL under FOIA, and that OSC has withheld the notices and information mandated during the course of an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

By framing its claim in this manner, AFL attempts to take advantage of the more permissive standing standards applicable in cases asserting procedural injury. In these instances, when plaintiffs contend that a statutory procedural right—enacted to safeguard their specific, tangible interest—has been violated, courts tend to ease, but not eliminate entirely, the traditional requirements concerning imminence and redressability. However, courts maintain the requirements of injury in fact and causation. See Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Plaintiffs in such procedural injury cases must still establish that the infringement of a procedural right has produced harm to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. Here, AFL fails to meet these obligations, including even the diminished threshold for redressability. Since AFL falls short of satisfying the procedural injury standing prerequisites, we need not resolve the distinction between procedural and substantive injuries in this context.

AFL’s primary theory is that OSC’s alleged neglect in initiating an investigation under section 1216 has ultimately resulted in AFL being deprived of the records it sought from DOJ through a FOIA request. This line of reasoning, however, is fundamentally flawed with respect to both causation and redressability. To begin with, any purported informational harm stemming from DOJ’s denial of access to agency documents is clearly attributable to DOJ, not to OSC. Furthermore, the hypothetical link between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s decision not to fulfill the FOIA request is far too conjectural and indirect to fulfill the requirements for Article III standing. The speculative sequence postulated by AFL is as follows: Had OSC conducted the allegedly obligatory investigation pursuant to section 1216, OSC might have opted to initiate corrective action under section 1214, or disciplinary measures under section 1215, against the DOJ personnel implicated. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could potentially have imposed sanctions on those employees for unjustified withholding of records under FOIA. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). As a result, DOJ might then have elected to release the requested documents. However, this hypothetical scenario rests on assumptions regarding the actions of two entities not party to the present litigation—the MSPB and DOJ—whereas “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (internal quotation marks omitted). Additionally, it hinges on conjecture regarding how OSC might deploy its enforcement authority, a domain in which judicial intervention is generally prohibited. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of such uncertainty, AFL cannot demonstrate that a favorable outcome in this case “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also neglects the well-established principle that redressability is typically a function of whether a judgment is binding on the actual parties to the lawsuit, not on whether its rationale “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL is unable to ground its standing on the asserted interest in the information it requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative basis for standing, which rests on an alleged informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation, proves equally unpersuasive. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, there must be a tangible harm stemming from the denial of information that a statute affirmatively grants the plaintiff the right to obtain. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In the present case, however, even assuming that section 1216(a)(3) imposes an obligation on OSC to investigate FOIA violations, the provision does not confer upon AFL a right to obtain information. AFL counters by arguing that, were OSC to undertake the mandatory investigation purportedly required by section 1216, it might at its discretion furnish AFL with notices and information analogous to those mandated during “prohibited personnel practice” investigations under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL stops short of arguing that OSC is compelled to incorporate the procedural notice requirements of section 1214 into section 1216 proceedings. In fact, AFL concedes precedent establishing that OSC retains discretion with respect to applying section 1214’s procedural framework to section 1216 cases. See Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no claim of a statutory right to information under section 1214, AFL cannot demonstrate a cognizable injury supporting its informational standing theory. For the same reasons, since OSC is not required to extend section 1214 notice procedures to section 1216 inquiries, a court order compelling a section 1216 investigation would not be likely to remedy the informational injury asserted by AFL.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
