Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency unreasonably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the matter against AFL on substantive grounds. We determine, however, that AFL does not possess Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body, created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC holds the authority to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, if appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board, seeking corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary remedies under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the procedures for obtaining corrective relief in cases of prohibited personnel practices, including requirements for updating the complainant regarding the status of an ongoing investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC the authority to investigate and pursue corrective measures for particular categories of misconduct committed by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, the statute directs the OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, information withheld in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When such FOIA violations are alleged, the OSC is authorized to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

Additionally, FOIA includes an independent provision regarding OSC enforcement. According to this provision, if a court (1) orders the release of agency records that were improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the question of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then the OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice certain email communications exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected this request on the questionable basis that it does not search its employees’ email accounts for relevant records unless the employee provides consent. Subsequently, AFL brought a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ. In that litigation, DOJ has released—and continues to release—documents responsive to the request. The adequacy of these disclosures is not presently before the Court.

Additionally, AFL requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee email accounts without their consent. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” in instances of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC, however, asserted that it lacked authority to investigate because the three prerequisites outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met. AFL contended that section 1216 compelled OSC to conduct such an investigation regardless of whether FOIA itself required one.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC’s authority to look into alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Consequently, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was predicated on a mistake of law. Id. However, the court refused to require OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, concluding that section 1216(c) leaves such a decision to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court sent the matter back to OSC for additional consideration. Id. After OSC once more declined to investigate, AFL requested the entry of a final judgment, and the district court dismissed the rest of its claims. J.A. 68. AFL then filed an appeal, which we review without deference. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine whether AFL fulfilled the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this is essential to the district court's subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since standing was not initially discussed by either side, we directed the parties to provide additional briefing on that matter.

Article III of the Constitution confines the federal judiciary’s authority to adjudicating particular “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The standing doctrine stems from this case-or-controversy limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to demonstrate Article III standing, they must establish that they have suffered an injury that was caused by the defendant and is likely to be remedied by a favorable judgment. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The alleged injury must amount to “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When a court considers a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must set forth factual allegations that plausibly support each aspect of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL argues that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this issue, AFL asserts that OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two specific, informational injuries: first, the DOJ has failed to release the documents that AFL requested under FOIA; second, OSC has not delivered the notices and information mandated for an investigation under section 1214.

By characterizing its claim in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards applicable in cases asserting procedural injuries. According to the D.C. Circuit, “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under this standard, plaintiffs are still required to demonstrate that the infringement of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet these prerequisites, nor does it satisfy even a lowered redressability threshold. Since AFL fails to clear the relaxed standing bar for procedural injuries, it is unnecessary for us to determine whether the asserted injuries are properly characterized as procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial theory of standing asserts that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has deprived AFL of access to records it sought under FOIA. However, this argument is fundamentally flawed due to insurmountable issues with causation and redressability. Any injury related to information stemming from DOJ’s denial to release agency records can be directly attributed to DOJ, not to OSC. Moreover, the link between OSC’s lack of a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s failure to respond to the FOIA request is too tenuous and hypothetical to meet Article III standing requirements. The purported causal sequence posits that, had OSC undertaken the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, OSC might have opted to initiate corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action pursuant to section 1215 against DOJ employees alleged to be at fault. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have elected to penalize those employees for arbitrary withholding of FOIA materials. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following this, DOJ could potentially have released the sought-after records. This scenario depends on conjecture about decisions by two independent government bodies—the MSPB and DOJ—that are not participants in this litigation. Yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it relies on speculation regarding how OSC would utilize its enforcement discretion, an area in which courts typically lack the authority to compel action. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of this speculative series of events, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable outcome here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Furthermore, AFL disregards the rule that redressability is ordinarily determined by the manner in which a judgment constrains the parties to the case, rather than whether the decision’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Consequently, AFL is unable to base standing on its claimed interest in obtaining the information it requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing, which relies on its claimed right to information generated by the OSC’s investigation, is equally unpersuasive. A plaintiff establishes informational standing only if denied access to information that a statute guarantees. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this instance, however, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) obligates the OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not grant any right to obtain information. AFL argues that, if the OSC proceeds with what it describes as the mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC might elect to furnish AFL with notices and information otherwise required during investigations into “prohibited personnel practices” under section 1214. However, AFL stops short of asserting that OSC is obligated to apply section 1214’s notice provisions to section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes that courts have determined OSC’s application of section 1214 procedures to section 1216 investigations is discretionary. See Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Since there is no claimed right to information under section 1214 in this context, AFL cannot establish the concrete injury needed to substantiate its second theory of standing. Similarly, because OSC is not required to provide section 1214 notices during a section 1216 investigation, compelling OSC by court order to conduct such an investigation would not likely remedy the second injury AFL alleges.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
