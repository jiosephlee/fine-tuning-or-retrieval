Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking an order requiring the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency had arbitrarily rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court disposed of AFL’s claims on their substantive merits. We determine that AFL does not have Article III standing to assert these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC is authorized to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, if appropriate, to initiate proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board to seek either corrective relief pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines the procedural requirements for obtaining corrective measures in response to prohibited personnel practices, including mandates for informing the complainant regarding the status of the OSC’s investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for certain additional types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, the section mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” referring to withholdings forbidden by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). For alleged FOIA violations, OSC is authorized to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA itself provides a distinct provision for OSC enforcement. This provision specifies that if a court (1) directs the release of agency records that were improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email communications exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contended that the Civil Division denied this request based on the questionable justification that employee emails are not searched for responsive materials without the employee’s approval. Subsequently, AFL initiated a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—records responsive to the request. The adequacy of DOJ’s document production is not in dispute in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refraining from searching employee emails in the absence of their consent. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary withholdings under FOIA. OSC, however, asserted it lacked authority to investigate because the three necessary conditions to trigger an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. AFL countered that section 1216 obligated OSC to investigate the alleged FOIA policy, irrespective of FOIA’s provisions.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court stated that OSC’s refusal to investigate was predicated on a mistake of law. Id. However, the court refused to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) grants that determination to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for additional consideration. Id. After OSC once more chose not to investigate, AFL requested final judgment, after which the district court dismissed the remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and our review proceeds de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, we must first determine whether AFL has met the requirements for standing under Article III, as this issue concerns the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because the parties did not initially discuss standing, we required additional briefing specifically on this matter.

Article III of the Constitution confines the exercise of federal judicial authority to the resolution of specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing derives from this limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to establish Article III standing, it must demonstrate an injury that is attributable to the defendant and is likely to be remedied by a favorable court ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The injury asserted must be “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When facing a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff is required to plead facts that plausibly establish each element necessary for standing. See Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation as provided by section 1216. However, “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” for establishing Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific, informational harms: First, that DOJ has not produced the records AFL sought via FOIA; and second, that OSC has not supplied the notices and information required for an investigation under section 1214.

By framing the issue this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards applicable in matters alleging procedural injuries. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these instances, plaintiffs are nonetheless required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural protection has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet these criteria, nor does it satisfy even the less stringent redressability standard. Since AFL is unable to fulfill the relaxed standing requirements for procedural injuries, there is no need for us to determine whether the alleged injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial argument for standing is that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has prevented AFL from obtaining the documents it sought through FOIA. However, this rationale fails due to insurmountable causation and redressability obstacles. Specifically, any informational harm caused by DOJ’s refusal to release agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, not OSC. Furthermore, any supposed link between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is overly conjectural and too remote to satisfy Article III standing requirements. The suggested causal sequence posits: If OSC had conducted the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it could have opted to initiate either a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ employees involved. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have imposed sanctions on those employees for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). This, in turn, might have led DOJ to release the requested documents. Yet, this scenario requires conjecture about decisions made by two non-party government entities (the MSPB and DOJ), and “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also involves speculation as to how OSC might exercise its enforcement discretion, which courts are generally not empowered to mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given this highly speculative chain of events, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable decision here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its asserted informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Moreover, AFL disregards the settled rule that redressability is ordinarily determined by how a judgment binds the actual parties, not whether the court’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot establish standing based on its asserted right to information requested from DOJ pursuant to FOIA.

AFL's alternative argument for standing—predicated on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fares no better. To demonstrate informational standing, a plaintiff must show a tangible injury resulting from the denial of information to which a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this instance, even assuming that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not grant a right to access any information. AFL argues that, should OSC undertake the allegedly mandatory investigation pursuant to section 1216, it might elect to provide AFL with the notifications and details that would be required if the investigation concerned an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. However, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to apply the notice requirements of section 1214 to its investigations under section 1216. Indeed, AFL concedes that judicial precedent recognizes OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in the context of a section 1216 investigation as “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL alleges no right to information under section 1214, it fails to demonstrate a concrete injury for purposes of its second standing argument. Similarly, since OSC is not required to adhere to section 1214 notice requirements during a section 1216 investigation, any court order compelling such an investigation would not likely remedy AFL’s second claimed injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
