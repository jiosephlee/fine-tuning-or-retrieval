Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation filed this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency unjustifiably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court entered a judgment against AFL’s claims. We determine that AFL does not have Article III standing to assert these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Through the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC is authorized to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, if appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board, seeking either corrective remedies under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines the procedures applicable to securing corrective measures for prohibited personnel practices, which include requirements to apprise the complainant of the status of an investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes OSC to examine and pursue remedies for certain additional types of government employee misconduct. Pertinent to this case, the section stipulates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—referring to unlawful withholdings under the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). Regarding these alleged violations of FOIA, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides a distinct mechanism for OSC enforcement. According to its terms, if a court (1) directs that agency records wrongfully withheld under FOIA be disclosed, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) explicitly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific emails from the Department of Justice, namely communications between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected the request on the questionable basis that it does not examine employee emails for responsive documents without obtaining the individual employee’s consent. Subsequently, AFL initiated a FOIA action against DOJ. In that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents that are responsive to AFL's request. The adequacy of this document production is not the subject of the present matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refraining from searching employee emails absent their consent. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary withholdings under FOIA. However, OSC asserted that it lacked jurisdiction to investigate since the three conditions necessary to require an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met concerning the alleged FOIA policy. AFL maintained that section 1216 required OSC to undertake such an investigation, irrespective of whether FOIA itself did so.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate alleged FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was premised on an error of law. Id. Nevertheless, the court refused to compel OSC to undertake any investigation into a FOIA policy, reasoning that under section 1216(c), that determination is entrusted to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court sent the matter back to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC again opted not to investigate, AFL sought final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss AFL’s remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and this court now reviews the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are obligated to examine whether AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this determines whether the district court possessed subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since neither side initially raised the issue of standing, we requested additional briefing to address this matter.

The Constitution’s Article III confines the federal judiciary’s authority to adjudicating specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing stems from this requirement. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish standing under Article III, a plaintiff must show that they experienced an injury attributed to the defendant that a favorable court ruling is likely to remedy. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” and be “concrete and particularized,” as well as “actual or imminent,” rather than speculative or hypothetical. Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When facing a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to plead facts that make it plausible that each standing element is met. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL contends that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural right to an investigation as provided by section 1216. However, merely being denied a procedural right, without a tangible interest impacted by this denial—a procedural right “in vacuo”—does not establish standing under Article III. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s failure to conduct an investigation resulted in two specific informational injuries: first, the Department of Justice has not furnished the records that AFL sought via FOIA; and second, OSC has failed to provide the notifications and details required in the course of an investigation under section 1214.

By presenting its case this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards that govern matters involving claims of procedural harm. As explained in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these instances, the plaintiffs must nonetheless demonstrate that breaching a procedural right has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL, however, fails to meet these standards—including the eased requirement for redressability—meaning it does not even clear the threshold for relaxed procedural injury standing. Accordingly, it is unnecessary for us to address whether the alleged harms are of a procedural or substantive nature.

AFL advances as its initial standing argument that OSC’s alleged noncompliance with section 1216—by not investigating DOJ—prevented AFL from obtaining records it sought pursuant to FOIA. This argument, however, is fundamentally flawed due to insurmountable issues of causation and redressability. To begin with, any harm resulting from DOJ’s decision to withhold records requested under FOIA is directly attributable to DOJ and not to OSC. Further, any claimed link between OSC’s failure to investigate pursuant to section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is overly tenuous and speculative to suffice for Article III standing. The sequence AFL proposes is: had OSC initiated the allegedly required investigation under section 1216, it might have chosen to take corrective or disciplinary action against the relevant DOJ employees pursuant to sections 1214 and 1215, respectively. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might then have determined to impose sanctions on DOJ employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following this, DOJ could have then opted to release the requested documents. This line of reasoning assumes discretionary actions by two governmental entities not present before the Court—the MSPB and DOJ—yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Moreover, it relies on conjecture regarding how OSC would utilize its enforcement authority, discretion the courts generally cannot mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Due to this highly conjectural chain, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a ruling in its favor “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards the fundamental point that redressability is generally tied to the binding effect of a judgment on the actual parties to the lawsuit, rather than whether the decision’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot base standing on its asserted entitlement to the information requested from DOJ through FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—predicated on its claimed right to access the results of the OSC investigation—fares no better. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff experiences a concrete injury only when deprived of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even presuming section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, this provision does not confer any right to obtain information. AFL contends that, were OSC to undertake the purportedly required investigation pursuant to section 1216, it might elect to provide AFL notices and information akin to those mandated when OSC investigates an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not argue that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notice requirements to section 1216 investigations. Indeed, AFL concedes, with reference to precedent, that OSC’s decision “whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” during a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no claimed right to information under section 1214, AFL cannot establish the kind of concrete injury required for this theory of standing. Moreover, since OSC is not required to adhere to section 1214 notice requirements within section 1216 investigations, any court order compelling such an investigation would not likely remedy AFL’s asserted informational injury.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss for absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
