Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency improperly refused AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved AFL’s claims by dismissing them on their merits. We determine that AFL does not have Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as both an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). The Civil Service Reform Act grants OSC authority to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board for either corrective relief under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or for disciplinary measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines the procedures for pursuing corrective remedies for such personnel practices, and includes requirements for keeping the complainant informed on the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes OSC to examine and pursue remedies for additional types of misconduct committed by federal employees. Pertinently, it stipulates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” which refers to withholdings in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). For FOIA violations of this kind, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides an independent mechanism for enforcement by OSC. According to its terms, when a court (1) directs the release of agency records that have been wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email communications between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division declined to fulfill the request based on the questionable justification that it does not examine employee emails for responsive materials absent the employee’s approval. Subsequently, AFL brought a FOIA action against DOJ. In that litigation, DOJ has released—and continues to release—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of these disclosures is not presently before the Court.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported practice under FOIA of declining to search employee emails unless authorization was given. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary withholding of FOIA materials. However, OSC maintained that it lacked jurisdiction to investigate, as the three preconditions outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. OSC asserted that it was not empowered to investigate the alleged FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216 did not obligate an investigation absent a FOIA mandate.

The district court partially found in favor of AFL but still dismissed the action on the grounds that a claim had not been stated. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to examine supposed FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate resulted from a misinterpretation of law. Id. Despite this, the court declined to mandate that OSC undertake an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) placed such decisions within OSC’s discretionary judgment. Id. at *6. The court therefore sent the matter back to OSC for additional review. Id. When OSC once more chose not to investigate, AFL sought entry of a final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss AFL’s outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and the standard of review in this court is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are required to examine whether AFL fulfilled the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue pertains to the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since neither side initially discussed standing, we directed the parties to submit supplemental briefs on the matter.

The federal “judicial Power” is limited under Article III of the Constitution to adjudicating only specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” See U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. This limitation underlies the doctrine of standing. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish standing under Article III, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury attributable to the defendant, which a court decision in the plaintiff’s favor is likely to redress. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). This injury must consist of “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the stage of a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to set forth factual allegations that make it plausible that each standing element is satisfied. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL contends that OSC infringed upon its asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, merely being denied a procedural right, absent any tangible interest that is impacted by such deprivation—that is, a procedural right "in vacuo"—does not establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s failure to investigate has resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, the Department of Justice has not furnished AFL with the records it sought via FOIA; second, OSC has not provided the requisite notices and information mandated for an investigation under section 1214.

In framing its argument this way, AFL attempts to rely on the more lenient standing criteria that courts apply to claims of procedural injury. As articulated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Plaintiffs in such circumstances are required to demonstrate that the breach of the procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fulfills neither this obligation nor even the less stringent standard concerning redressability. As AFL cannot meet the looser requirements for procedural standing, the Court is not required to resolve whether these alleged injuries should be considered procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing asserts that OSC’s inaction under section 1216 has resulted in AFL’s inability to obtain the records it sought via FOIA. This standing rationale, however, is undermined by insurmountable causation and redressability deficiencies. To begin with, any alleged informational harm stemming from DOJ’s denial of access to agency records is directly linked to DOJ, not OSC. Furthermore, any purported connection between OSC’s failure to initiate a section 1216 probe and DOJ’s alleged noncompliance with the FOIA request is overly speculative and too tenuous to fulfill Article III’s standing requirements. The purported chain of causation proceeds as follows: Had OSC performed what AFL claims was a mandatory section 1216 investigation, OSC could have opted to pursue corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Following that, the MSPB could have chosen to penalize those employees for unjustified FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Thereafter, DOJ might then have decided to release the requested materials. This sequence requires conjecture concerning actions by two governmental entities—the MSPB and DOJ—that are not parties to this lawsuit; as the Supreme Court has cautioned, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also presupposes how OSC would choose to employ its prosecutorial discretion, which courts are typically not authorized to mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In view of such conjecture, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable outcome in this case “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the claimed informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL ignores the established rule that redressability typically concerns how a court’s judgment impacts the litigating parties, not whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot base its standing on the asserted interest in information it sought from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—predicated on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fails as well. For informational standing, a plaintiff incurs a concrete injury only when denied information to which a “statute entitled him to receive.” See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this instance, however, even if section 1216(a)(3) obliges OSC to investigate potential FOIA violations, it does not confer upon any party a right to obtain information. AFL argues that, should OSC undertake the purportedly mandatory investigation under section 1216, it might opt to provide AFL with the same notices and information required under section 1214 when investigating a “prohibited personnel practice.” Still, AFL concedes that OSC is under no duty to incorporate section 1214’s notice provisions into section 1216 proceedings. In fact, AFL acknowledges existing precedents establishing that whether OSC observes section 1214’s procedural rules in conducting a section 1216 investigation remains a “discretionary” matter. See Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL asserts no entitlement to information under section 1214, it cannot establish a concrete injury to substantiate this standing theory. Similarly, since OSC is not obligated to follow section 1214’s notice procedures in a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation would be unlikely to remedy AFL’s claimed injury.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
