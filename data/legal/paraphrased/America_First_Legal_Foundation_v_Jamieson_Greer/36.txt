Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-5168  
Author: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation filed this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency wrongfully denied AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court rejected AFL’s claims based on their substance. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as both an investigative and prosecutorial body created to pursue “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the authority granted by the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC is authorized to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, where appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board—either seeking corrective remedies pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 describes the processes that oversee corrective actions involving prohibited personnel practices, incorporating requirements that the OSC inform the complainant about the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for certain additional types of wrongdoing by federal employees. Pertinent to this matter, Section 1216 states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—meaning, information that must be disclosed under the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases involving such alleged FOIA violations, OSC is authorized to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes an independent mechanism for OSC enforcement. According to its terms, when a court (1) mandates disclosure of agency records found to be wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email communications between an external attorney and the Civil Division of the DOJ. AFL contends that the Civil Division refused this request, offering what AFL characterizes as the questionable justification that employee email accounts are not searched for responsive materials absent the employee’s consent. Subsequently, AFL initiated litigation against DOJ under FOIA. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of this document production is not at issue in the present matter.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported policy under FOIA of declining to search employee email accounts absent their consent. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholding. However, OSC asserted it lacked authority to pursue an investigation because the three prerequisites set forth in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) for initiating such action were not met. Nonetheless, AFL contended that section 1216 imposed an investigative duty independent of the requirements under FOIA.

The district court partially ruled in AFL’s favor, yet ultimately dismissed the suit for failure to state a claim. The court found that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain the Office of Special Counsel’s authority to look into purported FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court held that OSC’s refusal to investigate stemmed from a mistaken understanding of the law. Id. Nevertheless, the court refused to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy because it considered section 1216(c) to entrust that judgment to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for further consideration. Id. Following OSC’s renewed refusal to investigate, AFL sought final judgment, and the district court dismissed the remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and we review the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine if AFL has met the prerequisites for Article III standing, which pertains to the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). As the parties did not originally discuss standing, we requested additional briefing to address that matter.

The federal judicial power under Article III of the Constitution is limited to the resolution of certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” See U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing emerges from this requirement. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to possess Article III standing, they must demonstrate an injury that was caused by the defendant and that is likely to be remedied by a favorable court outcome. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must involve “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent,” rather than speculative or hypothetical. Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the motion to dismiss stage, the plaintiff bears the burden of alleging sufficient facts to plausibly establish each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached what AFL describes as its procedural right to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, the denial of a procedural right, absent an associated concrete interest affected by that denial—a mere procedural right standing alone—is not enough to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two specific and tangible informational harms: first, DOJ’s failure to release records sought by AFL through FOIA; and second, OSC’s failure to deliver the notices and information mandated for an investigation under section 1214.

By characterizing its claim in this way, AFL attempts to take advantage of the less stringent standing standards that are applied to claims of procedural injury. According to Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these matters, plaintiffs are nevertheless required to demonstrate that infringing on a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet these requirements, including the more lenient redressability standard. Since AFL cannot fulfill even the procedural injury standing threshold, there is no need for us to address whether the asserted injuries are procedural rather than substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing contends that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has blocked AFL’s access to records it sought through FOIA. This assertion is undermined by insurmountable issues regarding causation and redressability. Specifically, any informational harm caused by DOJ’s nondisclosure of records is directly attributable to DOJ itself, rather than OSC. Additionally, any link between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s refusal to honor the FOIA request is too conjectural and remote to satisfy the requirements of Article III standing. According to the proposed sequence: if OSC had conducted the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it could have opted to initiate a corrective action under section 1214, or a disciplinary action under section 1215, targeting DOJ employees responsible for the violations. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have decided to impose sanctions on those employees for unjustified FOIA denials. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Thereafter, DOJ might have chosen to release the sought-after records. This scenario is speculative, as it presumes the discretionary choices of two government entities (MSPB and DOJ) that are not parties to the present suit, yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, it requires conjecture concerning OSC’s prosecutorial discretion, which courts typically lack authority to direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given the speculative nature of this alleged chain of events, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its alleged informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards the established rule that redressability typically depends on whether a judgment binds the litigants, rather than on whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Thus, AFL cannot demonstrate standing predicated on its desire for information requested from DOJ via FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—which relies on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fares no better. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, the injury must be concrete and result from being denied information that “a statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even if section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, this provision does not create a right to receive information. AFL argues that, were OSC to carry out the allegedly required investigation under section 1216, it might decide to provide AFL with notices and information that would have been mandated had the investigation concerned a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not maintain that OSC is required to apply section 1214’s notice provisions to its section 1216 inquiries. Indeed, AFL concedes precedent establishing that whether OSC observes section 1214’s procedural protocols during a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without any claimed statutory right to information under section 1214, AFL fails to demonstrate a concrete injury sufficient for its second standing theory. Similarly, given that OSC is not obligated to provide section 1214 notice during a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation would not be reasonably likely to remedy AFL’s asserted informational injury.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
