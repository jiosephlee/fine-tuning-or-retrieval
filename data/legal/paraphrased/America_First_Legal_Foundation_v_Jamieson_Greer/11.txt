Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency unjustifiably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the claims in AFL’s case adversely on the merits. We hold that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as both an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to investigate “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board either for corrective relief in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or for disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates the process by which corrective actions may be sought for prohibited personnel practices, and includes requirements to keep the complainant informed about the investigation’s status. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate and pursue remedies for specific additional types of government employee misconduct. Pertinent to this case, the section mandates that the OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” referring to improper withholdings under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When allegations of such FOIA violations arise, the OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

The FOIA statute itself includes a distinct enforcement mechanism involving OSC. Under FOIA, if a court (1) orders an agency to produce records that have been unlawfully withheld, (2) awards attorney’s fees against that agency, and (3) explicitly raises the question of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice seeking specific emails exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division refused this request on the questionable basis that it does not conduct searches of employee emails for pertinent documents unless the employee consents. Following this, AFL initiated a lawsuit against the DOJ pursuant to FOIA. In the context of that litigation, the DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—records that address the request. The adequacy of this disclosure is not before the Court in this matter.

Additionally, AFL requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA policy of requiring employee consent to search emails. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), under which OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC, however, asserted it lacked authority to investigate, citing that the three necessary elements to trigger an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met. AFL maintained that section 1216 obligated OSC to carry out such an investigation, irrespective of FOIA’s provisions.

The district court partially ruled in AFL’s favor but ultimately dismissed the case for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, according to the court, OSC’s refusal to investigate was premised on a misinterpretation of the law. Id. Despite this, the court refused to compel OSC to undertake an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) granted such determinations to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was then remanded to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC once again declined to proceed with an investigation, AFL sought entry of final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss the remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and our review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this affects the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Standing was not initially discussed by either party; consequently, we requested supplemental briefing on the matter.

Under Article III of the Constitution, federal judicial authority is limited to certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises from this “case-or-controversy” limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury that was caused by the defendant and that is likely to be remedied by a favorable court ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The requisite injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent,” rather than “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When confronting a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to assert facts that plausibly establish each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon what it identifies as its procedural right to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as stated in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish standing under Article III. See also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific informational harms: first, that DOJ failed to produce the records AFL sought through FOIA; and second, that OSC did not supply the notifications and information mandated for investigations under section 1214.

By presenting its argument in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards that apply when procedural injury is asserted. According to Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In such circumstances, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that breaching a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a specific “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet these criteria, nor does it fulfill the lower threshold for redressability associated with procedural injury. Since AFL falls short even of the less stringent standing showing demanded in procedural injury situations, it is unnecessary for us to determine whether the alleged injuries at issue here are procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing asserts that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has prevented AFL from obtaining records it sought under FOIA. However, this theory faces insurmountable issues with causation and redressability. Specifically, any informational harm arising from DOJ’s denial of access to agency records is directly linked to DOJ, not to OSC. Moreover, the connection between OSC’s alleged neglect to perform a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is too conjectural and remote to satisfy Article III’s standing requirements. The purported sequence of events is as follows: Had OSC carried out the claimed obligatory section 1216 investigation, it could have opted to initiate either a corrective action under section 1214, or a disciplinary action pursuant to section 1215, against DOJ employees responsible. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have chosen to discipline those employees for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Ultimately, DOJ may have decided to release the records requested by AFL. This scenario relies upon conjecture about decisions made by two government entities—the MSPB and DOJ—that are not parties in this litigation, even though “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). The theory also necessitates speculation regarding how OSC might utilize its enforcement discretion, a decision category that courts typically may not compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because of this tenuous causal chain, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable outcome in this case “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Furthermore, AFL disregards the established rule that redressability largely depends on whether a judgment binds the named parties rather than whether its reasoning might “persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot base its standing on the interest in information it sought from DOJ through FOIA.

AFL’s alternative standing argument, which hinges on its claimed right to information resulting from the OSC investigation itself, is equally unavailing. In order to establish informational standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate a tangible injury stemming from a denial of information that “a statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, in this matter, even if section 1216(a)(3) imposes an obligation on OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not grant any right to obtain information. AFL argues that, in carrying out the purportedly mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC might opt to provide AFL with notices and disclosures akin to those required during the investigation of a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL concedes that OSC is not required to incorporate the notice requirements from section 1214 into its section 1216 inquiries. Indeed, AFL recognizes precedent stating that OSC’s choice regarding “whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in a section 1216 investigation remains “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without any claim to a statutory right to information pursuant to section 1214, AFL does not suffer the kind of concrete injury necessary to sustain this second standing theory. Similarly, because OSC is under no obligation to apply the section 1214 notice requirements during a section 1216 investigation, ordering OSC to undertake such an investigation is not reasonably expected to remedy AFL’s second purported injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
