Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking a court order requiring the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency had improperly rejected AFL's Freedom of Information Act request. The district court resolved the dispute by dismissing AFL’s claims based on their merits. We determine, however, that AFL does not satisfy the requirements of Article III standing to proceed with these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as both an investigatory and prosecutorial body designed to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the authority granted by the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the power to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board either for corrective remedies as outlined in 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or for disciplinary measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures governing corrective actions for prohibited personnel practices are detailed in Section 1214, which also include requirements for updating the complainant on the progress of any investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to investigate and pursue corrective measures regarding certain additional categories of misconduct by federal employees. Specifically, it directs the OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, information withheld in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In instances of alleged FOIA violations, OSC has the authority to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA itself includes a distinct enforcement mechanism applicable to the OSC. Under that provision, if a court (1) issues an order requiring an agency to produce records that were improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the question of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

As part of its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice seeking specific emails exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division denied this request on what it characterizes as the questionable basis that employee emails are not searched for responsive records without the employee’s consent. Subsequently, AFL initiated a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ. In that action, DOJ has already provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to the request. The adequacy of this production is not presently before the Court.

AFL additionally requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of not searching employee emails without obtaining consent from those employees. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” into arbitrary denials under FOIA, AFL argued for such an inquiry. However, OSC declined to investigate on the basis that the three prerequisites necessary to trigger an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were unmet. AFL contended that section 1216 compelled OSC to conduct an investigation irrespective of FOIA’s requirements.

The district court partially found in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action on the grounds that AFL failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to look into purported FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate stemmed from a misinterpretation of the law. Id. Despite this, the court refused to mandate that OSC investigate any particular FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) left such decisions within OSC’s exclusive judgment. Id. at *6. The court therefore sent the matter back to OSC for further proceedings. Id. After OSC once more declined to pursue an investigation, AFL requested that final judgment be entered, and the district court dismissed AFL’s outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and we review the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are required to determine whether AFL met the Article III standing prerequisites, as this issue affects the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Standing was not originally raised by either side, prompting us to request further briefing on this matter.

Under Article III, the Constitution confines federal “judicial Power” to certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The principle of standing derives from this limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to have Article III standing, they must demonstrate an injury attributable to the defendant and one that a favorable court outcome would likely remedy. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The injury asserted must amount to “an invasion of a legally protected interest” which is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent,” rather than “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When confronting a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to set forth facts that plausibly establish each standing element. See Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached what it claims is a procedural entitlement to an investigation as set forth in section 1216. However, “the deprivation of a procedural right without a concomitant effect on a concrete interest—a procedural right in the abstract—does not suffice” for Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In an effort to remedy this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to pursue an investigation has resulted in two particularized informational injuries: first, the DOJ has not released the records AFL sought via FOIA; and second, OSC has failed to furnish the notices and information that are statutorily mandated as part of an investigation under section 1214.

By characterizing its claim this way, AFL attempts to avail itself of the less stringent standing standards applicable to alleged procedural injuries. “When plaintiffs assert harm from a denial of a statutory procedural right that is intended to protect their concrete interests, courts may ease—though not entirely remove—the requirements concerning imminence and redressability, but they do not relax the demands of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Accordingly, in these circumstances, plaintiffs must still demonstrate that breaching a procedural right “resulted in injury” to an “actual, individualized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither this burden nor even the comparatively lenient redressability requirement. Since AFL falls short of fulfilling even the relaxed procedural-injury standing prerequisites, there is no need for us to determine whether the asserted harms are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s primary standing argument contends that OSC’s inaction in investigating DOJ under section 1216 has blocked AFL from obtaining records it sought under FOIA. This contention is undermined by insurmountable issues with both causation and redressability. Any informational harm that AFL may have suffered as a result of DOJ declining to provide agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, not to OSC. Moreover, the supposed link between OSC’s failure to initiate a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s refusal to comply with the FOIA request is excessively tenuous and relies on conjecture, insufficient for Article III standing. The proposed causal sequence is as follows: had OSC carried out what AFL characterizes as a mandatory section 1216 investigation, OSC might then have opted to take corrective action pursuant to section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against implicated DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have chosen to discipline these employees for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Then, DOJ could have decided to disclose the requested materials. This chain relies on conjecture regarding decisions by two government entities—the MSPB and DOJ—that are not party to this litigation, and as the Supreme Court has observed, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, this reasoning calls for speculation as to how OSC might exercise its enforcement discretion, a domain typically not subject to judicial compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because of this speculative succession of events, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the purported informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL disregards the principle that redressability is determined largely by whether a judgment binds the parties to the action, rather than whether the reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL’s claimed interest in the information sought from DOJ via FOIA does not furnish it with standing.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—relying on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation—fares no better than its first. For a plaintiff to have suffered a concrete injury based on informational standing, he must show he was denied information to which a statutory provision granted him access. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, although section 1216(a)(3) might impose an investigative obligation on OSC regarding FOIA violations, it does not confer any right to obtain information. AFL contends that, should OSC proceed with the mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC might choose to provide notices and information typically required under section 1214 when investigating a “prohibited personnel practice.” Yet, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to apply the notification requirements of section 1214 to investigations conducted pursuant to section 1216. On the contrary, AFL concedes the existence of case law, including Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011), holding that whether OSC employs section 1214’s procedures in the context of section 1216 investigations lies within its discretion. Given AFL’s lack of any asserted entitlement to information under section 1214, its informational injury claim does not amount to a concrete injury for standing purposes. Similarly, since OSC is not obligated to follow section 1214’s notice provisions during a section 1216 inquiry, an order compelling an investigation under section 1216 would not be likely to remedy AFL’s alleged injury related to lack of information.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
