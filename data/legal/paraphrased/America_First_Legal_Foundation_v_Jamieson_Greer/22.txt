Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency unlawfully rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court ruled against AFL on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to bring these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as both an investigative and prosecutorial body, created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Pursuant to the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, where appropriate, pursue enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board, seeking corrective remedies in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or pursuing disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures detailed in § 1214 outline the process for corrective responses to prohibited personnel practices and require that the party filing the complaint receives updates about the investigation’s status. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for particular other types of government employee misconduct. Specifically, the section stipulates that the OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” referring to withholdings not allowed by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When FOIA violations of this kind are alleged, the OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides an independent mechanism for enforcement by OSC. According to its terms, if a court (1) directs that agency records wrongfully withheld under FOIA be released, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In pursuit of information related to judicial recusals, AFL sought access from the Department of Justice to particular email communications between an external attorney and the DOJ's Civil Division. AFL alleges that the Civil Division rejected this request, purportedly reasoning—improperly—that it does not examine staff email accounts for pertinent documents without obtaining the staff member’s permission. AFL subsequently filed a FOIA lawsuit against the DOJ. In that litigation, the DOJ has provided, and is still providing, documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of this ongoing production is not in dispute in the present matter.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee email accounts unless consent is given. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” in cases of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, AFL pressed for action. OSC, however, maintained that it lacked the authority to investigate on the grounds that the three prerequisites set forth in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) required to initiate such an investigation had not been met. AFL responded by asserting that section 1216 imposed an obligation to investigate, regardless of whether FOIA itself would mandate it.

The district court partially ruled in AFL’s favor but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC's authority to investigate alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Consequently, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was predicated on a legal mistake. Id. However, the court declined to compel OSC to pursue an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) grants OSC discretion over such matters. Id. at *6. The court remanded the issue to OSC for additional consideration. Id. After OSC once more decided against investigating, AFL sought the entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and the standard of review for this appeal is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are obligated to determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for standing under Article III, an inquiry that affects the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither party originally discussed the standing issue, we directed them to provide additional briefing concerning that matter.

The federal “judicial Power” under Article III of the Constitution is limited to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The principle of standing is derived from this limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order for a plaintiff to meet the requirements of Article III standing, it must demonstrate that it has sustained an injury which is attributable to the actions of the defendant and that is likely to be remedied by a favorable court ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must involve “an invasion of a legally protected interest” and must be “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent” rather than merely hypothetical or conjectural. Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the pleading stage on a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff must assert facts sufficient to make each aspect of standing plausible. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL asserts that OSC breached its purported procedural right to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as established in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and reaffirmed by Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), “the loss of a procedural right without an associated concrete interest that is impacted by that loss—a procedural right standing alone—does not suffice” to establish standing under Article III. In an attempt to address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two distinct and tangible informational injuries: first, that DOJ has failed to provide the records AFL sought through FOIA; and second, that OSC has withheld the notifications and information mandated by section 1214 for the conduct of an investigation.

By presenting its argument in this manner, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing criteria that apply to claims of procedural injury. According to the D.C. Circuit, “[w]hen plaintiffs assert harm due to the breach of a statutory procedural right intended to safeguard their at-risk concrete interests, courts apply less stringent—though not completely dispensed with—standards concerning imminence and redressability, but retain the requirements for injury in fact and causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs are still obligated to demonstrate that a violation of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet these requirements, or even the eased standard concerning redressability. Since AFL does not fulfill even these relaxed standing prerequisites for procedural injuries, it is unnecessary for us to resolve whether the alleged injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL's initial argument for standing is that OSC's omission in investigating DOJ under section 1216 has kept AFL from obtaining documents it sought via FOIA. This theory is critically flawed by issues relating to causation and redressability. Specifically, any informational harm caused by DOJ’s refusal to release agency records is directly attributable to DOJ itself, not to OSC. Furthermore, the link between OSC’s lack of a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s alleged failure to fulfill the FOIA request is overly tenuous and conjectural, falling short of satisfying Article III's standing requirements. The supposed causal sequence proceeds as follows: Had OSC performed the allegedly obligatory investigation under section 1216, it might have initiated a corrective action pursuant to section 1214, or disciplinary proceedings under section 1215, against DOJ employees deemed responsible. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have chosen to sanction those individuals for arbitrary denials of FOIA requests. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). This, in turn, could potentially have led DOJ to release the records at issue. This hypothetical sequence depends on assumptions about the discretionary actions of both the MSPB and DOJ, entities not involved in the present case. However, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Moreover, it hinges on conjecture regarding OSC’s prosecutorial discretion, which courts are typically unable to direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because of this speculative chain, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable decision here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also neglects the general rule that redressability ordinarily concerns the binding effect of a court’s judgment on the actual parties, rather than the chance that its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Thus, AFL cannot establish standing grounded in its purported interest in the DOJ records requested via FOIA.

AFL’s alternative standing argument, premised on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation itself, is equally unavailing. A plaintiff demonstrates a concrete injury sufficient for informational standing only when denied information that a statute provides a right to obtain. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even if section 1216(a)(3) obligates the OSC to investigate violations of FOIA, it does not grant an entitlement to obtain information as a result. AFL argues that if OSC undertakes the purportedly mandatory investigation pursuant to section 1216, OSC could opt to provide AFL with notifications and details similar to those required in cases involving investigations of alleged “prohibited personnel practices” under section 1214. Nonetheless, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to impose section 1214’s notice procedures on its section 1216 inquiries. On the contrary, AFL concedes precedent holding that OSC’s choice regarding whether to apply section 1214 procedures during a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” See Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no claim that AFL is entitled to notice or information under section 1214, it cannot demonstrate a concrete injury on this ground. Likewise, since OSC is not obligated to provide section 1214 notifications during section 1216 investigations, a court order requiring such an investigation would not be likely to redress the second asserted injury of AFL.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
