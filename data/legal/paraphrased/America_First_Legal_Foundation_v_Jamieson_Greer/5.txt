Case Name: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 24-5168  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court ruled against AFL on the substance of its claims. We hold that AFL does not have Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency with investigative and prosecutorial duties, created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC holds the authority to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, where appropriate, to initiate proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board, seeking either corrective action pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the processes governing corrective actions in response to prohibited personnel practices, and contains requirements for keeping the complainant informed about the investigation’s status. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue corrective measures regarding certain types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, the statute directs that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—referring to withholdings barred by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In instances of such alleged FOIA violations, OSC is permitted to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were at issue.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA itself includes a distinct provision addressing OSC enforcement. Under this provision, if a court (1) mandates disclosure of agency records that have been improperly withheld in violation of FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) explicitly questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice for specific emails exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL maintains that the Civil Division rejected the request on the questionable basis that it does not review employees’ emails for relevant documents without those employees’ permission. Subsequently, AFL brought a FOIA action against DOJ. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to the request. Whether this production is adequate is not at issue in the present matter.

Additionally, AFL requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refraining from searching employee emails without consent. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary withholdings under FOIA, AFL pressed for such an inquiry. However, OSC denied it had the authority to proceed, asserting that the prerequisites for an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. AFL countered by asserting that section 1216 imposed an investigative duty even in circumstances where FOIA itself did not.

The district court partially ruled in AFL’s favor but ultimately dismissed its complaint on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC’s authority to examine supposed FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). As a result, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate stemmed from a misinterpretation of the law. Id. However, the court declined to require OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) leaves that decision to the OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was therefore remanded to OSC for additional proceedings. Id. After OSC once more refused to conduct an investigation, AFL sought entry of final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss its remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and we review the matter de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substance of the dispute, it is necessary to determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue implicates the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). As neither side initially discussed standing, we requested additional briefing specifically on that point.

Article III of the Constitution confines the exercise of federal “judicial Power” to the resolution of specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing derives from this case-or-controversy limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to establish Article III standing, it must demonstrate that it has experienced an injury attributable to the defendant and that a favorable judicial ruling is likely to remedy that injury. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The harm must amount to “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized,” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When moving to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to plead facts that plausibly support each component of standing. See Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached what it claims is its procedural right to an investigation under section 1216. However, as stated in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing; see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific informational harms: first, the Department of Justice has not released the documents AFL sought via FOIA; and second, OSC has failed to provide the notices and information required for an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

By presenting its claim in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards applicable in cases that allege procedural injury. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that infringement of a procedural right has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither the ordinary nor the more permissive standard regarding redressability. Since AFL fails to clear even the relaxed procedural-injury standing bar, we are not required to determine whether its asserted injuries are procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing is that OSC’s inaction under section 1216 deprived AFL of access to records sought via FOIA. This claim is fundamentally flawed regarding both causation and redressability. Any informational harm resulting from DOJ’s refusal to provide agency records under FOIA is directly attributable to DOJ, not OSC. Moreover, any link between OSC’s alleged failure to investigate under section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is excessively conjectural and indirect to satisfy Article III standing requirements. The supposed sequence is: Had OSC carried out the purportedly required section 1216 inquiry, OSC might then have initiated corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary proceedings under section 1215 against DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have penalized those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following this, DOJ might have elected to release the requested documents. This causal chain is dependent on the hypothetical and independent actions of two non-party federal entities (MSPB and DOJ), yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it presupposes how OSC would use its enforcement discretion, a matter courts are typically powerless to compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Due to this uncertain chain of events, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its asserted informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Furthermore, AFL ignores the general rule that redressability hinges on the legal effect a judgment has on the parties before the court, not on the possibility that its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Consequently, AFL cannot demonstrate standing premised on its alleged informational interest in DOJ records sought under FOIA.

AFL's second argument for standing, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation, is similarly unpersuasive. A plaintiff asserting informational standing demonstrates a concrete injury only if denied access to information that a statute specifically entitles the plaintiff to obtain. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, in this case, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not grant a right to obtain information. AFL contends that, should OSC conduct the purportedly required investigation under section 1216, OSC could opt to provide AFL with notices and information of the kind mandated if OSC were proceeding under section 1214 for “prohibited personnel practice” investigations. Yet, AFL does not argue that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notice requirements during section 1216 investigations. On the contrary, AFL concedes there are decisions holding that whether OSC observes section 1214 procedures in a section 1216 investigation is a matter of “discretion.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL does not assert any entitlement to information under section 1214, it fails to establish a concrete injury under this second standing theory. Similarly, since OSC is not required to provide section 1214 notices in the context of a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation would not likely remedy AFL’s second alleged injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
