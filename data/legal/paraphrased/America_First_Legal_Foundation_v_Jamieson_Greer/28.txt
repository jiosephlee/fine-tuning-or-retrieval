Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court disposed of AFL’s claims on substantive grounds. We determine, however, that AFL does not have Article III standing to assert these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) operates as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the authority to examine alleged “prohibited personnel practices” and, if appropriate, initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board to pursue corrective remedies in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1214, or to seek disciplinary actions under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates the procedures for handling corrective measures regarding prohibited personnel practices, which include requirements to inform the individual who filed the complaint about developments in the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for specific additional types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this matter, the statute states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—meaning withholdings not permitted by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In instances of alleged FOIA violations of this kind, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

The FOIA includes an independent mechanism for enforcement by the OSC. According to its terms, when a court (1) mandates the release of agency records unlawfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In pursuit of its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific emails exchanged between an external lawyer and the Department of Justice’s Civil Division. AFL claims that the Civil Division declined the request, allegedly arguing—questionably—that employee emails are not searched for relevant documents unless the individual employee agrees. Subsequently, AFL filed a FOIA action against DOJ. In the context of that litigation, DOJ has produced—and continues to produce—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s document production is not being contested in this proceeding.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refraining from searching employee email accounts absent their approval. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” into arbitrary withholdings under FOIA. OSC, however, asserted it lacked jurisdiction because the prerequisites outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) that would trigger an investigation had not been met regarding the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that section 1216 did not compel it to investigate absent those conditions, while AFL contended that section 1216 independently required such an inquiry regardless of FOIA’s requirements.

The district court partially granted relief to AFL, yet dismissed the action on the basis that the complaint failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to examine alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate rested on an error of law. Id. However, the court declined to compel OSC to conduct any investigation into FOIA policy, finding that section 1216(c) reserved such determination to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was then remanded to OSC for additional proceedings. Id. After OSC again refused to pursue an investigation, AFL sought entry of final judgment, resulting in the district court dismissing the remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and this court’s review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary for us to determine whether AFL met the criteria for Article III standing, as this issue pertains to the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since neither side initially discussed standing, we directed the parties to submit additional briefing on this matter.

The Constitution’s Article III limits the federal judiciary’s authority to adjudicating only certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The principle of standing is derived from this case-or-controversy constraint. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to establish standing under Article III, they must show an injury inflicted by the defendant that is likely to be remedied through a favorable ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must amount to “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When faced with a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to allege facts that make it plausible for each standing element to be met. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental memorandum, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon what it characterizes as a procedural right to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as established in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and referenced in Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), merely being deprived of a procedural right, absent a tangible interest impacted by that deprivation—a “procedural right in vacuo”—does not satisfy Article III’s standing requirements. To address this obstacle, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, that DOJ has failed to provide the records which AFL sought via FOIA; second, that OSC did not furnish the notifications and information mandated for a section 1214 investigation.

By characterizing its claim in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the less stringent standing standards applicable to claims involving procedural injury. According to precedent, “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these instances, plaintiffs are still required to demonstrate that the procedural right’s violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. Here, AFL fails to meet both these criteria and even the comparatively lenient standard for redressability under procedural standing. Because AFL cannot satisfy these more permissive standing prerequisites applicable to procedural injuries, it is unnecessary to resolve whether the asserted injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial argument for standing is that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has blocked AFL from obtaining records it sought via FOIA. This position is fatally flawed regarding both causation and redressability. First, any harm to AFL arising from DOJ’s refusal to produce agency records is directly attributable to DOJ rather than to OSC. Additionally, the link between OSC’s omission to conduct a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s alleged noncompliance with the FOIA request is far too conjectural and remote to fulfill the requirements of Article III standing. The asserted sequence of events is as follows: Had OSC initiated the supposedly required section 1216 investigation, it might have pursued corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ personnel who acted improperly. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Following that, the MSPB might have imposed penalties on those DOJ employees for improper withholding under FOIA. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Afterward, DOJ could have decided to release the sought-after records. This causal chain depends on conjecture about decisions by two independent government entities not presently before the Court—the MSPB and DOJ—as “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also relies on speculating how OSC might wield its enforcement discretion, which is typically immune from judicial compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given these uncertainties, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable ruling would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Moreover, AFL disregards the rule that redressability generally depends on a judgment’s binding effect on the litigants, not whether the decision “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Consequently, AFL cannot meet the requirements for standing based on its desire for information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s second argument for standing—which is predicated on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fares no better than its first. For informational standing to exist, a plaintiff must show a concrete injury resulting from the denial of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, assuming arguendo that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not confer a right to obtain information. AFL contends that, were OSC to conduct the supposed mandatory investigation under section 1216, it could decide to provide AFL with notices and information akin to what is required if OSC investigates a purported “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Yet, AFL does not argue that OSC is legally compelled to apply section 1214’s notice requirements to investigations under section 1216. In fact, AFL concedes authority establishing that whether OSC applies section 1214’s procedural provisions in section 1216 investigations is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL does not allege an entitlement to information under section 1214, it has not demonstrated the concrete injury needed to establish its second theory of standing. Similarly, as OSC is not required to extend section 1214’s notice requirements to a section 1216 investigation, ordering such an investigation would not be likely to redress AFL’s second purported injury.

III

As AFL does not have standing under Article III, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to a lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
