Case Caption: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: 24-5168  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation filed suit seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court ruled against AFL on the substantive issues and dismissed the case. We determine, however, that AFL does not have standing under Article III to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as both an investigative and prosecutorial body, created with the objective of promoting “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Pursuant to the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC is authorized to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, when justified, may institute enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board. Such proceedings can seek corrective relief pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The procedures governing corrective actions for prohibited personnel practices, including requirements to inform the complainant regarding the status of the investigation, are detailed in § 1214. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to investigate and pursue remedial measures for specific categories of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this case, the statute mandates that the OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” meaning information withheld in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In instances of such alleged FOIA infractions, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

The Freedom of Information Act also sets forth its own distinct framework for OSC enforcement. Under FOIA, if a court (1) directs the release of agency records found to have been improperly withheld, (2) imposes attorneys’ fees upon the agency, and (3) expressly raises the question of whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” then the OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice certain email communications exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL claims that the Civil Division rejected the request based on the questionable reasoning that it refrains from searching employees' emails for pertinent documents without the employees’ permission. Consequently, AFL filed a lawsuit against DOJ pursuant to FOIA. In that action, DOJ has already produced—and continues to produce—documents that respond to the request. The adequacy of this production is not disputed in the present context.

Additionally, AFL requested that OSC look into the DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of not conducting searches of employee emails absent their consent. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” in cases of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, AFL argued that OSC was required to investigate. However, OSC declined to assert jurisdiction over the matter, determining that the conditions precedent to requiring an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. Nevertheless, AFL contended that section 1216 obligated OSC to proceed with an investigation even if the requirements of FOIA itself were not fulfilled.

The district court partially sided with AFL but ultimately dismissed its lawsuit on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate alleged FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Consequently, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate rested on a legal misunderstanding. Id. However, the court refused to compel OSC to undertake an investigation into any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) grants such decisions to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The case was remanded to OSC for additional proceedings. Id. Following OSC’s renewed refusal to investigate, AFL requested entry of final judgment, leading the district court to dismiss AFL’s outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and our standard of review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, we must first determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for standing under Article III, a threshold matter impacting the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). As neither party initially raised the issue of standing, we requested additional briefing specifically on that point.

Article III of the Constitution confines the federal “judicial Power” to adjudicating specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” See U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this case-or-controversy limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order to establish Article III standing, a plaintiff is required to show that it has sustained an injury that is attributable to the defendant and is likely to be remedied by a decision in the plaintiff's favor. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). This injury must involve “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent,” as opposed to “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the stage of a motion to dismiss, plaintiffs are obligated to allege facts sufficient to plausibly demonstrate each element of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached what it claims is its procedural right to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as stated in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish standing under Article III; see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s failure to investigate has inflicted two particular, informational injuries upon it: first, DOJ has failed to release the records that AFL sought under FOIA; second, OSC has withheld the notices and information required for an investigation in accordance with section 1214.

By presenting the case in this way, AFL aims to benefit from the more lenient standing criteria applicable to claims of procedural injury. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005): “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under this framework, plaintiffs are required to establish that a breach of a procedural right has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet these prerequisites, including the standard for redressability, even under the more permissive procedural-injury doctrine. Therefore, since AFL does not fulfill the relaxed procedural standing requirements, it is unnecessary to resolve whether these alleged injuries are procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing contends that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has blocked AFL from obtaining records sought through FOIA. However, this rationale faces insurmountable causation and redressability issues. The purported informational harm resulting from DOJ’s refusal to release records is, by its nature, directly attributable to DOJ and not to OSC. Moreover, the linkage between OSC’s decision not to initiate a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is overly tenuous and hypothetical, falling short of Article III standing requirements. The chain of alleged causation is as follows: If OSC had undertaken the supposedly compulsory investigation under section 1216, it might have then opted to bring a corrective action pursuant to section 1214 or a disciplinary action pursuant to section 1215 against DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have penalized those individuals for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Finally, DOJ might have decided to provide the requested information. This sequence depends on conjecture regarding decisions made by two agencies not parties to this case—the MSPB and DOJ—yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it requires guessing how OSC would exercise its enforcement discretion, a domain typically beyond judicial compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given the speculative nature of this causal path, AFL cannot demonstrate that a favorable court ruling would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Furthermore, AFL fails to recognize that redressability is usually contingent on the extent to which a court’s judgment controls the parties before it, not on whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Thus, AFL is unable to establish standing based on any interest it has in the records requested from DOJ via FOIA.

AFL’s alternative basis for standing—relying on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation—also fails. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, they must demonstrate that they were denied access to information which a “statute entitled [them] to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not provide any right for AFL to obtain information as a result. AFL argues that if OSC were to undertake the supposedly compulsory investigation under section 1216, OSC might voluntarily provide notices and information akin to those mandated in section 1214 investigations of alleged “prohibited personnel practice.” Yet, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notice provisions when conducting a section 1216 investigation. In fact, AFL admits prior precedent holds that OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in the context of a section 1216 investigation remains “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL does not allege any entitlement to information under section 1214, it cannot show the concrete injury necessary for its second standing theory. Furthermore, because OSC is not required to provide section 1214 notices when investigating under section 1216, a judicial mandate compelling OSC to investigate would not likely redress the second alleged injury identified by AFL.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
