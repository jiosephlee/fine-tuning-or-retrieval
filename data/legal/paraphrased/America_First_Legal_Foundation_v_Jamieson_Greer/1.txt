Case Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: 24-5168  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action, seeking a mandate for the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency unjustifiably refused AFL’s Freedom of Information Act request. The district court ruled against AFL’s claims on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not have standing under Article III to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body created to ensure "a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the authority to investigate “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board to obtain corrective relief pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates the procedures applicable to obtaining corrective remedies for prohibited personnel practices and includes requirements to inform the complainant regarding the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes OSC to look into and pursue remedies for particular types of misconduct committed by federal employees. Pertinent to this matter, the statute directs OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, withholding actions not permitted by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In cases of purported FOIA violations, OSC is empowered to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA itself has an independent enforcement mechanism for OSC. According to FOIA, if a court (1) requires an agency to disclose records wrongfully withheld in violation of FOIA, (2) awards attorney fees against the agency, and (3) expressly questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its efforts to examine matters related to judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice seeking certain emails exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL contends that the Civil Division rejected the request on the questionable basis that it does not search employees’ email accounts for relevant records unless those employees provide consent. AFL subsequently initiated a lawsuit against DOJ under FOIA. In that action, DOJ has released—and continues to release—documents that are responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of this production is not the subject of this proceeding.

Additionally, AFL requested that OSC investigate DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of not searching employee email accounts absent consent. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary withholdings under FOIA, AFL argued for such an inquiry. OSC, however, disavowed jurisdiction to conduct the investigation, reasoning that the three conditions required to trigger an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. AFL maintained that section 1216 compelled OSC to investigate, even in circumstances where FOIA itself did not.

The district court partially ruled in AFL's favor but ultimately dismissed the action on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC's authority to examine alleged FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). As a result, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was predicated on an error of law. Id. However, the court refused to direct OSC to undertake an investigation of any FOIA policy because it interpreted section 1216(c) as vesting such decisions within OSC's discretion. Id. at *6. The court therefore remanded the matter to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC again opted not to conduct an investigation, AFL sought the entry of final judgment, after which the district court dismissed the remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and the standard of review before us is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substance of the case, it is necessary to determine whether AFL has met the criteria for Article III standing, an inquiry that is essential to establishing the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because standing was not raised by either party at the outset, we directed the parties to submit supplemental briefs on the matter.

The Constitution’s Article III confines the federal judiciary’s authority to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The principles of standing derive from this requirement. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate that they have suffered an injury attributable to the defendant’s conduct and that a favorable ruling is likely to remedy that injury. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The harm alleged must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (internal quotations omitted). At the stage of a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to plead facts that plausibly establish each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached what it claims is its procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, the Supreme Court has held that the loss of a procedural right alone—without a corresponding concrete interest being impacted—does not suffice for Article III standing. See Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this obstacle, AFL argues that OSC’s failure to investigate resulted in two specific informational harms: (1) DOJ’s failure to furnish the records AFL sought through FOIA; and (2) OSC’s failure to supply the notices and information mandated for an investigation by section 1214.

By characterizing its claim in this way, AFL attempts to take advantage of the more lenient standing standards that are applicable to alleged procedural injuries. According to precedent, “[w]here plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under this standard, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet either this requirement or the relaxed standard regarding redressability. Since AFL cannot establish standing even under these more flexible standards for procedural injury, it is unnecessary for us to determine whether the claimed injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial standing argument contends that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has resulted in AFL not obtaining the documents it sought under FOIA. This assertion encounters insurmountable causation and redressability obstacles. Specifically, any harm AFL suffered from not receiving agency records under FOIA is directly attributable to DOJ, not OSC. Moreover, the purported link between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is too tenuous and conjectural to meet Article III standing requirements. The supposed sequence is: had OSC initiated the required section 1216 investigation, it potentially could have chosen to take corrective action under section 1214, or disciplinary action under section 1215, against DOJ employees responsible. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have opted to penalize those employees for unjustified FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following that, DOJ might have released the requested documents. This line of reasoning necessitates speculation regarding the independent decisions of two agencies that are not before the Court (MSPB and DOJ), whereas “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also relies on uncertain predictions about how OSC might utilize its enforcement discretion, an area the judiciary typically cannot compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Due to this highly conjectural causal sequence, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable outcome here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its asserted informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards that redressability usually hinges on the extent to which a judgment is binding on the parties involved, rather than whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL cannot claim standing based on its asserted interest in obtaining information from DOJ through FOIA.

AFL’s alternative standing argument—predicated on its claimed right to access the results of the OSC investigation—fails as well. Informational standing exists when a plaintiff suffers a concrete harm because information that a statute guarantees is withheld. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, here, assuming arguendo that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it nevertheless does not confer a right to receive any resulting information. AFL counters that, if OSC undertakes the allegedly required investigation under section 1216, it might elect to provide AFL with notices and disclosures similar to those mandated in investigations of alleged “prohibited personnel practice[s]” under section 1214. Yet AFL does not maintain that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notification requirements to its section 1216 inquiries. In fact, AFL concedes authority establishing that whether OSC applies section 1214 procedures during a section 1216 investigation is a matter of “discretion.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no claimed right to receive information under section 1214, AFL has not shown a concrete injury to sustain its second standing argument. Similarly, since OSC is not required to afford section 1214 notifications in a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling an investigation would not be likely to redress AFL’s alleged second injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
