Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-5168  
Opinion by Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s request made under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court ruled against AFL on the substantive issues. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as an investigative and prosecutorial body, created with the goal of promoting “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is authorized to examine allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board seeking either corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 outlines the protocols for seeking corrective action in response to prohibited personnel practices, including requirements for updating the complainant on the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to conduct investigations and pursue remedies for specific additional types of government employee misconduct. Pertinent to this case, the section states that OSC “shall ... conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” referring to withholdings that contravene the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In circumstances involving alleged FOIA breaches, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides an independent mechanism for enforcement by OSC. Under this provision, if a court (1) directs the release of agency documents that were wrongfully withheld in violation of FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” then OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific email communications between an external attorney and the Department of Justice’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected its request on the questionable basis that employee email accounts are not searched for pertinent records without the individual employee’s consent. AFL subsequently initiated a FOIA action against DOJ. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of this production is not the matter presently before the Court.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of refraining from searching employee email accounts without their permission. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which directs that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholding decisions. However, OSC asserted it lacked the authority to undertake such an investigation because the three requirements necessary to trigger one under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were unmet. Nevertheless, AFL maintained that section 1216 independently required OSC to investigate the alleged FOIA policy, regardless of whether FOIA itself did.

The district court partially found in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA infractions under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, the court concluded that OSC erred legally in declining to investigate. Id. However, the court refused to require OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) left that determination within OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration. Id. When OSC again declined to investigate, AFL sought the entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and our standard of review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine whether AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue relates to the district court’s subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither side initially discussed standing, we required the parties to submit additional briefing on this issue.

The Constitution’s Article III confines the federal judiciary's authority to the resolution of designated “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises directly from this requirement. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to satisfy Article III standing requirements, it is necessary to show that they have experienced an injury traceable to the defendant’s conduct, which a favorable court ruling is likely to remedy. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the pleading stage on a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must set forth factual allegations sufficient to plausibly establish all standing elements. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, a mere loss of a procedural right, absent any impact on a tangible interest—a procedural right “in vacuo”—does not suffice to establish standing under Article III. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In response to this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two distinct and particularized informational harms: first, that the DOJ has failed to release records that AFL sought under FOIA; second, that OSC did not furnish the notices and other information that section 1214 requires be provided during an investigation.

By presenting its claims in this manner, AFL attempts to take advantage of the more lenient standing criteria that govern actions asserting a procedural injury. As explained in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these situations, plaintiffs are therefore required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural protection “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither this threshold nor even the eased standard regarding redressability. Since AFL cannot fulfill even the relaxed requirements applicable to procedural injuries, it is unnecessary to determine whether the injuries in question are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s primary argument for standing contends that OSC’s failure to investigate the DOJ under section 1216 blocked AFL’s access to the documents it sought through FOIA. However, this theory faces insurmountable problems with respect to both causation and redressability. Any harm from not obtaining the agency records is clearly attributable to the DOJ’s actions, not to OSC. The connection between OSC not conducting a section 1216 investigation and the DOJ’s refusal to release the records requested under FOIA is too remote and speculative to satisfy Article III’s requirements. The chain of causation put forward is as follows: had OSC initiated the mandatory investigation under section 1216, it may have decided to pursue either corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary proceedings under section 1215 against DOJ personnel allegedly at fault. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Following that, the MSPB could have imposed sanctions on those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). As a result, DOJ may have chosen to provide the requested information. This scenario rests on conjecture regarding actions of two agencies not present in this case—the DOJ and the MSPB—yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Further, it relies on speculation regarding OSC’s own enforcement decisions, which courts typically lack authority to dictate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Due to this tenuous causal chain, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable ruling would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL disregards the rule that redressability is generally determined by whether a judgment binds the litigants before the court, not whether its rationale could influence nonparties. Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Consequently, AFL is unable to assert standing based on its interest in accessing the information requested from DOJ via FOIA.

AFL's alternative argument for standing—predicated on an alleged informational interest in the outcome of the OSC's investigation—fares no better. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff must show a tangible injury from being deprived of information “a statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). Yet, even assuming that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not confer any statutory right to obtain information. AFL argues that, should OSC carry out the purportedly compulsory investigation under section 1216, OSC may opt to issue AFL notices and disclosures required in cases of investigating “prohibited personnel practices” pursuant to section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not claim OSC is compelled to apply the notification requirements of section 1214 to section 1216 investigations. On the contrary, it concedes that precedent establishes OSC’s application of section 1214 procedures to section 1216 investigations is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without any assertion of entitlement to information under section 1214, AFL fails to demonstrate a concrete injury necessary for its second theory of standing. Similarly, because section 1214 notice requirements are not mandated in a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation would be unlikely to redress the alleged injury AFL cites in its second theory.

III

As AFL does not have standing under Article III, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to the absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
