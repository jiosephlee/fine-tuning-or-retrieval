Case Name: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Jurisdiction: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Decision Date: October 3, 2025  
Case Number: 24-5168  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation filed this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency unlawfully denied AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the matter by dismissing AFL’s claims on their merits. We determine, however, that AFL does not possess Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) operates as an investigative and prosecutorial body designed to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Through the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC is granted authority to examine “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, pursue enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board, seeking either corrective measures in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. The process for obtaining corrective relief for prohibited personnel practices is outlined in § 1214, which also includes requirements to keep the complainant informed of the status of an investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for specific types of government employee misconduct. Pertinent to this case, the statute requires OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," referring to the prohibitions established by the Freedom of Information Act. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In the case of alleged FOIA infractions, OSC is authorized to "investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA has a distinct enforcement mechanism involving the OSC. Under its terms, if a court (1) directs the release of agency records found to be improperly withheld pursuant to FOIA, (2) imposes attorneys’ fees on the agency, and (3) makes a specific finding questioning whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then the OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought specific emails from the Department of Justice involving correspondence between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division declined the request based on the questionable rationale that it refrains from searching employee email accounts for relevant records absent the consent of the employee. AFL subsequently brought a FOIA action against DOJ. During that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of these disclosures is not before the Court in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ’s purported practice under FOIA of declining to search employee emails without permission. Citing section 1216(a)(3), AFL noted that OSC is required to “conduct an investigation” in instances of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC denied having jurisdiction to investigate, stating that the three prerequisites under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) necessary to initiate an investigation were not present regarding the alleged FOIA policy. OSC contended that an investigation is not mandated under FOIA in this situation, despite AFL's assertion that section 1216 independently compels one.

The district court partially ruled in AFL’s favor, but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Therefore, the court found that OSC’s refusal to conduct an investigation was predicated on a legal mistake. Id. However, the court declined to require OSC to pursue an investigation into any FOIA policy because it interpreted section 1216(c) as assigning that judgment to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for additional review. Id. Upon OSC’s renewed refusal to investigate, AFL sought entry of final judgment, after which the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL filed an appeal, and we undertake our review anew. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are obligated to determine if AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this pertains to the district court's subject matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since the parties did not discuss standing in their original briefs, we directed them to provide supplemental briefing on the matter.

The federal "judicial Power" granted by Article III of the Constitution is confined to addressing specific "Cases" and "Controversies." U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is a direct consequence of this requirement. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to demonstrate Article III standing, it must show an injury attributable to the defendant and that the injury would likely be remedied by a decision in the plaintiff's favor. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). This injury must constitute an “invasion of a legally protected interest,” be “concrete and particularized,” and must be “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When faced with a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must put forward factual allegations that plausibly establish each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC infringed upon its purported procedural right to an investigation under section 1216. However, merely being denied a procedural right, absent any concrete interest impacted by such denial—i.e., possessing only “a procedural right in vacuo”—does not establish Article III standing. See Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two actual, informational injuries: first, that DOJ failed to supply the documents AFL sought through FOIA; and second, that OSC did not provide the notifications and details mandated by section 1214 for an investigation.

By characterizing its claim in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing rules applicable when procedural injuries are at issue. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005): “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under this framework, plaintiffs are obligated to demonstrate that infringing upon a procedural right has caused actual harm to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet both this burden and the less demanding test for redressability. Since AFL cannot establish standing under even the more forgiving standards applicable to procedural injuries, it is unnecessary for us to determine whether these asserted injuries should be classified as procedural or substantive.

AFL advances an initial standing theory that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has resulted in AFL being denied access to records sought under FOIA. However, this theory is fundamentally flawed with respect to causation and redressability. To start, any harm arising from DOJ’s refusal to provide agency records constitutes an informational injury attributable directly to DOJ, not to OSC. Furthermore, the proposed causal link between OSC not initiating a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s alleged noncompliance with the FOIA request is overly tenuous and hypothetical for the requirements of Article III standing. The suggested sequence of causation entails: OSC, if it had conducted the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, might then have elected to initiate corrective proceedings under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against the responsible DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). After that, MSPB could potentially have imposed sanctions on those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following this, DOJ might have chosen to release the requested documents. This scenario relies on conjecture regarding the independent actions of two governmental entities not present as litigants (MSPB and DOJ), yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, the theory requires guessing how OSC could exercise its prosecutorial discretion, which is generally not subject to judicial compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In view of these conjectural steps, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable judicial determination in this matter “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its claimed informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards the settled principle that redressability usually hinges on the direct effect of the judgment upon the litigants, rather than the possibility its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL cannot show standing based on an asserted right to information it sought from DOJ through FOIA.

AFL's alternative argument for standing, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation, is equally unsuccessful. A plaintiff asserting informational standing demonstrates a concrete injury only when denied access to information that a "statute entitled him to receive." Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even if section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not provide a right to receive information. AFL argues that, should OSC conduct the allegedly required investigation under section 1216, OSC may decide to provide AFL with notices and information that would be mandatory if the investigation fell under a "prohibited personnel practice" as defined in section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not claim that OSC is required to impose section 1214’s notification requirements onto investigations conducted under section 1216. On the contrary, it concedes existing precedent that classifies OSC’s choice "whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements" during a section 1216 investigation as "discretionary." Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without any asserted statutory right to obtain information pursuant to section 1214, AFL lacks the necessary concrete injury to establish its second theory of standing. Similarly, because OSC is not obligated to adhere to section 1214 notice requirements within the context of a section 1216 investigation, a judicial order compelling such an investigation would not likely remedy AFL's purported second injury.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
