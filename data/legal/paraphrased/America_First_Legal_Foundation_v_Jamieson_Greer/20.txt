Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-5168  
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency unlawfully rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court adjudicated AFL’s claims on their merits and dismissed them. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to maintain these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC), created to promote a “fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service,” functions as both an investigative and prosecutorial body. Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC has the authority to examine claims of “prohibited personnel practices” and, as appropriate, to initiate enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board, either for corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or for disciplinary measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the procedural framework for undertaking corrective actions when prohibited personnel practices are identified and includes requirements for keeping the complainant informed of the investigation's status. See, for example, 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to investigate and pursue remedies for certain additional types of misconduct committed by government employees. Pertinently, this section directs that the OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—referring to withholdings not permitted by the Freedom of Information Act. See 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). In instances of such alleged FOIA violations, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were implicated.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes a distinct enforcement mechanism for OSC. According to its terms, if a court: (1) directs the release of agency records that have been wrongfully withheld under FOIA; (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency; and (3) explicitly raises the issue of whether the agency’s “withholding was arbitrary or capricious,” OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific emails exchanged between an external lawyer and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division refused the request, citing as justification its policy of not searching employees’ emails for responsive documents without those employees’ approval—a rationale AFL describes as questionable. AFL subsequently filed a FOIA action against DOJ. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has released—and continues to release—documents that are responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of these disclosures is not currently before the Court.

AFL further requested that OSC look into DOJ's purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee emails unless permission was given. In support, it cited section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” regarding arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC asserted that it lacked jurisdiction to investigate since the three prerequisites outlined in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met with respect to the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that section 1216 did not obligate it to initiate an investigation under these circumstances, even if FOIA itself did not impose such a duty.

The district court issued a partial ruling in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to probe alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). According to the court, OSC’s refusal to investigate was premised on an error of law. Id. Even so, the court refused to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, concluding that section 1216(c) gave OSC the discretion to make that determination. Id. at *6. The case was therefore remanded to OSC for further proceedings. Id. Upon OSC’s renewed decision not to pursue an investigation, AFL requested entry of final judgment, after which the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then filed an appeal, which is reviewed de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are obligated to determine if AFL met the prerequisites for standing under Article III, which affects the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). As neither side discussed standing in their original filings, we directed the parties to submit supplemental briefs on this issue.

Article III of the United States Constitution confines the exercise of federal “judicial Power” to the adjudication of specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises from this cases-and-controversies limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to establish Article III standing, it is necessary to demonstrate an injury attributable to the defendant’s conduct that would likely be remedied by a decision in the plaintiff’s favor. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When opposing a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must set forth plausible factual allegations supporting all components of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental memorandum, AFL argues that OSC infringed upon its asserted procedural right to an investigation as provided by section 1216. However, “[t]he deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In an attempt to address this hurdle, AFL asserts that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two distinct, concrete informational injuries: first, that the Department of Justice has withheld records that AFL sought under FOIA; and second, that OSC has not furnished the notices and information mandated for an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

By presenting the issue in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards applicable to claims of procedural injury. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation”; see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs are still required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest,” as set out in Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. Here, AFL fails to meet those obligations and does not even fulfill the eased redressability criterion. Since AFL cannot meet even these relaxed standing rules for procedural claims, it is unnecessary for us to determine if its asserted injuries are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial argument for standing contends that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has obstructed AFL from obtaining the records it sought via FOIA. However, this rationale is fundamentally flawed regarding both causation and redressability. Any harm resulting from DOJ’s decision to withhold agency records is directly attributable to DOJ itself—not OSC. Further, any linkage between OSC’s failure to initiate a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s alleged noncompliance with AFL’s FOIA request is excessively conjectural and indirect, falling short of supporting Article III standing. The supposed causal sequence is as follows: If OSC had performed the investigation allegedly mandated by section 1216, OSC might have pursued corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ employees alleged to have violated the law. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have chosen to discipline those employees for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). At that point, DOJ might have chosen to release the desired records. This scenario requires presuming decisions by MSPB and DOJ, neither of which are parties to the current suit, yet, as the Supreme Court has said, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also depends on hypothetical decisions within OSC’s prosecutorial discretion, which courts are typically not permitted to compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Due to the speculative nature of these links, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable ruling “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards that redressability generally depends on the judgment’s binding effect on the parties rather than on whether the court’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL cannot claim standing on the basis of its asserted right to information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s second argument for standing, which is predicated on an asserted informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation itself, is similarly unavailing. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, there must be a concrete injury resulting from the denial of information that a statute requires to be disclosed to them. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this case, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to look into FOIA violations, the provision does not give any right to receive information. AFL argues that, should OSC undertake the supposedly compulsory investigation pursuant to section 1216, it might opt to supply AFL with notices and information, akin to what would be obligatory under section 1214 for investigations concerning “prohibited personnel practice.” However, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notification obligations to investigations under section 1216. Indeed, AFL concedes that existing case law maintains OSC’s choice “whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in the context of a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no claimed right to obtain information under section 1214, AFL’s asserted injury is not sufficiently concrete to meet the requirements for standing under its second theory. Similarly, since OSC is not required to provide section 1214 notices when performing a section 1216 investigation, even a court order compelling such an investigation would be unlikely to remedy the alleged second injury.

III

Since AFL does not possess standing under Article III, we set aside the district court's judgment and send the case back with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
