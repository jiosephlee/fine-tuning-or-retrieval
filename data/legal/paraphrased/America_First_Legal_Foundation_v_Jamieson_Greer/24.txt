Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly refused AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court rejected AFL’s claims based on their substance. We determine, however, that AFL does not have Article III standing to assert these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial body tasked with promoting “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). The Civil Service Reform Act grants OSC the authority to probe into “prohibited personnel practices” and, when justified, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board for either corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates the processes for obtaining corrective relief in cases of prohibited personnel practices, including requirements to inform the complainant about the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to investigate and pursue corrective measures for certain additional types of government employee misconduct. Pertinent to the present matter, this section states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” that is, withholdings that violate the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). Regarding such asserted FOIA violations, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides an independent enforcement mechanism involving OSC. Under its terms, where a court (1) directs the release of agency records found to have been wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly questions whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email correspondence between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division refused the request, ostensibly because it purportedly requires employee consent before searching their email accounts for responsive documents. AFL subsequently initiated a FOIA action against DOJ. Within that litigation, DOJ has already provided—and is still in the process of providing—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of DOJ’s ongoing production is not before the Court in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine the Department of Justice's purported FOIA practice of declining to search staff email accounts absent their permission. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into instances of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. However, OSC asserted it lacked authority to pursue such an investigation because the three prerequisites set forth in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) for initiating an inquiry were unmet regarding the alleged FOIA policy. OSC maintained that, absent these triggers under FOIA, section 1216 did not compel it to investigate.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed its action for failing to state a claim. It found that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC’s authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court determined that OSC’s refusal to investigate stemmed from a mistaken interpretation of law. Id. However, the court refused to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) assigned such a decision to OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for further proceedings. Id. Following OSC’s renewed refusal to investigate, AFL sought a final judgment, and the district court dismissed AFL’s outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and we now consider the case under a de novo standard. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, we are obligated to determine if AFL met the prerequisites for Article III standing, as this issue impacts the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since standing was not discussed by either party in their initial briefs, we requested additional briefing specifically on this matter.

Article III of the Constitution limits federal courts’ “judicial Power” to the adjudication of certain “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises from this requirement. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order to meet Article III standing requirements, a plaintiff must demonstrate that they have experienced an injury that was caused by the defendant and that is likely to be remedied by a favorable court ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” and must be “concrete and particularized,” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When facing a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must set forth allegations supporting each component of standing in a plausible manner. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental brief, AFL argues that OSC infringed its purported procedural entitlement to an investigation as outlined in section 1216. However, as stated in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and reiterated in Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” for Article III standing. To address this issue, AFL asserts that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two specific, informational injuries: namely, that DOJ has withheld records sought by AFL pursuant to FOIA, and that OSC has failed to provide the notifications and information required under section 1214 as part of its investigatory process.

By presenting its claim in this manner, AFL aims to benefit from the more lenient standing standards applicable to claims of procedural injury. As articulated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), when plaintiffs claim harm from the breach of a statutory procedural right intended to safeguard a concrete, threatened interest, courts ease—though do not dispense with—the criteria of imminence and redressability, but not those of injury in fact or causation. See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In such situations, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that an infringement of a procedural right has "resulted in injury" to a "concrete, particularized interest." Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL fails to meet these prerequisites, as well as the less stringent standard for redressability. Given AFL's inability to fulfill even the relaxed standing test for procedural injuries, we are not obliged to determine whether the injuries alleged are procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial argument for standing contends that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has blocked AFL from obtaining documents it sought via FOIA. However, this argument is undermined by insurmountable causation and redressability issues. Specifically, any informational harm AFL suffered due to the DOJ’s refusal to disclose records is clearly attributable to DOJ rather than OSC. Moreover, the alleged link between OSC’s lack of a section 1216 inquiry and DOJ’s denial of the FOIA request is too tenuous and conjectural to satisfy Article III standing requirements. The proposed causal sequence is: Had OSC undertaken the purportedly obligatory section 1216 inquiry, it might have chosen to initiate either a corrective action under section 1214, or a disciplinary action under section 1215, against relevant DOJ staff. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have penalized those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following that, DOJ might then have decided to provide the requested documents. This series of events relies on conjecture regarding the independent decisions of two entities outside this litigation—the MSPB and DOJ—despite the general rule that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Furthermore, this theory involves speculation as to how OSC would utilize its enforcement discretion—an area courts typically cannot direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because of these speculative assumptions, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards the general principle that redressability is determined by how a judgment binds the litigants, not by the potential persuasive effect of its reasoning on non-parties. Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Therefore, AFL cannot rely on its desire for information requested from DOJ under FOIA to establish standing in this action.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing—resting on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation—fares no better. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, there must be a concrete injury arising from being denied information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). In this instance, however, even if section 1216(a)(3) obliges OSC to look into FOIA violations, this provision does not grant a right to obtain any information. AFL argues that should OSC conduct the purportedly obligatory investigation under section 1216, it might elect to provide AFL with notices and information analogous to those that are mandated when OSC investigates a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. However, AFL stops short of arguing that OSC is required to apply section 1214’s notice obligations to its section 1216 investigative process. In fact, AFL concedes precedent stating that OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” when conducting a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no claim to an entitlement to information under section 1214, AFL cannot demonstrate a concrete injury for purposes of its second standing theory. Similarly, to the extent that OSC is not obligated to observe the notice requirements of section 1214 in a section 1216 inquiry, a court order compelling OSC to undertake such an investigation is not reasonably likely to redress AFL’s asserted injury.

III

As AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
