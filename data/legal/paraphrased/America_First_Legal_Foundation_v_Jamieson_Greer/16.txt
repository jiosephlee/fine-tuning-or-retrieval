Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly refused AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court decided the merits against AFL and dismissed its claims. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency responsible for investigating and prosecuting matters, with the purpose of promoting “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). The Civil Service Reform Act grants the OSC the authority to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, if appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board—either seeking corrective measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures in accordance with 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 specifies the processes that apply to corrective actions involving prohibited personnel practices, and includes requirements to keep the complainant informed regarding the status of the investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to examine and pursue corrective measures regarding specific categories of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this matter, Section 1216 stipulates that the OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” which pertains to withholdings not permitted by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When such alleged violations of FOIA occur, the OSC is authorized to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

Separately, FOIA includes a distinct provision that governs OSC’s enforcement obligations. According to FOIA, when a court (1) compels the release of agency records found to be improperly withheld, (2) awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) explicitly raises the question of whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” the OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In pursuit of its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice certain correspondence between an external lawyer and the DOJ's Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division refused this request on the questionable basis that it does not examine employee email accounts for responsive material without the employees' approval. AFL consequently filed a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ. In that proceeding, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents responsive to AFL's request. The adequacy of this production is not the subject of the present matter.

AFL further petitioned OSC to examine DOJ's purported FOIA policy of not searching employee emails absent the consent of the employee. Citing section 1216(a)(3), AFL maintained that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” into arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC, however, asserted it lacked the authority to investigate because the three conditions required to initiate an inquiry under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met with regard to the alleged FOIA policy. AFL countered that section 1216 obligated OSC to investigate regardless of whether FOIA imposed a similar requirement.

The district court issued a partial ruling in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action for failure to state a claim. It determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to look into claimed FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate was premised on an incorrect legal interpretation. Id. Nevertheless, the district court refused to require OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that the determination was entrusted to OSC’s discretion by section 1216(c). Id. at *6. The court therefore remanded the matter to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC again opted not to investigate, AFL requested the entry of a final judgment, and the district court dismissed its outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and our review is conducted de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, we must first examine whether AFL has met the criteria for standing under Article III, which is essential to the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither side initially discussed standing, we requested additional briefing on this point.

Article III of the Constitution confines the exercise of federal “judicial Power” to specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this requirement. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For Article III standing to exist, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury that the defendant caused and that is likely to be remedied by a favorable ruling. See Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The asserted injury must involve “an invasion of a legally protected interest” which is both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the plaintiff must allege facts that, if true, would plausibly establish each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL argues that OSC breached its asserted procedural entitlement to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, “the deprivation of a procedural right without a corresponding concrete interest affected by that deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—does not suffice” to establish Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this issue, AFL asserts that OSC’s decision not to investigate resulted in two particularized informational harms: First, the DOJ has not released the records that AFL sought via FOIA; second, OSC has failed to furnish the notices and information mandated for an investigation under section 1214.

By characterizing its claim in this manner, AFL aims to benefit from the more lenient standing criteria that apply when a procedural injury is alleged. According to precedent, “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In such procedural injury cases, plaintiffs are still required to demonstrate that the violation of a procedural right has “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet these prerequisites, nor does it satisfy even a relaxed form of redressability. Since AFL is unable to fulfill the more forgiving standing requirements applicable to procedural injuries, there is no need to determine whether the injuries described are, in fact, procedural or substantive in nature.

AFL’s initial argument for standing contends that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has obstructed AFL’s access to records sought through FOIA. However, this argument is undermined by insurmountable issues regarding causation and redressability. Any informational harm stemming from DOJ's denial of access to agency records originates with DOJ itself, not OSC. Furthermore, the purported link between OSC’s decision not to pursue a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s rejection of the FOIA request is overly conjectural and remote for Article III standing. The supposed causal sequence is: had OSC undertaken the purportedly required section 1216 inquiry, it may have chosen to initiate either a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary action under section 1215 against responsible DOJ employees. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB may have opted to penalize those employees for improper FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). This could possibly have led DOJ to release the records AFL requested. This narrative depends on conjecture regarding discretionary decisions by two separate governmental agencies not before the court (MSPB and DOJ), despite the principle that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also rests on supposition concerning how OSC would exercise its enforcement authority, a decision category that courts typically lack the power to mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). In light of such speculation, AFL has failed to demonstrate that a favorable judgment “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its alleged informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Additionally, AFL ignores the rule that redressability is usually determined by the degree to which a judgment binds the litigation parties rather than by whether its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL’s standing cannot be grounded in its claimed interest in obtaining the FOIA-requested information from DOJ.

AFL's alternative standing argument, which relies on its purported informational interest in the outcomes of the OSC investigation, is equally unavailing. For a plaintiff to establish informational standing, he must have suffered a concrete harm due to the denial of information to which a "statute entitled him to receive." Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, assuming arguendo that section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, this provision does not confer any right to obtain information. AFL argues that if OSC conducts what it deems a mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC may, at its discretion, provide AFL with notifications and details similar to those required when OSC investigates alleged "prohibited personnel practice[s]" under section 1214. Yet, AFL does not assert that OSC is required to apply section 1214's notice requirements to investigations under section 1216. Indeed, AFL concedes precedent affirming that OSC’s choice to apply section 1214’s procedural provisions in the context of a section 1216 investigation remains “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because AFL has not demonstrated any statutory right to receive information under section 1214, it lacks the requisite concrete injury for its second standing theory. For similar reasons, because OSC is not obliged to provide section 1214 notices in connection with section 1216 inquiries, issuing a judicial order compelling such an investigation would not be likely to remedy AFL's second asserted injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
