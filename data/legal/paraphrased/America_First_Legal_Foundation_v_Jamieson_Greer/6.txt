Case Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: 24-5168  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking a mandate requiring the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency unjustifiably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the case by dismissing AFL’s claims on their substantive grounds. We determine, however, that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an agency tasked with investigating and prosecuting cases to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). The Civil Service Reform Act grants the OSC authority to look into allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, if appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board seeking either corrective remedies pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 details the procedures applicable to obtaining corrective relief for such personnel violations, and includes requirements for informing the complainant about the status of the OSC’s investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 authorizes OSC to investigate and pursue remedies for certain additional types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinent to this matter, the statute states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, withholdings not allowed by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). Regarding these alleged FOIA violations, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

The Freedom of Information Act also includes a distinct provision concerning OSC enforcement. According to FOIA, if a court (1) orders the release of agency records unlawfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email communications between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected the request on what AFL describes as the questionable basis that it does not review employee emails for relevant records without first obtaining the employee’s consent. AFL subsequently initiated litigation against DOJ under FOIA. During that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—documents that are responsive to the request. The adequacy of these disclosures is not contested in this context.

Additionally, AFL requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee emails without their approval. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” in instances of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC, however, declined to assert jurisdiction to investigate on the grounds that the three conditions necessary to trigger an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. AFL maintained that section 1216 obligated OSC to undertake such an investigation even where FOIA itself did not impose such a duty.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the action on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC’s authority to investigate alleged FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to investigate stemmed from an error of law. Id. Nevertheless, the court declined to compel OSC to investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) left that determination to the agency’s discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for further proceedings. Id. When OSC once more declined to open an investigation, AFL requested entry of final judgment, after which the district court dismissed the outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL then appealed, and our standard of review is de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Before addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine whether AFL has met the requirements for Article III standing, as this impacts the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). As neither side initially discussed standing, we directed the parties to submit supplemental briefs on the issue.

The federal “judicial Power” under Article III of the Constitution is confined to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this case-or-controversy limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). To establish standing under Article III, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury caused by the defendant that is likely to be remedied by a favorable judicial outcome. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When faced with a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff bears the burden of pleading facts that plausibly establish each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached its asserted procedural right to an investigation pursuant to section 1216. However, as stated in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and further cited in Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), the mere loss of a procedural right—without the deprivation impacting a concrete interest—is not sufficient to establish Article III standing. To address this issue, AFL argues that OSC’s failure to investigate has resulted in two specific informational harms: first, the Department of Justice has not released the documents that AFL sought under FOIA; second, OSC has withheld the notices and information mandated during an investigation under section 1214.

By presenting its claim in this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing criteria that apply to alleged procedural injuries. As stated in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Plaintiffs in such situations must therefore demonstrate that a procedural right’s violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL meets neither this burden nor the lowered bar for redressability. Since AFL fails to establish standing even under the more permissive standards applicable to procedural injuries, there is no need for us to determine whether these claimed injuries are procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing contends that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has blocked AFL from obtaining records it sought under FOIA. This argument, however, is undermined by insurmountable issues related to causation and redressability. Specifically, any informational harm arising from DOJ’s denial of access to agency records is directly attributable to DOJ rather than to OSC. Furthermore, the link between OSC’s omission to initiate a section 1216 inquiry and DOJ’s failure to honor the FOIA request is too conjectural and remote to meet Article III’s requirements for standing. The supposed causal sequence is: Had OSC performed the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it may have chosen to initiate corrective measures under section 1214, or pursue disciplinary proceedings under section 1215, against DOJ employees deemed responsible. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have imposed sanctions on those employees for unjustified FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Afterward, DOJ might have released the requested materials. This sequence depends on speculation regarding the actions of two separate government entities that are not litigants here (the MSPB and DOJ), even though “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also presumes conjecture regarding OSC’s discretionary enforcement decisions, which courts typically do not have the authority to mandate. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Because of this tenuous causal chain, AFL has not demonstrated that a favorable ruling here “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Moreover, AFL fails to account for the principle that redressability usually hinges on the extent to which a judgment binds the parties before the court, not on the possibility that its reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). For these reasons, AFL cannot demonstrate standing based on its claimed interest in the information it requested from DOJ via FOIA.

AFL’s alternative argument for standing, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation, is also unavailing. In order to establish informational standing, a plaintiff must show a tangible injury resulting from the denial of information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, although section 1216(a)(3) may obligate OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not confer any right to receive information. AFL argues that, should OSC undertake the allegedly compulsory investigation pursuant to section 1216, it could elect to provide AFL with notices and information comparable to what would be mandated if OSC were investigating a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to apply the notice provisions of section 1214 to section 1216 inquiries. Indeed, AFL concedes that judicial precedent recognizes OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in a section 1216 matter as “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without any claim of entitlement to information under section 1214, AFL has not demonstrated a concrete injury to sustain this alternative standing theory. Similarly, since OSC is not obligated to supply section 1214 notices in a section 1216 investigation, an order from this Court compelling such an investigation would not be likely to remedy AFL’s asserted informational injury.

III

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
