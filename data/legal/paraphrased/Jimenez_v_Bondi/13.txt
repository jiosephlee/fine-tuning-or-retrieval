Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge rejected these claims, a decision subsequently affirmed by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, Petitioners challenge solely the BIA's denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners do not establish that they were subject to persecution, that any alleged persecution is linked to a ground protected by statute, or that the Colombian government is either unwilling or unable to protect them. The evidence likewise fails to indicate that Petitioners are unable to find safe internal relocation within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family both owned and operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

All four family members have been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012, and they supported Federico Gutierrez as a candidate during the 2022 Colombian presidential race. During this period, Petitioners canvassed door-to-door in local communities in an effort to garner votes in support of their preferred candidate. Toward the end of May 2022, Jimenez began receiving phone threats once or twice each week in connection with his Liberal Party activities. Other leaders and associates from the Liberal Party were similarly threatened. In June 2022, a fellow party member was abducted and killed, despite having previously reported the threats against him. Despite these incidents, Petitioners persisted in their campaign activities.

Jimenez held a provisional law license and was still a law student in 2022, when the events at issue occurred. He was also employed as an intern with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted individuals who were victims of domestic violence, extortion, and various other crimes. Acting in his provisional legal capacity, Jimenez was approached by a woman from San Bernardo in June 2022 who sought assistance regarding a dispute over her property. She informed Jimenez that her tenant had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested that he speak with the tenant about compensating her and vacating the premises. Jimenez visited the property in an attempt to broker an agreement, but was unsuccessful; the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and declined to enter negotiations. The client opted not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, instead expressing the hope that the tenant would relinquish the property as a result of the lawyer’s involvement.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was actually the client’s former spouse. While the ex-husband had previously held ownership of the property, he transferred it to the client pursuant to a divorce agreement. Jimenez additionally identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also known as “Don Rafa,” who serves as a leader within a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization that maintains political connections to Colombia’s sitting president, Gustavo Petro.

When visiting the property, Jimenez noticed that the third floor had been closed off by the health authorities. His further inquiries revealed that the closure was due to the premises being utilized for daily rentals to migrants and as a base for narcotics trafficking and prostitution. The property was also linked to reports involving kidnapping and homicide. Despite multiple efforts by law enforcement to execute searches and seizures at the location, these attempts apparently did not yield results. One officer from the local police warned Jimenez to keep away from the property and advised him not to involve himself or seek trouble.

Later in that same month, Jimenez received a menacing brochure at his workplace, which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez paid no attention to this warning and threw away the brochure. However, in the following month, while Jimenez was working at the furniture factory during the night, two individuals confronted him at gunpoint, instructing him to leave the neighborhood. One of the individuals accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and held him responsible for the searches and seizures conducted at the location. Once the two men departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to carefully supervise their children's journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the identical two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were away. These men attacked one of the factory’s employees and brandished a firearm while demanding information regarding the Petitioners’ whereabouts. Once more, they accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” involved in politics. The men reiterated their threat to kill Petitioners should they reappear, emphasizing that these directives originated from Don Rafa. Upon the employee's response that Petitioners were traveling, the men departed without inflicting further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual placing his arm around the shoulder of his younger son as the boy was leaving school. The individual covered the child's mouth with his hand while the boy attempted to get away, and instructed him to "run to your dad." Jimenez ran toward his son and shoved the individual, after which the boy ran toward Jimenez's vehicle. Following this event, the family stayed at Jimenez's mother's residence in rural Bogota for three weeks; during this period, they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer responded that nothing could be done and remarked that the police themselves were contributing to the issue. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave, which is what the Petitioners subsequently did.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without official admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the factual allegations put forth by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners then filed applications for asylum and withholding of removal, listing the minor children as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte as well as additional evidence presented in the record, the Immigration Judge denied Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ determined that Petitioners were credible, the IJ nonetheless concluded that Petitioners did not demonstrate: (1) the harm they experienced constituted past persecution; (2) a connection existed to a statutorily protected ground; (3) internal relocation was not a viable alternative; and (4) the Colombian authorities were unable or unwilling to offer protection against their persecutors. The IJ further denied Petitioners’ claims for relief under the CAT. Petitioners sought review, but the BIA upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA further found no merit in Petitioners’ due process claims that the IJ had infringed upon their right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was motivated by their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution for which the government is either unable or unwilling to provide protection. Petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT, nor do they pursue their due process claim on appeal.

A. Standard of Review

Ordinarily, our authority extends to examining final removal orders, which encompasses a combined review of applications for asylum. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our assessment of the Board’s rejection of asylum relief is constrained to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Given that a single BIA member endorsed the IJ’s ruling, our review is of the BIA’s judgment; nevertheless, “we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We examine the Board’s factual determinations under the substantial-evidence test. With this standard, the Board’s factual conclusions are binding unless the evidence in the record establishes that any reasonable fact-finder would be required to reach a contrary result. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General has the authority to extend to noncitizens who satisfy the eligibility criteria.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for this discretionary relief, it is the applicant’s responsibility to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that he meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is situated outside the country of their nationality . . . and who is either unable or unwilling to return to, and cannot or does not wish to seek the protection of, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a specific social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant may secure refugee status by “demonstrating past persecution, which establishes a rebuttable presumption that they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To establish eligibility, the applicant is required to satisfy three criteria: “(1) an event or a series of events that constitute persecution; (2) that such persecution was on account of a protected ground under the statute; and (3) that the acts were carried out by the government, or by entities that the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not provide a definition for persecution; however, this Court has determined that persecution involves the infliction of suffering or harm on individuals who are different because of race, religion, or political opinion, and such harm must be considered offensive and involve more than mere limitations or threats to an individual’s life or freedom. Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Furthermore, the Court does not analyze each event separately, but instead reviews them together, since their cumulative impact may rise to the level of persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The determination of whether a noncitizen has demonstrated past private persecution in their country of origin—as is at issue in this case—is considered a question of fact. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the several threats directed at them amount to persecution. While we do take threats into account when evaluating the overall effect of the mistreatment endured by Petitioners, our precedent states that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, Petitioners admitted at their merits hearing that they suffered no physical harm, and likewise acknowledged in their opening brief that they did not incur any lasting physical injury. Although it is not required that persecution involve physical harm, id. at 804, the harm experienced must be sufficiently egregious and must exceed mere limitations or occasional threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask that we take into account their ongoing emotional and psychological suffering. For instance, Duarte reports she struggles with insomnia and her son has developed panic attacks and experiences stress. Although Petitioners have faced unfortunate situations, the fears resulting from the threats they received do not meet the threshold of persecution. Even when events have caused significant physical stress or psychological discomfort, we have seldom, if ever, approved asylum claims based only on threats or emotional and psychological injuries. See id. (determining that an applicant’s claimed harm—including a 10-hour arrest and detention as well as threats—did not amount to persecution, notwithstanding that the incidents were “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged during oral argument, our precedent has denied asylum in cases presenting even more severe examples of alleged harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (concluding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not qualify as persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that threats involving machetes and being robbed six times was insufficient for persecution where “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Considering the evidence and these circumstances, we conclude that there is substantial support for the BIA’s determination that Petitioners’ experiences, even when viewed collectively, do not constitute past persecution as contemplated under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they endured was based on grounds protected by statute, specifically their collective political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not provided argument regarding the existence of such a social group nor have they demonstrated that they belong to one. Instead, their argument concerning the nexus requirement rests on the claim that Don Rafa persecuted them due to their political opinion. Thus, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners have shown the necessary connection between their alleged persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them because of their involvement in campaigning for an opponent of Colombia’s sitting president. In order to prove persecution on the basis of political opinion, an applicant must demonstrate that their political opinion constituted “at least one central reason” for their persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, the applicant’s political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm,” nor can it “play a minor role” in the harm experienced in the past or the fear of future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is well established that a persecutor may act based on multiple motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, in these mixed-motive scenarios, it remains necessary that one protected ground is a central reason for the persecution. Id. Accordingly, Petitioners are required to prove that their political opinion was or will be at least one central reason for the persecutors’ actions against them.

Petitioners have not demonstrated the necessary showing because substantial evidence exists to support the determinations of the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were primarily targeted as a result of Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s finances and security, rather than because of their political beliefs. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners persisted in their political activism despite menacing phone calls and the killing of a fellow campaigner. Petitioners do not reference any evidence in the record indicating that Don Rafa was responsible for those earlier threats linked to political activity. Notably, Petitioners only began to experience more serious threats after Jimenez became embroiled in a property disagreement with Don Rafa and Don Rafa discovered that Jimenez was investigating his leased property. During one threatening episode, an individual accused Jimenez of being affiliated with the prosecutor’s office and of orchestrating the searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. Additionally, when these same individuals later threatened Petitioners’ employees, they made clear that Don Rafa had sent them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not make a mistake in concluding that the principal reason for the threats was Jimenez’s professional and private dealings with Don Rafa, rather than Petitioners’ political opinions. Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to demonstrate past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of facing persecution in the future, partly due to the Colombian government's incapacity or unwillingness to safeguard them. They claim that the individuals persecuting them are closely connected to and collaborate with the Colombian government, making safe relocation within Colombia unfeasible.

The unable-or-unwilling inquiry "assesses evidence regarding a government's actions in response to the applicant's experience of past persecution." Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. "When evaluating a government’s response to persecution, two principal considerations are significant: (1) whether the authorities intervened or attempted to intervene to stop the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the applicant was provided with protection by the government, such as by being placed in protective custody." Id.

This analysis also requires consideration of whether the government was informed of the claimed persecution and had a chance to act. Id. at 861–62. As stated, “[w]ithout such notice, it is impossible to determine how the authorities would have responded, or whether the government would have offered assistance.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “an essential point in the analysis is whether the applicant reported their past persecution to governmental authorities.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “failing to report the incident can undermine an argument that the government was incapable or unwilling to prevent the persecutors’ actions,” it does not automatically result in denial of the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, the applicant may fulfill the requirement of seeking government protection by establishing that submitting a report to the authorities would have been pointless or posed a significant risk. Id.

There is ample evidence in the record backing up the findings of both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made, at best, limited attempts to seek assistance from governmental authorities. Both Jimenez and his father have held or currently hold government employment in Colombia, yet neither took action to report the alleged misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners’ testimony indicates that they only spoke with two police officers, who advised them not to pursue the matter; however, there is no indication that this constitutes the position of the Colombian government as a whole. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As found by the IJ, the record does not indicate that the Petitioners sought governmental protection beyond their local area or made use of Jimenez’s connections within law enforcement.

Petitioners contend that seeking assistance from government authorities would have been both pointless and hazardous, claiming that their persecutors are intimately connected with the Colombian government. However, the record does not substantiate this claim. The government was cognizant of the property at issue and made several attempts to conduct searches and seizures to confront the ongoing criminal activity. Thus, the evidence undercuts Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors are closely linked with the government and that authorities would have been unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners fail to demonstrate that, had the government been informed of the threats against them, it was or would have been incapable or unwilling to offer protection. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding that the evidence was lacking to establish the government's inability or unwillingness to protect where the applicant did not contact police based on a belief the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not satisfied the criteria necessary for asylum, we briefly consider their last contention concerning whether it would have been both feasible and reasonable for them to safely relocate elsewhere within Colombia.

The government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and bar an asylum claim if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence either that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of future persecution. Nonetheless, assuming arguendo that such a fear existed, the evidence supports the conclusion that Petitioners could avoid harm by relocating elsewhere in Colombia. Family members and relatives of Petitioners remain in Colombia without incident, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment in the criminal justice sector there. Importantly, Petitioners resided without harm with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks prior to choosing to move to the United States. Moreover, it is relevant that those who issued threats directed Petitioners simply to leave their neighborhood, rather than to depart from the country altogether. Accordingly, we conclude that any risk of future persecution faced by Petitioners could be reasonably lessened through safe internal relocation within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Moreover, because Petitioners cannot show a well-founded fear of persecution required for asylum, they also cannot satisfy the more stringent criteria necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that it is not feasible for them to relocate because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (maintaining that these relatives could themselves hold political views opposed to the government). However, we cannot grant asylum relief based on such speculative difficulties. Petitioners further argue that relocation is impractical owing to bureaucratic and financial obstacles, deficiencies in the judicial system, and because their alleged persecutors have connections with the president of Colombia. Nevertheless, Petitioners only cursorily address these assertions in their briefing, and for the reasons articulated above, these contentions do not withstand scrutiny.
