Title: Jimenez v. Bondi  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-9573  
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, Colombian nationals, sought relief by applying for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were denied by the immigration judge, and that decision was upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. Petitioners now contest solely the BIA’s denial of their asylum and withholding of removal claims.

We exercise jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and DENY the petition for review. The petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that the alleged persecution was connected to any statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian authorities are unable or unwilling to provide assistance. Additionally, the record does not support a finding that petitioners are unable to relocate safely within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons originated from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture factory in Bogota.

Since 2012, the family has maintained membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez, who ran in the 2022 Colombian presidential race. During that period, Petitioners canvassed local neighborhoods to solicit support for Gutierrez. Beginning at the end of May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening phone calls once or twice per week concerning his association with the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates within the Liberal Party were also threatened. In early June 2022, a colleague was abducted and killed, even though he had reported the threats directed at him. Despite these developments, Petitioners persisted in their political campaigning.

Jimenez, holding a provisional law license, was enrolled as a law student in 2022 when the events at issue occurred. At the same time, he interned with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, assisting individuals who were victims of domestic violence, extortion, and various other offenses. Acting in his provisional legal capacity, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman residing in San Bernardo who sought his assistance with a property dispute. She reported that her tenant had not paid rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez communicate with the tenant concerning the return of the property and vacating the premises. Jimenez traveled to the property in an attempt to facilitate an agreement, but was unable to do so—the tenant instructed Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and would not enter negotiations. The client chose not to initiate a formal eviction, opting instead to rely on the possibility that the tenant would voluntarily vacate in response to the involvement of a lawyer.

Upon reviewing the client’s property title, Jimenez determined that the tenant was actually the client’s former spouse. While the ex-husband had previously held ownership, the property was conveyed to the client through a divorce settlement. Jimenez also found out that the tenant, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa,” was identified as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization with political ties to the sitting president of Colombia, Gustavo Petro.

When Jimenez visited, he noticed the health department had sealed off the third floor of the property. Further inquiry revealed that authorities had closed the premises because it was allegedly being used for short-term rentals catering to migrants, as well as serving as a base of operations for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The location was also linked to incidents of kidnapping and homicide. Although law enforcement had made repeated attempts at executing searches and seizures, these efforts appeared to have been ineffective. Additionally, a local police officer warned Jimenez to avoid the property and to refrain from causing trouble.

Later in the same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing pamphlet at his workplace that declared, "death to snitches from Liberal Party." Jimenez ignored this warning and threw away the pamphlet. However, the following month, as he was working a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted him with firearms, instructing him to leave the area. One of them labeled Jimenez a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office, blaming him for the raids and confiscations at the premises. Following this incident, Jimenez and his spouse secured the factory and began to carefully oversee their children’s journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the previous threat, the identical two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a time when Petitioners were absent due to travel. During this visit, they attacked an employee and, brandishing a firearm, demanded information regarding Petitioners' whereabouts. Once more, the men remarked that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They reiterated their threat to kill Petitioners if they returned, emphasizing that this directive originated from Don Rafa. Upon being informed by the employee that Petitioners were away, the men departed without inflicting further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual placing his arm around the shoulders of his younger son as the boy was leaving school. The individual covered the child's mouth with his hand as the boy attempted to break free, and instructed him to "run to your dad." Jimenez hurried to his son and shoved the individual, while the boy ran toward Jimenez’s vehicle. In response to this event, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for a period of three weeks. While there, they made the decision to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez reported the incident to a patrolling police officer. However, the officer responded that nothing could be done and indicated that police officers themselves were contributing to the issue. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners followed this advice and left.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without lawful admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that Petitioners were subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded to the factual allegations presented by DHS. The Immigration Judge deemed them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners then filed applications for asylum and withholding of removal, listing their minor children as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimony of Jimenez and Duarte as well as other record evidence, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners' applications. Despite determining that the Petitioners were credible, the IJ held that they did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered constituted past persecution; (2) a connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation was not a reasonable alternative; and (4) that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to provide protection from their alleged persecutors. The IJ further rejected the Petitioners' applications for protection under the CAT. The Petitioners appealed this decision, but the BIA upheld the IJ and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also found no merit to Petitioners’ due process claims regarding denial of their right to counsel by the IJ.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s determination rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA incorrectly concluded they failed to demonstrate: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was on the basis of their political opinion; and (3) that they have a well-founded fear of future persecution which the government is either unable or unwilling to prevent. Petitioners do not contest the denial of CAT protection or their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Ordinarily, our authority extends to the review of final orders of removal, which encompasses the collective examination of asylum-related claims. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our assessment of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is confined to the scope of “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). As the BIA’s decision was affirmed by a single Board member, our focus is on that decision, though “we may also refer to the IJ’s more detailed rationale for the same reasons.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

The BIA’s factual determinations are reviewed under the substantial-evidence standard. According to this review, such findings remain binding unless the evidentiary record compels any reasonable fact-finder to reach the opposite conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (altered citation).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum is a form of relief that the Attorney General may, at his or her discretion, provide to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for asylum as a matter of discretion, the applicant must demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that he meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is outside their country of nationality . . . who is either unwilling or unable to return to that country, and unwilling or unable to avail themselves of the protection of that country, due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on the basis of race, religion, nationality, membership in a specific social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant can obtain refugee status by “demonstrating past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To do so, the applicant is required to establish three factors: “(1) there was conduct amounting to persecution; (2) the persecution was motivated by one of the grounds protected by statute; and (3) the perpetrator was the government or a group the government was unwilling or unable to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners claim they experienced physical, psychological, and emotional injury, as well as the aforementioned threats, amounting to past persecution.

Although the INA does not specifically define persecution, this Court has previously determined that, for persecution to exist, “there must be suffering or harm inflicted on individuals based on their differences (such as race, religion, or political opinion) in a manner considered offensive, and it must involve more than mere limitations or threats to life and liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Further, incidents are not evaluated separately; rather, they are viewed in total, since “the combined impact of several occurrences may be sufficient to establish persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The determination of whether a noncitizen has established past persecution by private actors in their country of origin—the primary issue in this case—is a question of fact. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the series of threats directed at them constitutes persecution. While threats are relevant when we evaluate the overall severity of the mistreatment faced by Petitioners, our precedent states that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, Petitioners admitted during their merits hearing that they did not experience any physical harm, and likewise acknowledged in their opening brief that they suffered no enduring physical injury. Although actual physical harm is not required to meet the persecution standard, id. at 804, the harm experienced must be serious and go beyond simple limitations or occasional threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask the court to take into account the enduring emotional and psychological impacts they have suffered. Duarte, for instance, asserts that she experiences an inability to sleep or find rest, and that her son has begun to experience panic attacks and anxiety. Although the Petitioners have faced unfortunate events, fears resulting from the threats directed at them do not reach the standard required for persecution. While certain incidents may be physically taxing or cause considerable psychological disturbance, this Court has seldom, if ever, recognized asylum claims founded solely on threats or psychological and emotional distress. See id. (holding that the alleged mistreatment, which included a ten-hour arrest and detention as well as threats, did not constitute persecution, despite being "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). During oral argument, Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged that previous decisions have denied asylum even where applicants faced significantly more severe harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (determining that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not amount to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that threats with machetes and suffering six robberies did not rise to persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Under these circumstances, we conclude that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s determination that, even considering the totality of the harm suffered by Petitioners, their experience does not meet the threshold for past persecution as outlined by the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to statutorily protected bases—specifically, their common political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners did not submit briefing on the existence of their asserted particular social group or prove their membership within it. Instead, their nexus argument centers exclusively on the claim that Don Rafa persecuted them because of their political opinion. Consequently, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners demonstrate a nexus between the alleged persecution and their political opinion. In this regard, Petitioners claim that Don Rafa and his associates harmed them based on their work for a rival political campaign opposing Colombia’s current president. As established in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), to prove persecution on account of political opinion, an applicant must show that their political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the persecution. In other words, the political opinion at issue "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm" or play "a minor role" in the petitioner’s prior persecution or anticipated future mistreatment. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor may be driven by several motives for targeting a person. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, in mixed-motive situations, at least one protected ground must constitute a central reason for the harm suffered. Id. Thus, in this instance, Petitioners are required to show that their political opinion was or would be at minimum one central reason that led the persecutors to target them, or would lead them to do so in the future.

Petitioners have not met the required showing, as substantial evidence underpins the conclusions of the BIA and the IJ that Petitioners were mainly targeted due to Jimenez jeopardizing Don Rafa’s profits and safety, rather than based on any political opinion. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners persisted with their political involvement despite receiving threatening calls and the killing of another campaign participant. Petitioners do not cite any material in the record indicating Don Rafa was responsible for those earlier, politically motivated threats. Indeed, it was only after Jimenez became involved in a property dispute with Don Rafa—and Don Rafa learned of Jimenez’s efforts to investigate his leased property—that threats became more severe. On one occasion, an individual accused Jimenez of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for law enforcement actions at Don Rafa’s property. When those individuals subsequently threatened Petitioners’ workers, they specified that Don Rafa had dispatched them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not make a mistake in concluding that the primary impetus for the threats was attributable to Jimenez’s professional and personal associations with Don Rafa, and not to the Petitioners’ political views. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that the Petitioners’ political opinions played only a minor and incidental role compared to the central motive for the harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, regardless of their inability to prove past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly due to the Colombian government's incapacity or unwillingness to shield them. They maintain that their persecutors have strong connections and affiliations with the Colombian authorities, which makes internal relocation unsafe.

The analysis of whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “examines the government’s reaction to an applicant’s prior experiences of persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. This analysis is particularly informed by two categories of evidence: “(1) whether the authorities intervened or attempted to intervene against the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the applicant received protective measures from the government, for example, through being placed in protective custody.” Id.

A related aspect of this examination involves determining if the government was informed about the claimed persecution and provided a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. The absence of such notice makes it impossible to determine the authorities' potential response or whether assistance from the government would have been available. Id. (citation modified). Thus, a significant consideration in this context is whether the applicant informed government authorities about previous incidents of persecution. Id. (citation omitted). Although an applicant's decision not to notify authorities may weaken the argument that the government was either unwilling or unable to prevent the persecution, this alone does not automatically invalidate the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In certain cases, an applicant can meet the requirement to seek government protection by showing that submitting a police report would have been either pointless or would have put the applicant at risk. Id.

There is again ample support in the record for the conclusions drawn by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made little, if any, substantive effort to seek assistance from governmental authorities. Both Jimenez and his father have been, or currently are, employed by the Colombian government. Nevertheless, neither made reports regarding any alleged wrongdoing or corrupt activity. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). According to their own testimony, Petitioners engaged with two police officers who merely advised them not to pursue conflict, but there is no indication this advice reflected an official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As noted by the IJ, the evidence does not demonstrate that Petitioners attempted to obtain help beyond their local neighborhood or by using Jimenez’s existing law enforcement connections.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both futile and dangerous due to the alleged close connections between their persecutors and the Colombian government. However, this assertion lacks support in the record. The government was informed about the disputed property and had executed multiple searches and seizures in an effort to address the criminal activity occurring there. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners' claim that their persecutors are closely associated with the government and that the authorities would be either unwilling or incapable of intervening. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners have not demonstrated that the Colombian government was, or would have been, incapable or unwilling to offer protection if it had been made aware of the threats against Petitioners. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding that insufficient evidence existed to show government inability or unwillingness to protect, particularly when the applicant avoided contacting law enforcement based on a belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Therefore, we concur that Petitioners cannot meet the requirement of a well-founded fear of future persecution necessary for asylum eligibility.

F. Safe Relocation

Though Petitioners have not met the standards for asylum, we will briefly consider their remaining claim concerning the practicality and reasonableness of internal relocation within Colombia.

The government may overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and thereby deny an asylum claim if it establishes, by a preponderance of the evidence, that either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances such that the applicant no longer possesses a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, assuming arguendo that they had, the evidence of record substantiates the determination that Petitioners could reasonably and safely relocate to another region within Colombia. Family members and relatives of Petitioners continue to reside in Colombia without incident, and Jimenez’s father maintains his employment in the criminal justice system there. Importantly, Petitioners stayed without harm at Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area surrounding Bogota for three weeks prior to their decision to move to the United States. Additionally, it is significant that the individuals who issued threats to Petitioners instructed them only to leave the neighborhood, not to depart from the country altogether. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners could adequately reduce the risk of future persecution by relocating safely and reasonably to another part of Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, because Petitioners have not shown a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they likewise do not satisfy the more stringent threshold for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Although Petitioners contend that relocation within their country is not viable since the existence of family members there does not necessarily guarantee safety—arguing in their brief that these relatives could also possess dissenting political views—such speculative difficulties cannot serve as grounds for asylum. Petitioners further assert that relocation is impractical due to bureaucratic, economic challenges, issues within the judiciary, and the alleged connections between their persecutors and the President of Colombia. However, Petitioners present these points in an incomplete manner, and, in light of our prior conclusions, their arguments are unpersuasive.
