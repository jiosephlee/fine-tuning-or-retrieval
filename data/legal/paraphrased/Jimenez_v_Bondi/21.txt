Title: Jimenez v. Bondi  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-9573  
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The Petitioners, who are citizens and natives of Colombia, submitted requests for asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge denied all their claims, and this decision was upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, Petitioners challenge solely the BIA’s denial of their asylum and withholding of removal applications.

Having jurisdiction pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any purported persecution was connected to a ground protected by statute, or that the Colombian authorities are either unwilling or unable to offer protection. Additionally, nothing in the record substantiates that the Petitioners are unable to safely resettle elsewhere in Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, along with his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte and their two young sons, hail from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to their move to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and worked at a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, they have been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and backed Federico Gutierrez in the 2022 Colombian presidential race. During that time, the Petitioners engaged in canvassing, visiting homes in their neighborhoods to solicit votes. Beginning in late May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls—one or two each week—related to his participation with the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates within the Party were also subject to threats. In June 2022, a colleague who had previously reported similar threats was abducted and killed. Despite these incidents, Petitioners persisted in their campaigning activities.

Jimenez possessed a provisional law license and, during the year 2022 when the pertinent incidents occurred, was still a law student. Additionally, he served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where his responsibilities included assisting individuals affected by domestic violence, extortion, and other criminal acts. Acting under his provisional lawyer status, he was approached by a woman from San Bernardo in June 2022 seeking his aid with a dispute over property. She reported that she had not received rental payments from the current occupant for more than a year and requested that Jimenez contact the tenant to discuss recovering rent and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to visit the property in order to facilitate a resolution but his efforts failed; the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and declined any negotiations. Ultimately, the client chose not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, expressing a preference instead that the tenant might give up the property voluntarily upon learning a lawyer was involved.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s property, Jimenez determined that the individual renting the premises was the client’s former husband. While the ex-husband once held ownership, the property had been conveyed to the client through a divorce agreement. Further inquiry revealed that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, commonly referred to as “Don Rafa,” who leads a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization that shares political alignment with Colombia’s sitting president, Gustavo Petro.

During an inspection, Jimenez noticed that the property’s third floor had been cordoned off by the health department. Continued investigation led Jimenez to discover that authorities had shuttered the property because it served as a transient rental for migrants and functioned as a hub for both drug trafficking and prostitution. The location was also implicated in cases involving abduction and homicide. Law enforcement had made several efforts to conduct searches and seizures, which evidently did not succeed. A police officer from the area advised Jimenez to avoid the property altogether and to refrain from getting involved.

Later that same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing brochure at his factory that read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez paid no attention to the threat and threw the brochure away. However, the following month, as he worked at the furniture factory during the night, two individuals confronted Jimenez at gunpoint and ordered him to vacate the neighborhood. One of them accused Jimenez of acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures conducted at the property. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and thereafter vigilantly supervised their children's journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial threat, the same individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were absent from town. During this visit, they physically attacked a factory worker and brandished a firearm while insisting on the whereabouts of Petitioners. The individuals reiterated that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” involved in politics and issued another death threat should Petitioners reappear, specifying that the directive originated from Don Rafa. Upon hearing from the employee that Petitioners were traveling, the men departed without causing further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez saw a man place his arm around his younger son's shoulders as the boy was leaving school. The individual covered the son's mouth with his hand while the child attempted to break free, instructing him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried toward his son and shoved the man away, enabling the boy to run to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this incident, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for three weeks, during which they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez reported the events to a police patrol officer. However, the officer responded that no action could be taken and asserted that law enforcement officers were themselves contributing to the issue. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners subsequently followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following their entry into the United States without inspection or parole by an immigration official, Petitioners were each served by the Department of Homeland Security with separate Notices to Appear. These notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the veracity of the allegations set forth by DHS. The Immigration Judge determined them to be inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently filed applications for asylum and withholding of removal, listing the minor children as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

After considering the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte along with additional evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners’ applications. The IJ determined that, while Petitioners were credible, they did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a protected statutory ground; (3) that internal relocation within Colombia was not a viable option; and (4) that the Colombian authorities were unable or unwilling to offer protection from those who threatened them. The IJ further rejected Petitioners’ applications for relief under the CAT. Petitioners sought review, but the BIA upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal, also disagreeing with Petitioners’ claims that their right to counsel had been violated by the IJ, finding no due process violation.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s ruling, which rejected their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in determining that they failed to demonstrate: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that such persecution was based on their political opinion; and (3) that they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution for which the government cannot or will not offer protection. Petitioners do not challenge the denial of relief under the CAT, nor do they contest the adjudication of their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, our jurisdiction extends to reviewing final removal orders, which permits us to address all consolidated asylum issues. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our assessment of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since the BIA decision was issued by a single member upholding the IJ’s ruling, we examine the BIA’s rationale, but “we may still consider the IJ’s more thorough discussion of those same issues.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We apply a substantial evidence standard to the BIA’s factual determinations. This standard renders the BIA’s factual findings final unless the evidence compels any reasonable adjudicator to find otherwise. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation altered).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General has the authority to extend to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). For an individual to qualify for this discretionary asylum relief, he must establish by a preponderance of the evidence that he meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any person who is outside his or her country of nationality . . . and who is either unable or unwilling to return to that country, as well as unable or unwilling to seek that country's protection, due to past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases added) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)), one method for an applicant to establish refugee status is by demonstrating past persecution, which then creates a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future. See Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To satisfy this requirement, the applicant needs to establish three components: “(1) an incident, or a series of incidents, that constitute persecution; (2) persecution based on one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) acts carried out either by the government or by parties that the government is unwilling or unable to control.” Id. (citing Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

The petitioners claim that, beyond the threats previously mentioned, they experienced physical, psychological, and emotional injuries that amount to past persecution.

Although the INA does not define persecution, "we have determined that to establish persecution, there must be suffering or harm inflicted on individuals because of their differing race, religion, or political opinion in a manner that is considered objectionable, and this requires more than mere restrictions or threats to one's life and liberty." Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). "Our analysis does not treat each event independently but rather reviews them in aggregate, since the combined impact of multiple events can amount to persecution." Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). "The determination of whether a noncitizen has demonstrated past private persecution in their country of origin—which is the principal matter before us—is a factual inquiry." Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the various threats directed at them amount to persecution. While we take into account threats as part of evaluating the overall effect of mistreatment on Petitioners, our precedent states that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, during the merits hearing, Petitioners admitted that they had not suffered any physical harm, and likewise indicated in their opening brief that they had not incurred any enduring physical injury. While it is true that physical injury is not a prerequisite to demonstrate persecution, id. at 804, the harm endured must be egregious, going beyond simple limitations or occasional threats. See Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask that we additionally take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological effects they have experienced. Duarte, for instance, reports that she suffers from an inability to sleep or relax, and notes that her son has begun to experience panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced difficult situations, fears resulting from threats made against them are not enough to meet the threshold of persecution. Regardless of how physically taxing or mentally distressing certain incidents may be, we have very rarely, if ever, recognized threats and emotional or psychological distress alone as sufficient grounds for asylum. See id. (holding that even alleged harms such as a ten-hour detention and threats, which were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," did not constitute persecution). In fact, at oral argument, Petitioners’ counsel admitted that our precedent has declined to grant asylum in cases where even more serious harm occurred. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (“four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not constitute persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (receiving threats with machetes and being robbed six times was insufficient to establish persecution, given that “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

In light of these circumstances, we conclude that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s determination that, even when considered collectively, the harms suffered by Petitioners do not meet the definition of past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners further contend that the harm they endured was due to grounds safeguarded by statute—specifically, their common political opinion and their membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not provided briefing on the existence of this particular social group, nor have they demonstrated their inclusion in such a group. Instead, when discussing the nexus requirement, Petitioners argue that Don Rafa persecuted them based on their political opinion. Consequently, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners have shown a sufficient link between their alleged persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners specifically claim that Don Rafa and his affiliates harmed them because they were involved in a campaign against the sitting Colombian president. To demonstrate persecution on political grounds, the applicant must prove that the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, the political opinion of Petitioners "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm" and must not have only a “minor role” in their past or anticipated mistreatment. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that persecutors may act out of multiple motivations. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even in mixed-motive scenarios, at least one protected ground must serve as a central reason for the harm inflicted. Id. Therefore, in this case, Petitioners are required to establish that their political opinion was or will be a central reason their persecutors targeted or will target them.

Petitioners do not meet the necessary requirement, as ample evidence backs the BIA and IJ’s determination that Petitioners were primarily targeted due to Jimenez jeopardizing Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than because of their political views. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners disregarded menacing calls and the killing of a campaign colleague, choosing to persist in their political work. Petitioners fail to cite any evidence indicating that Don Rafa was responsible for those early threats linked to their political activities. Indeed, the increased threats directed at Petitioners began only after Jimenez became entangled in a property conflict with Don Rafa and once Don Rafa learned of Jimenez’s investigation into his rental property. On one such occasion, an individual accused Jimenez of being affiliated with the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for law enforcement actions against Don Rafa’s property. Later, when these same individuals threatened Petitioners’ employees, they conveyed that they were acting on behalf of Don Rafa.

Given these circumstances, the BIA was not mistaken in concluding that the primary impetus for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s business and personal association with Don Rafa, rather than any political opinion held by Petitioners. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that Petitioners’ political views played only a minimal and secondary role compared to the principal motive for the harm suffered.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even absent proof of past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of persecution in the future, partly because the Colombian government is either unable or unwilling to provide them with protection. They claim that those responsible for the persecution maintain close ties with, and are associated with, the Colombian government, thereby eliminating the possibility of safe relocation within the country.

The analysis regarding a government's inability or unwillingness to protect "considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution." Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In evaluating a government's reaction to persecution, "two types of information weigh heavily: (1) whether the government stopped or attempted to stop the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government extended protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody." Id.

Part of this examination involves determining whether the government was informed of the alleged persecution and was provided the chance to take action. Id. at 861–62. The court noted, “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” such a failure does not invariably defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In some cases, the applicant can meet the burden of the government protection requirement by proving that submitting a police report would have been either pointless or hazardous. Id.

There is considerable evidence in the record that upholds the determinations made by the BIA and IJ. The Petitioners made very limited, if any, attempts to solicit assistance from the government. Both Jimenez and his father have held or currently hold positions within the Colombian government, yet neither has reported any instances of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners indicated that they spoke with two individual police officers who advised them to avoid trouble; however, there is no indication that this advice represents the stance of the Colombian government as a whole. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As found by the IJ, the record fails to demonstrate that Petitioners attempted to obtain assistance beyond their own neighborhood or by utilizing Jimenez’s contacts in law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both futile and dangerous, based on their assertion that those who persecuted them had strong ties to the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this claim. Evidence demonstrates that government authorities were aware of the disputed property and had conducted various search and seizure operations aimed at addressing the criminal activities there. Thus, this evidence undermines Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors were closely affiliated with the government and that the government would have been either unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Moreover, Petitioners fail to demonstrate that the Colombian government was, or would have been, unable or unwilling to protect them if it had been informed of the threats they received. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding that evidence was inadequate to show governmental inability or unwillingness to provide protection, especially where the applicant chose not to involve law enforcement due to a belief that the persecutors were connected to the government).

Therefore, we concur that Petitioners are unable to demonstrate a well-founded fear of future persecution necessary to support their asylum claim.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have already not met the necessary criteria for asylum, we will briefly consider their last contention, which concerns whether safe and reasonable relocation within Colombia is a viable option.

The government is permitted to overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution, thereby precluding the granting of asylum, if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence that either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (referencing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming otherwise, the evidence substantiates the conclusion that internal relocation within Colombia is feasible for them. Members of Petitioners’ family, as well as other relatives, remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment with the criminal justice system. Importantly, Petitioners spent three weeks residing without incident with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota prior to choosing to depart for the United States. Additionally, it is noteworthy that the individuals who issued threats merely demanded Petitioners’ departure from the neighborhood, not from the country. Accordingly, we determine that Petitioners are able to avoid any risk of future persecution by relocating safely and reasonably to another area within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Moreover, because Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they inherently do not satisfy the more stringent criteria for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners argue that return is not practicable since “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (noting that those relatives might also possess anti-government political views). However, we are unable to grant asylum based merely on such speculative difficulties. Petitioners also contend that relocation is infeasible due to bureaucratic and financial obstacles, deficiencies in the country’s legal system, and the fact that their alleged persecutors are connected to Colombia’s president. Nonetheless, these contentions are insufficiently developed in their briefing and, in view of our preceding conclusions, fail to persuade.
