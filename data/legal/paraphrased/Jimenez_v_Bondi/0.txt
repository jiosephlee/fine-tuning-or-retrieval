TYMKOVICH, Circuit Judge.

Petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, sought relief by submitting claims for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. Their requests were denied by the immigration judge, and this decision was subsequently upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, Petitioners contest solely the Board’s decision to deny asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we REJECT the petition for review. Petitioners do not establish that they experienced persecution, that any mistreatment they claim was connected to a statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian authorities are unable or unwilling to offer protection. Additionally, the evidence does not indicate that Petitioners would be unable to find safety elsewhere within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, along with his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte and their two young children, are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family both operated and managed a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, the family members have been active participants in the Colombian Liberal Party and were supporters of Federico Gutierrez during his 2022 bid for the Colombian presidency. During this period, Petitioners canvassed in various local neighborhoods, soliciting votes on behalf of their preferred candidate. Starting in late May 2022, Jimenez began to receive menacing phone calls once or twice weekly, which appeared to be linked to his political activities with the Liberal Party. Threats of a similar nature were also made against other Liberal Party figures and associates. In early June 2022, a colleague was abducted and killed, despite having previously reported the threats he had faced. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their political advocacy and continued their campaigning efforts.

Jimenez held a provisional law license and was still a law student during 2022, the time at which these incidents occurred. Additionally, he interned with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, assisting individuals who had experienced domestic abuse, extortion, and various other crimes. In June 2022, acting in his provisional legal role, Jimenez was approached by a resident of San Bernardo seeking assistance with a conflict over property rights. She reported that her tenant had failed to pay rent for more than a year and asked Jimenez to approach the tenant to discuss both restitution and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to negotiate a resolution by visiting the property; however, the tenant rebuffed these efforts, warning Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refusing to enter discussions. Ultimately, the client opted against initiating a formal eviction, instead expressing hope that the tenant would relinquish the property simply as a result of legal involvement.

Upon reviewing the client’s property records, Jimenez determined that the tenant was the client’s former husband. While the ex-husband had previously held ownership of the property, it had been conveyed to the client pursuant to their divorce settlement. Further inquiry revealed that the tenant, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, widely referred to as “Don Rafa,” was a prominent figure within a narco-trafficking guerrilla faction that maintained political ties to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

During an on-site inspection, Jimenez noticed that the third floor had been cordoned off by the health department. Subsequent investigation revealed that authorities had shuttered the building on grounds that it was being utilized both as temporary accommodation for migrants and as a hub for narcotics distribution and prostitution. The location was additionally associated with allegations of kidnapping and homicide. Despite multiple attempts at searches and seizures by law enforcement, these operations appeared to have yielded no tangible results. A police officer from the area cautioned Jimenez to avoid the premises and advised him against getting involved.

Later that month, Jimenez found a menacing pamphlet at his factory bearing the message, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez dismissed the warning and threw the pamphlet away. However, the following month, while he was working a night shift at the furniture factory, two armed individuals confronted Jimenez, instructing him to vacate the area. One of them accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and confiscations carried out on the premises. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and subsequently became vigilant in supervising their children’s routes to and from school.

Approximately two months after the initial threat, the same pair of men returned to the furniture factory while Petitioners were away. They assaulted an employee and brandished a firearm, demanding the whereabouts of Petitioners. The men reiterated that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” who were also involved in politics. They issued a threat to kill Petitioners if they returned, specifying that the directive originated from Don Rafa. Upon hearing from the employee that Petitioners were traveling, the assailants exited without inflicting further harm.

A few days afterwards, Jimenez saw a man approach his younger son as the boy was leaving school, draping an arm around his shoulders. As the son attempted to break free, the man covered his mouth and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Responding quickly, Jimenez rushed over, shoved the man away, and the son fled to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated for three weeks to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area near Bogota. While there, they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez recounted the episode to a police patrol officer. The officer, however, indicated that there was nothing to be done, suggesting that even law enforcement officers were exacerbating the issue. He advised Jimenez and his family to leave. This prompted Petitioners to do exactly that.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following their entry into the United States without official admission or parole from an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to Petitioners. Each notice alleged that the respective Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of DHS’s factual allegations. The Immigration Judge subsequently found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Thereafter, Petitioners sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries under Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte as well as additional material presented in the case file, the IJ rejected the Petitioners’ applications. The IJ determined that, despite finding the Petitioners credible, they did not sufficiently demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered constituted past persecution; (2) a causal connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) the unavailability of reasonable internal relocation; or (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to provide protection from their alleged persecutors. The IJ further denied relief under the CAT. The Petitioners sought review, but the BIA upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. Additionally, the BIA dismissed the Petitioners’ due process claims regarding alleged interference with their right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) the occurrence of past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was motivated by their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution where the state would be unable or unwilling to provide protection. A. Standard of Review

Typically, our authority extends to reviewing final orders of removal, which encompasses a unified review of claims for asylum. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). The scope of our assessment concerning the Board’s rejection of asylum relief is restricted to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (citing § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since the BIA’s decision affirmed the Immigration Judge’s determination through a single member, our review focuses on the BIA’s opinion; nonetheless, we may refer to the IJ’s fuller reasoning for those identical issues. See Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We assess the BIA’s factual determinations according to a substantial-evidence standard. Pursuant to this standard, the factual findings made by the BIA are considered binding unless the record clearly shows that no reasonable factfinder could agree with the Board’s conclusions. See Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (altered citation).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may extend to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for asylum, applicants must establish—by a preponderance of the evidence—that they satisfy the statutory definition of refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). According to the INA, a refugee is defined as: an individual who, being outside his or her country of nationality, is either unable or unwilling to return to that country, and similarly unable or unwilling to seek its protection, due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on the basis of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant may achieve refugee designation by demonstrating past persecution, which then establishes a rebuttable presumption that the applicant harbors a well-founded fear of future persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To succeed, the applicant must establish three criteria: “(1) one or more events amounting to persecution; (2) motivated by one of the protected statutory categories; and (3) perpetrated either by governmental actors or by entities the government is unwilling or unable to control.” Id. (citing Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not provide a definition for persecution; nevertheless, our precedent establishes that persecution entails causing suffering or harm to individuals based on distinctions such as race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner considered objectionable, and must involve more than mere limitations or threats to one’s life or freedom. See Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (altered citation) (emphasis in original). Furthermore, individual events are not assessed in isolation; instead, we evaluate their totality, since a series of actions, when viewed together, may rise to the level of persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (internal citation omitted). The determination of whether a noncitizen has demonstrated past private persecution in their country of origin—a central concern in the present matter—is a factual issue. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (referencing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

Initially, Petitioners contend that the numerous threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are factored into our evaluation of the overall mistreatment experienced by Petitioners, precedent dictates that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, Petitioners acknowledged during their merits hearing that they did not sustain any physical injury, and likewise indicated in their opening brief that no enduring physical harm resulted. Though demonstrating physical harm is not a requisite for establishing persecution, id. at 804, the harm endured must be egregious and must go beyond minimal limitations or isolated threats, as articulated in Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners further contend that the enduring emotional and psychological trauma they have experienced should be taken into account. Duarte, for instance, reports persistent insomnia and inability to rest, while her son has reportedly begun experiencing panic attacks and increased stress. Although the Petitioners have undoubtedly faced distressing situations, their apprehensions and psychological distress resulting from the threats they received do not satisfy the criteria for persecution. While some incidents may be profoundly stressful or disturbing, this Court has seldom—if ever—granted asylum solely on the basis of threats accompanied by emotional or psychological impact. See id. (determining that an applicant’s purported harm, including arrest and detention for ten hours and threats, did not constitute persecution despite being physically and psychologically taxing).

Furthermore, during oral argument, Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged that precedent in our case law has declined asylum in circumstances with even more severe instances of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not amount to persecution).

Considering these facts, we conclude there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that, even when considered cumulatively, Petitioners’ alleged harm does not constitute past persecution as defined under the INA.

“To demonstrate persecution on the basis of political opinion, the applicant must show that the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the adverse treatment.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). This means that the political opinion in question must not be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm,” nor should it play “a minor role” in past events or the applicant’s apprehensions about possible future persecution. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). Naturally, it is possible for a persecutor to act with mixed motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Even in mixed-motive scenarios, however, at least one protected ground must serve as a central reason for the harm in question. Id. Thus, Petitioners are required to show that their political opinion was, or will be, at minimum one central motivation for the persecution they experienced or may face.

Petitioners have not demonstrated the necessary requirement, as substantial evidence underpins both the BIA’s and IJ’s determinations that Petitioners were principally targeted due to Jimenez’s impact on Don Rafa’s financial interests and security, as opposed to their political beliefs. Before the confrontation involving Jimenez and Don Rafa, Petitioners persisted in their political activities despite receiving threatening calls and the fatality of another campaigner. Petitioners have not identified any record evidence linking Don Rafa to those initial threats with a political association. The threats directed at Petitioners intensified only after Jimenez became embroiled in a property disagreement with Don Rafa and when Don Rafa discovered that Jimenez was examining his rental property. On one occasion, a threat involved an accusation that Jimenez was employed by the prosecutor’s office and responsible for law enforcement actions at Don Rafa’s property. Subsequent threats to Petitioners’ employees referenced Don Rafa as the person behind those actions.

Given this record, the BIA correctly determined that the principal impetus for the threats was Jimenez’s professional and personal dealings with Don Rafa, rather than any political viewpoint held by Petitioners. Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

The Petitioners contend that, even if they are unable to demonstrate prior persecution, they nonetheless possess a well-founded fear of persecution in the future, partly due to the Colombian government’s inability or unwillingness to provide protection. They claim that the persecutors maintain a strong affiliation with the Colombian authorities, thereby eliminating the possibility of safe relocation within Colombia.

The inquiry into whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “entails examining evidence regarding how the government responded to the applicant’s earlier experiences of persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. The analysis focuses significantly on two key considerations when assessing a government’s response: (1) whether the authorities intervened to halt or attempted to halt the persecutors, and (2) whether the government extended protection to the applicant, for instance, through measures such as placing them in protective custody. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 26. Failure to meet these standards may preclude eligibility for asylum and negate a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.

Central to this analysis is the consideration of whether the government was informed of the purported persecution and afforded an opportunity to address it. Id. at 861–62. In the absence of such notice, it remains uncertain how law enforcement might have responded or whether governmental assistance would have been forthcoming. Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “a significant factor in the analysis is whether the applicant brought prior instances of persecution to the attention of governmental authorities.” Id. (citation omitted). Although neglecting to inform the authorities can undermine an applicant’s assertion that the government was either unwilling or unable to intervene, such an omission does not necessarily preclude relief. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). The requisite showing of governmental inability or unwillingness may still be made if the applicant can establish that reporting to law enforcement would have been futile or placed them in jeopardy. Id.

There is ample support in the record for the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made limited, if any, attempts to request assistance from government authorities. Although both Jimenez and his father are currently or were previously employed by the Colombian government, they failed to report instances of corruption or misconduct. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners stated that they had conversations with two police officers, who cautioned them against getting involved; however, there is no indication that this represents an official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As the IJ concluded, there is no evidence demonstrating that Petitioners reached out for assistance beyond their local community or used Jimenez’s professional relationships in law enforcement to pursue help.

Petitioners contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both ineffective and risky, as they assert their persecutors have significant ties to the Colombian government. However, the record does not corroborate this claim. Authorities were cognizant of the relevant property and had undertaken multiple operations, including searches and seizures, to mitigate ongoing criminal activity. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors maintain close connections with the government and that authorities would either be unable or unwilling to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners have not established that the Colombian government was, or would have been, incapable or disinclined to offer protection had it been informed of the threats in question. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (determining that evidence was insufficient to demonstrate the government’s inability or unwillingness to safeguard the applicant, especially given that the applicant refused to notify police on the belief that the persecutors were affiliated with the government).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not satisfied the criteria for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining claim concerning whether relocating safely within Colombia is both practicable and reasonable.

The government may overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thus defeat an asylum claim—if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence either: “(1) a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances has occurred, so the applicant’s fear of persecution is no longer well-founded; or (2) the applicant could evade future persecution through internal relocation to another region of the country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even if such a fear were present, the evidence in the record indicates they have the option to safely move to a different region within Colombia. Their family members, as well as other relatives, continue to reside in Colombia without experiencing harm, and Jimenez’s father remains employed in the country’s criminal justice system. Importantly, Petitioners themselves were able to live without incident alongside Jimenez’s mother in the rural area outside Bogota for a period of three weeks before choosing to emigrate to the United States. Furthermore, it is pertinent that those who threatened Petitioners instructed them to leave the neighborhood, rather than demanding their departure from Colombia as a whole. Consequently, we conclude that Petitioners are able to reduce the risk of any future persecution through internal relocation within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, considerable evidence underpins the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they consequently do not satisfy the more stringent criteria for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for the aforementioned reasons, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that relocation is not feasible, noting that “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (suggesting that these family members could possess political views hostile to the government). However, asylum cannot be granted on the basis of speculative hardships of this nature. Petitioners further claim that relocation would be impractical due to administrative and economic challenges, deficiencies in the judicial system, and the alleged connections between their persecutors and the Colombian president. Nevertheless, these assertions are only superficially addressed in their brief, and, in light of our earlier findings, do not withstand scrutiny.
