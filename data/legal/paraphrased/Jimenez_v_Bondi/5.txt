Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Petitioners, who are citizens and nationals of Colombia, submitted requests for asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge rejected these requests, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld that decision. On appeal, Petitioners challenge only the BIA’s denial of their claims for asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we exercise jurisdiction and DENY the petition for review. Petitioners do not establish that they experienced persecution, that any purported persecution was connected to a statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian government is unwilling or unable to provide assistance. Additionally, the evidence does not indicate that Petitioners are unable to safely relocate within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to their move to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

The family has been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012 and participated in supporting Federico Gutierrez during his candidacy in the 2022 Colombian presidential election. During that period, Petitioners canvassed local neighborhoods, soliciting votes in favor of Gutierrez. Starting near the end of May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening phone calls one or two times per week, which referenced his activities with the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates within the Liberal Party also received similar threats. In early June 2022, one such colleague, after having reported threats, was kidnapped and killed. Despite these incidents, Petitioners continued their campaigning efforts undeterred.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and was a law student during the events in question in 2022. At that time, he was also serving as an intern with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted victims of crimes such as domestic violence, extortion, and others. In June 2022, acting as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached by a woman from San Bernardo who sought his assistance regarding a dispute over her property. She informed him that the individual renting her property had failed to pay rent for more than a year, and she requested that Jimenez contact the tenant about both restitution and vacating the premises. Jimenez went to the property to attempt to broker an agreement, but his efforts were unsuccessful—the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused any negotiation. The client opted not to initiate formal eviction proceedings and instead wished that the tenant would voluntarily leave upon learning that a lawyer was involved.

Upon reviewing the client’s property title, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was actually the client’s former spouse. Although the ex-husband had previously held ownership of the property, it was conveyed to the client as part of the parties’ divorce agreement. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa,” who is recognized as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization with political ties to Colombia’s sitting president, Gustavo Petro.

While at the property, Jimenez noticed that the health department had cordoned off the third floor. Further inquiry revealed that authorities had shuttered the premises because it had been operated as a day-rental for migrants and served as a hub for narcotics trafficking and prostitution. The property was also connected to reports of kidnapping and homicide. Law enforcement had carried out multiple searches and attempts at seizure, but these efforts appeared to have failed. A local police officer advised Jimenez to avoid involvement with the property and cautioned him against inviting trouble.

Later that same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing brochure at his factory that read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” He ignored the warning and threw away the brochure. However, in the following month, while working a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted Jimenez at gunpoint, instructing him to vacate the area. One of them accused Jimenez of acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures that had taken place at the property. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to carefully supervise their children’s trips to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were away. They physically attacked an employee of the factory and, brandishing a firearm, insisted on being told the whereabouts of Petitioners. The men reiterated that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They once more threatened that Petitioners would be killed if they appeared again, emphasizing that these instructions originated from Don Rafa. The employee informed the men that Petitioners were traveling, after which the men departed without further incident.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around the shoulder of his younger son as the child was leaving school. This individual covered the boy’s mouth as the child attempted to break free and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez rushed toward his son, pushed the man away, and the son ran to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated for three weeks to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area surrounding Bogota; it was there that they made the decision to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez reported the incident to a patrolling police officer. However, the officer responded that nothing could be done and further indicated that police themselves were exacerbating the issue. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners subsequently followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without lawful admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that Petitioners were subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the factual allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application.

Drawing from the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, as well as other evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners' applications. While the IJ determined that the Petitioners were credible witnesses, the IJ found that they did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they experienced constituted past persecution; (2) a nexus to a ground protected by statute; (3) that internal relocation was not a reasonable option; or (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to offer protection from their alleged persecutors. Additionally, the IJ denied the Petitioners' requests for relief under the Convention Against Torture. The Petitioners sought review, but the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld the IJ's decision and dismissed their appeal. The BIA further rejected the Petitioners' due process claims, concluding that the IJ had not violated their right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s determination rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was based on their political opinion; and (3) that they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, with the government being unable or unwilling to offer protection. Petitioners do not contest the BIA’s denial of relief under the CAT or their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Our authority typically extends to the review of final removal orders, which may encompass a combined evaluation of asylum applications. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). The scope of our assessment concerning the Board’s rejection of asylum relief is restricted to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since the BIA’s decision was rendered by a single member affirming the Immigration Judge’s findings, our review is directed at the BIA’s decision; nevertheless, “we may also look to the Immigration Judge’s more detailed reasoning on the same issues.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We apply a substantial-evidence standard in reviewing the BIA’s factual determinations. Under this approach, such findings are deemed conclusive unless the evidence in the record is such that any reasonable factfinder would necessarily reach a contrary conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (alteration in citation).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may extend to noncitizens who satisfy eligibility criteria.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for this discretionary form of relief, the applicant must demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is outside the country of his or her nationality . . . and who cannot or does not wish to return to, and cannot or does not wish to seek protection from, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant can establish refugee status by “demonstrating past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To satisfy this requirement, the applicant must establish three components: “(1) one or more incidents amounting to persecution; (2) motivated by one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) perpetrated either by the government itself or by entities the government is unwilling or unable to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not provide a definition for persecution; however, our precedent establishes that persecution involves the imposition of suffering or harm on individuals who are different due to race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner considered offensive, and it must go beyond mere limitations or threats to life and freedom. Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Instead of analyzing each episode separately, we evaluate them as a whole, since the combined impact of multiple events may amount to persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). Determining whether an applicant has demonstrated past persecution by private actors in their country of origin—which is the principal question in this matter—is a factual issue. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the numerous threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are taken into account when evaluating the overall effect of the mistreatment they experienced, this Court has determined that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Notably, Petitioners admitted during their merits hearing that they did not suffer any physical harm and also acknowledged in their opening brief that there was no enduring physical injury. Although actual physical harm is not required to establish persecution, id. at 804, the harm endured must be egregious and must exceed simple limitations or isolated threats. Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners contend that we should also account for their ongoing emotional and psychological injuries. Duarte, for instance, reports experiencing sleeplessness and an inability to rest, and states that her son began suffering from panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced unfortunate events, fears arising from the threats they received do not satisfy the threshold for persecution. Regardless of how physically taxing or mentally disturbing certain incidents might be, asylum has seldom—if ever—been granted solely on the basis of threats and accompanying emotional or psychological distress. See id. (holding that an applicant’s purported suffering, which included a ten-hour arrest and detention as well as threats, did not constitute persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As acknowledged by Petitioners’ counsel during oral argument, our precedent has denied asylum even in cases presenting more severe harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (determining that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” fell short of persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that threats with machetes and being robbed six times did not constitute persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Under these circumstances, we conclude that there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that the harm alleged by Petitioners, even when considered collectively, does not constitute past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they endured was due to grounds protected by statute, specifically, their common political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners failed to present any arguments in their briefing about the existence of such a particular social group or about their inclusion in that group. Instead, when addressing the nexus requirement, Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa persecuted them on account of their political beliefs. Therefore, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners can demonstrate a nexus between their alleged persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners specifically claim that Don Rafa and his associates harmed them because they were involved in the campaign of a political opponent of Colombia’s sitting president. In order to establish persecution based on political opinion, an applicant must show that their political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). This means that the political opinion in question “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” and must not have “a minor role” in the mistreatment experienced or feared. Id. (citing Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor may be motivated by more than one factor. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, in cases involving mixed motives, it remains necessary that at least one protected ground constitutes a central reason for the harm imposed. Id. Accordingly, Petitioners must establish that their political opinion was, or will be, at least a central reason why they were or will be targeted by the persecutors.

Petitioners have not met their burden because substantial evidence supports the conclusions of the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were principally targeted as a result of Jimenez jeopardizing Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than for their political beliefs. Before Jimenez’s confrontation with Don Rafa, Petitioners continued to engage in their political activities, even after receiving threatening phone calls and despite the death of a fellow campaign participant. Petitioners have not identified any evidence in the record indicating that Don Rafa was responsible for those early threats connected to political activity. The record reflects that heightened threats began only after Jimenez became embroiled in a property dispute with Don Rafa and following Don Rafa’s discovery that Jimenez was probing into his rental property. In one threat, an individual accused Jimenez of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches and seizures conducted at Don Rafa’s premises. When the same individuals later threatened Petitioners’ employees, they explicitly stated that Don Rafa had sent them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not err in concluding that Jimenez’s work and personal conflicts with Don Rafa, rather than Petitioners’ political views, constituted the primary reason for the threats. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to prove past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly on the basis that the Colombian government is either incapable of or unwilling to shield them. They claim that the perpetrators have strong affiliations with the Colombian government, making safe internal relocation unattainable.

The analysis regarding a government's inability or unwillingness to protect “considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. When evaluating a government’s response, two types of evidence are particularly significant: “(1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody.” Id.

This analysis also requires consideration of whether the government was made aware of the purported persecution and given a chance to act. Id. at 861–62. As the court noted, “[w]ithout such notice, it is impossible to determine how the authorities might have responded or if the government would have intervened.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “an important aspect of the inquiry is whether the applicant informed the government about the past persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting the persecution can weaken an assertion that the government was unable or unwilling to offer protection,” this omission does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can fulfill the requirement for seeking government protection by showing that making a report to the police would have been either useless or unsafe. Id.

There is ample evidence in the record to uphold the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made little, if any, effort to seek assistance from the authorities. Both Jimenez and his father are, or have been, employed by the Colombian government, yet neither chose to report any misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners indicated in their testimony that they conversed with two police officers who advised them not to cause trouble, but there is no indication that this interaction reflects the stance of the Colombian government as a whole. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ noted that the evidence does not demonstrate that Petitioners sought governmental assistance beyond their local community or through Jimenez’s professional connections in law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both ineffective and hazardous, given that their persecutors are allegedly closely connected to the Colombian government. However, this assertion finds no support in the record. The government had knowledge of the property in question and had undertaken multiple searches and seizures in an effort to address the ongoing criminal activity. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ claim that their persecutors are closely associated with the government and that the authorities would be unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners are unable to demonstrate that the Colombian government was, or would have been, either unwilling or unable to protect them had it been informed of the threats they faced. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding that there was inadequate evidence to show the government’s inability or unwillingness to provide protection, especially where the applicant refused to contact the police based on his belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not met the necessary criteria for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining contention concerning the practicality and reasonableness of relocating safely within Colombia.

The government may overcome an applicant’s presumption of possessing a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thereby defeat his asylum claim—if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence either that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming such fear existed, the evidence in the record substantiates the conclusion that Petitioners could reasonably relocate elsewhere in Colombia. Petitioners’ family members and relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment within the criminal justice sector. Additionally, Petitioners were able to reside safely with Jimenez’s mother in the rural area near Bogota for a period of three weeks prior to choosing to move to the United States. Importantly, the individuals who issued threats directed Petitioners to leave their neighborhood, not to depart the country entirely. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners have the ability to avoid any possible future persecution by relocating safely and reasonably to another area within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence exists to uphold the BIA’s determinations that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners do not establish a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they also do not satisfy the more stringent criteria necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we reject the petition for review. While Petitioners contend that return to their home country is not feasible because "the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety," Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (suggesting those family members might also hold political views opposing the government), we cannot grant asylum relief based on such speculative challenges. Petitioners further claim that relocation is impracticable due to administrative and financial obstacles, deficiencies in the country's judicial system, and connections between their alleged persecutors and Colombia's president. However, Petitioners offer only cursory argumentation on these points, and considering our prior conclusions, their assertions are unpersuasive.
