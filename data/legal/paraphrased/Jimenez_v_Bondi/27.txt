Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, who are Colombian nationals and citizens, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. Their requests were denied by the immigration judge, a decision that the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld. In this appeal, petitioners challenge only the BIA's decision regarding their asylum and withholding of removal claims.

Pursuant to our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any alleged persecution was connected to a protected statutory ground, or that the Colombian government is either unable or unwilling to provide assistance. Additionally, the record fails to indicate that petitioners could not reasonably relocate to a safer region within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to their move to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and were employed at a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, they have all been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and backed Federico Gutierrez in his 2022 presidential campaign. Petitioners engaged in grassroots efforts, canvassing local areas in search of support for the candidate. Starting in late May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening telephone calls once or twice each week linked to his participation in the Liberal Party. Other party officials and associates faced similar threats. In early June 2022, a fellow party member was abducted and killed after having reported similar threats. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their campaigning activities.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and was a law student during 2022, when the pertinent incidents occurred. At that time, he also served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, providing assistance to victims of domestic violence, extortion, and other offenses. Acting under his provisional authority, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo seeking assistance with a property conflict. She informed Jimenez that her tenant had not paid rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez communicate with the tenant about returning the unpaid rent and vacating the premises. Jimenez went to the property to attempt to broker a resolution, but was unable to do so. The tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused to engage in negotiations. Ultimately, Jimenez’s client opted against initiating a formal eviction proceeding, preferring instead to wait and see if the tenant would relinquish the property after becoming aware of Jimenez’s legal involvement.

Upon examining the client’s property title, Jimenez found that the tenant occupying the premises was actually the client’s former husband. Although the property had once been owned by the ex-husband, it had since been conveyed to the client pursuant to their divorce agreement. Jimenez also determined that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also known as “Don Rafa,” who is recognized as a leader within a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization that holds political allegiance with Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While at the property, Jimenez noted that the third floor was cordoned off by order of the health department. Further inquiry revealed that authorities had closed the property after discovering its use as a short-term rental for migrants as well as a site for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The location was also linked to reported incidents of kidnapping and homicide. Despite repeated efforts by law enforcement to execute searches and seizures at the site, such attempts appeared to have failed. A local law enforcement officer advised Jimenez to avoid the property and to refrain from involving himself in any disputes related to it.

Later in the same month, Jimenez found a menacing pamphlet at his workplace that read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez ignored this threat and threw the pamphlet away. However, the following month, while Jimenez was working a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted him at gunpoint and ordered him to leave the area. One of them called Jimenez a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and held him accountable for the searches and seizures conducted on the premises. After their departure, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to supervise their children's journeys to and from school more attentively.

Approximately two months following the prior death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were away. On this occasion, they attacked an employee of the factory and brandished a firearm, insisting on the whereabouts of Petitioners. The men once more referred to Petitioners as “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They reiterated their threat to kill Petitioners if they reappeared, indicating that these instructions had come from Don Rafa. After the employee informed them that Petitioners were out of town, the men departed without causing harm to anyone else.

Several days afterwards, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around the shoulders of his younger son as the boy was leaving his school. The individual covered the boy’s mouth with his hand as the boy attempted to break free, instructing him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried to his son’s aid, pushed the individual away, and his son ran to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for a period of three weeks; during this time, they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez recounted the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer responded that nothing could be done and indicated that law enforcement officers were part of the problem. The officer advised Jimenez and his family that they should leave. Petitioners followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without inspection or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued each Petitioner a separate Notice to Appear. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was removable from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the veracity of the allegations brought by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children designated as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries to Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with additional evidence presented in the record, the Immigration Judge (IJ) denied Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ found Petitioners credible, the IJ determined that Petitioners did not prove: (1) the harm they experienced rose to the level of past persecution; (2) there was a connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option; and (4) the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to shield them from their persecutors. Additionally, the IJ denied their claims for relief under the CAT. Petitioners subsequently appealed, but the BIA upheld the IJ's decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also found Petitioners’ due process allegations, including claims that the IJ infringed upon their right to counsel, to be without merit.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s order rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA incorrectly concluded that they failed to demonstrate: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that any past persecution occurred due to their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution that the government would not be able or willing to prevent. Petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or pursue their due process argument on appeal.

A. Standard of Review

We ordinarily possess authority to review final removal orders, which encompasses consolidated consideration of asylum applications. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our examination of the Board’s denial of asylum is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). When, as here, the Board’s decision was issued by a single member affirming the Immigration Judge’s findings, our review focuses on the Board’s analysis, though we may refer to the Immigration Judge’s more thorough reasoning on the same issues. Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

The Board’s factual determinations are subject to review for substantial evidence. Under this approach, its findings are binding unless the record compels any reasonable factfinder to decide differently. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum is a form of relief granted at the Attorney General’s discretion to noncitizens who qualify.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for discretionary asylum, the applicant has the responsibility to show, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he or she meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is described as: any individual located outside any country of that individual’s nationality . . . who cannot or does not wish to return to, and cannot or does not wish to seek the protection of, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An individual seeking refugee designation may establish eligibility by demonstrating past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption that the person harbors a well-founded fear of future persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To do so, the applicant must establish three requirements: “(1) the occurrence of one or more incidents that meet the threshold of persecution; (2) that occurred because of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) that these acts were carried out by the government or by groups that the government is either unwilling or unable to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners claim they endured physical, psychological, and emotional injuries, along with the previously mentioned threats, which they argue amount to past persecution.

Although the INA does not specifically define persecution, this court has determined that such a finding necessitates “the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). In evaluating persecution, “we do not assess each event separately, but rather examine them in the aggregate, since the totality of multiple incidents may amount to persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). Determining whether a noncitizen has demonstrated that they experienced past persecution by private actors in their home country—the primary question before us—is a factual matter. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the various threats made against them amount to persecution. While we take threats into account when evaluating the cumulative effect of the alleged mistreatment, our precedent dictates that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Notably, Petitioners admitted during their merits hearing that they suffered no physical harm and likewise stated in their opening brief that they did not endure any lasting physical injury. Although showing physical harm is not required to meet the standard for persecution, id. at 804, the harm endured must be egregious and exceed mere limitations or isolated threats. Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners request that we take into account their enduring emotional and psychological injuries as well. Duarte, for instance, reports that she is unable to sleep or relax, and her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and increased stress. Although Petitioners have unfortunately faced difficult experiences, apprehensions arising from threats they received do not satisfy the threshold of persecution. Despite the possibility that certain events may be highly distressing or cause significant psychological discomfort, we have seldom, if ever, granted asylum solely based on threats together with emotional or psychological distress. See id. (determining that harm suffered by an applicant—such as a ten-hour arrest and detention along with threats—did not constitute persecution, even though these events were “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As counsel for Petitioners acknowledged during oral argument, our precedent has dismissed asylum claims in cases involving greater harm than that alleged here. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not meet the persecution standard); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that threats involving machetes and being robbed six times failed to reach persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Based on these facts and the surrounding circumstances, we determine that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s conclusion that, even when considered collectively, the harms experienced by Petitioners do not rise to the level of past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to grounds protected by statute, specifically, their collective political opinion and their membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners failed to present arguments or briefing addressing the definition or existence of the claimed particular social group, nor did they demonstrate their inclusion within such a group. Instead, Petitioners’ arguments concerning the nexus requirement assert that Don Rafa persecuted them based on their political beliefs. Therefore, our review is confined to whether Petitioners have shown a sufficient nexus between their alleged persecution and their political opinion. In particular, Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa and his affiliates harmed them due to their involvement in a campaign supporting an opponent of Colombia’s current president. As set forth in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), an applicant claiming persecution on political grounds “must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Thus, the political opinion of Petitioners must not be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” nor may it “play ‘a minor role’ in their past or feared future mistreatment.” Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It remains possible that a persecutor may act for several reasons, as recognized in O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nonetheless, even when motives are mixed, at least one protected ground must be a central reason for the harm at issue. Id. Thus, in the present matter, Petitioners are required to show that their political opinion was, or will be, at least one central reason for the persecutors’ actions toward them.

Petitioners have not met that burden, as considerable evidence exists to support the conclusions reached by both the BIA and IJ that the primary motivation for targeting Petitioners stemmed from Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s income and safety, rather than from Petitioners’ political opinions. Before Jimenez became entangled in conflict with Don Rafa, Petitioners did not cease their political engagement despite receiving intimidating calls and witnessing the death of a fellow campaign worker. Petitioners also do not identify any evidence in the record connecting Don Rafa to those early, politically motivated threats. Indeed, it was only after Jimenez entered a dispute over property with Don Rafa and began investigating a property rented by Don Rafa that Petitioners encountered more severe threats. On one occasion, an individual accused Jimenez of acting on behalf of the prosecutor's office and of being linked to searches and seizures at Don Rafa's premises. Subsequently, when Petitioners’ employees were threatened by the same men, those men indicated they had been sent by Don Rafa.

Given these circumstances, the BIA properly concluded that the primary motivation for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s occupational and personal connections to Don Rafa, rather than from Petitioners’ political opinions. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that any connection to Petitioners’ political opinion was incidental and secondary to the principal cause of the harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to prove past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly based on the claim that the Colombian government is either incapable or unwilling to offer them protection. They further allege that the persecutors maintain close connections with the Colombian authorities, thereby rendering internal relocation within the country unsafe.

The “unable or unwilling” inquiry examines evidence regarding how a government has responded to an applicant’s prior experiences of persecution. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In assessing a government's reaction to such persecution, two principal factors are particularly significant: first, whether the authorities intervened or made efforts to intervene to stop the persecutors; and second, whether the government provided protection to the applicant, for instance, through placing the applicant in protective custody. Id.

This analysis also involves determining whether the government was informed of the alleged persecution and had an opportunity to address it. Id. at 861–62. As the opinion notes, “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” it does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In certain circumstances, an applicant may still fulfill the requirement of seeking government protection by showing that filing a police report would have been useless or put the applicant in danger. Id.

There is ample evidence in the record to justify the conclusions reached by both the BIA and the IJ. Petitioners made little, if any, meaningful attempt to seek governmental assistance. Both Jimenez and his father are currently or formerly employed by the Colombian government. Despite this, neither reported incidents of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners’ testimony described conversations with two individual police officers, who advised them not to pursue trouble; however, the record lacks evidence indicating these statements represent the official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ also determined that the evidence does not demonstrate that Petitioners sought support beyond their immediate community or made use of Jimenez’s connections in law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both futile and hazardous, asserting that their persecutors had strong connections with the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this claim. The government was cognizant of the relevant property and had conducted multiple searches and seizures to combat the criminal activity there. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors are closely associated with the government and that the government would be either unwilling or incapable of intervening to stop the persecutors. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Moreover, Petitioners fail to demonstrate that the Colombian government was or would be unable or unwilling to offer them protection if made aware of the threats. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (finding inadequate evidence that the government was unable or unwilling to protect where the applicant had refused to approach police due to a belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Therefore, we concur that Petitioners are unable to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution as required for asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not met the established standards for asylum, we will briefly consider their last contention concerning the practicality and reasonableness of relocating safely within Colombia.

The government may overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution, thereby defeating the asylum request, if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have not shown a well-founded fear of future persecution; nonetheless, even assuming such fear existed, the record substantiates the conclusion that Petitioners could feasibly move to a different region of Colombia. Their family members and other relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment in the criminal justice sector. Significantly, Petitioners resided without incident alongside Jimenez’s mother in a rural area near Bogota for three weeks prior to their decision to move to the United States. Additionally, the individuals who issued threats to Petitioners specifically told them to leave the neighborhood, rather than to exit the country. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners may reduce any risk of future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, there is ample evidence to support the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Moreover, since Petitioners have not shown a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they also cannot satisfy the more stringent threshold for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that relocation is not viable since “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (asserting that those relatives may also hold political views opposed to the government). Nevertheless, we cannot award asylum relief based on merely speculative difficulties. Petitioners further maintain that relocation is impractical due to bureaucratic and financial obstacles, inadequacies in the judicial system, and the influence their persecutors possess through connections to the Colombian president. However, these points are addressed only superficially by Petitioners, and, in light of our conclusions above, their arguments are unpersuasive.
