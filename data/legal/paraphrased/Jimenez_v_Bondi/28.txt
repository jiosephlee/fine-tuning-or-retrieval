Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, who are both natives and citizens of Colombia, submitted applications seeking asylum, withholding of removal, and protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge rejected all their applications, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld that decision. On appeal, Petitioners challenge solely the BIA's denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they were subject to persecution, that any persecution was connected to a statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian government is unwilling or unable to provide protection. Additionally, the record does not indicate that Petitioners would be unable to safely relocate within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two underage children hail from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and worked at a furniture factory in Bogota.

Since 2012, the entire family has maintained membership in the Colombian Liberal Party, and supported Federico Gutierrez during his 2022 campaign for the Colombian presidency. During the campaign, the Petitioners engaged in canvassing efforts, soliciting support from neighborhood residents. Starting near the end of May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening telephone calls once or twice each week that pertained to his political activities within the Liberal Party. Additional members and leaders of the Liberal Party similarly faced threats. In early June 2022, despite warnings about such threats, a colleague of theirs was abducted and killed. Nevertheless, the Petitioners persisted in their campaigning efforts.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and was still a law student during the events in question in 2022. He was interning at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted victims of crimes such as domestic violence, extortion, and others. While acting in his provisional legal role, Jimenez was approached by a woman from San Bernardo in June 2022 regarding a dispute over her property. She informed him that her tenant had failed to pay rent for more than a year and asked Jimenez to communicate with the tenant concerning the return of the property and payment of back rent. Jimenez went to the property to try to reach an agreement, but was unsuccessful; the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and would not engage in negotiations. The client opted not to pursue formal eviction proceedings, instead preferring to rely on the possibility that the tenant would vacate voluntarily due to a lawyer’s involvement.

Upon reviewing the property title, Jimenez determined that the individual occupying the premises was actually the client's former spouse. While the ex-husband had previously held ownership of the property, ownership had been transferred to the client following their divorce. Jimenez further ascertained that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa,” who is identified as a leader among narco-trafficking guerrilla factions politically allied with Gustavo Petro, the sitting president of Colombia.

While onsite, Jimenez noticed that the property’s third floor had been sealed by the health authorities. Upon conducting additional inquiries, Jimenez found that the property had been shuttered due to its operation as a day-rental for migrants and as a hub for drug trafficking and prostitution. There were also connections to reported incidents involving abductions and homicides. Multiple efforts by law enforcement to execute search and seizure warrants had reportedly failed. A local police officer advised Jimenez to avoid the property and cautioned him against becoming involved.

Later in the same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory that read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez ignored this warning and threw away the brochure. However, the following month, while working at the furniture factory during the night, Jimenez was confronted by two men who aimed guns at him and instructed him to leave the area. One of these individuals called Jimenez a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures conducted on the premises. After the men departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to vigilantly oversee their children’s daily travel to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same individuals returned to the furniture factory while Petitioners were away. During this visit, they attacked one of the factory employees and brandished a firearm, insisting on the whereabouts of Petitioners. Once again, they accused Petitioners of being "snitches for the police" involved in politics. They reiterated their threat to kill Petitioners should they reappear, stating the directive came from Don Rafa. The employee informed them that Petitioners were traveling, after which the men departed without causing further harm.

A few days later, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around his younger son's shoulders as the child was leaving school. The man covered the boy’s mouth with his hand while the son tried to get away, instructing him to "run to your dad." Jimenez rushed to his child and shoved the individual, at which point the boy ran to Jimenez’s vehicle. In response to the incident, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for three weeks, during which they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer informed him that nothing could be done and indicated that law enforcement officers were part of the problem. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners subsequently did so.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without inspection or parole by an immigration official, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States in accordance with the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners acknowledged the accuracy of the allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners then filed applications for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children designated as rider respondents and qualifying as derivative beneficiaries under Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, as well as additional evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge (IJ) denied the Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ determined the Petitioners were credible, the IJ found they did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered constituted past persecution; (2) a connection to a ground protected by statute; (3) that internal relocation was not a reasonable alternative; or (4) that the Colombian authorities were unable or unwilling to shield them from their persecutors. Additionally, the IJ denied the Petitioners' requests for relief under the CAT. The Petitioners sought review, but the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA further rejected the Petitioners' due process claims alleging that the IJ infringed upon their right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s determination rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA incorrectly concluded that they failed to demonstrate: (1) they had suffered past persecution; (2) the past persecution was due to their political opinion; and (3) they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution that the government is either unable or unwilling to prevent. Petitioners do not contest the denial of CAT relief or their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, our authority extends to evaluating final removal orders, which encompasses a consolidated assessment of asylum petitions. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). The scope of our examination of the Board’s refusal to grant asylum is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Where a single BIA member upholds the Immigration Judge’s ruling, our analysis centers on the BIA’s decision, though “we may rely on the IJ’s more thorough articulation of those same reasons.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We employ the substantial-evidence standard when reviewing the Board’s factual determinations. According to this standard, such factual findings are deemed conclusive unless the evidence in the record indicates that any reasonable decisionmaker would necessarily reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a form of relief granted at the discretion of the Attorney General to noncitizens who are found to be eligible.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). An applicant seeking such discretionary asylum relief must demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he qualifies as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as “any person who is outside any country of such person’s nationality . . . who is unable or unwilling to return to, and is unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of, that country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.”

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)), the court explained that an applicant may qualify for refugee status by demonstrating past persecution, which establishes a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To satisfy this burden, the applicant is required to establish three components: “(1) one or more incidents that amount to persecution; (2) persecution occurring because of one of the statutorily specified grounds; and (3) the persecution was either perpetrated by the government or by entities the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners assert that, apart from the alleged threats, they endured physical, psychological, and emotional injuries amounting to past persecution.

Although the INA does not expressly define persecution, “we have previously determined that persecution involves the imposition of harm or suffering on individuals who differ (due to race, religion, or political opinion) in a manner considered objectionable, and it must consist of more than mere constraints or menaces to life and liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). “Rather than analyzing each event separately, we assess them in combination, as the aggregate impact of several incidents may rise to the level of persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). “Determining whether a noncitizen has established past private persecution in their country of origin—which is the central issue in this matter—is a question of fact.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the numerous threats directed at them amount to persecution. While we take threats into account when evaluating the overall effect of the mistreatment that Petitioners faced, precedent states that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Significantly, Petitioners admitted during their merits hearing that they did not suffer physical harm, and likewise acknowledged in their opening brief that there was no enduring physical injury. Although demonstrating physical harm is not a prerequisite for meeting the persecution standard, id. at 804, the harm experienced must be significant and must rise above mere restrictions or isolated threats, as explained in Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask the Court to take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological damage they have suffered. Duarte, in particular, reports that she has difficulty sleeping or resting and that her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced unfortunate situations, the fears arising from threats directed at them do not meet the threshold required for persecution. However severe the physical or psychological effects of these incidents may be, this Court has seldom, if ever, granted asylum solely on the basis of threats and emotional or psychological distress. See id. (concluding that the alleged harm to an applicant—including a ten-hour arrest and detention, as well as threats—did not amount to persecution, despite their being "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling"). Furthermore, Petitioners' counsel acknowledged during oral argument that our precedent has denied asylum in cases featuring even more severe instances of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not constitute persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (determining that threats with machetes and being robbed six times was not sufficient to establish persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Based on this record, we conclude that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s determination that, even when viewed cumulatively, the harm alleged by Petitioners does not rise to the level of past persecution as that term is used in the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to statutorily protected bases—specifically, their common political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not set forth any substantive argument addressing the existence of their particular social group, nor have they demonstrated their inclusion in such a group. Instead, when discussing the nexus element, Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa persecuted them based on their political opinion. As a result, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners have shown a connection between their claimed persecution and their political opinion. In particular, Petitioners claim they suffered harm at the hands of Don Rafa and his associates because they participated in a campaign opposing the current president of Colombia. To establish persecution “on account of” political opinion, an applicant must prove that their political opinion constituted “at least one central reason” for the persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). Therefore, Petitioners’ political opinion “may not be incidental, tangential, superficial, or secondary to another motive for harm,” nor may it play “a minor role” in their prior harm or in any fear of future harm. Id. (citing Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor may have more than one motive. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even in mixed-motive circumstances, at least one protected ground must be a central reason for the harm inflicted. Id. Accordingly, Petitioners are required to prove that their political opinion was, or will be, at minimum one central reason why their persecutors targeted or will target them.

Petitioners have not demonstrated that requirement, as the record contains substantial evidence supporting the determinations of the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were principally targeted due to Jimenez posing a threat to Don Rafa’s income and safety, rather than on account of any political opinion. Before Jimenez's altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners were not dissuaded by threatening calls or by the murder of another campaign participant, and persisted in their political engagement. Petitioners also do not identify any evidence within the record indicating that Don Rafa was responsible for those earlier, politically related threats. Indeed, it was only following Jimenez’s involvement in a dispute over property with Don Rafa, and after Don Rafa learned Jimenez was probing into his leased property, that the threats intensified. On one such occasion, an individual accused Jimenez of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and held him responsible for searches and seizures at Don Rafa's location. Subsequently, when similar threats were made to Petitioners’ employees, the individuals asserted that Don Rafa had dispatched them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not commit error in determining that the primary reason for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s professional and personal connections to Don Rafa, rather than from Petitioners’ political opinion. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that Petitioners’ political opinion played only a minor and incidental role relative to the principal basis for the harm suffered.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even absent a showing of past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly due to the Colombian government’s inability or unwillingness to offer protection. They allege that their persecutors maintain strong connections with the Colombian government, making internal relocation within Colombia unsafe.

The evaluation of governmental inability or unwillingness involves a review of “evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In this inquiry, two main types of evidence are particularly significant: first, whether the government took action or attempted to intervene against the persecutors, and second, whether the applicant was provided protection by the government, such as placement in protective custody. Id.

Embedded within this assessment is the question of whether the government had both notice of the claimed persecution and a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. The absence of such notice leaves uncertainty about how the police might have responded or if government assistance would have been provided. Id. (citation modified). Thus, “whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government” constitutes an important factual consideration in this analysis. Id. (citation omitted). Although “failing to report may weaken the argument that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” this omission does not automatically doom the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In some circumstances, an applicant may fulfill the requirement of seeking government protection by establishing that submitting a police report would have been ineffective or posed a risk to safety. Id.

There is ample evidence in the record to back the conclusions reached by the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made little, if any, effort to solicit assistance from the government. Both Jimenez and his father have held or currently hold government jobs in Colombia; nevertheless, neither brought reports of misconduct or corruption to authorities. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners testified that they had conversations with two police officers, who advised them not to pursue trouble, but the record lacks any indication that this advice represents the broader position of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ additionally determined that there was no evidence in the record that the Petitioners looked for help outside of their immediate locality or through Jimenez’s connections in law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both futile and perilous, given their claim that their persecutors maintain close connections with the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this contention. Evidence demonstrates that the authorities were informed about the property in question and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in efforts to combat ongoing criminal activity there. Thus, the record contradicts Petitioners' assertion that their persecutors are in close association with the government and that the authorities would therefore be incapable or unwilling to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners have not demonstrated that the Colombian government was, or would be, powerless or unwilling to shield them if notified about the threats made against them. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding that evidence was insufficient to show the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect the applicant, especially since the applicant opted not to approach police due to the belief that his persecutors were “part of the government”).

Consequently, we concur that Petitioners are unable to demonstrate a well-founded fear of future persecution required for asylum eligibility.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not met the burden necessary for asylum, we will briefly consider their remaining claim concerning whether internal relocation within Colombia is both possible and reasonable.

The government has the ability to overcome an applicant’s presumption of having a well-founded fear of persecution—thus precluding a grant of asylum—by proving by a preponderance of the evidence either that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances so that the applicant no longer faces a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could reasonably escape future persecution by moving to another region within the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming they had, the evidence in the record supports the conclusion that they could reasonably resettle in a different region of Colombia. The Petitioners’ relatives and family members continue to reside safely in Colombia, and Jimenez’s father remains employed within the country’s criminal justice system. Furthermore, the Petitioners themselves lived without harm alongside Jimenez’s mother in the rural area surrounding Bogota for three weeks before choosing to move to the United States. Additionally, the individuals who issued threats against Petitioners instructed them to leave the neighborhood, not to depart the country entirely. Consequently, we conclude that Petitioners have the option to avoid any risk of future persecution through safe and reasonable relocation elsewhere within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, because Petitioners are unable to show a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they likewise cannot satisfy the more rigorous threshold necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (quoting Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that internal relocation is not feasible since “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (noting those relatives might also hold anti-government political views). However, asylum cannot be granted on the basis of such speculative difficulties. Petitioners further claim that relocation is impracticable due to administrative and financial obstacles, deficiencies in the country's judiciary, and their persecutors’ connections with Colombia’s president. Yet Petitioners address these arguments only superficially, and, in light of our preceding analysis, their contentions are insufficient.
