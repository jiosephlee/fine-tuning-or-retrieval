Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Petitioners, who are citizens and nationals of Colombia, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were denied by the immigration judge, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld this decision. On appeal, Petitioners challenge only the BIA’s rulings regarding asylum and withholding of removal.

We exercise jurisdiction pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not shown that they experienced persecution, that any persecution they allege is connected to a ground protected by statute, or that the Colombian government is either unable or unwilling to protect them. Additionally, the evidence does not indicate that Petitioners could not relocate to another part of Colombia safely.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, they both owned and worked at a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, the family collectively held membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and actively supported Federico Gutierrez during the 2022 Colombian presidential campaign. Petitioners participated in canvassing neighborhoods door-to-door to garner support. Starting near the end of May 2022, Jimenez started receiving threatening telephone calls about once or twice per week concerning his activities with the Liberal Party. Similarly, other individuals associated with the Liberal Party, including its leaders and colleagues, were also threatened. In early June 2022, despite having previously reported threats made against him, one such colleague was abducted and killed. Petitioners nonetheless persisted in their campaigning activities.

Jimenez held a provisional law license and was enrolled as a law student in 2022, the period when the incidents at issue occurred. He was also serving as an intern with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted victims of domestic violence, extortion, and various other offenses. Acting in his provisional legal capacity, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo who sought his assistance in a property matter. She informed him that the individual renting her property had not paid rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez communicate with the tenant regarding restitution and vacating the premises. Jimenez proceeded to the property to attempt to negotiate a settlement, but his efforts failed; the tenant warned Jimenez not to "look for trouble" and refused to engage in discussions. Subsequently, the client opted not to pursue formal eviction proceedings, choosing instead to rely on the prospect that the tenant would relinquish possession after being made aware of legal involvement.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s property, Jimenez determined that the tenant was, in reality, the client’s former husband. While the ex-husband had been the prior owner, ownership of the property had been transferred to the client pursuant to a divorce decree. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also known as “Don Rafa,” who is recognized as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization with political ties to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While at the property, Jimenez noticed that the health department had closed off the third floor. Through additional inquiry, Jimenez learned that authorities had shut down the building because it was allegedly used as a short-term rental for migrants, and as a hub for narco-trafficking and prostitution activities. The property also had connections to incidents of kidnapping and homicide. Although law enforcement had conducted multiple search and seizure operations at the location, these efforts appeared to have been unproductive. A local police officer cautioned Jimenez to avoid the property and not to involve himself in troublesome matters.

Later in the same month, Jimenez received a menacing brochure at his factory, which read, "death to snitches from Liberal Party." Jimenez chose not to pay attention to this warning and threw the brochure away. However, the following month, as he was working a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted him at gunpoint and ordered him to leave the area. One of them accused Jimenez of acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office, blaming him for the searches and seizures that had taken place at the location. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to vigilantly supervise their children’s travel to and from school.

Approximately two months following the previous threat, the same pair of men returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were away. While there, they attacked an employee and produced a firearm, demanding information regarding Petitioners’ whereabouts. The men once again accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” involved in political matters. They reiterated their intent to kill Petitioners upon encountering them again, specifying that this directive originated from Don Rafa. The employee informed them that Petitioners were traveling, after which the men departed without inflicting further harm.

A few days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual placing an arm around the shoulders of his younger son as the boy was leaving school. As the son attempted to break free, the man covered the boy’s mouth with his hand and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried to his son’s aid, shoved the man away, and the son ran to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this incident, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for a period of three weeks; during this time, they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the episode to a police patrol officer. However, the officer indicated that nothing could be done, additionally stating that the police themselves were partly responsible for the issue. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave the area. Petitioners followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without official admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that Petitioners were removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of DHS’s claims. The Immigration Judge determined that they were inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners then filed applications for asylum and withholding of removal, naming the minor children as rider respondents and as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application.

Drawing from the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with other evidence presented in the record, the Immigration Judge (“IJ”) denied the Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ found the Petitioners to be credible witnesses, the IJ nevertheless determined that they had not demonstrated: (1) that the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) that there was a connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option; and (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to provide them protection from their persecutors. The IJ further denied their requests for relief under the Convention Against Torture (“CAT”). On appeal, the Petitioners challenged the IJ’s decision, but the Board of Immigration Appeals (“BIA”) upheld the IJ’s ruling and dismissed the appeal. Additionally, the BIA rejected the Petitioners’ due process claims, finding no violation of their right to counsel by the IJ.

II. Discussion

The petitioners challenge the BIA’s order denying their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in concluding they failed to demonstrate: (1) prior persecution; (2) that such persecution was on the basis of their political opinion; and (3) a reasonable fear of future persecution which the government is unable or unwilling to prevent. The petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or their due process argument.

A. Standard of Review

Our jurisdiction typically extends to reviewing final removal orders, which permits us to examine combined asylum claims. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). When reviewing the Board’s denial of asylum, our consideration is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since the Board’s decision was affirmed by a single BIA member, our review centers on the BIA’s ruling; nevertheless, we “may look to the IJ’s more thorough analysis of the identical grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We assess the BIA’s factual determinations using a substantial evidence standard, meaning those findings are deemed final unless the evidence compels any reasonable adjudicator to a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may provide to qualified noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for asylum, the applicant must satisfy the burden of proof—by a preponderance of the evidence—that he meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual located outside the country of their nationality . . . who either cannot or does not wish to return to, and cannot or does not wish to seek protection from, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)), the court stated that an applicant may demonstrate refugee status by establishing past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. See Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (internal citation omitted). To meet this standard, the applicant is required to establish three elements: “(1) one or more incidents that amount to persecution; (2) that persecution must be on account of a ground protected by statute; and (3) it must have been carried out either by the government itself or by entities that the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not specifically define persecution; however, our precedent establishes that persecution involves causing suffering or harm to individuals who are different based on race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner considered offensive and which goes beyond simple limitations or threats to life and liberty. Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Rather than evaluating each event separately, we assess them together, as the aggregate impact of numerous incidents can amount to persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). Determining whether a noncitizen has demonstrated prior private persecution in their country of origin—the principal question at issue—is a factual matter. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the numerous threats directed at them amount to persecution. While we do take threats into account when evaluating the overall effect of the harm experienced by Petitioners, our precedent dictates that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, Petitioners admitted during their merits hearing that they did not suffer any physical harm and likewise acknowledged in their opening brief that they were not subject to any lasting physical injury. Although physical harm is not required to meet the standard for persecution, id. at 804, the harm inflicted must be egregious and exceed mere limitations or occasional threats, as explained in Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask the court to take into account their ongoing emotional and psychological injuries. Duarte, for instance, asserts that she is unable to sleep or find rest, and that her son has experienced panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced adverse situations, fears arising from the threats they received do not, on their own, meet the threshold for persecution. Even where events are physically taxing or psychologically distressing, our court has seldom, if ever, granted asylum solely on the basis of threats and related emotional or psychological suffering. See id. (holding that alleged mistreatment, such as a 10-hour arrest and detention and threats, did not qualify as persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). Additionally, as Petitioners’ attorney acknowledged during oral argument, our precedent has denied asylum claims involving even more severe forms of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not amount to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (determining that threats involving machetes and being robbed on six occasions were insufficient for persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Based on this record, we conclude that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s determination that, even when considered cumulatively, Petitioners’ experiences do not rise to the level of past persecution as that term is defined under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they endured was due to grounds protected by statute, specifically, a shared political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners failed to brief any contentions regarding the definition of their asserted social group or to establish that they belonged to such a group. Instead, in addressing the nexus requirement, Petitioners assert that the persecution they faced from Don Rafa was due to their political opinion. Thus, our review is confined to whether Petitioners have shown a nexus between the alleged persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them because of their involvement in a campaign supporting an opponent of Colombia’s sitting president. “To show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, the petitioner’s political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm,” nor may it be only “a minor role” in the petitioner’s experience of past persecution or anticipated future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor might act from several motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nonetheless, even in mixed-motive scenarios, a protected ground must serve as at least one central impetus for the harm imposed. Id. Therefore, Petitioners in this case must show that their political opinion was, or is expected to be, at least one central reason why the persecutors targeted or would target them.

Petitioners do not meet this requirement because ample evidence supports the determinations of both the BIA and the IJ that Petitioners were principally targeted due to Jimenez posing a threat to Don Rafa’s income and safety, rather than on account of their political views. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners remained undeterred in their political activity, continuing their efforts despite threatening phone calls and the murder of a fellow campaigner. Petitioners also fail to identify any record evidence indicating that Don Rafa orchestrated those initial threats connected to political activities. Escalation in threats only occurred after Jimenez became embroiled in a property conflict with Don Rafa, and subsequent to Don Rafa discovering Jimenez’s inquiry into his leased property. During one of these threats, an individual accused Jimenez of being associated with the prosecutor’s office and of instigating search and seizure operations at Don Rafa’s premises. Furthermore, when the same group later threatened Petitioners’ employees, they explicitly stated that Don Rafa was behind those actions.

Given these circumstances, the BIA correctly concluded that Jimenez’s business and personal conflict with Don Rafa, rather than any political opinion, was the primary cause of the threats. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, despite being unable to demonstrate past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of persecution in the future, partly because the Colombian government either cannot or will not provide them protection. They claim that their persecutors have strong ties and connections to the Colombian government, making safe internal relocation impossible.

The unable-or-unwilling inquiry “examines evidence regarding the government’s reaction to any previous persecution suffered by the applicant.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. According to the case, “Two primary considerations are significant in evaluating a government’s response to persecution: (1) whether the authorities took action or attempted to take action against the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government extended protection to the applicant, such as by placing the individual in protective custody.” Id.

A related aspect of this evaluation involves determining if the government was made aware of the claimed persecution and had a chance to act. Id. at 861–62. As stated, “Without such notice, it is impossible to determine how the authorities might have responded or whether the government would have intervened.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “an essential consideration in this analysis is whether the applicant reported the previous persecution to government officials.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “failing to make a report can undermine an assertion that the government was either unwilling or unable to protect against the persecutors,” such failure does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant may fulfill the requirement to seek government protection by showing that filing a report with the police would have been either ineffective or would have placed the applicant at risk. Id.

There is considerable evidence in the record to back the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. Petitioners made very limited, if any, attempts to solicit assistance from the government. Both Jimenez and his father have held or currently hold government positions in Colombia; nonetheless, neither reported any instances of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners gave testimony indicating they had conversations with two individual police officers, who advised them to avoid attracting trouble—yet nothing in the record demonstrates that these remarks represent the official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As determined by the IJ, there is no evidence in the record to indicate that Petitioners looked for assistance beyond their immediate neighborhood or through Jimenez’s connections in law enforcement.

Petitioners contend that seeking governmental assistance would have been pointless and hazardous due to the alleged close ties between their persecutors and the Colombian government. However, this assertion is not corroborated by the evidence in the record. The authorities were aware of the property in question and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in an effort to address criminal activities occurring there. Thus, the factual record contradicts Petitioners’ claim that the persecutors had significant connections with the government, such that the government would be unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners are unable to demonstrate that the Colombian government was, or would have been, either powerless or unwilling to provide them protection if informed of the threats they faced. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding insufficient evidence existed to find governmental inability or unwillingness to offer protection, particularly where the applicant refused to approach the police because of a belief that the persecutors were affiliated with the government).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not met the threshold for asylum relief, we will briefly consider their remaining contention about the plausibility and fairness of safe internal relocation in Colombia.

The government can rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thereby defeat the asylum claim—if it establishes, by a preponderance of the evidence, either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; nonetheless, even assuming such fear were shown, the evidence indicates that Petitioners could avoid harm by relocating within Colombia. The Petitioners’ family members and relatives remain unharmed in Colombia, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment in the criminal justice sector. Furthermore, Petitioners resided without incident with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota for three weeks prior to departing for the United States. It is also relevant that those who issued threats to Petitioners only instructed them to leave the neighborhood, not the country as a whole. Therefore, we conclude that Petitioners have the ability to effectively reduce any risk of future persecution by relocating to another region of Colombia in a safe and reasonable manner.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence exists to uphold the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they accordingly cannot satisfy the more stringent standard necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that internal relocation is not practical because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety,” referencing Pet’rs’ Br. 18 and suggesting that those relatives may oppose the government politically. However, asylum cannot be granted based on mere speculative difficulties. Petitioners also argue that relocating would be impracticable due to administrative and economic obstacles, deficiencies in the judicial system, and their persecutors’ connections to Colombia’s president. Nevertheless, Petitioners address these points only in a perfunctory manner, and in light of the foregoing analysis, their arguments are insufficient.
