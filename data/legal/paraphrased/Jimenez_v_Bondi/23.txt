Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, citizens and nationals of Colombia, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge rejected their applications, a decision subsequently upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. The present appeal only contests whether the BIA erred in its denial of asylum and withholding of removal.

Having jurisdiction pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any claimed persecution is connected to a ground protected by statute, or that the Colombian government is unable or unwilling to provide protection. Additionally, the evidence does not support Petitioners' claim that internal relocation within Colombia would not be safe.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and were employed at a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, all family members have been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez in his candidacy for the Colombian presidency in 2022. During that period, Petitioners canvassed neighborhoods to encourage voter support. Starting around late May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls once or twice weekly, which referenced his participation with the Liberal Party. Additional threats were also received by fellow party leaders and associates. In June 2022, a coworker who had reported being threatened was kidnapped and killed. Despite these incidents, Petitioners persisted in their campaigning activities.

Jimenez held a provisional law license and, during 2022 when the pertinent incidents occurred, was still a law student. At that time, he served as an intern at Colombia's National Prosecutor’s office, where he assisted victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and various other offenses. Acting in his role as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached by a woman from San Bernardo in June 2022 concerning a dispute over real property. She reported that her tenant had failed to pay rent for more than a year and sought Jimenez’s assistance in speaking to the tenant regarding both restitution and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to broker an agreement by visiting the property; however, the tenant rebuffed his efforts, cautioning Jimenez not to "look for trouble" and declining to engage in discussions. Instead of moving forward with formal eviction proceedings, Jimenez’s client elected to rely on the hope that the tenant, upon learning a lawyer was involved, would choose to relinquish possession of the property.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s real estate, Jimenez determined that the tenant was the client’s former spouse. While the ex-husband was the prior owner, ownership had been transferred to the client pursuant to the terms of a divorce agreement. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also referred to as “Don Rafa,” reputedly a leader among a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization that maintains political ties to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While inspecting the premises, Jimenez noted that the health department had shut off access to the property’s third floor. Additional inquiry revealed that authorities had closed the site due to its operation as a short-term rental for migrants, as well as a location associated with narcotics distribution and prostitution. Reports also linked the property to incidents of kidnapping and murder. Efforts by law enforcement to execute searches and seizures on the property had apparently met with no success. A local police officer cautioned Jimenez to avoid involvement with the property and advised against becoming entangled in any related difficulties.

Later that same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez paid no attention to the warning and threw the brochure away. However, the following month, as he was working a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted Jimenez at gunpoint and ordered him to leave the area. One of the assailants accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” working with the prosecutor’s office, blaming him for the searches and seizures that had taken place on the premises. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and, from that point onward, carefully watched their children’s journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the prior death threat, the same individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were absent from town. On this occasion, they attacked a factory worker and, brandishing a firearm, insisted on being told Petitioners’ whereabouts. Once again, the men accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” and involved in politics. They reiterated their intention to kill Petitioners if they were encountered again, specifying that the directive originated from Don Rafa. Upon being informed by the employee that Petitioners were away, the men departed without inflicting further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual placing his arm around the shoulder of his younger son as the boy was leaving school. This individual covered the son's mouth with his hand while the boy attempted to break free and instructed him to "run to your dad." Jimenez rushed to his son, shoved the individual, and his son sprinted toward Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated for three weeks to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area of Bogota, where they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer responded that no action could be taken and indicated that police personnel were themselves exacerbating the issue. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. The Petitioners then did so.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without proper admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued each Petitioner a separate Notice to Appear. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners acknowledged the accuracy of DHS’s factual allegations. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners then sought asylum and withholding of removal, designating the minor children as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum claim.

Relying on the testimonies provided by Jimenez and Duarte, along with additional evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge denied Petitioners’ applications. While finding Petitioners to be credible, the Immigration Judge determined that Petitioners did not prove: (1) the harm they experienced constituted past persecution; (2) a connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation was not a reasonable option; and (4) that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them from those they feared. Additionally, the Immigration Judge denied Petitioners’ requests for relief under the Convention Against Torture. Petitioners appealed this decision, but the Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed and dismissed their appeal. The Board likewise rejected Petitioners’ arguments that their right to counsel had been violated by the Immigration Judge, thereby denying their due process claims.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA's decision denying their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA committed error in concluding that they failed to prove: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that such persecution occurred due to their political opinion; and (3) that they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, with the government being unable or unwilling to offer protection. Petitioners do not seek review of the denial of protection under the CAT or their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, our authority extends to the review of final removal orders, which permits us to undertake a unified consideration of asylum-related appeals. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). In examining the Board’s refusal of asylum, “our review is confined to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Given that a single BIA member upheld the decision by the IJ, our analysis focuses on the BIA’s decision; nevertheless, “this does not prevent us from turning to the IJ’s more extensive rationale regarding those same grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

The BIA’s factual determinations are reviewed under the substantial-evidence standard. According to this approach, such findings are deemed binding unless the evidence compels a reasonable factfinder to reach an opposite conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General has the authority to bestow upon qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for this discretionary asylum, the applicant holds the responsibility to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is located outside the country of his or her nationality . . . and who is unable or unwilling to return to, as well as unable or unwilling to seek protection from, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on the basis of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)), the court stated that one avenue for an applicant to secure refugee status is “through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (internal citation omitted). To satisfy this, the applicant must demonstrate three elements: “(1) one or more incidents that amount to persecution; (2) perpetrated on account of a statutorily protected ground; and (3) carried out either by the government or by entities that the government is unwilling or unable to control.” Id. (citing Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

The petitioners claim to have endured physical, psychological, and emotional injuries, along with the threats described, which they contend amount to past persecution.

Although the INA does not explicitly define “persecution,” this court has determined that persecution involves “the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Our analysis does not treat each event independently, but rather assesses them together, recognizing that the aggregate impact of several incidents may meet the threshold of persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The question of whether an individual has demonstrated that they suffered past private persecution in their country of origin—the main question before us—constitutes a factual inquiry. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the series of threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are considered when evaluating the overall effect of mistreatment claimed by Petitioners, precedent holds that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Notably, at the merits hearing, Petitioners admitted they had not suffered physical injury, and likewise stated in their opening brief that they did not endure any enduring physical harm. Although establishing persecution does not require evidence of physical harm, id. at 804, the harm experienced must be sufficiently egregious and not limited to basic restrictions or occasional threats. Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask us to take into account the continuing emotional and psychological injuries they have experienced. For instance, Duarte asserts that she suffers from sleeplessness and fatigue, while her son has developed panic attacks and feelings of anxiety. Although Petitioners have faced regrettable situations, the apprehensions caused by the threats they received are not enough to meet the legal threshold for persecution. Regardless of how physically taxing or mentally distressing certain incidents might be, our precedents have seldom, if ever, granted asylum solely based on threats and resultant emotional or psychological distress. See id. (holding that even when a petitioner endured a 10-hour arrest, detention, and threats—which were described as “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”—such harm did not rise to persecution). As Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged during oral argument, precedent has denied asylum in matters with even more egregious harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” insufficient to qualify as persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that being threatened with machetes and robbed six times failed to meet the persecution standard because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Based on these facts and the record, we conclude that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s determination that, considered collectively, the harm Petitioners suffered does not constitute past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to statutorily protected grounds, specifically their purported shared political opinion and status as members of a particular social group. However, Petitioners did not submit any briefing supporting the existence of such a particular social group or substantiate their inclusion within it. Instead, their nexus argument is based solely on the claim that Don Rafa persecuted them on account of their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners have shown a causal connection between the claimed persecution and their political opinion. In particular, Petitioners claim that the harm inflicted by Don Rafa and his associates stemmed from their involvement in the political campaign of an opponent to Colombia’s current president. As the court noted in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), in order to establish persecution based on political opinion, an applicant “must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” In other words, the petitioner’s political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm,” nor can it play merely “a minor role” in the harm previously suffered or feared. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). While it is recognized that a persecutor might act out of several motives, as addressed in O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025), even in mixed-motive cases, at least one central motivating reason for the harm must be a protected ground. Id. Thus, in the present case, Petitioners are required to show that their political opinion was, or is expected to be, at minimum a central reason motivating the persecutors’ actions against them.

Petitioners do not meet this requirement because there is ample evidence supporting the conclusions of the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were targeted chiefly due to Jimenez’s impact on Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than because of political opinion. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners did not cease their political engagement despite threatening phone calls and the killing of a fellow campaign participant. Petitioners have not identified any evidence in the record linking Don Rafa to the earlier politically motivated threats. In actuality, the threats intensified only after Jimenez became involved in a dispute over property with Don Rafa, and once Don Rafa learned of Jimenez’s investigation concerning property he rented. On one occasion, a man threatened Jimenez by claiming he worked for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for law enforcement actions at Don Rafa’s property. When these individuals later threatened the Petitioners’ employees, they expressly stated that Don Rafa had sent them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not make a mistake in concluding that the primary reason for the threats was Jimenez’s occupational and personal connections with Don Rafa, and not the Petitioners’ political views. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that Petitioners’ political beliefs were only incidental and played a secondary role compared to the principal cause of the harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to demonstrate past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly due to the Colombian government’s inability or unwillingness to provide protection. They claim that the individuals persecuting them have strong ties with, and associations to, the Colombian government, thereby making it impossible for them to safely relocate within Colombia.

The analysis of whether a government is “unable or unwilling” to protect looks at evidence regarding how the government responded to the applicant’s prior persecution. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In evaluating a government’s reaction to persecution, two primary factors are considered: (1) whether the government intervened to halt or attempted to halt the persecutors; and (2) whether the government furnished the applicant with any protective measures, such as offering protective custody. Id.

Part of this analysis involves determining if the government was informed of the purported persecution and had the chance to act in response. Id. at 861–62. “Absent such notice, it is impossible to determine how authorities might have responded, or whether the government would have intervened.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “a central consideration in the analysis is whether the applicant notified the government about the alleged past persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting the incident can weaken an assertion that the government was incapable or unwilling to prevent the persecution,” it does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant may fulfill the requirement to seek government protection by showing that reporting the persecution would have been either futile or dangerous. Id.

There is again ample support in the record for the determinations reached by the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made, at most, minimal attempts to request assistance from the government. Both Jimenez and his father have been, or are currently, government employees in Colombia. Nevertheless, neither of them reported any acts of corruption or misconduct. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners stated that they spoke with two police officers who advised them not to cause trouble; however, the evidence does not establish that these interactions are representative of the Colombian government’s stance. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ correctly observed that the record fails to demonstrate that Petitioners sought governmental assistance beyond their own neighborhood or through Jimenez’s professional contacts in law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to claim that seeking assistance from the government would have been both futile and risky because their alleged persecutors are purportedly connected with the Colombian government. However, this claim is not substantiated by the record. The government was aware of the disputed property and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in response to the ongoing illegal activity there. Thus, the evidence actually undermines Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors are closely tied to the government and that the government would be either unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners have not demonstrated that the Colombian government was, or would have been, incapable or unwilling to safeguard them if it had been informed of the threats made against them. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding that there was a lack of evidence showing the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect, particularly where the applicant chose not to approach authorities due to a belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Therefore, we concur that Petitioners are unable to demonstrate a well-founded fear of future persecution required for asylum eligibility.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not satisfied the necessary elements for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining contention concerning whether relocation to another area within Colombia would be practicable and reasonable.

The government has the ability to overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution, and thereby bar a grant of asylum, if it demonstrates by a preponderance of the evidence either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances such that the applicant no longer maintains a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could escape future persecution by moving to a different region of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (referencing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming such fear existed, the evidence substantiates the conclusion that Petitioners could reasonably and safely relocate elsewhere in Colombia. The family members and relatives of Petitioners remain unharmed within Colombia, with Jimenez’s father maintaining employment in the country’s criminal justice system. Furthermore, Petitioners resided securely for three weeks with Jimenez’s mother in a rural area near Bogota prior to choosing to depart for the United States. It is significant that the individuals who issued threats against Petitioners instructed them merely to leave the neighborhood, not to exit Colombia altogether. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners have the means to avoid any future persecution through feasible and safe internal relocation within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, there is considerable evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Moreover, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution under the standards required for asylum, they are likewise unable to satisfy the more stringent burden for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that relocation is not viable since “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (maintaining that relatives may also harbor political views opposed to the government). However, asylum cannot be granted on the basis of such speculative hardships. Petitioners further allege that relocation is not feasible due to administrative and financial difficulties, flaws in the country’s judicial system, and their persecutors’ connections with Colombia’s president. Yet, these points are only superficially addressed in Petitioners’ briefing, and in light of our conclusions above, these arguments are unpersuasive.
