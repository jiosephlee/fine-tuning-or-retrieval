Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, submitted requests for asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge rejected these requests, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld that decision. On appeal, petitioners challenge only the BIA's denial of their asylum and withholding of removal claims.

Pursuant to our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they endured persecution, that any purported persecution was linked to a statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian authorities are unwilling or unable to provide protection. Additionally, the evidence does not support a finding that Petitioners are unable to relocate safely to another region within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and worked at a furniture factory in Bogota.

Since 2012, they have all been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and actively campaigned for Federico Gutierrez during the 2022 Colombian presidential race. The Petitioners engaged in canvassing by visiting local residences to solicit voter support. Starting in late May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening telephone calls once or twice per week related to his association with the Liberal Party. Similar threats were directed at other leaders and associates within the Party. In early June 2022, despite having reported the threats he was receiving, a colleague was abducted and killed. Despite these incidents, Petitioners remained resolute and continued their campaign activities.

Jimenez held a provisional law license and, at the time of the pertinent events in 2022, was a law student. Additionally, he served as an intern with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted individuals who were victims of crimes such as domestic abuse and extortion. Acting under his provisional license, he was approached in June 2022 by a resident of San Bernardo seeking assistance with a property dispute. She informed Jimenez that the tenant on her property had not paid rent for more than a year and asked him to contact the tenant regarding repayment and vacating the premises. Jimenez went to the property in an attempt to negotiate a settlement; however, his efforts were unsuccessful, as the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and declined any negotiations. The client decided not to initiate a formal eviction proceeding and preferred instead to hope the tenant would relinquish the property upon learning that legal counsel was now involved.

Upon reviewing the property title, Jimenez determined that the tenant was actually the client’s former spouse. Though the ex-husband had been the prior owner, ownership of the property was conveyed to the client through the divorce settlement. Jimenez further found that the tenant, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros—referred to as “Don Rafa”—was identified as the leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla faction with political ties to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While on the premises, Jimenez noticed that the third floor had been shut by the health authorities. His subsequent inquiries revealed that authorities had closed the property due to its use as a day-rental facility catering to migrants, as well as a hub for drug trafficking and prostitution. Additionally, the location had been linked to incidents of kidnapping and homicide. Multiple search and seizure attempts by police had reportedly not resulted in meaningful outcomes. A member of the local police advised Jimenez against approaching the property and cautioned him not to become involved.

Later in the same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory, which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez paid no heed to this warning and threw the brochure away. However, in the following month, as Jimenez worked a night shift at the furniture factory, he was confronted by two armed individuals. They threatened him with firearms and ordered him to vacate the neighborhood. One assailant labeled Jimenez as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the recent searches and seizures conducted on the premises. Following this incident, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to carefully monitor their children's journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the incident involving the death threat, the identical two individuals returned to the furniture factory while Petitioners were away. During this visit, they physically attacked one of the factory workers and brandished a firearm, insisting on being told Petitioners' whereabouts. The men repeated their assertion that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They issued another threat to kill Petitioners if they were seen in the future, explaining that these orders were from Don Rafa. Upon the employee informing them that Petitioners were traveling, the men departed without inflicting further harm.

A few days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around his younger son's shoulders as the child was leaving school. The individual covered the boy’s mouth while the son attempted to break free and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried over and shoved the man, allowing his son to run to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the countryside near Bogota for three weeks, during which they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to departing, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer indicated that no action could be taken and stated that police officers were partly responsible for the issue. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave. Subsequently, Petitioners did so.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without authorization by an immigration official, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that Petitioners were subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

By August 2023, Petitioners conceded to the allegations set forth by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Subsequently, Petitioners submitted applications for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children listed as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Drawing on the testimonies provided by Jimenez and Duarte, as well as additional evidence contained in the record, the Immigration Judge denied the applications submitted by Petitioners. Despite finding the Petitioners credible, the IJ determined that Petitioners had not demonstrated: (1) that the harm they experienced rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to any statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation within Colombia would not have been a reasonable option; and (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to provide protection from their persecutors. The IJ further declined to grant relief under the CAT. Petitioners pursued an appeal, but the BIA upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. Additionally, the BIA rejected the Petitioners’ claims that the IJ’s handling of the case violated their due process right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s ruling rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) persecution in the past; (2) that any past persecution was due to their political opinion; and (3) a credible fear of future persecution where the government would be unable or unwilling to offer protection. They do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT, nor do they pursue their due process claim on appeal.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, our authority extends to the review of final removal orders, which may encompass combined consideration of asylum petitions. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our examination of the Board’s refusal to grant asylum is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (citing § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Where the BIA’s decision is rendered by a single member affirming the IJ, our review is directed to the BIA’s ruling, yet “we may also consult the IJ’s more extensive discussion of identical issues.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

The BIA’s factual determinations are reviewed for substantial evidence. According to this standard, those factual findings are binding unless the evidence compels any reasonable adjudicator to reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may extend to qualified noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for such discretionary asylum, the applicant has the responsibility to demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is located outside of any country of that individual’s nationality . . . and who is unable or unwilling to return to, and unwilling or unable to seek the protection of, that country on account of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a specific social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)), the court stated that an applicant can establish refugee status “through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” See Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To prevail, the applicant needs to demonstrate three things: “(1) the occurrence of an incident, or incidents, amounting to persecution; (2) that such persecution is based on one of the protected grounds listed in the statute; and (3) that it was perpetrated by the government or by entities that the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (citing Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not specify a definition for persecution; however, our precedent states that to establish persecution, there must be “the infliction of suffering or harm on individuals who are different (in terms of race, religion, or political opinion) in a manner considered offensive, and it must involve more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Rather than evaluating individual incidents separately, we examine them together, as their combined impact may amount to persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The determination of whether an applicant has demonstrated past private persecution in their country of origin—a central question in this case—is a factual issue. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the numerous threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are a factor in evaluating the overall effect of the mistreatment suffered by Petitioners, our precedent dictates that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, Petitioners acknowledged during their merits hearing that they had not been physically harmed, and similarly stated in their opening brief that they did not endure any lasting physical injury. Although physical harm is not a requisite element for meeting the persecution requirement, id. at 804, the harm experienced must be egregious and go beyond simple restrictions or occasional threats, see Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask that we weigh the ongoing emotional and psychological injuries they have suffered. For instance, Duarte describes persistent sleeplessness and an inability to rest, while noting that her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced regrettable situations, such anxiety arising from threats they received does not meet the threshold required for persecution. Despite the physical distress or psychological discomfort some experiences may produce, our precedent seldom—if ever—recognizes asylum claims grounded solely in threats or emotional and psychological trauma. See id. (holding that an applicant’s experiences, such as a 10-hour arrest and detention and threats, did not qualify as persecution, even when deemed “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). Indeed, as acknowledged by Petitioners’ counsel during oral argument, our existing jurisprudence has denied asylum requests in matters presenting even more severe examples of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not rise to the level of persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that threats with machetes and being robbed on six occasions fell short of constituting persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

In light of these considerations, we conclude that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s determination that the harm suffered by Petitioners, even when viewed collectively, fails to constitute past persecution within the meaning of the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to grounds protected by statute, specifically, their collective political opinion and particular social group membership. However, Petitioners failed to present any arguments addressing the formation of their proposed particular social group or proving their inclusion in that group. Instead, their arguments regarding the nexus focus on their claim that Don Rafa persecuted them based on their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is restricted to whether Petitioners can demonstrate the necessary link between their claimed persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners, in particular, claim that Don Rafa and his associates harmed them because they worked on an election campaign opposing Colombia's sitting president. As established in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), an applicant alleging persecution based on political opinion must show that this political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the mistreatment suffered. In other words, the political opinion in question "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm" nor play "a minor role" in the person’s previous mistreatment or fear of future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor may act from multiple motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nonetheless, in such mixed-motive scenarios, the existence of at least one protected ground as a central reason for the harm is required. Id. Thus, in this matter, Petitioners must prove that their political opinion constituted, or will constitute, at least a central reason for why the persecutors targeted or would target them.

Petitioners have not met their burden because ample evidence underpins the BIA and IJ’s determinations that Petitioners were chiefly targeted due to Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s financial interests and personal security, rather than their political beliefs. Before the incident involving Jimenez and Don Rafa, Petitioners continued to engage in political activities despite intimidating phone calls and the killing of another campaign worker. Petitioners also fail to reference any record evidence indicating that Don Rafa orchestrated those initial threats, which were connected to political activity. In actuality, the threats toward Petitioners only intensified after Jimenez became entangled in a dispute over property with Don Rafa and once Don Rafa learned of Jimenez’s inquiry into his rented premises. On one occasion, an individual threatened Jimenez by accusing him of working for the prosecutor’s office and holding him accountable for law enforcement actions at Don Rafa’s property. Later, when these same individuals menaced Petitioners’ staff, they claimed that Don Rafa had dispatched them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA reasonably concluded that the primary motivation for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s occupational and personal disputes with Don Rafa, not from Petitioners’ political affiliations. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to demonstrate past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future, partly due to the alleged incapacity or unwillingness of the Colombian government to offer protection. They claim that their persecutors maintain close connections with and ties to the Colombian government, thereby making it unsafe for them to relocate within the country.

The “unable-or-unwilling” inquiry assesses the government’s actions in response to the applicant’s alleged past persecution. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. This evaluation primarily relies on two kinds of information: (1) whether the authorities intervened to stop the persecutors, and (2) whether the government provided any form of protection to the applicant, including, for example, placement in protective custody. Id.

Central to this analysis is the question of whether the government was aware of the claimed persecution and had a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. As stated, “[w]ithout such notice, it is impossible to determine how law enforcement might have responded or if governmental assistance would have been provided.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “whether an applicant informed the authorities about the alleged past persecution” becomes “a key fact to the analysis.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “an applicant’s failure to report may undermine the assertion that authorities were unwilling or unable to offer protection,” this does not necessarily defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can meet the requirement to seek government protection by establishing that making a police report would have been ineffective or too dangerous. Id.

There is ample support in the record for the conclusions reached by both the BIA and IJ. Petitioners’ attempts to request governmental assistance were either very limited or nonexistent. Both Jimenez and his father were, or continue to be, employed by the Colombian government. Nevertheless, neither brought any allegations of misconduct or corruption to the authorities. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners stated in their testimony that they spoke with two police officers, who advised them not to pursue trouble; however, this does not establish the stance of the Colombian government itself. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ noted that there is no evidence Petitioners tried to obtain assistance beyond their local area or used Jimenez’s connections within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to assert that seeking assistance from the government would have been both futile and perilous, due to their persecutors’ alleged close connections and affiliations with the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this contention. The authorities were already aware of the disputed property and had conducted various searches and seizures in an attempt to address the ongoing criminal activities associated with it. Thus, the evidence does not support Petitioners’ claim that the persecutors are in close association with the government or that the government would be either unwilling or incapable of intervening. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Additionally, Petitioners have not demonstrated that the Colombian government was, or would be, incapable or unwilling to offer them protection if it had knowledge of the threats directed at them. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding that evidence was inadequate to establish the government's inability or unwillingness to protect the applicant, especially where the applicant declined to involve the police due to the belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not satisfied the necessary elements for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining contention concerning whether safe and reasonable internal relocation within Colombia is possible.

Under the law, the government can overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thereby bar a grant of asylum—if it establishes, by a preponderance of the evidence, either that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, assuming arguendo that such fear was shown, the evidence in the record affirms that internal relocation within Colombia would be both reasonable and safe. The fact that Petitioners’ family members and other relatives remain in Colombia without harm, with Jimenez’s father continuing his employment in the criminal justice system, supports this conclusion. Furthermore, Petitioners themselves lived securely with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota for three weeks prior to opting to immigrate to the United States. It is also significant that the individuals who threatened Petitioners directed them only to leave their neighborhood, and did not insist they depart from Colombia altogether. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners have the option to avoid any threat of future persecution through reasonable relocation within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not shown a well-founded fear of persecution required for asylum, they also cannot satisfy the more stringent burden necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, the petition for review is denied. Petitioners suggest that relocation is not viable because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety,” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (noting that relatives might also oppose the government politically). However, asylum cannot be granted on the basis of such speculative difficulties. Petitioners also contend that relocation is impractical because of administrative and financial challenges, issues with the judicial system, and because those persecuting them have connections with the President of Colombia. Nonetheless, these arguments are raised only in a cursory manner, and, in light of our previous conclusions, do not withstand scrutiny.
