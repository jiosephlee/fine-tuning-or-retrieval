Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, who are both natives and citizens of Colombia, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were rejected by the immigration judge, and that decision was upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. The only issue raised on appeal concerns whether the BIA erred in its denial of the petitioners’ asylum and withholding of removal claims.

Invoking jurisdiction pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners do not establish that they experienced persecution, that any alleged persecution was linked to a protected statutory ground, or that the Colombian authorities are unable or unwilling to provide protection. In addition, the evidence does not demonstrate that the petitioners would be unable to relocate safely within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, all family members have been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez during his candidacy in the 2022 Colombian presidential election. Petitioners canvassed in local communities, urging residents to support their preferred candidate. Beginning in late May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening telephone calls once or twice per week due to his association with the Liberal Party. Other party leaders and associates were subjected to threats as well. In June 2022, one of Jimenez’s colleagues, after previously reporting threats, was abducted and killed. Despite these events, Petitioners persisted in their campaigning activities.

Jimenez, holding a provisional law license and being a law student during 2022 when the pertinent incidents occurred, was also serving as an intern with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted individuals impacted by domestic violence, extortion, and related crimes. While functioning as a provisional attorney, he was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo seeking his assistance regarding a dispute over real property. She reported that her tenant had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez communicate with the tenant to discuss both repayment and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to visit the property to mediate a settlement, but his efforts were unsuccessful—the tenant told him not to “look for trouble” and refused any negotiation. The client opted not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, instead expressing the hope that the tenant might relinquish possession of the property due to the involvement of a lawyer.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s real estate, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was, in reality, the client’s former spouse. Although the ex-husband had previously held ownership of the property, it had been conveyed to the client as part of the parties’ divorce agreement. Jimenez further determined that the tenant in question was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa,” who serves as a leader in a narco-trafficking guerrilla faction politically supportive of Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While visiting the site, Jimenez noted that the health department had sealed off the property’s third floor. After additional investigation, Jimenez found that authorities had closed the premises due to its use as a day-rental for migrants, as well as a location for narco-trafficking operations and prostitution. The property was also implicated in incidents involving kidnapping and homicide. Multiple law enforcement search and seizure efforts had taken place, yet none appeared to have succeeded. A police officer from the area advised Jimenez to avoid involvement with the property and cautioned him not to provoke trouble.

Later in the same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez paid no attention to the threat and threw away the brochure. However, in the following month, while working a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals approached Jimenez, brandished firearms, and ordered him to leave the area. One of these individuals accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and held him accountable for the property searches and seizures. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to watch their children’s journeys to and from school with heightened vigilance.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the identical pair returned to the furniture factory during Petitioners' absence from town. On this occasion, they attacked an employee at the factory and brandished a firearm while demanding the whereabouts of Petitioners. Once more, the men accused Petitioners of being "snitches for the police" and involved in politics. They warned that Petitioners would be killed if found again, attributing the order to Don Rafa. After the employee informed them that Petitioners were traveling, the men departed without causing further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around his younger son's shoulder as the child was leaving school. The individual then covered the boy's mouth while the son attempted to break free and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried to his son's side and shoved the individual, after which his son ran to Jimenez's vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated to Jimenez's mother's residence in the rural area of Bogota for three weeks, during which they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer responded that no action could be taken and remarked that police officers were themselves contributing to the problem. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave. Petitioners then did as instructed.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without inspection or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. Through these Notices, DHS charged each individual as removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the factual allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Subsequently, Petitioners sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and listed as derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

After considering the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte along with additional evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge denied Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ found the Petitioners to be credible witnesses, the IJ determined that Petitioners had not demonstrated: (1) that the harm suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option; and (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to protect them from their alleged persecutors. The IJ further denied Petitioners’ claims for relief under the CAT. Petitioners appealed this decision, but the BIA affirmed the IJ’s findings and dismissed the appeal. Additionally, the BIA found no merit in Petitioners’ due process claims alleging that the IJ infringed upon their right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in determining that they failed to demonstrate: (1) past persecution; (2) past persecution based on their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution where the government would be unable or unwilling to provide protection. Petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or their claim alleging a due process violation.

A. Standard of Review

Generally, we possess authority to review final orders of removal, and this review encompasses a consolidated assessment of asylum applications. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our examination of the Board’s denial of asylum is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). When a single BIA member affirms the immigration judge’s decision, our review targets the BIA’s reasoning, but “we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

The BIA’s factual determinations are reviewed by us for substantial evidence. This means such factual findings are treated as conclusive unless the record compels any reasonable factfinder to reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a form of discretionary relief that the Attorney General has authority to confer upon qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). To qualify for such discretionary asylum, the applicant must establish by a preponderance of the evidence that he meets the definition of refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, the term "refugee" refers to: any person who is located outside of any country of that person’s nationality . . . and who is either unable or unwilling to return to, as well as unable or unwilling to seek the protection of, that country due to past persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant may establish refugee status by demonstrating past persecution, which results in a rebuttable presumption of possessing a well-founded fear of future persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To satisfy this standard, the applicant must establish three criteria: “(1) the occurrence of one or more incidents that constitute persecution; (2) that the persecution was on account of a statutorily protected ground; and (3) that the persecution was carried out by the government or by entities or individuals whom the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not specify the meaning of persecution, but this court has determined that persecution entails suffering or harm inflicted on individuals who are distinguished by race, religion, or political opinion in a manner considered offensive, and must involve more than mere limitations or threats to life and liberty. Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis in original). Our analysis does not consider each event separately; rather, we evaluate them as a whole, as the overall impact of several incidents can amount to persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The determination of whether an individual has established past private persecution in their country of origin—the principal issue in this case—is a factual matter. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the various threats made against them amount to persecution. While threats are a factor when evaluating the overall effect of mistreatment endured by Petitioners, our precedent dictates that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, during their merits hearing, Petitioners admitted they had not suffered any physical harm, and likewise, their opening brief indicates an absence of permanent physical injury. While demonstrating physical harm is not a requirement for establishing persecution, id. at 804, the harm experienced must be significant and go beyond simple limitations or occasional threats, as set forth in Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask that we take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological injuries they have suffered. For instance, Duarte asserts that she is unable to sleep or find rest, and that her son has begun to experience panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced unfortunate events, their fear resulting from threats directed at them does not meet the threshold of persecution. No matter how physically taxing or mentally disturbing certain occurrences may be, our precedent almost never recognizes asylum eligibility on the basis of threats and emotional or psychological injury alone. See id. (holding that the harm reported by an applicant—including a ten-hour arrest and detention along with threats—fell short of the persecution standard, despite the events being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As acknowledged by Petitioners’ counsel during oral argument, our prior decisions have denied asylum requests even where the facts reflect more severe harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not qualify as persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that multiple threats with machetes and being robbed six times did not constitute persecution since “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Considering these circumstances and facts, we conclude there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that, even when viewed collectively, the harm experienced by Petitioners does not meet the statutory definition of past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was because of grounds protected by statute, specifically their shared political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners did not present any briefing addressing the existence of a particular social group or prove their inclusion in such a group. Instead, when discussing the nexus requirement, Petitioners maintain that the persecution by Don Rafa resulted from their political views. Therefore, our analysis is confined to determining whether Petitioners have shown a connection between the alleged persecution and their political opinion. In particular, Petitioners claim that Don Rafa and his affiliates targeted them due to their participation in a political campaign opposing the current Colombian president. “To demonstrate persecution on account of political opinion, an applicant must show that the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). Thus, the political opinion in question “must not be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another motive for the harm” nor may it “play a minor role” in the applicant’s past persecution or their apprehensions about possible future persecution. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is well recognized that a persecutor may act based on more than one motive. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Still, even in such mixed-motive scenarios, one of the motivations for the harm inflicted must be a protected ground and must constitute a central reason. Id. Thus, Petitioners bear the burden of establishing that their political opinion was or will be at least one central reason for their persecutors’ past or anticipated future actions against them.

Petitioners do not meet this requirement because ample evidence supports the determinations by the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were principally targeted as a result of Jimenez threatening Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than due to any political opinion. Before the incident involving Jimenez and Don Rafa, Petitioners were not dissuaded by threatening phone calls or the death of a fellow campaigner and persisted in their political endeavors. Petitioners fail to identify any evidence in the record connecting Don Rafa to those initial threats tied to political activities. In reality, it was only after Jimenez became involved in a land dispute with Don Rafa, and Don Rafa discovered Jimenez was looking into his rental property, that Petitioners began to encounter increased threats. On one occasion, an individual accused Jimenez of being affiliated with the prosecutor’s office and responsible for law enforcement actions at Don Rafa’s property. When the same men subsequently threatened employees of Petitioners, they indicated that Don Rafa had dispatched them.

Given this evidence, the BIA properly found that Jimenez’s work-related and personal connections with Don Rafa, not Petitioners’ political opinions, constituted the main reason for the threats. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even lacking proof of past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future, partly on the grounds that the Colombian government is either incapable or unwilling to offer them protection. They maintain that their persecutors have significant ties to the Colombian government, thus making it impossible for them to relocate safely within the country.

The analysis regarding whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “evaluates evidence relating to how a government responded to an applicant’s experiences of persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. When assessing a government’s response, two key considerations are: “(1) whether the authorities intervened or attempted to intervene against the persecutors . . . and (2) whether the authorities provided the applicant any protection, such as by ensuring protective custody.” Id.

Part of this analysis involves determining whether the government was informed about the alleged persecution and given a chance to respond. Id. at 861–62. “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “an important factor in the analysis is whether the applicant informed the government about the past persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “failing to make a report can weaken the assertion that the government was unwilling or unable to control the persecutors,” this does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can fulfill the requirement of seeking government protection by proving that filing a police report would have been either futile or dangerous. Id.

There is ample evidence in the record to uphold the determinations made by the BIA and the IJ. Petitioners made little to no effort to contact governmental authorities for assistance. Both Jimenez and his father have been, or are currently, employed by the Colombian government. Nevertheless, neither took steps to report any misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners stated that they talked to two individual police officers, who advised them not to seek trouble; however, there is no indication that this represents the stance of the Colombian government itself. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As noted by the IJ, the evidence does not indicate that Petitioners attempted to obtain help beyond their local area or via Jimenez’s connections within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the authorities would have been both pointless and risky, due to the purported close connection between their persecutors and the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this claim. Evidence shows that the government had knowledge of the property in question and had already made multiple attempts at searches and seizures to combat ongoing criminal activity there. Thus, the record undermines Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors have strong governmental ties that would prevent the government from acting against them. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Moreover, Petitioners have not demonstrated that the Colombian government either was or would have been unable or unwilling to offer protection, had the government been informed of the threats directed at Petitioners. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding that the evidence did not suffice to establish that authorities were unable or unwilling to help, especially where the applicant did not approach the police out of concern that his persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not satisfied the requirements for asylum, we will briefly consider their final claim concerning whether it would be feasible and reasonable for them to safely relocate within Colombia.

The government can overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and thereby deny asylum if it establishes, by a preponderance of the evidence, either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; nevertheless, even if such fear were established, the evidence substantiates the conclusion that Petitioners could reasonably resettle elsewhere within Colombia. Members of Petitioners’ family and extended relatives continue to reside unharmed in Colombia, and Jimenez’s father maintains his employment within the criminal justice system. Significantly, Petitioners also lived securely with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota for a period of three weeks before choosing to depart for the United States. Additionally, the individuals who issued threats directed Petitioners only to leave the neighborhood, not the country. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners can diminish any risk of future persecution by relocating to another region within Colombia where it is both safe and reasonable to do so.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, there is ample evidence in the record supporting the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners are unable to establish a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they consequently do not satisfy the more stringent criteria for withholding of removal. Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners argue that relocation is not feasible because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety,” Pet’rs’ Br. 18, stating that those family members might also hold political beliefs contrary to the government. However, we cannot grant asylum based on such speculative or hypothetical difficulties. Petitioners further contend that relocation would be impracticable due to administrative and financial challenges, deficiencies in the judicial system, and because their persecutors are connected to the president of Colombia. Yet, their briefing on these points is cursory, and in view of our above conclusions, these contentions are insufficient.
