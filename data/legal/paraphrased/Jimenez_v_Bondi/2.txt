Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The Petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, sought relief through applications for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. Their requests were rejected by the immigration judge, and the Board of Immigration Appeals subsequently upheld this decision. On appeal, Petitioners challenge only the Board’s denial of their asylum and withholding of removal claims.

With jurisdiction established under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any purported persecution was connected to a statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian authorities are either unwilling or unable to provide assistance. Furthermore, the evidence does not indicate that Petitioners would be unable to safely relocate within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, along with his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte and their two minor children, are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and worked in a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, all family members have belonged to the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez’s candidacy during the 2022 Colombian presidential race. Petitioners campaigned door-to-door in various neighborhoods, encouraging residents to vote in favor of Gutierrez. Beginning in late May 2022, Jimenez started to receive threatening telephone calls approximately once or twice weekly, all related to his participation in the Liberal Party. Other party leaders and associates were similarly threatened. In early June 2022, a colleague was kidnapped and killed, even though he had previously reported receiving threats. Despite these incidents, Petitioners persisted in their campaign efforts.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and was a law student in 2022, the time period during which the events in question occurred. He also interned at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, assisting victims of crimes such as domestic violence, extortion, and others. Acting as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo who sought his assistance regarding a dispute over property. She informed Jimenez that the tenant occupying her property had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez speak to the tenant about repaying the rent and vacating the premises. Jimenez went to the property to attempt to broker an agreement, but his efforts were unsuccessful; the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and would not engage in negotiation. The client chose not to pursue a formal eviction proceeding, instead hoping that the tenant, aware of the involvement of legal counsel, would eventually relinquish possession of the property.

Upon reviewing the title to the client's property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was, in reality, the client's former husband. Although the ex-husband had been the previous owner, ownership had passed to the client through a divorce settlement. Jimenez also identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa,” who is recognized as a leader among narco-trafficking guerrilla groups with political ties to the current president of Colombia, Gustavo Petro.

While inspecting the property, Jimenez noticed that the health department had closed off the third floor. Upon further inquiry, he determined that the property had been shut down for its use as a day-rental for migrants and as a site for drug trafficking and prostitution. The location had also been linked to allegations of kidnapping and murder. Although law enforcement had made multiple attempts to search and seize the premises, these efforts appeared to have failed. A local police officer advised Jimenez to avoid the property and not to involve himself in any issues.

Later in the same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez chose to ignore this threat and threw the brochure away. However, the following month, as Jimenez was working a night shift at the furniture factory, he was confronted by two men armed with guns. They instructed him to vacate the area. One of the individuals accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures conducted on the property. Following the assailants’ departure, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and subsequently took extra precautions to supervise their children’s journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the death threat, the identical two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a time when Petitioners were away. They physically attacked an employee of the factory and, displaying a firearm, insisted on learning Petitioners’ whereabouts. The individuals once more referred to Petitioners as “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They issued a renewed threat to kill Petitioners should they reappear, stating the directive originated from Don Rafa. The employee informed them that Petitioners were out of town, after which the men departed without causing additional harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual placing his arm around his younger son’s shoulders as the child left school. The individual covered the boy’s mouth with his hand while the boy attempted to break free, and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried to his son’s aid, pushing the man aside as the boy fled to Jimenez’s vehicle. After this event, the family stayed at Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for three weeks, during which time they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. The officer responded that no action could be taken and stated that members of the police were themselves contributing to the issue. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave. Petitioners subsequently followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without lawful admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of the allegations set forth by DHS. The Immigration Judge determined they were inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, designating the minor children as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte as well as additional evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge (IJ) denied the Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ found the Petitioners’ accounts to be credible, the IJ determined that Petitioners did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a protected ground as required by statute; (3) that internal relocation was not a reasonable option; or (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to protect them from those who harmed them. The IJ likewise denied Petitioners’ requests for protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture (CAT). Petitioners appealed, but the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed their appeal. Additionally, the BIA rejected the Petitioners’ contentions that the IJ had violated their due process rights to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in determining that they failed to demonstrate: (1) past persecution; (2) that such persecution was based on their political opinion; and (3) a reasonable fear of future persecution with inadequate protection from the government. Petitioners do not contest the adverse decision regarding CAT protection or their due process allegation.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, we possess jurisdiction to examine final removal orders, which may encompass a consolidated assessment of asylum applications. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our scrutiny of the Board’s refusal to grant asylum relief is restricted to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since a single BIA member upheld the Immigration Judge’s determination, our review focuses on the BIA’s decision; however, “we may look to the IJ’s more comprehensive reasoning on the same issues.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We evaluate the BIA’s factual determinations according to the substantial evidence standard. This means the Board’s factual findings are binding unless the record indicates that any reasonable adjudicator would be obliged to decide otherwise. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may extend to noncitizens who meet the statutory criteria.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for asylum as a matter of discretion, the applicant has the responsibility to demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he qualifies as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). According to the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is outside the country of his or her nationality . . . and who is either unable or unwilling to return to, or to seek the protection of, that country owing to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant can establish refugee status by “demonstrating past persecution, which then creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To succeed, the applicant must establish three requirements: “(1) an act or series of acts that amount to persecution; (2) that was motivated by one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) was perpetrated either by the government or by individuals or groups that the government is unwilling or unable to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

Although the INA does not provide a definition of persecution, this Court has determined that persecution necessitates the imposition of suffering or harm on individuals based on differences in race, religion, or political opinion in a manner deemed offensive, and requires more than mere limitations or threats to personal safety or freedom. See Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Rather than examining each event separately, we assess the incidents as a whole, as their combined impact may rise to the level of persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The question of whether an individual has demonstrated past private persecution in their country of origin, which is the principal issue here, constitutes a factual determination. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the repeated threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are a factor in evaluating the overall severity of mistreatment experienced by Petitioners, our precedent has determined that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, at their merits hearing, Petitioners admitted they did not suffer any physical harm, and their opening brief likewise stated they endured no lasting physical injury. Though physical harm is not a requisite element for meeting the persecution standard, id. at 804, the harm alleged must be egregious and exceed minor limitations or occasional threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners request that we take into account their ongoing emotional and psychological injuries as well. For instance, Duarte asserts that she experiences insomnia and restlessness, and that her son has developed panic attacks and anxiety. Although Petitioners have encountered regrettable situations, fears arising from the threats they received do not, by themselves, constitute persecution. Regardless of the physical or psychological distress these events may cause, this court has seldom—if ever—granted asylum solely on the basis of threats combined with emotional or psychological harm. See id. (concluding that an applicant’s claimed harms, including a ten-hour arrest and detention along with threats, did not rise to the level of persecution, despite their being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As Petitioners’ attorney acknowledged during oral argument, our precedent has denied asylum even in cases with more severe facts involving harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (“four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” was found not to amount to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (threats with machetes and being robbed on six occasions did not qualify as persecution since “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

In light of these considerations, we conclude that there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that, even when considered collectively, the harm suffered by Petitioners does not constitute past persecution as defined by the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they endured was due to statutorily protected grounds, specifically their common political opinion and their alleged membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners did not submit any arguments in their briefs regarding the definition or existence of their asserted particular social group, nor did they demonstrate their inclusion in such a group. Instead, Petitioners, in addressing the nexus requirement, maintain that Don Rafa persecuted them on account of their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners can show a nexus between their purported persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners specifically claim that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them because they participated in a campaign supporting a political opponent of Colombia's current president. “To show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, Petitioners’ political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” or “play a minor role” in the harm they suffered in the past or their fear of future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor may have several reasons for targeting an individual. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even in cases involving mixed motives, at least one protected ground must constitute a central reason for the harm inflicted. Id. Accordingly, Petitioners are required to establish that their political opinion was or would be, at a minimum, one central reason motivating the persecutors’ actions against them.

Petitioners have not met their burden because ample evidence supports the conclusions of the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were mainly targeted due to Jimenez posing a threat to Don Rafa’s income and safety, rather than because of any political opinion. Before Jimenez’s conflict with Don Rafa, Petitioners were undeterred by intimidating phone calls or the death of another campaign participant and continued their political work. Petitioners do not identify any record evidence connecting Don Rafa to these earlier politically motivated threats. Indeed, the threats intensified only after Jimenez became embroiled in a land dispute with Don Rafa and began looking into activities related to property Don Rafa was leasing. On one occasion, an individual accused Jimenez of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches conducted at Don Rafa’s property. Subsequently, when Petitioners’ employees were threatened by the same individuals, they reported that Don Rafa had sent them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA correctly determined that the primary reason for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s occupational and personal conflicts with Don Rafa, and not from Petitioners’ political views. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even lacking proof of past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly due to the Colombian government’s inability or unwillingness to offer protection. They maintain that the persecutors are tightly connected with the Colombian authorities, making internal relocation within Colombia unsafe.

The analysis regarding a government's inability or unwillingness to protect “examines evidence regarding how the government responded to an applicant’s prior experiences of persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. When evaluating a government’s reaction to persecution, two main types of information are particularly significant: “(1) whether the authorities intervened or attempted to intervene against the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether protective measures were extended to the applicant, for example by providing protective custody.” Id.

This analysis also involves determining if the government was aware of the claimed persecution and had a chance to respond. Id. at 861–62. As the court stated, “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government” constitutes “a key fact to the analysis.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” this alone does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, the applicant may fulfill the requirement to seek government protection by showing that making a police report would have been futile or put the applicant at risk. Id.

There is ample evidence in the record to support the conclusions reached by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made only minimal, if any, attempts to seek assistance from the government. Both Jimenez and his father have worked or currently work for the Colombian government, but neither reported any misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners testified that they spoke with two individual police officers, who advised them not to get involved in trouble; however, there is no evidence that these comments reflect an official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ further found that nothing in the record indicates the Petitioners pursued assistance beyond their local neighborhood or utilized Jimenez’s contacts within law enforcement.

Petitioners contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both ineffective and perilous due to their persecutors' purported close ties and associations with the Colombian government. However, the record does not substantiate this claim. The government was already cognizant of the property in question and had conducted multiple searches and seizures to combat the ongoing criminal activity there. Thus, the evidence refutes Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors have a close relationship with the government, or that the government would be unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners fail to establish that the Colombian government was, or would be, unwilling or unable to offer them protection if it had been informed of the threats they faced. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding there was inadequate evidence of government inability or unwillingness to protect where the applicant chose not to contact authorities based on a belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not satisfied the criteria necessary for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining contention concerning whether internal relocation within Colombia is both possible and reasonable.

The government may overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution, thereby defeating the asylum claim, if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence either that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, assuming arguendo that such a fear exists, the evidence substantiates the conclusion that internal relocation within Colombia would be a reasonable option for them. Petitioners' relatives and family members remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment in the criminal justice system there. Significantly, Petitioners resided without incident with Jimenez’s mother in a rural area near Bogota for three weeks prior to their departure for the United States. Additionally, it is relevant that the individuals who made threats against Petitioners only instructed them to leave the neighborhood, not to depart the country entirely. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners have the ability to reduce any risk of future persecution by relocating to a different region within Colombia in a manner that is both safe and reasonable.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence underpins the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution under the asylum framework, they also cannot satisfy the more stringent requirements for withholding of removal. Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, the petition for review is denied. While Petitioners contend that relocation within Colombia is not feasible because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety,” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (suggesting those family members may also hold anti-government political opinions), we are unable to grant asylum on the basis of mere speculative hardships. Petitioners further argue that internal relocation is impractical due to administrative and financial challenges, deficiencies in the country’s judicial system, and the fact that their alleged persecutors are connected to the president of Colombia. However, these assertions are only superficially addressed in their briefing and, in light of our preceding analysis, do not prevail.
