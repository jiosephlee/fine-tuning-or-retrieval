Petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, submitted requests for asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were rejected by the immigration judge, and this decision was upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, Petitioners challenge solely the BIA’s denial of their claims for asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any alleged harm bore the required connection to a statutorily protected ground, or that the government of Colombia was unable or unwilling to provide protection. Additionally, the evidence does not indicate that Petitioners would be unable to find safe relocation within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children hail from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and worked at a furniture factory in Bogota.

Since 2012, the entire family has been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and backed Federico Gutierrez’s candidacy in the 2022 Colombian presidential election. During that period, Petitioners canvassed local neighborhoods, urging residents to vote for their preferred candidate. Starting in late May 2022, Jimenez started to receive threatening phone calls once or twice per week that were related to his participation in the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates within the Liberal Party were similarly threatened. In early June 2022, a colleague was abducted and killed, even though that individual had reported receiving threats. Petitioners, nonetheless, persisted in their political campaigning.

Jimenez possessed a provisional law license and was enrolled as a law student in 2022 during the period in question. Additionally, he served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, assisting individuals who were victims of domestic violence, extortion, and various offenses. Acting as a provisional attorney, he was approached by a woman from San Bernardo in June 2022 regarding a dispute over real estate. She informed Jimenez that the individual renting her property had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested that he contact the tenant regarding both the recovery of back rent and vacating the premises. Jimenez went to the property in an attempt to mediate a settlement, but his efforts did not succeed—the tenant warned Jimenez against “looking for trouble” and refused to discuss terms. Choosing not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, the client instead preferred to wait, hoping that the tenant’s awareness of a lawyer’s involvement would prompt the tenant to relinquish possession of the property voluntarily.

By examining the title to the client’s property, Jimenez determined that the tenant residing there was actually the client's former husband. While the ex-husband had originally possessed ownership, the property was conveyed to the client pursuant to a divorce agreement. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, commonly referred to as "Don Rafa," who is recognized as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla faction with political ties to the sitting president of Colombia, Gustavo Petro.

When Jimenez visited the property, he noticed that the third story had been cordoned off by the health department. Upon further inquiry, he uncovered that authorities had closed the property due to its use as a short-term rental for migrants, and as a site for narco-trafficking and prostitution operations. The location was additionally connected to allegations involving kidnapping and homicide. Several search and seizure efforts had been made by law enforcement, but these appeared to have been ineffective. A member of the local police cautioned Jimenez to avoid involvement with the property and advised him not to create trouble.

Later that same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory bearing the message, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez paid no heed to the threat and threw the brochure away. However, in the following month, as he was working late at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted Jimenez at gunpoint and ordered him to leave the area. One of them labeled Jimenez a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and confiscations conducted at the premises. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to vigilantly supervise their children’s journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the prior death threat, the same individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were absent from town. They attacked a worker at the factory and brandished a firearm while inquiring about the Petitioners’ whereabouts. The men reiterated their accusations that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” involved in political matters. They issued another threat to kill Petitioners upon seeing them again, specifying that this directive originated from Don Rafa. The factory employee informed them that Petitioners were away traveling, after which the men departed without inflicting further harm.

A few days afterward, Jimenez saw a man place his arm around his younger son's shoulders as the boy was leaving school. The individual covered the boy’s mouth with his hand while the boy attempted to break free and told him, “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried to his son and shoved the man, enabling his son to run toward Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area outside Bogota for three weeks; during that period, they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the incident to a patrolling police officer. However, the officer responded that there was nothing that could be done and informed Jimenez that police officers themselves were part of the issue. The officer advised that Jimenez and his family should leave. Petitioners proceeded to do just that.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without inspection or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued to each Petitioner a Notice to Appear. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of the allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge found Petitioners to be inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners submitted applications for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children participating as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries based on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte as well as additional evidence presented in the record, the Immigration Judge denied Petitioners’ applications. The IJ, despite finding Petitioners credible, determined that they did not show: (1) the harm suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a protected ground under the statute; (3) internal relocation would be unreasonable; and (4) the Colombian government is either unwilling or unable to shield them from their alleged persecutors. The IJ further rejected Petitioners’ claims for relief under the CAT. Petitioners appealed, but the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld the IJ's decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also dismissed Petitioners’ assertions that their due process right to counsel had been violated by the IJ.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA's ruling that rejected their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA incorrectly determined that they failed to show: (1) they had suffered past persecution; (2) their past persecution was based on their political opinion; and (3) they have a well-founded fear of future persecution for which the government is either unable or unwilling to offer protection. Petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or the dismissal of their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Our authority typically extends to reviewing final removal orders, which may encompass a unified examination of asylum claims. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). The scope of our review for the Board’s denial of asylum relief is restricted to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Because the Board’s decision was rendered by a single member affirming the Immigration Judge, we primarily assess the BIA’s ruling, though we may reference the IJ’s more detailed reasoning on the same issues. Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

When evaluating the BIA’s factual findings, we apply the substantial-evidence standard. This approach treats the BIA’s factual determinations as conclusive unless the evidence in the record compels any reasonable factfinder to reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum is a remedy granted at the discretion of the Attorney General to noncitizens who meet the necessary requirements.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for discretionary asylum, the applicant has the responsibility of demonstrating, by a preponderance of the evidence, his status as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). The Immigration and Nationality Act characterizes a refugee as: an individual who is located outside their country of nationality . . . and who cannot or does not wish to return to, or seek protection from, that country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)), it was recognized that an applicant can obtain refugee designation “through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To satisfy this standard, the applicant is required to establish three components: “(1) an incident, or series of incidents, amounting to persecution; (2) occurring because of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) perpetrated by the government or by parties whom the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (citing Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners claim that, beyond the threats already described, they endured physical, psychological, and emotional harm that constitutes past persecution.

Although the INA does not define persecution, our court has determined that persecution requires suffering or harm inflicted on individuals because they differ in race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner considered offensive, and it must be more significant than merely imposing restrictions or threats to life and liberty. See Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Our analysis does not view each event separately; instead, we assess all incidents together, as the aggregate impact of multiple episodes may amount to persecution. See Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The determination of whether a noncitizen has demonstrated past private persecution in their country of origin—the central issue before us—is a factual matter. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the series of threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are considered when evaluating the overall effect of the mistreatment suffered by Petitioners, our precedent establishes that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Significantly, Petitioners admitted during their merits hearing that they did not sustain any physical harm, and also acknowledged in their opening brief that they did not experience any lasting physical injury. While physical harm is not a requirement to prove persecution, id. at 804, the harm endured must be egregious and must exceed minor restrictions or isolated threats. See Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask the Court to take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological impacts they have experienced. Duarte, as an illustration, asserts that she struggles with sleep and rest, and that her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced unfortunate situations, the fears resulting from the threats made to them do not meet the threshold for persecution. Regardless of how physically taxing or mentally distressing certain incidents may be, asylum has seldom, if ever, been granted solely on the basis of threats or emotional and psychological harm. See id. (determining that an applicant’s harm—including a 10-hour period of arrest and detention along with threats—did not amount to persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As recognized by Petitioners’ counsel during oral argument, existing precedent has denied asylum in cases where more severe harm was inflicted. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not rise to the level of persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that threats with machetes and six robberies did not constitute persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Based on the record and these considerations, we conclude there is substantial evidence to affirm the BIA’s determination that, even in the aggregate, the harms alleged by Petitioners do not qualify as past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to reasons protected under statute, specifically their common political opinion and purported membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners did not present any arguments or evidence addressing the existence of the alleged social group or demonstrating their inclusion in such a group. Instead, their nexus argument focuses on claims that Don Rafa persecuted them because of their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is confined to assessing whether Petitioners can show a nexus between their alleged persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners in particular contend that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them due to their involvement with a rival’s campaign opposing Colombia’s current president. To prove persecution on the basis of political opinion, “the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, the political opinion in question “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” or represent merely “a minor role” in explaining past harm or future risk. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor may be driven by more than one motive. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nonetheless, in cases involving mixed motives, it remains necessary for the persecutor to be motivated by at least one statutorily protected ground that is a central reason for the harm suffered. Id. Thus, in the present case, Petitioners must prove that their political opinion constituted, or will constitute, at least one central reason for their being targeted by the persecutors.

Petitioners have not met this requirement because substantial evidence backs the determinations of the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were mainly targeted due to Jimenez’s impact on Don Rafa’s income and safety, rather than their political views. Before Jimenez’s encounter with Don Rafa, Petitioners remained undaunted by threatening phone calls and the death of a fellow campaign participant, persisting in their political efforts. Petitioners do not indicate any evidence in the record connecting Don Rafa to these early threats with political links. Instead, the record indicates that heightened threats against Petitioners only commenced after Jimenez became engaged in a property conflict with Don Rafa, and after Don Rafa learned of Jimenez’s inquiry into his leased property. On one occasion, a man threatened Jimenez, accusing him of working for the prosecutor’s office and attributing responsibility for searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property to him. Furthermore, when Petitioners’ employees were subsequently threatened by the same individuals, they conveyed that Don Rafa had dispatched them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not commit error in concluding that the principal motive for the threats was Jimenez's professional and personal relationship with Don Rafa, rather than the Petitioners’ political opinion. Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that the Petitioners' political views were only incidental and secondary to the primary cause of the alleged harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even if they are unable to demonstrate past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of facing persecution in the future, partly due to the alleged incapacity or unwillingness of the Colombian government to provide protection. They claim that their persecutors have significant connections and affiliations with the Colombian authorities, thereby making internal relocation within Colombia unsafe.

The analysis of whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In assessing a government’s response to persecution, courts place significant weight on two main categories of evidence: “(1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody.” Id.

Central to this analysis is the question of whether the government had awareness of the alleged persecution and a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. “Absent such notice, it remains unknown how law enforcement would have acted or if the government would have intervened.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “an important aspect of the analysis concerns whether an applicant informed the government of past persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting the persecution may weaken an assertion that the government was unwilling or unable to protect against the harm,” this omission is not necessarily dispositive. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant may still meet the requirement regarding government protection by establishing that submitting a police report would have been pointless or put them at risk. Id.

Once more, the record contains ample evidence supporting the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made little to no real attempt to seek governmental assistance. Both Jimenez and his father either are or were employed by the Colombian government, yet neither reported any misconduct or corrupt activity. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners claimed that they spoke with two police officers, who advised them not to involve themselves in trouble; however, there is no indication that this advice represents an official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As noted by the IJ, the record does not indicate that Petitioners attempted to seek assistance beyond their local area or through Jimenez’s contacts in law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking governmental assistance would have been both futile and hazardous due to the alleged close association between their persecutors and the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this claim. The authorities were cognizant of the property involved and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in efforts to curb the ongoing criminal activity there. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors are closely connected to the government and that the authorities would be either unwilling or incapable of stopping those persecutors. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Moreover, Petitioners fail to demonstrate that the Colombian government was, or would have been, unable or unwilling to offer protection if it had been informed of the threats made against them. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding there was insufficient proof that the government was unable or unwilling to protect the applicant, especially since the applicant refused to contact the police based on his belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Consequently, we concur that Petitioners are unable to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution as required for asylum eligibility.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not met the requirements for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining claim pertaining to whether internal relocation within Colombia is possible and reasonable.

The government may overcome an applicant's presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and thereby bar the applicant from asylum by demonstrating, under a preponderance of the evidence standard, either that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of persecution in the future; however, even assuming such fear existed, the evidence indicates that Petitioners could feasibly move to a different region within Colombia. Petitioners’ relatives and family members remain in Colombia without incident, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment with the criminal justice system. Additionally, Petitioners resided without issue with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota for three weeks before electing to immigrate to the United States. Furthermore, it is significant that the individuals who issued threats instructed Petitioners to leave the neighborhood—not the country as a whole. Consequently, we conclude that Petitioners could effectively reduce any risk of future persecution by relocating safely and reasonably within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence underpins the BIA's determinations that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Moreover, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they also cannot satisfy the more stringent requirements for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (quoting Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, the petition for review is denied. Petitioners argue that return to their home country is not feasible because, as they contend, “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (alleging that those family members might also oppose the government politically). Nevertheless, asylum cannot be granted solely on the basis of such speculative difficulties. Petitioners further claim that relocation is impractical due to administrative and financial challenges, the country’s legal system, and because their persecutors purportedly have connections to the Colombian president. However, Petitioners raise these points only superficially, and for the reasons discussed above, their contentions are unpersuasive.
