Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Petitioners, who are citizens and nationals of Colombia, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were denied by the immigration judge, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld that decision. On appeal, Petitioners challenge solely the BIA’s denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any alleged persecution was connected to a statutorily protected ground, or that the government of Colombia is either unwilling or unable to offer protection. Additionally, the record does not support the conclusion that Petitioners are unable to safely relocate elsewhere within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture factory in Bogota.

Since 2012, all family members have participated in the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez, a presidential candidate in Colombia's 2022 election. During the campaign, Petitioners canvassed neighborhoods, seeking votes for their preferred candidate. Starting in late May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls once or twice each week because of his association with the Liberal Party. Similarly, other Liberal Party leaders and associates were threatened. In early June 2022, one colleague was kidnapped and killed after reporting similar threats. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their political activities and continued campaigning.

Jimenez held a provisional law license and was still a law student in 2022, during which the incidents at issue occurred. He was also serving as an intern with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, providing assistance to victims of crimes such as domestic abuse, extortion, and others. In his role as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo who sought his assistance regarding a dispute over property. She informed him that her tenant had not paid rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez communicate with the tenant about restoring the unpaid rent and vacating the premises. Jimenez subsequently went to the property in an effort to reach an agreement, but his efforts failed; the tenant told Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused any negotiation. The woman ultimately chose not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, instead expressing hope that the tenant, aware of a lawyer’s involvement, would voluntarily vacate the property.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was actually the client’s former husband. The ex-husband, though previously the property owner, had conveyed it to the client pursuant to the terms of their divorce settlement. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, who is also known as “Don Rafa” and recognized as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group with political connections to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While inspecting the premises, Jimenez noticed that the health department had sealed off the third floor. Further inquiry revealed that the closure was due to the property being utilized as a day-rental location for migrants, as well as serving as a base for narco-trafficking and prostitution. There were also connections to reports of kidnapping and homicide. Attempts by law enforcement to execute searches and seizures at the location had apparently not been successful. A police officer in the area cautioned Jimenez to keep away from the property and to avoid causing any trouble.

Later that same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory bearing the message, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez ignored this warning and threw the brochure away. However, the following month, as he was working during the night at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted Jimenez at gunpoint and ordered him to leave the area. One of them accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures conducted at the premises. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to carefully watch over their children’s trips to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were absent from town. During this visit, they physically attacked an employee of the factory and brandished a firearm while demanding the whereabouts of Petitioners. The men reiterated that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” involved in political activities. They issued another threat to kill Petitioners if they were found again, specifying that this directive was from Don Rafa. After the employee informed them that Petitioners were away, the men departed without inflicting further harm.

Several days later, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around the shoulders of his younger son as the child was leaving school. The individual then covered the boy’s mouth with his hand when the boy attempted to get away, instructing him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried to his son, shoved the man, and his son ran to Jimenez’s vehicle. After this event, the family stayed at Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the countryside outside Bogota for three weeks; during that time, they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the incident to a patrolling police officer. However, the officer responded that nothing could be done and remarked that police themselves were contributing to the issue. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without official admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of the allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children proceeding as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application.

Drawing upon the testimonies provided by Jimenez and Duarte, along with additional evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge denied Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ determined that Petitioners were credible, the IJ nonetheless found that Petitioners did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation was not reasonably feasible; or (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to protect them from those who persecuted them. The IJ further denied Petitioners’ applications for protection pursuant to the CAT. Petitioners sought review, but the BIA upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. Additionally, the BIA rejected Petitioners’ due process claims alleging that the IJ infringed upon their right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s order denying their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend the BIA incorrectly concluded that they failed to demonstrate: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that any past persecution occurred due to their political opinion; and (3) that they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution which the government cannot or will not prevent. Petitioners do not seek review of the denial of CAT protection or their due process allegation.

A. Standard of Review

Our jurisdiction ordinarily extends to the review of final orders of removal, which encompasses a combined review of asylum-related claims. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our examination of the Board’s denial of asylum is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since a single BIA member affirmed the immigration judge’s ruling, our review focuses on the BIA’s decision, but we “may also rely on the immigration judge’s more detailed rationale regarding those same grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We evaluate the BIA’s factual determinations using a substantial-evidence standard. Under this approach, such findings are deemed conclusive unless the evidence in the record establishes that any reasonable factfinder would be required to reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may extend to qualified noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). To qualify for asylum as a matter of discretion, the applicant must establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he satisfies the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). The Immigration and Nationality Act describes a refugee as: any individual who is outside the country of his or her nationality . . . and who is either unable or unwilling to return to, and unable or unwilling to seek the protection of, that country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)). One method by which an applicant can qualify for refugee status is by demonstrating past persecution, which establishes a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of persecution in the future. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). The applicant bears the burden of establishing three criteria: “(1) the occurrence of one or more events constituting persecution; (2) such persecution occurred due to one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) the acts were either perpetrated by the government or by forces the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not provide a definition for persecution; however, as established in Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added), persecution requires that individuals who are different in race, religion, or political opinion must endure suffering or harm in a manner considered offensive, and this must go beyond mere limitations or threats to one’s life and liberty. Furthermore, as stated in Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted), individual incidents are not analyzed separately; instead, all incidents are evaluated together since their combined impact may amount to persecution. The determination of whether an individual has demonstrated prior persecution by private actors in their country of origin—an issue central to this case—presents a factual question. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the series of threats directed at them amounts to persecution. While threats are taken into account when evaluating the overall effect of the mistreatment endured by Petitioners, our precedent makes clear that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, at their merits hearing, Petitioners acknowledged they were not subject to physical harm, and likewise stated in their opening brief that they did not sustain any permanent physical injuries. Although it is not required to show physical harm in order to meet the persecution requirement, id. at 804, the harm suffered must be egregious and greater than simple limitations or isolated threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners also request that we take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological injuries they have suffered. Duarte, as an illustration, reports difficulty sleeping or resting, and states her son began experiencing panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have undoubtedly faced adverse circumstances, fears and anxiety resulting from the threats they received do not meet the threshold for persecution. Even when incidents are physically taxing or cause significant psychological distress, this Court has seldom, if ever, recognized asylum claims grounded solely on threats and resultant emotional or psychological harm. See id. (holding that harm alleged by the applicant—including a ten-hour arrest and detention and threats—did not constitute persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). Indeed, as Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged during oral argument, our precedent has declined to grant asylum even in matters involving more severe factual scenarios of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (determining that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not rise to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that being threatened with machetes and robbed on six occasions did not establish persecution since “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Taking into account these facts and circumstances, we conclude that there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that the harm experienced by Petitioners, even when considered cumulatively, does not constitute past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they endured was due to grounds protected by statute—specifically, their common political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners failed to provide any argumentation concerning the existence of their alleged particular social group or demonstrate their inclusion in such a group. Instead, their arguments regarding the nexus requirement are based solely on the assertion that Don Rafa persecuted them because of their political beliefs. Therefore, our analysis is confined to determining whether Petitioners have shown a connection between the purported persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa and his associates harmed them due to their participation in a campaign for a candidate opposing the sitting president of Colombia. To demonstrate persecution based on political opinion, an applicant must prove that the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for their persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). This requires that the petitioner’s political opinion must not be merely incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to other motivations for the harm, nor should it have played only “a minor role” in the past or anticipated mistreatment. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). A persecutor may act based on multiple motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nonetheless, even where motives are mixed, the protected ground must constitute at least one central reason for the persecution. Id. Thus, Petitioners are required to show that their political opinion was, or will be, at minimum, one central motivation for the actions of their persecutors.

Petitioners have not met their burden because ample evidence supports the determinations by the BIA and IJ that the primary reason Petitioners were targeted was Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s financial interests and security, not Petitioners’ political beliefs. Before the altercation between Jimenez and Don Rafa, Petitioners persisted in their political activities despite receiving threatening phone calls and the death of a fellow campaigner. Petitioners have not identified any record evidence indicating that Don Rafa was responsible for those earlier threats related to political activities. The record shows that threats intensified only after Jimenez became involved in a dispute over property with Don Rafa, and after Don Rafa learned that Jimenez was investigating his rental property. In one instance, Jimenez was threatened by a man who accused him of working for the prosecutor’s office and of causing searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. Subsequently, when Petitioners’ employees were threatened, the men explicitly stated that Don Rafa had sent them.

Given this evidence, the BIA’s conclusion that the main impetus for the threats was Jimenez’s professional and personal entanglements with Don Rafa—rather than Petitioners’ political opinions—was not in error. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to demonstrate past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly because the Colombian authorities are either incapable or unwilling to offer them protection. They claim that those responsible for the persecution have strong connections and associations with the Colombian government, which makes internal relocation within Colombia unsafe.

The assessment of whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In evaluating a government’s reaction to persecution, two main factors are particularly significant: “(1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody.” Id.

This analysis also involves examining whether the government was informed of the alleged persecution and had a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. “In the absence of such notice, it is impossible to determine how authorities might have acted or if governmental assistance would have been provided.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “an important consideration is whether the applicant notified authorities of prior persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting can weaken an argument that the government was unable or unwilling to protect against persecutors,” it does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can meet the requirement to seek government protection by showing that reporting to the police would have been pointless or put them at risk. Id.

There is considerable evidence in the record that supports the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made little, if any, meaningful attempts to seek assistance from the authorities. Both Jimenez and his father either have been or are currently employed by the Colombian government. Nevertheless, neither of them reported any instances of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners’ testimony indicated that they spoke to two individual police officers who advised them to avoid getting into trouble; however, there is no evidence to suggest that this advice represents the official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As determined by the IJ, the record fails to establish that Petitioners sought assistance beyond their immediate vicinity or made use of Jimenez’s connections within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both pointless and hazardous due to the alleged close relationship between their persecutors and the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this contention. The government was aware of the relevant property and had made multiple attempts to search and seize it in response to the ongoing criminal activity. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors are in close association with the government and that the government would lack either the willingness or the ability to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Moreover, Petitioners fail to demonstrate that the Colombian government was, or would have been, either unwilling or unable to offer protection had it been informed of the threats made against them. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding that the evidence was insufficient to show government inability or unwillingness to protect the applicant, especially where the applicant chose not to contact the police based on the belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not satisfied the requirements for asylum, we will briefly consider their remaining claim concerning whether it would be possible and reasonable for them to safely relocate elsewhere in Colombia.

To counter an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and thus defeat an asylum claim, the government may establish by a preponderance of the evidence that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances such that the applicant no longer possesses a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could escape future persecution by relocating to a different area within the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, assuming arguendo that such a fear existed, the evidence in the record substantiates the conclusion that Petitioners could reasonably move to another area within Colombia. Their family members and relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment in the criminal justice sector. Importantly, Petitioners resided without incident for three weeks with Jimenez’s mother in the rural area surrounding Bogota before electing to emigrate to the United States. Additionally, it is significant that the individuals who issued threats directed Petitioners to leave their neighborhood, not to depart the country altogether. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners are able to alleviate any risk of future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence underpins the BIA’s determinations that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Moreover, since Petitioners do not establish a well-founded fear of persecution under the applicable asylum criteria, they are likewise unable to satisfy the more rigorous threshold required for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, the petition for review is denied. Petitioners contend that internal relocation is not feasible, asserting that “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety,” Pet’rs’ Br. 18, because those relatives might also possess political opinions contrary to the government. However, asylum cannot be granted on the basis of such speculative hardships. Petitioners further claim that relocation is impracticable due to administrative and economic challenges, inadequacies in the nation’s judicial system, and their persecutors’ connections to the president of Colombia. Nevertheless, these arguments are only superficially presented and, in light of our prior conclusions, are unpersuasive.
