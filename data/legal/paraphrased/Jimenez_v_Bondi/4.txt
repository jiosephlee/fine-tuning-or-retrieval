Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, submitted requests for asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were rejected by the immigration judge, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld this decision. On appeal, petitioners contest solely the BIA’s denial of their asylum and withholding of removal claims.

Pursuant to jurisdiction conferred by 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any purported persecution was connected to a protected statutory ground, or that the government of Colombia is either unwilling or unable to provide assistance. The record also does not support the claim that petitioners would be unable to relocate safely within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young children originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family both owned and worked at a furniture factory in Bogota.

They have been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012 and provided support for Federico Gutierrez during the 2022 Colombian presidential race. During that election, the Petitioners canvassed local neighborhoods to encourage voters to support their candidate. Starting at the end of May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening phone calls once or twice weekly, related to his participation in the Liberal Party. Other leaders and members of the Liberal Party were similarly threatened. In June 2022, a colleague was abducted and killed after reporting the threats he had faced. Nonetheless, the Petitioners persisted with their campaign activities.

Jimenez held a provisional law license and was enrolled as a law student during 2022, when the pertinent incidents occurred. He additionally served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, providing assistance to individuals affected by domestic violence, extortion, and similar offenses. Acting within his provisional attorney role, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo seeking his assistance regarding a property dispute. She reported that the tenant residing in her property had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez communicate with the tenant regarding restitution and vacating the premises. Jimenez went to the property to attempt to reach a resolution, but negotiations failed; the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and declined to enter discussions. The client chose not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, preferring instead to wait in hopes that the tenant would relinquish the property due to the involvement of a lawyer.

Upon reviewing the title to the client's real estate, Jimenez determined that the tenant occupying the premises was actually the client's former spouse. The ex-husband had once held ownership of the property, but ownership had been conveyed to the client as part of a divorce agreement. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, who is also known as "Don Rafa," a prominent figure leading a narco-trafficking guerrilla faction with political ties to Gustavo Petro, the sitting president of Colombia.

While inspecting the property, Jimenez noticed that the third floor had been cordoned off by the health authorities. Additional inquiries by Jimenez revealed that the building had been shut down for operating as a day-rental site for migrants, as well as serving as a hub for narco-trafficking and prostitution activities. The property had also been connected to instances of kidnapping and homicide. Multiple attempts by law enforcement to conduct searches and seize evidence had apparently not succeeded. A police officer in the vicinity warned Jimenez to keep his distance from the property and to avoid becoming involved.

Later in that same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing pamphlet at his factory which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez chose to ignore this threat and threw the pamphlet away. However, during the following month, as he was working a night shift at the furniture factory, Jimenez was confronted by two armed individuals. These men threatened him at gunpoint and ordered him to leave the area. One of them accused Jimenez of acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and held him accountable for the recent searches and seizures conducted at the property. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and subsequently paid close attention to their children’s journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a time when Petitioners were away from town. During this visit, they physically attacked a factory worker and brandished a firearm while inquiring about the whereabouts of Petitioners. The individuals reiterated their claim that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They repeated their threat to kill Petitioners if they appeared again, indicating that these instructions originated from Don Rafa. After the employee informed them that Petitioners were out of town, the men departed without causing further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez saw a man place his arm around his younger son’s shoulders as the boy was leaving school. The man covered the son’s mouth with his hand when the child attempted to get away and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried over to his son, pushed the man away, and his son ran to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated for three weeks to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota; while there, they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer responded that nothing could be done and remarked that police officers themselves were making the situation worse. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners then followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without admission or parole by an immigration official, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. Each Notice alleged that the respective Petitioner was subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of the allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children participating as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries under Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with other evidence presented, the Immigration Judge (IJ) denied the Petitioners’ applications. The IJ determined that, although the Petitioners were credible, they had not demonstrated: (1) that the harm they experienced constituted past persecution; (2) a connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation was not a reasonable option; or (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to offer them protection from their alleged persecutors. Additionally, the IJ rejected Petitioners’ claims for relief under the CAT. Petitioners sought review, but the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also denied the Petitioners’ contentions that the IJ had infringed upon their right to counsel, rejecting their due process claims.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s determination denying their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA committed error in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) past persecution; (2) past persecution based on their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution for which the government would be unable or unwilling to provide protection. Petitioners do not challenge the denial of relief under the CAT or the disposition of their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Our jurisdiction typically extends to the review of final orders of removal, which may encompass the consolidated evaluation of asylum applications. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our examination of the Board’s denial of asylum is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). When a single BIA member affirms the immigration judge’s ruling, our review is directed at the BIA’s determination; nevertheless, “we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We assess the BIA’s factual determinations according to a substantial-evidence standard. Under this framework, such findings are binding unless the evidence demonstrates that no reasonable factfinder could reach the same conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary relief that the Attorney General may provide to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). To qualify for asylum at the Attorney General’s discretion, the applicant must demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he qualifies as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is located outside their country of nationality . . . and who is unable or unwilling to return to, and is unable or unwilling to seek protection from, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)), the court stated that one method for an applicant to establish refugee status is by demonstrating past persecution, which establishes a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (internal citation omitted). To do so, the applicant is required to satisfy three criteria: “(1) the existence of an event or events that constitute persecution; (2) persecution based on one of the grounds protected by statute; and (3) perpetration of such acts by the government or by parties that the government cannot or will not control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not provide a definition of persecution; however, this court has previously determined that persecution involves causing suffering or harm to individuals based on differences in race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner deemed offensive, and requires more than mere limitations or threats to one’s life or freedom. Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Rather than analyzing each event separately, we assess all incidents together, recognizing that the aggregate impact of several occurrences can rise to the level of persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). Determining whether an individual has demonstrated prior persecution by private actors in their country of origin—an issue central to this matter—is a factual question. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the various threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are considered as part of evaluating the total effect of mistreatment experienced by Petitioners, this Court has previously determined that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Significantly, during the merits hearing, Petitioners acknowledged that they had not been physically harmed and also stated in their opening brief that they did not suffer any lasting physical injury. Although physical injury is not a prerequisite for establishing persecution, id. at 804, the harm suffered must be egregious and exceed mere limitations or isolated threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners request that we take into account the enduring emotional and psychological effects they have suffered. For instance, Duarte claims she is unable to sleep or find rest, and that her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced regrettable situations, fears arising from the threats they received do not meet the threshold required for persecution. Regardless of how physically taxing or psychologically disturbing certain experiences might be, our precedent rarely, if ever, recognizes asylum claims based solely on threats or emotional and psychological harm. See id. (concluding that the alleged harm—comprising a 10-hour arrest and detention as well as threats—did not constitute persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged during oral argument, our prior decisions have denied asylum even in instances involving more severe infliction of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (“four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” held not to be persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (multiple robberies and threats with machetes found inadequate to establish persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

In light of these facts and the applicable legal standards, we conclude there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that, even when considered collectively, Petitioners’ harm does not constitute past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to statutorily protected reasons—specifically, their shared political opinion and their alleged membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not presented any briefing on the formation of such a particular social group or demonstrated their inclusion within it. Instead, when addressing the nexus requirement, Petitioners assert persecution by Don Rafa because of their political beliefs. Therefore, our examination is confined to whether Petitioners have shown a nexus between their claimed persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners, in particular, maintain that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them because of their involvement in a political campaign opposing Colombia’s current president. To establish persecution on account of a political opinion, an applicant must demonstrate that the political opinion constituted “at least one central reason” for the persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). Thus, the asserted political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” nor can it play only “a minor role” in the treatment suffered or in fears of future mistreatment. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor may act with more than one motive. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nonetheless, in such mixed-motive scenarios, at least one protected ground must constitute a central reason for the harm inflicted. Id. Consequently, Petitioners are required to show that their political opinion was, or will be, at least one central reason for being targeted by their persecutors.

Petitioners have not met their burden to show otherwise, as there is substantial evidence supporting the findings of both the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were chiefly targeted due to Jimenez posing a threat to Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than on account of their political beliefs. Before Jimenez’s conflict with Don Rafa, Petitioners were not deterred by threatening phone calls or by the killing of a fellow campaign participant, and continued their political engagement. Petitioners also do not identify any evidence in the record that links Don Rafa to those initial politically motivated threats. Rather, it was only after Jimenez became entangled in a dispute over property with Don Rafa, and once Don Rafa discovered Jimenez was investigating property he had rented, that Petitioners began receiving more serious threats. In one such incident, Jimenez was accused by an individual of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and being responsible for raids conducted at Don Rafa’s property. Furthermore, when Petitioners’ employees were threatened by the same individuals, they specified that they had been sent by Don Rafa.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not make a mistake in concluding that the main impetus for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s occupational and personal disputes with Don Rafa rather than from Petitioners’ political views. Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to prove past persecution, they nonetheless possess a well-founded fear of persecution in the future. They partly base this assertion on the claim that the Colombian government is either incapable of, or unwilling to, provide protection. Petitioners further claim that their persecutors maintain close ties to and affiliations with the Colombian government, thereby making safe relocation within Colombia unattainable.

The unable-or-unwilling analysis evaluates the government’s actions in response to the applicant’s claims of past persecution. Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. When determining the adequacy of a government’s response, two principal factors are particularly significant: (1) whether authorities intervened or attempted to intervene to stop the persecutors, and (2) whether protective measures, such as placement of the applicant in protective custody, were extended to the applicant. Id.

Central to this assessment is the determination of whether the government was made aware of the purported persecution and had a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. The court notes, “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” such a failure does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). The applicant can still fulfill the requirement for seeking government protection by showing that attempting to file a police report would have been pointless or would have put them in danger. Id.

There is ample support in the record for the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made little to no attempt to seek assistance from the government. Both Jimenez and his father have worked, or are currently employed, by the Colombian government. Despite this, neither brought any reports of misconduct or corruption forward. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners stated they spoke with two police officers, who advised them not to provoke trouble; however, there is no evidence that this interaction represents the official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ concluded that there is nothing in the record to indicate that Petitioners attempted to seek government protection beyond their local area or utilized Jimenez’s connections within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both pointless and hazardous, given their belief that the individuals persecuting them are closely connected to the Colombian government. However, this assertion is not corroborated by the record. The government had knowledge of the property involved and had made multiple attempts at searches and seizures to counteract the ongoing illegal activity there. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ claim that their persecutors are tightly linked with the government and that authorities would be unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners fail to establish that the Colombian government was, or would have been, unable or unwilling to protect them had it been made aware of the threats they received. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding that there was insufficient evidence showing the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect, especially where the applicant did not approach the police due to his belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not met the necessary criteria for asylum, we will briefly consider their remaining contention concerning whether safe and reasonable internal relocation within Colombia is possible.

The government may overcome an applicant's presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thereby bar the grant of asylum—if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution. Nevertheless, assuming arguendo that such fear was shown, the evidence in the record substantiates the conclusion that Petitioners have the option to relocate safely to another region within Colombia. Petitioners' relatives and family members remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment within the criminal justice sector. Importantly, Petitioners resided without incident alongside Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota for three weeks before choosing to move to the United States. Additionally, it is significant that those who made threats to Petitioners instructed them merely to leave the neighborhood, not the country as a whole. Consequently, we conclude that Petitioners are able to alleviate any risk of future persecution by reasonably and safely relocating to another area of Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence exists to support the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners are unable to show a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they likewise do not satisfy the more stringent requirements for withholding of removal. Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, the petition for review is denied. Petitioners argue that relocation is not feasible because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (contending that such relatives may also harbor political opinions adverse to the government). However, we may not grant asylum on the basis of mere speculative difficulties. Petitioners further contend that internal relocation would be impractical due to administrative and economic challenges, issues within the country’s judicial system, and because their persecutors are connected to Colombia’s president. Nevertheless, these arguments are only superficially addressed in their briefing, and, given our conclusions above, fail to meet the necessary threshold.
