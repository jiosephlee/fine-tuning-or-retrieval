Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The Petitioners, who are both citizens and natives of Colombia, submitted requests for asylum, withholding of removal, and relief pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were denied by the immigration judge, with the Board of Immigration Appeals subsequently upholding that decision. On appeal, Petitioners challenge only the BIA’s denial of their claims for asylum and withholding of removal.

Exercising our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any purported persecution was connected to a statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian authorities are unable or unwilling to provide assistance. Furthermore, the record does not indicate that Petitioners are unable to relocate safely within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, along with his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte and their two young sons, are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family both owned and operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, all family members have maintained affiliation with the Colombian Liberal Party, and they supported Federico Gutierrez in his 2022 presidential campaign. During this period, Petitioners canvassed neighborhoods in person to solicit votes for Gutierrez. Starting at the end of May 2022, Jimenez began receiving weekly or bi-weekly threatening telephone calls related to his Liberal Party activities. Several other leaders and members of the Liberal Party were also subject to similar threats. In June 2022, despite reporting the intimidation he experienced, one of their colleagues was abducted and killed. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their campaigning efforts.

Jimenez possessed a provisional law license and was a law student during 2022, the time when the pertinent events occurred. He also served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, providing assistance to victims of domestic violence, extortion, and other criminal acts. Acting in his role as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo who sought his assistance in a property dispute. She informed Jimenez that the occupant of her property had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez speak with the tenant about either making restitution or vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to negotiate a resolution by visiting the property, but was met with resistance—the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused any discussion. The client opted not to pursue a formal eviction proceeding, instead expressing hope that the tenant would relinquish the property upon learning of a lawyer’s involvement.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was actually the client’s former spouse. The property had originally belonged to the ex-husband, but ownership was conveyed to the client through a divorce settlement. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, or “Don Rafa,” who is a prominent figure among a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization with political ties to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While on site, Jimenez noted that the property’s third floor had been closed off by the health department. Further inquiry revealed that authorities had shut down the property due to its use as a day-rental location for migrants and as an operational base for narcotics trafficking and prostitution. Additionally, the site was linked to allegations involving kidnapping and homicide. Attempts by law enforcement to execute searches and seizures at the location appeared to have been unsuccessful. A local police officer cautioned Jimenez to avoid involvement with the property and advised against causing trouble.

Later in that same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory bearing the message, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez chose to ignore this warning and threw the brochure away. However, in the following month, while working at the furniture factory during the night, two individuals confronted Jimenez at gunpoint, ordering him to leave the area. One of the assailants accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and held him responsible for the searches and seizures that took place at the property. Once the men departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and subsequently paid close attention to their children’s routes to school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were absent from town. During this visit, they physically attacked an employee and brandished a firearm while demanding information regarding Petitioners’ whereabouts. Once more, the men accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” who were involved in politics. They reiterated their intention to kill Petitioners if they reappeared, specifying that these instructions originated from Don Rafa. The employee informed them that Petitioners were away traveling, after which the men departed without inflicting further harm.

A few days afterward, Jimenez observed a man place his arm around the shoulders of his younger son as the child was leaving school. The individual covered the boy’s mouth with his hand when the boy attempted to get away and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried over to his son, shoved the man aside, and the boy ran to Jimenez’s vehicle. After this event, the family stayed at Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for three weeks, during which time they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer informed him that no action could be taken and explained that law enforcement officers were also part of the problem. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Consequently, Petitioners did so.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following their entry into the United States without inspection or parole by an immigration officer, Petitioners were each served by the Department of Homeland Security with separate Notices to Appear. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners acknowledged the accuracy of DHS’s factual assertions. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with additional evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge (“IJ”) denied the Petitioners’ applications. The IJ determined that, while Petitioners were credible witnesses, they did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they experienced constituted past persecution; (2) a connection to a ground protected by statute; (3) that relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option; or (4) that the Colombian authorities were unable or unwilling to shield them from their persecutors. Additionally, the IJ denied the Petitioners’ applications for relief under the Convention Against Torture (“CAT”). Petitioners sought review, but the Board of Immigration Appeals (“BIA”) affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed their appeal. The BIA further rejected the Petitioners’ arguments that the IJ’s actions deprived them of their right to counsel, thereby rejecting their due process claims.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s determination rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) past persecution; (2) past persecution based on their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution, with the government being unable or unwilling to provide protection. Petitioners do not contest the denial of CAT protection or the adverse ruling on their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Ordinarily, our authority extends to examining final removal orders, which may encompass the combined assessment of asylum applications. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our consideration of the Board’s refusal to grant asylum relief is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since the BIA’s decision was rendered by a single member who upheld the immigration judge’s ruling, our review is directed to the BIA’s determination; nonetheless, we may “consult the IJ’s more comprehensive explanation of the same grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We assess the BIA’s factual determinations using a substantial-evidence standard, under which such findings are deemed binding unless the evidence compels any reasonable factfinder to reach a contrary result. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum is a form of relief that the Attorney General may, at their discretion, grant to noncitizens who meet the eligibility requirements.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). An applicant seeking this discretionary asylum relief must demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he qualifies as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is outside their country of nationality . . . and who is either unable or unwilling to return to, and unable or unwilling to seek the protection of, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)), the court explained that an applicant can demonstrate eligibility for refugee status by establishing past persecution, which then creates a rebuttable presumption that there is a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future. See Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To succeed, the applicant must establish three requirements: (1) the occurrence of one or more events that amount to persecution; (2) that such persecution occurred on the basis of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) that the acts were carried out by the government, or by entities that the government cannot or will not control. Id. (citing Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not specifically define persecution; however, our precedent establishes that persecution involves the imposition of suffering or harm upon individuals who are distinguished by race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner deemed offensive, and that it must go beyond mere limitations or threats to an individual's life or freedom. Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). We assess incidents not as isolated events but collectively, as the combined impact of several occurrences may amount to persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The determination of whether a noncitizen has demonstrated past private persecution in their country of origin—a central matter in this case—is a question of fact. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the numerous threats directed at them amount to persecution. While we do take threats into account when evaluating the overall effect of mistreatment endured by Petitioners, our precedent states that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Significantly, during their merits hearing, Petitioners admitted that they had not suffered any physical injury, and similarly stated in their opening brief that they did not sustain any permanent physical harm. Although it is not required to demonstrate physical injury to meet the persecution standard, id. at 804, the harm alleged must be serious and go beyond minor limitations or occasional threats. Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask that we take into account the enduring emotional and psychological injuries they have suffered. Duarte, for instance, asserts that she is unable to sleep or find rest, and that her son has begun to experience panic attacks and heightened stress. While it is evident that Petitioners have faced distressing experiences, fears and anxiety resulting from threats received do not meet the threshold for persecution. Regardless of the physical or psychological discomfort such incidents may cause, asylum is seldom granted on the basis of threats and emotional or psychological harm alone. See id. (holding that an applicant’s reported harm, which included a 10-hour arrest and detention along with threats, did not rise to persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). Moreover, as Petitioners’ attorney acknowledged during oral argument, precedent in this Circuit has denied asylum even in instances with more severe factual backgrounds regarding harm suffered. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (“four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” found not to amount to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (repeated threats involving machetes and being robbed on six occasions did not satisfy the standard for persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Based on the record and these considerations, we conclude there is substantial evidence to uphold the BIA’s determination that the cumulative harm experienced by Petitioners does not constitute past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to grounds protected by statute—specifically, their common political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not presented arguments or briefing to define their asserted social group or to demonstrate that they belong to such a group. Instead, their argument concerning the nexus element rests on the claim that Don Rafa persecuted them based on their political opinion. Consequently, our analysis is confined to determining whether Petitioners have shown a sufficient nexus between their alleged persecution and their political opinion. In particular, Petitioners claim that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them because they were involved in campaigning for an opponent of Colombia's current president. As set forth in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), “[t]o show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” In other words, the political opinion at issue “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm,” nor can it play “a minor role” in the harm suffered or feared. Id. (citing Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor may be motivated by more than one reason. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even in cases involving mixed motives, the protected ground must constitute at least one central reason for the harm inflicted. Id. Therefore, Petitioners are required to show that their political opinion was or will be at least one central motivating factor behind the actions of their persecutors.

Petitioners have not met their burden because ample evidence underpins the BIA and IJ’s determinations that the threats were primarily motivated by Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s business interests and safety, rather than by Petitioners’ political beliefs. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners remained steadfast in their political activism despite receiving threatening calls and after another campaign member was killed. Petitioners also do not identify any record evidence linking Don Rafa to those early threats with political overtones. Indeed, it was only after Jimenez became embroiled in a land dispute with Don Rafa, and Don Rafa discovered that Jimenez was investigating his rental property, that the threats against Petitioners intensified. On one occasion, a threatening individual accused Jimenez of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for law enforcement actions targeting Don Rafa’s property. When the same individuals later issued threats to Petitioners’ employees, they claimed to be acting at Don Rafa’s direction.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not make a mistake in concluding that Jimenez’s professional and personal conflict with Don Rafa was the principal cause of the threats, not Petitioners’ political opinion. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even if they are unable to demonstrate past persecution, they nevertheless possess a well-founded fear of persecution in the future, partly because they claim the Colombian government is either incapable or unwilling to offer them protection. They maintain that the individuals responsible for the alleged persecution have significant connections and associations with the Colombian government, thereby making safe internal relocation impossible.

The analysis regarding whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “examines evidence related to the government's actions in response to any past persecution suffered by the applicant.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In evaluating a government's response, “two primary considerations are: (1) whether the authorities intervened to halt or attempted to halt the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the authorities extended any form of protection to the applicant, including but not limited to placing the applicant in protective custody.” Id.

An essential aspect of this analysis involves examining whether the government was informed of the claimed persecution and had a chance to act in response. Id. at 861–62. “Absent such notice, it is impossible to determine how authorities might have acted or if governmental assistance would have been forthcoming.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “an important consideration in the analysis is if the applicant informed the government about prior persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting to authorities may weaken an argument that the government was unable or unwilling to control those responsible for the persecution,” this does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can meet the requirement of seeking governmental protection by establishing that reporting to the police would have been pointless or would have posed a danger. Id.

Once more, ample evidence in the record underpins the determinations of both the BIA and the IJ. Petitioners made, at most, minimal attempts to solicit assistance from the government. Both Jimenez and his father have worked or are employed by the Colombian government; nevertheless, neither took steps to report instances of wrongdoing or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners claimed they conversed with two individual police officers who advised them not to create problems—however, no evidence suggests that these statements represent the stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). According to the IJ’s findings, nothing in the record establishes that Petitioners pursued assistance beyond their local area or utilized Jimenez’s professional connections within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both pointless and risky due to the alleged close connections between their persecutors and the Colombian government. However, the record does not substantiate this claim. The government had knowledge of the relevant property and had made several efforts, including searches and seizures, to confront the criminal activity occurring there. Thus, the available evidence undermines Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors had a close relationship with the government and that the government would have been unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners have not established that the Colombian government was, or would have been, incapable or unwilling to offer them protection if made aware of the threats against them. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding there was inadequate evidence to demonstrate the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect where the applicant chose not to contact authorities based on a belief that the persecutors were associated with the government).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not satisfied the criteria necessary for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining contention concerning the practicality and reasonableness of relocating safely within Colombia.

The government may overcome an applicant's presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and thereby defeat the asylum claim by establishing, through a preponderance of the evidence, that either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, assuming arguendo they had done so, the evidence in the record nonetheless supports the conclusion that they could reasonably resettle elsewhere in Colombia. The fact that Petitioners’ family members and relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and that Jimenez’s father continues his employment within the criminal justice system, further substantiates this finding. Significantly, Petitioners resided safely with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota for a period of three weeks prior to their decision to move to the United States. Additionally, the individuals who made threats against Petitioners instructed them only to leave the neighborhood, not to exit the country. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners could avoid any potential risk of future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating to another area within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not shown a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they also do not satisfy the more stringent standard necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, the petition for review is denied. Petitioners contend that internal relocation is not viable because, as noted in Pet’rs’ Br. 18, “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety,” arguing that those relatives might also hold political views in opposition to the government. However, asylum cannot be granted on the basis of such speculative difficulties. Petitioners further argue that relocation would be impractical due to administrative and economic obstacles, deficiencies in the judicial system, and the connections their alleged persecutors have with the president of Colombia. Yet, Petitioners present these points in a cursory manner, and in light of our prior conclusions, their arguments are unpersuasive.
