Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Petitioners, who are citizens and natives of Colombia, sought relief through applications for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge denied all forms of relief, a decision that was subsequently upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, Petitioners contest solely the BIA’s denial of their claims for asylum and withholding of removal.

Having jurisdiction pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners do not establish that they experienced persecution, that any claimed persecution was connected to a ground protected by statute, or that the Colombian government is unable or unwilling to provide protection. Additionally, there is insufficient evidence in the record to conclude that Petitioners are unable to safely relocate within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and worked in a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

All family members have been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012 and were supporters of Federico Gutierrez during the 2022 Colombian presidential campaign. During this time, the Petitioners engaged in canvassing, going door-to-door in nearby neighborhoods to encourage voter support. Toward the end of May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls once or twice weekly concerning his participation in the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates within the party were similarly threatened. In early June 2022, despite having reported threats, one of their colleagues was abducted and killed. Nevertheless, the Petitioners persisted with their campaigning efforts.

Jimenez, who held a provisional license to practice law and was a law student during the events of 2022, also interned at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted victims of crimes such as domestic violence and extortion. In June 2022, acting as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached by a woman from San Bernardo seeking assistance in a property dispute. She informed him that her tenant had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested Jimenez’s intervention to address restitution and to encourage the tenant’s departure from the property. Jimenez attempted to negotiate directly with the tenant by visiting the premises, but these efforts were fruitless, as the tenant refused discussions and warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble.” The client ultimately decided against initiating formal eviction proceedings, preferring instead to rely on the possibility that the tenant would vacate the premises due to a lawyer’s involvement.

Upon reviewing the title to the client's property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the property was actually the client's former husband. Although the property had previously belonged to the ex-husband, it had been conveyed to the client through the terms of their divorce settlement. Additionally, Jimenez identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, commonly referred to as “Don Rafa,” who is a prominent leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group with political ties to the current president of Colombia, Gustavo Petro.

While inspecting the premises, Jimenez noticed that the third story had been cordoned off by the health department. Further inquiry revealed that the authorities had closed the property due to its use both as a short-term rental for migrants and as a hub for narco-trafficking operations and prostitution. The property was further associated with alleged incidents of kidnapping and homicide. Multiple attempts by law enforcement to conduct searches and confiscate evidence at the location appeared to have been ineffective. A local police officer advised Jimenez to avoid the property and not to become involved.

Later in the same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing brochure at his place of work which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez chose not to pay attention to the threat and disposed of the brochure. However, in the following month, as Jimenez was working at the furniture factory during nighttime hours, two individuals approached him with firearms and instructed him to vacate the neighborhood. One of these individuals alleged that Jimenez was acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the raids and confiscations that had occurred at the location. After these men departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and, from that point forward, vigilantly supervised their children’s travel to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were absent from town. During this visit, they attacked an employee at the factory and, brandishing a firearm, demanded information regarding Petitioners’ whereabouts. Once more, the men referred to Petitioners as “snitches for the police” and as persons involved in politics. They reiterated threats to kill Petitioners should they return, stating that this directive originated from Don Rafa. The employee informed the men that Petitioners were traveling, and, subsequently, the men departed without inflicting additional harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around the shoulder of Jimenez’s younger son as the child was leaving school. This individual covered the boy’s mouth with his hand when the boy attempted to flee, instructing him to "run to your dad." Jimenez responded by running to his son and physically pushing the individual, allowing his son to escape to Jimenez’s car. In the wake of this event, the family relocated for three weeks to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota, where they made the decision to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer indicated that there was nothing that could be done, adding that members of the police were involved in exacerbating the situation. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave. Petitioners followed this advice and left.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without having been admitted or paroled by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States pursuant to the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of the allegations brought by DHS. The Immigration Judge deemed them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently filed applications for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte along with other evidence presented, the Immigration Judge (“IJ”) denied Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ determined that Petitioners were credible witnesses, the IJ ultimately concluded that Petitioners did not prove: (1) the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) there existed a nexus to a protected ground under the statute; (3) reasonable internal relocation was unavailable; or (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to offer protection from those persecuting them. Additionally, the IJ rejected Petitioners’ applications for relief under the Convention Against Torture (“CAT”). Petitioners subsequently appealed, but the Board of Immigration Appeals (“BIA”) upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA further rejected Petitioners’ contentions that their due process rights—specifically, the right to counsel—had been violated by the IJ.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s order that denied their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend the BIA was mistaken in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) prior persecution; (2) that any past persecution was because of their political opinion; and (3) a reasonable fear of future persecution for which the government would be unable or unwilling to provide protection. They do not challenge the denial of relief under the CAT or raise their due process claim on appeal.

A. Standard of Review

Our authority typically extends to reviewing final removal orders, which may encompass a combined review of asylum requests. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). The scope of our examination regarding the Board’s refusal of asylum relief is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since the decision by the Immigration Judge (IJ) was affirmed by a single BIA member, our review is directed at the BIA’s ruling, though we may also “consult the IJ’s more detailed reasoning regarding the same issues.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We analyze the factual determinations made by the BIA under the substantial-evidence standard. Pursuant to this standard, such findings are deemed conclusive unless the record indicates that any reasonable adjudicator would be obliged to reach a different result. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may bestow upon noncitizens who meet eligibility requirements.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for this form of discretionary asylum, the applicant must establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he qualifies as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any person located outside their country of nationality . . . who is unable or unwilling to return to and is unable or unwilling to seek the protection of that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant may establish refugee status by “demonstrating past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). The applicant is required to demonstrate three components: “(1) the occurrence of one or more incidents that amount to persecution; (2) that such persecution was motivated by one of the grounds protected by statute; and (3) that the persecutory acts were perpetrated by the government or by parties which the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not provide a definition for persecution; however, this court has determined that persecution requires imposing suffering or harm upon individuals who are different in terms of race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner deemed offensive, and must involve more than mere limitations or threats to one’s life or freedom. See Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (altered citation) (emphasis in original). Our analysis does not evaluate each event individually; rather, we examine them as a whole, since the overall impact of multiple events may amount to persecution. See Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (internal citation omitted). Determining if a noncitizen has established that they have suffered prior private persecution in their country of origin—the principal matter in this case—is a factual question. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (referencing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the series of threats directed toward them amount to persecution. While threats are relevant when evaluating the total effect of the alleged mistreatment, precedent has established that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Notably, during their merits hearing, Petitioners admitted they were not physically harmed, and in their opening brief similarly stated they did not suffer enduring physical injuries. Although physical injury is not required to fulfill the persecution requirement, id. at 804, the harm endured must be egregious and greater than simply limitations or isolated threats, see Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask that we take into account the continuing emotional and psychological effects they have suffered. For instance, Duarte reports insomnia and difficulty resting, along with her son’s development of panic attacks and heightened stress. While Petitioners have faced unfortunate situations, fears resulting from the threats directed at them do not meet the legal standard for persecution. Even when events are physically taxing or mentally disturbing, this Court has seldom, if ever, granted asylum based solely on threats or mental and emotional distress. See id. (holding that an applicant’s claimed harm, which involved a 10-hour arrest and detention and threats, still fell short of persecution despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). Moreover, as Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged at oral argument, our precedent has denied asylum where the claimed harm was even greater. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (concluding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not rise to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that being threatened with machetes and robbed six times was insufficient to establish persecution since “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Considering the totality of these facts, we conclude that there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that Petitioners’ experiences, viewed collectively, do not constitute past persecution within the meaning of the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced resulted from grounds protected by statute—specifically, their collective political opinion and their alleged membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not provided argumentation regarding the formation or existence of their asserted particular social group, nor have they demonstrated that they belong to such a group. Instead, their nexus argument centers solely on their claim of persecution by Don Rafa based on their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis focuses solely on whether Petitioners can establish the requisite connection between their alleged persecution and their political opinion. In particular, Petitioners claim that the harm inflicted by Don Rafa and his associates stemmed from their involvement in a political campaign opposed to the sitting president of Colombia. As established in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), to demonstrate persecution on account of political opinion, an applicant must prove that political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the persecution. This means the political opinion cannot be merely “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm,” nor can it have played “a minor role” in the harm suffered or feared. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor’s motivation may be multifaceted, see O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025), but even in cases involving mixed motives, at least one protected ground must constitute a central reason for the persecution. Id. Thus, in this case, Petitioners are required to show that their political opinion was, or will be, at least a central reason for why the persecutors did or will target them.

Petitioners do not meet their burden, as substantial evidence supports the BIA’s and IJ’s determinations that Petitioners were principally targeted because Jimenez posed a threat to Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than due to their political views. Before Jimenez’s confrontation with Don Rafa, Petitioners persisted in their political advocacy, even after receiving threatening phone calls and after another campaign participant was killed. Petitioners do not identify any evidence in the record connecting Don Rafa to these earlier politically motivated threats. Instead, it was only following Jimenez’s involvement in the property dispute with Don Rafa, and Don Rafa’s discovery that Jimenez was investigating a property he rented, that threats became more severe. One threat involved an individual accusing Jimenez of working for the prosecutor’s office and of being linked to searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. Additionally, when these individuals later threatened the Petitioners’ employees, they indicated they were sent by Don Rafa.

Given these circumstances, the BIA properly concluded that Jimenez’s personal and professional activities with Don Rafa, rather than the Petitioners’ political beliefs, were the primary reasons for the threats. Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even absent a showing of past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partially on the grounds that the Colombian government lacks either the ability or willingness to protect them. They maintain that their alleged persecutors are closely affiliated with, and act in concert with, the Colombian government, thereby precluding the possibility of safe relocation within Colombia.

The analysis regarding whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “examines evidence relating to how the government responded to the applicant’s past persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In evaluating a government’s reaction to persecution, two categories of information are particularly significant: “(1) whether the government halted or attempted to halt the actions of the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government provided protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody.” Id.

This analysis also requires examination of whether the government was aware of the claimed persecution and had a chance to respond. Id. at 861–62. As explained, “Without such notice, it is impossible to determine how authorities might have acted or if the government would have provided assistance.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “an important consideration is whether the applicant informed the government of prior persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting the persecution can undermine the argument that the government was unwilling or unable to prevent harm,” such failure to report is not automatically determinative. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant may still meet the burden of showing government protection was unavailable by providing evidence that filing a complaint would have been futile or posed a danger. Id.

Once more, the record contains ample evidence backing the findings reached by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made very limited attempts, if any, to contact government authorities for assistance. Both Jimenez and his father have government employment in Colombia, past or present. Nevertheless, they did not report any illegal activity or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners claimed they spoke with two individual police officers, who advised them not to provoke trouble; however, there is no indication that this response represents an official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As stated by the IJ, the evidence does not establish that the Petitioners sought aid outside their local neighborhood, nor that they attempted to use Jimenez’s connections in law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both futile and hazardous, as their alleged persecutors are closely linked to the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this contention. The government had knowledge of the disputed property and conducted multiple searches and seizures in attempts to address the ongoing criminal activity occurring there. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ claim that their persecutors are closely associated with the government and that the authorities would not intervene to halt the persecutors. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Additionally, Petitioners cannot establish that the Colombian government was, or would have been, incapable or unwilling to protect them had it been made aware of the threats against them. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding there was inadequate proof that the government was unable or unwilling to safeguard the applicant, particularly since the applicant chose not to notify the police because he believed the persecutors “were part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not met the criteria necessary for asylum, we briefly consider their final claim concerning whether relocating safely within Colombia would be both possible and reasonable.

According to the government, it may overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and thereby bar the asylum application if it establishes, by a preponderance of the evidence, either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances such that the applicant no longer possesses a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could evade future persecution by moving to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming such a fear existed, the evidence in the record indicates that Petitioners have the option to relocate to a different region within Colombia. Petitioners’ family members and relatives remain in Colombia without incident, and Jimenez’s father continues to be employed within the criminal justice system. Significantly, Petitioners resided without harm for three weeks with Jimenez’s mother in a rural area near Bogota prior to electing to move to the United States. Furthermore, the individuals who issued threats to Petitioners instructed them only to leave their neighborhood, not to depart from Colombia entirely. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners can effectively diminish the risk of future persecution by relocating in a safe and reasonable manner within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of persecution required for asylum, they inherently do not satisfy the more stringent requirements for withholding of removal. Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners maintain that relocation is not feasible because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (contending that those relatives may also possess political opinions in opposition to the government). However, we cannot grant asylum on the basis of such speculative hardships. Petitioners also claim that relocation is impracticable because of administrative and economic difficulties, deficiencies in the country’s judicial infrastructure, and the alleged connections between their persecutors and the president of Colombia. Yet, Petitioners do not provide thorough briefing of these assertions, and, in light of our preceding findings, their arguments are unpersuasive.
