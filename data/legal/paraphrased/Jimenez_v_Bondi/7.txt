Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The Petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, sought relief through asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were denied by the immigration judge, and this decision was upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, Petitioners challenge only the BIA’s denial of asylum and withholding of removal.

We exercise jurisdiction pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any persecution claimed was connected to a protected statutory ground, or that the Colombian authorities are either unable or unwilling to offer protection. Additionally, the evidence does not indicate that Petitioners would be unable to safely relocate elsewhere in Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, along with his spouse, Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children, originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, the family as a whole has maintained membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and offered their support to Federico Gutierrez during the 2022 Colombian presidential race. During that period, Petitioners canvassed door-to-door within their local neighborhoods in an effort to encourage support. Around late May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening phone calls once or twice per week connected to his political activity with the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates affiliated with the Liberal Party similarly faced threats. In early June 2022, despite a colleague having reported prior threats, he was kidnapped and killed. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their campaign activities.

Jimenez possessed a provisional license to practice law and was enrolled as a law student during 2022, when the incidents in question occurred. He was also employed as an intern with Colombia's National Prosecutor’s office, where he provided assistance to victims of domestic violence, extortion, and various other offenses. Acting in his provisional legal capacity, he was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo who sought his representation in a property dispute. She informed Jimenez that she had not received rent payments from the individual occupying her property for more than a year and requested that he speak with the tenant regarding the return of the property and compensation. Jimenez went to the property in an attempt to broker a resolution, but was unable to reach an agreement; the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused to discuss the matter. The client chose not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, instead expressing the hope that the tenant would relinquish the property upon realizing a lawyer was now involved.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s real estate, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the premises was actually the client’s former husband. Although the ex-husband had previously held ownership of the property, it had been conveyed to the client as a result of their divorce agreement. Jimenez further determined that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, who was also referred to as “Don Rafa,” and who led a faction of narco-trafficking guerrillas with political affiliations to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While at the property, Jimenez noted that the third floor had been closed off by order of the health department. Additional inquiries revealed that authorities had shut down the property due to its use as a short-term rental for migrants as well as a hub for drug trafficking and prostitution. The location had also been linked to incidents involving kidnapping and homicide. Attempts by law enforcement to execute searches and seizures had apparently not met with success. A local police officer advised Jimenez to avoid involvement with the property and to steer clear of trouble.

Later that same month, Jimenez found a menacing pamphlet at his factory that read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez chose to ignore this warning and threw the pamphlet away. However, in the following month, as he was working at the furniture factory during nighttime hours, two individuals confronted him at gunpoint and ordered him to vacate the area. One of these individuals accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and confiscations conducted on the premises. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to carefully watch over their children’s journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the earlier threat, the same individuals returned to the furniture factory during Petitioners' absence from town. During this visit, they attacked an employee of the factory and, while brandishing a firearm, insisted on being told Petitioners’ whereabouts. The men once more accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” and being involved in politics. They issued another death threat against Petitioners if they returned, emphasizing that these instructions had been given by Don Rafa. The employee informed them that Petitioners were away traveling, after which the men departed without further incident.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed a man place his arm around his younger son’s shoulder as the child was leaving school. The man covered the boy’s mouth with his hand when the boy attempted to get away and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried to his son’s aid, shoved the man, and the son fled to Jimenez’s vehicle. In response to this incident, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the countryside near Bogota for three weeks, during which they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez reported the incident to a patrolling police officer. However, the officer responded that there was nothing that could be done and indicated that police involvement was exacerbating the situation. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave. Petitioners followed this advice and left.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded to the veracity of the allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners submitted applications for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children proceeding as rider respondents and as derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with additional evidence in the record, the IJ denied Petitioners' applications. While the IJ determined that Petitioners were credible witnesses, the IJ held that they did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation was not a reasonable alternative; and (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to protect them from their persecutors. The IJ further denied Petitioners’ claims for relief under the CAT. Petitioners sought review, but the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed their appeal. Additionally, the BIA rejected Petitioners’ assertions that the IJ infringed their right to counsel, finding no due process violation.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s ruling that rejected their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) prior persecution; (2) that any past persecution occurred because of their political opinion; and (3) a credible fear of future persecution that the government would be unable or unwilling to prevent. Petitioners do not contest the BIA’s denial of relief under the CAT or their claim regarding due process.

A. Standard of Review

Generally, our jurisdiction extends to the review of final removal orders, which may encompass a unified review of asylum-related claims. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our examination of the Board’s denial of asylum is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). When a single BIA member upholds the Immigration Judge’s ruling, our review targets the BIA’s order, yet we retain the authority to “consult the IJ’s more comprehensive reasoning on the same issues.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We assess the BIA’s factual determinations according to the substantial evidence standard. This approach renders the BIA’s factual findings binding unless the record indicates that any reasonable factfinder would necessarily reach an opposite conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a form of relief granted at the Attorney General’s discretion to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for this discretionary asylum, the applicant has the responsibility of demonstrating by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). According to the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is located outside their country of nationality . . . who cannot or does not wish to return to, and cannot or does not wish to seek the protection of, that country due to past persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on grounds of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)), the court explained that an individual seeking refugee designation may do so “through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To meet this burden, the applicant is required to demonstrate three elements: “(1) one or more events that constitute persecution; (2) that occurred on the basis of one of the statutorily protected categories; and (3) was perpetrated either by the government itself or by actors the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not provide a definition for persecution; however, “we have determined that persecution involves imposing suffering or harm on individuals who are distinguished by race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner considered objectionable, and must be more than mere limitations or threats to life and liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Additionally, “[r]ather than evaluating each episode separately, we assess them together, since the combined impact of several incidents may amount to persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). Determining “[w]hether a noncitizen has established prior private persecution in their country of origin—the central issue in this matter—is a factual inquiry.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the numerous threats directed at them amount to persecution. While we do take threats into account when evaluating the totality of harm experienced by Petitioners, our precedent establishes that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Significantly, at their merits hearing, Petitioners admitted that they did not suffer any physical injury, and similarly stated in their opening brief that they did not sustain any lasting physical harm. Though actual physical injury is not required to meet the persecution standard, id. at 804, the harm endured must be serious, rising above minor restrictions or occasional threats. Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask the Court to take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological effects they have suffered. Duarte, for instance, asserts that she experiences sleeplessness and restlessness, and that her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and anxiety. Although Petitioners have faced distressing events, the apprehensions resulting from threats received do not satisfy the threshold for persecution. No matter how physically or emotionally taxing such incidents might be, our precedent seldom, if ever, grants asylum solely on the basis of threats and resultant emotional or psychological harm. See id. (concluding that an applicant’s claimed mistreatment, which involved a 10-hour arrest and detention and threats, failed to reach the level of persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). During oral argument, Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged that our jurisprudence has denied asylum even in situations involving more severe instances of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (“four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not qualify as persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (being threatened with machetes and robbed six times did not meet the standard for persecution, as “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Considering these circumstances, we determine there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s conclusion that the harm alleged by Petitioners, even when viewed collectively, does not constitute past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they endured was due to statutorily protected bases—specifically, their collective political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners failed to present any substantive argument regarding the definition of their alleged particular social group or to prove that they belong to such a group. Instead, Petitioners focus their nexus argument on the claim that Don Rafa persecuted them because of their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners have shown a nexus between the persecution they allege and their political opinion. In particular, Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them because of their involvement in campaigning for a political rival of Colombia's current president. As stated in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), “[t]o show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” This requires that Petitioners’ political opinion must not be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” nor can it “play ‘a minor role’” in the mistreatment they experienced or fear. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is well recognized that a persecutor may be driven by several motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nonetheless, in situations involving mixed motives, the persecutor must be motivated by at least one protected ground that constitutes a central reason for the harm imposed. Id. Consequently, Petitioners are required to prove that their political opinion was or would be at least one central reason for which the persecutors targeted or would target them.

Petitioners have not demonstrated the necessary requirement, as the record contains substantial evidence supporting the determinations of the BIA and the IJ that Petitioners were chiefly targeted because Jimenez posed a threat to Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than on account of their political beliefs. Before the incident involving Jimenez and Don Rafa, Petitioners persisted in their political activities despite having received threatening phone calls and after another campaign member was killed. Petitioners also fail to cite any evidence indicating Don Rafa was responsible for these initial threats related to politics. The escalation of threats occurred only after Jimenez became entangled in a property dispute with Don Rafa and after Don Rafa learned that Jimenez was investigating his rental property. During one threatening encounter, Jimenez was accused by an individual of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and being the cause of law enforcement searches at Don Rafa’s premises. Subsequently, when Petitioners’ employees were threatened by the same individuals, it was stated that Don Rafa had sent them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA correctly concluded that the primary reason for the threats was rooted in Jimenez’s business and personal disputes with Don Rafa, rather than Petitioners’ political views. Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even if they are unable to demonstrate past persecution, they nonetheless possess a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future, partially because the Colombian authorities are either incapable or unwilling to safeguard them. They claim that the individuals who would persecute them maintain close connections with the Colombian government, thereby making it impossible for petitioners to relocate safely within the country.

The “unable-or-unwilling” inquiry evaluates the government's actions in response to the applicant's alleged prior persecution. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. When assessing a government's reaction to persecution, two types of information are particularly significant: first, whether the authorities took action or attempted to take action against the persecutors; and second, whether the government provided the applicant with protection, such as by placing them in protective custody. Id.

A related aspect of this analysis involves determining whether the government was informed of the purported persecution and had a chance to take action. Id. at 861–62. As stated, “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” this omission does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, the applicant may fulfill the requirement to seek government protection by showing that submitting a police report would have been futile or placed the applicant in danger. Id.

There is considerable evidence in the record to support the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. Petitioners made only limited, if any, attempts to seek assistance from governmental authorities. Both Jimenez and his father have held or presently hold positions with the Colombian government, but neither reported any instances of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). According to Petitioners’ testimony, they had conversations with two police officers who advised them to avoid trouble; however, this does not establish that these views represent the stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ concluded that the record does not reflect any effort by Petitioners to request assistance beyond their local area or through Jimenez’s connections in law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both ineffective and hazardous, on the grounds that their persecutors are closely linked to and affiliated with the Colombian government. However, the record does not substantiate this claim. The authorities were already cognizant of the property involved and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in an effort to curb the criminal activity occurring there. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ contention that their persecutors are intimately connected with the government and that the authorities would be either unable or unwilling to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners are unable to demonstrate that the Colombian government was, or would have been, incapable or disinclined to provide them with protection if it had been informed about the threats directed at them. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding that the evidence did not sufficiently establish the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect the applicant, especially where the applicant had chosen not to contact law enforcement due to his belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not satisfied the necessary criteria for asylum, we will nonetheless briefly consider their remaining contention concerning whether it would be practicable and reasonable for them to relocate safely within Colombia.

The government can overcome an applicant's presumption of having a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thereby defeat the asylum claim—by establishing by a preponderance of the evidence either: "(1) there has been a 'fundamental change' in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country." Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming such a fear exists, the evidence supports the conclusion that reasonable internal relocation within Colombia is available to them. Petitioners’ family members and other relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues to be employed within the criminal justice sector. Significantly, Petitioners resided without incident alongside Jimenez’s mother in a rural area near Bogota for three weeks prior to choosing to emigrate to the United States. Additionally, it is important to note that the individuals who issued threats directed Petitioners only to leave their immediate neighborhood, rather than to depart the country. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners have the ability to avoid any potential future persecution by relocating safely and reasonably to another location within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence underpins the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners do not establish a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they, by extension, do not satisfy the more stringent standard for withholding of removal. Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners argue that return is not safe solely because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (contending that those relatives might also possess political opinions contrary to the government’s). However, we cannot approve asylum based on speculative or hypothetical difficulties. Petitioners further claim that internal relocation is infeasible due to bureaucratic and economic obstacles, inadequacies within the judicial system, and because their persecutors purportedly maintain connections to the president of Colombia. Yet, these points are raised only cursorily, and in light of our earlier conclusions, Petitioners’ arguments are unpersuasive.
