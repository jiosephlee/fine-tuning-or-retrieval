The Petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, submitted requests for asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge rejected these requests, and this decision was subsequently upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. The Petitioners now challenge only the BIA's rejection of their claims for asylum and withholding of removal.

With jurisdiction pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they were persecuted, that any persecution was connected to a protected ground under the statute, or that the Colombian authorities are either unable or unwilling to provide protection. Additionally, the evidence does not support that Petitioners are unable to relocate safely to another part of Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated a furniture factory in Bogota.

Since 2012, all members of the family have belonged to the Colombian Liberal Party and actively campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, who ran for president of Colombia in 2022. During the campaign, the Petitioners solicited support by canvassing neighborhoods to encourage votes. Starting in late May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threats by phone one or two times each week connected to his role in the Liberal Party. Similarly, threats were made to other leaders and members of the Party. In June 2022, despite having previously reported receiving threats, one of Jimenez's colleagues was abducted and killed. Nevertheless, the Petitioners persisted in their campaigning activities.

Jimenez, who held a provisional license to practice law and was a law student during the events in 2022, was also serving as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, assisting victims of domestic violence, extortion, and various crimes. In June 2022, acting in his provisional legal capacity, he was approached by a woman from San Bernardo regarding a dispute over her property. She reported that her tenant had not paid rent for more than a year and requested Jimenez’s assistance in speaking to the tenant about compensation and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to broker an agreement by visiting the property, but the tenant refused both restitution and negotiation, warning Jimenez not to “look for trouble.” Although Jimenez’s client opted not to pursue a formal eviction, she chose to wait, hoping that the tenant would relinquish possession after learning a lawyer had become involved.

After examining the client's property title, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the premises was actually the client's former spouse. The property, while once owned by the ex-husband, had been conveyed to the client through a divorce agreement. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, commonly referred to as "Don Rafa," who is recognized as a leader within a faction of narcotics-trafficking guerrillas who politically support Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While onsite, Jimenez noticed that the third story of the building had been officially closed by the health department. Pursuing additional information, Jimenez found that the closure was due to the property’s use as a temporary rental for migrants as well as a center for narcotics trafficking and prostitution activities. Additionally, the property had connections to incidents involving kidnapping and homicide. Although law enforcement had carried out multiple searches and attempts to seize evidence, these efforts appeared to have yielded no results. A member of the local police advised Jimenez to avoid involvement with the property and to refrain from instigating complications.

That same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing pamphlet at his workplace which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” He ignored this message and threw the pamphlet away. However, in the following month, as he worked a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted Jimenez at gunpoint, instructing him to leave the area. One of them accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office, blaming him for the law enforcement searches and seizures conducted at the premises. Once the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and subsequently took measures to carefully watch over their children’s journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory during Petitioners' absence from town. They attacked an employee at the factory, brandished a firearm, and insisted on learning the whereabouts of Petitioners. Again, the men accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They reiterated their threats to kill Petitioners should they reappear, stating the command originated from Don Rafa. Upon hearing from the employee that Petitioners were away, the men departed without inflicting further harm.

Several days later, Jimenez observed a man place his arm around the shoulder of his younger son as the boy was leaving school. The man covered the son's mouth with his hand when the boy attempted to get away, instructing him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez approached his son and pushed the man aside while his son ran to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for three weeks; during this period, they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the events to a police patrol officer. However, the officer responded that no action could be taken and indicated that police involvement was part of the issue. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners subsequently followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without admission or parole by an immigration official, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to Petitioners. These Notices accused each Petitioner of being subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners acknowledged the accuracy of the allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge deemed them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners submitted applications for asylum and withholding of removal, with their minor children named as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, as well as additional evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners’ applications. Despite finding the Petitioners credible, the IJ determined that they did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection between the harm and a protected statutory ground; (3) that internal relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option; and (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to shield them from their alleged persecutors. The IJ further rejected the Petitioners’ applications for relief under the CAT. Following this decision, Petitioners appealed to the BIA, which affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. Additionally, the BIA found no merit in Petitioners’ due process claims alleging the IJ infringed upon their right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal, asserting that the BIA improperly concluded they failed to demonstrate: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was on account of their political opinion; and (3) that they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution for which the government cannot or will not provide protection. Petitioners do not contest the BIA's denial of relief under the CAT or their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Generally, we possess authority to examine final removal orders, which encompasses the consolidated assessment of asylum petitions. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (relying on 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our evaluation of the Board’s rejection of asylum relief is restricted to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since the BIA’s decision was issued by a single member who affirmed the Immigration Judge, we primarily assess the BIA’s ruling, yet we remain permitted to review the Immigration Judge’s more detailed rationale for the same issues. Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

The BIA’s factual determinations are reviewed under the substantial-evidence standard. By this measure, the BIA’s factual findings are binding unless the evidence requires any reasonable factfinder to reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may extend to noncitizens who satisfy the necessary qualifications.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). For an individual to qualify for this discretionary asylum, he or she carries the responsibility of establishing, by a preponderance of the evidence, that they meet the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is located outside of any country of their nationality . . . and who, due to past persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on the basis of race, religion, nationality, membership in a specific social group, or political opinion, is either unable or unwilling to return to, or receive protection from, that country.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant may establish eligibility for refugee status by demonstrating past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To satisfy this standard, the applicant needs to establish three components: “(1) there was an act or acts amounting to persecution; (2) such persecution was because of one of the legally protected grounds; and (3) the persecution was either by the government or by individuals or groups the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners assert that, along with the documented threats, they endured physical, psychological, and emotional injuries amounting to past persecution.

Although the INA does not explicitly define persecution, “we have determined that persecution requires suffering or harm inflicted on individuals because they differ by race, religion, or political opinion, and that the harm must be of a kind deemed offensive, going beyond mere restrictions or threats to one's life or liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). “Rather than evaluating each event separately, we assess them in combination, since the overall impact of several incidents may constitute persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). “Determining whether a noncitizen has established evidence of private persecution in their country of origin—the main issue at hand—is a question of fact.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the repeated threats directed at them constitute persecution. While threats are taken into account when determining the cumulative effect of mistreatment, our precedent holds that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Significantly, Petitioners acknowledged during the merits hearing that they did not suffer physical harm, and likewise stated in their opening brief that they did not endure any lasting physical injury. Although physical harm is not required in order to prove persecution, id. at 804, the harm experienced must be egregious and rise above the level of mere inconveniences or occasional threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask that we take into account their ongoing emotional and psychological suffering. Duarte, for instance, asserts that she experiences insomnia and restlessness, and her son has begun to suffer from panic attacks and stress. Although the Petitioners have faced unfortunate events, fear resulting from threats they have received does not, by itself, meet the threshold for persecution. Regardless of how physically taxing or psychologically disturbing certain incidents may be, it is rare for us to grant asylum solely on the basis of threats and psychological or emotional distress. See id. (holding that an applicant’s claimed harm—including a 10-hour arrest, detention, and threats—did not amount to persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). Indeed, as Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged during oral argument, precedent has denied asylum even in cases featuring more severe inflicted harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not rise to the level of persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (threats involving machetes and being robbed six times was inadequate to establish persecution since “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

In light of these facts and circumstances, we conclude that there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that, even when considered collectively, Petitioners’ claimed harms do not constitute past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was motivated by grounds protected under statute—specifically, their common political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners failed to develop any argument concerning the existence of a particular social group or demonstrate their own membership in one. Instead, their nexus argument is centered solely on the claim that Don Rafa persecuted them due to their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis will focus only on whether Petitioners have shown a connection between their asserted political opinion and their alleged persecution. In support, Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them because of their participation in a campaign opposed to the sitting president of Colombia. Under Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), in order to demonstrate persecution on the basis of a political opinion, an applicant must prove that the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for their mistreatment. This means that the individual’s political opinion must not be incidental, peripheral, superficial, or secondary to some other basis for the harm, nor may it constitute only a minor aspect of the reasoning for their prior or anticipated persecution. Id. (citing Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). Importantly, a persecutor may be motivated by several different reasons for targeting a person. See O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, in such mixed-motive circumstances, a protected ground must serve as at least one central motive for the persecution. Id. Accordingly, Petitioners must show in this case that their political opinion was—or is anticipated to be—at least one central factor in the reasons for which they were or would be persecuted.

Petitioners do not meet this requirement because ample evidence underpins the BIA and IJ’s determinations that Petitioners were mainly singled out due to Jimenez threatening Don Rafa’s profits and safety, rather than on account of any political opinion. Before Jimenez’s conflict with Don Rafa arose, Petitioners persisted in their political activities in spite of intimidating phone calls and the killing of another campaigner. Petitioners fail to identify any evidence in the record indicating Don Rafa was responsible for those early threats linked to politics. Indeed, Petitioners only began receiving heightened threats after Jimenez became entangled in a property dispute with Don Rafa and once Don Rafa learned that Jimenez was looking into his leased property. On one occasion, an individual threatening Jimenez accused him of working for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for law enforcement actions at Don Rafa’s property. Later, when the same individuals threatened Petitioners’ employees, they explicitly stated they had been sent by Don Rafa.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not make a mistake in concluding that the primary motivation for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s business and personal connections with Don Rafa, and not from the Petitioners’ political opinion. Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that the Petitioners’ political opinion was only incidental and of secondary importance compared to the actual motive for the harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, despite their inability to prove past persecution, they nonetheless possess a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future. This claim is partially grounded in their view that the Colombian government is either incapable or unwilling to offer protection. They further maintain that the persecutors maintain significant alignment and connections with the Colombian authorities, thereby making internal relocation within Colombia unsafe.

The analysis regarding government inability or unwillingness focuses on the evidence showing how the government has responded to any persecution suffered by the applicant in the past. Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. When evaluating the government's reaction to persecution, particular emphasis is given to two main factors: “(1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody.” Id.

A related aspect of this analysis involves determining whether the government was aware of the claimed persecution and had a chance to act. Id. at 861–62. The absence of such notice makes it impossible to determine how authorities might have responded or whether the government would have intervened. Id. (citation modified). Thus, whether the applicant informed the government about prior persecution is a significant element in this assessment. Id. (citation omitted). Although not notifying the authorities may weaken an argument that the government could not or would not prevent the harm, it does not necessarily defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). The applicant can still meet the requirement for seeking government protection by showing that filing a report would have been pointless or exposed them to risk. Id.

Once more, the record contains ample evidence that supports the determinations of both the BIA and the IJ. The steps Petitioners took to request assistance from the government were limited at best. Both Jimenez and his father either formerly or presently held positions with the Colombian government, yet neither reported any incidents of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners claimed in their testimony that they approached two police officers, who advised them to avoid getting involved; however, there is no evidence that this advice represented the stance of the Colombian government as a whole. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As found by the IJ, the evidence does not reflect that Petitioners attempted to seek protection beyond their immediate community or by utilizing Jimenez’s law enforcement relationships.

Petitioners contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been pointless and posed risks because their alleged persecutors maintain strong connections and affiliations with the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this assertion. The authorities were cognizant of the property in question and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in an effort to curtail the ongoing criminal activities there. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ claim that their persecutors are closely associated with the government, or that the authorities would be unwilling or unable to intervene against them. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Additionally, Petitioners fail to demonstrate that the Colombian government either was, or would be, unwilling or unable to safeguard Petitioners if it had been informed about the threats they encountered. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding that evidence was lacking to show that the government was unable or unwilling to offer protection where the applicant chose not to contact law enforcement based on a belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Therefore, we concur that Petitioners are unable to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution necessary to qualify for asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not met the requirements for asylum, we briefly consider their last contention concerning whether it would be possible and reasonable for them to safely relocate within Colombia.

To overcome an applicant's presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and defeat an application for asylum, the government must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even if such fear were present, the evidence in the record substantiates the conclusion that Petitioners could reasonably resettle in a different region of Colombia. Petitioners’ extended family members remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues to be employed in the criminal justice sector. Importantly, Petitioners resided without incident with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota for a period of three weeks prior to choosing to move to the United States. Furthermore, it should be emphasized that those individuals who issued threats to Petitioners instructed them to leave their neighborhood—not to exit Colombia. Consequently, we conclude that Petitioners have the option to alleviate any risk of future persecution through safe and reasonable relocation elsewhere in Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA’s determinations that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of persecution under the asylum criteria, they consequently cannot satisfy the more stringent requirement for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for the reasons outlined above, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that internal relocation within their home country is not viable because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (noting that such relatives could also possess political views adverse to the government). However, asylum cannot be granted on the basis of speculative difficulties. Petitioners further claim that relocation would impose administrative and financial hardships, and cite Colombia’s judicial system and the influence of their alleged persecutors, who are reportedly connected to the country’s president. Yet, these contentions are only superficially addressed in their briefing and, in view of our prior analysis, do not succeed.
