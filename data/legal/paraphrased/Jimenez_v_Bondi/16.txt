Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The Petitioners, who are citizens and natives of Colombia, sought relief through applications for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. Their requests were denied by the immigration judge, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld this decision. On appeal, Petitioners challenge solely the BIA’s rulings regarding their claims for asylum and withholding of removal.

We exercise jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any purported persecution was linked to a statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian authorities are unwilling or unable to provide protection. Additionally, the evidence fails to demonstrate that Petitioners are unable to relocate safely elsewhere within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture factory in Bogota.

Since 2012, the entire family has maintained membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and participated in supporting Federico Gutierrez during the 2022 Colombian presidential campaign. Petitioners canvassed door-to-door in local communities in an effort to garner support for Gutierrez. Around late May 2022, Jimenez began to receive phone threats once or twice weekly, which concerned his political activity with the Liberal Party. Additional party leaders and associates encountered similar threats. In June 2022, despite having reported prior threats, a colleague was abducted and killed. Nonetheless, the Petitioners persisted with their campaign activities.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and was enrolled as a law student in 2022 when the pertinent incidents occurred. Additionally, he served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he provided assistance to victims of domestic violence, extortion, and various other offenses. Acting in his role as a provisional attorney, he was approached by a woman from San Bernardo in June 2022, who sought his assistance in a dispute over property. She reported that her tenant had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez contact the tenant regarding compensation and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to resolve the matter by visiting the property to discuss a possible agreement, but his efforts were unsuccessful—the tenant advised Jimenez not to "look for trouble" and declined to engage in discussions. The client chose not to initiate a formal eviction and instead preferred to wait in the hope that the tenant would relinquish the property after becoming aware of a lawyer's involvement.

Upon examining the title to the client’s property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the premises was actually the client’s former spouse. Although the ex-husband had been the original owner, ownership had been conveyed to the client pursuant to a divorce settlement. Further investigation revealed that the tenant’s identity was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also identified as “Don Rafa,” a prominent figure leading a group of narco-trafficking guerrillas with political ties to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While touring the property, Jimenez observed that the health department had sealed off the third floor. Conducting additional inquiries, Jimenez found out that authorities had closed the location because it was serving as a day-rental for migrants and operating as a base for activities related to narco-trafficking and prostitution. Reports also linked the property to incidents of kidnapping and murder. Law enforcement authorities had made multiple attempts to conduct searches and seizures at the location; however, those attempts appeared to be fruitless. Jimenez was warned by a police officer in the vicinity to steer clear of the property and to avoid becoming involved in any disputes.

Later in the same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing pamphlet at his factory declaring, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” He paid no heed to this threat and threw the pamphlet away. However, the following month, while Jimenez was engaged in nighttime work at the furniture factory, two individuals approached him with firearms, instructing him to vacate the neighborhood. One assailant labeled Jimenez a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office, blaming him for the searches and seizures conducted at the premises. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began vigilantly supervising their children’s journeys to school.

Approximately two months following the previous death threat, the identical two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a time when Petitioners were away. They attacked an employee at the factory, produced a firearm, and insisted on learning Petitioners’ whereabouts. Once more, the men accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They reiterated their intent to kill Petitioners if they reappeared, indicating the directive originated from Don Rafa. The employee informed the men that Petitioners were traveling, prompting their departure without inflicting further harm.

Several days later, Jimenez observed an individual place an arm around his younger son’s shoulder as the child left his school building. The man covered the boy’s mouth with his hand while the boy attempted to break free and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez ran towards his son and shoved the man, allowing his son to flee to Jimenez’s vehicle. In response to this event, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area near Bogota for a period of three weeks, during which they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the incident to a patrolling police officer. The officer responded that nothing could be done and indicated that law enforcement personnel themselves were involved in the issue. The officer further advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Consequently, Petitioners followed that advice.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without inspection or parole by an immigration official, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of DHS’s claims. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries to Jimenez’s asylum application.

After considering the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with additional evidence on the record, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ determined that the Petitioners were credible witnesses, the IJ found that they did not prove: (1) that the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation was not a reasonable option; and (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to offer protection from their alleged persecutors. The IJ additionally denied the Petitioners’ applications for relief under the CAT. Petitioners sought review, but the Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA further rejected Petitioners’ claims that the IJ infringed their right to counsel, finding no due process violation.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s order denying their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend the BIA erred in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was due to their political opinion; and (3) a credible fear of future persecution with respect to which the government would be unable or unwilling to offer protection. Petitioners do not dispute the denial of relief under the CAT nor do they pursue their due process argument on appeal.

A. Standard of Review

Ordinarily, our jurisdiction extends to the review of final removal orders, which may encompass the combined evaluation of asylum applications. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our assessment of the Board’s denial of an asylum application is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (citing § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Where, as here, a single BIA member has upheld the immigration judge’s ruling, our review centers on the BIA’s decision; however, we may “consult the IJ’s more detailed reasoning regarding those identical grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

The BIA’s factual findings are subject to review for substantial evidence. Under this deferential standard, those factual determinations remain binding unless the evidence requires that every reasonable factfinder would necessarily reach a different result. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a remedy that the Attorney General may, at his discretion, extend to qualified noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for asylum as a discretionary benefit, the applicant must demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that they satisfy the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). The Immigration and Nationality Act defines a refugee as any individual who is outside the country of his or her nationality . . . and is either unable or unwilling to return to that country, and unable or unwilling to seek its protection, due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on the basis of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)). A means by which an applicant can qualify for refugee status is “by demonstrating past persecution, which establishes a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (internal citation omitted). The applicant bears the burden of establishing three components: “(1) one or more events that constitute persecution; (2) motivated by one of the statutorily protected bases; and (3) perpetrated by either the government or by entities the government is unwilling or unable to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

Although the INA does not provide a definition of persecution, “we have determined that establishing persecution requires proof of the infliction of harm or suffering upon individuals based on differences such as race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner considered offensive, and this must rise above mere restrictions or threats to liberty or life.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Furthermore, “our analysis considers all incidents in their entirety, rather than separately, because taken together, multiple actions may amount to persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). Determining “whether a noncitizen has established past private persecution in their home country—the primary issue presented here—is a question of fact.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the several threats made against them amount to persecution. While we take threats into account when evaluating the overall effect of Petitioners' mistreatment, our precedent provides that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, during their merits hearing, Petitioners admitted that they did not suffer physical harm, and similarly acknowledged in their opening brief that they did not endure any lasting physical injuries. Although establishing persecution does not require physical harm, id. at 804, the harm suffered must rise to a level that is egregious, exceeding mere limitations or occasional threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners request that we take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological impacts they have experienced. Duarte, for instance, reports difficulty sleeping or resting, and notes that her son has begun suffering from panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have certainly faced distressing circumstances, fears arising from threats alone do not meet the threshold for persecution. While some events may indeed be physically taxing or cause significant psychological distress, our precedent has seldom, if ever, recognized asylum claims premised solely on threats coupled with emotional or psychological harm. See id. (concluding that an applicant’s claimed harm, which included a ten-hour arrest and detention in addition to threats, did not rise to persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). During oral argument, Petitioners’ attorney acknowledged that our existing jurisprudence has denied asylum in cases involving even more severe harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not amount to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that experiencing threats involving machetes and being robbed six times was insufficient for persecution, as “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Based on these considerations, we conclude there is substantial evidence in the record to affirm the BIA’s determination that Petitioners’ harm, even when considered collectively, does not constitute past persecution as that term is defined in the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they endured was due to grounds protected by statute—specifically, their collective political opinion and particular social group membership. However, Petitioners did not submit any arguments discussing the existence of their claimed particular social group nor did they demonstrate that they are members of such a group. Instead, when addressing the nexus element, Petitioners claim that Don Rafa persecuted them on the basis of their political opinion. Therefore, our review is restricted to whether Petitioners can show a nexus between their claimed persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners’ position is that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them because they participated in the campaign of an opponent of Colombia’s sitting president. “To demonstrate persecution based on political opinion, an applicant must show that the political opinion constituted ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, a political opinion “must not be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another motivation for harm,” nor can it “play only a minor role” in prior mistreatment or in apprehensions of future mistreatment. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). Notably, a persecutor may have several motives for acting against an individual. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even when multiple motives exist, at least one protected ground must serve as a central reason for the harm imposed. Id. Consequently, Petitioners are required to establish that their political opinion was or will be at least one central reason why the persecutors targeted or would target them.

Petitioners have not met their burden because there is substantial evidence supporting the findings of both the BIA and the IJ that Petitioners were principally targeted as a result of Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s income and personal security, rather than due to their political beliefs. Before Jimenez’s conflict with Don Rafa arose, Petitioners had not been deterred by threatening phone calls or by the killing of a fellow campaigner, and continued their political involvement. Petitioners do not reference any record evidence linking Don Rafa to these earlier threats, which were apparently politically motivated. Indeed, Petitioners only began to experience increased threats after Jimenez became involved in a land dispute with Don Rafa and after Don Rafa learned that Jimenez was investigating property he rented. On one occasion, a man threatening Jimenez accused him of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and held him responsible for searches and seizures conducted at Don Rafa’s property. Furthermore, when Petitioners’ employees were later threatened by the same group of men, those men indicated they had been sent by Don Rafa.

In view of these circumstances, the BIA reasonably concluded that the primary motive for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s personal and professional conflicts with Don Rafa, rather than any political opinion held by Petitioners. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, despite being unable to prove past persecution, they nonetheless possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly due to the Colombian government's inability or unwillingness to protect them. They maintain that their persecutors maintain a strong association and alignment with the Colombian authorities, thereby negating the possibility of safe internal relocation.

The assessment of whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect "involves examining the evidence pertaining to how a government responded to past persecution experienced by the applicant." Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In this evaluation, "two main considerations are especially significant: (1) whether the authorities intervened or attempted to intervene to prevent the actions of the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government provided any form of protection to the applicant, such as through placement in protective custody." Id.

Central to this examination is whether the government was aware of the purported persecution and had a chance to respond. Id. at 861–62. As stated, “Without such notice, it cannot be determined how the police might have reacted or if the government would have intervened.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “a significant factor in the analysis is whether the applicant notified the government of the past persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting the persecution may weaken the argument that the government could not or would not control the persecutors,” this alone does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant may fulfill the requirement for government protection by showing that reporting to the police would have been futile or posed danger. Id.

Once again, ample evidence within the record supports the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made little, if any, attempt to seek governmental assistance. Both Jimenez and his father are or have been employed by the Colombian government, yet neither chose to report instances of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners did state that they spoke with two police officers, who advised them not to pursue trouble; however, no evidence demonstrates that these interactions represent the Colombian government’s official stance. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As noted by the IJ, there is no evidence in the record that the Petitioners attempted to obtain protection outside their immediate locality or utilized Jimenez’s contacts within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been pointless and unsafe due to their persecutors’ alleged close ties and affiliation with the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this assertion. The authorities were aware of the disputed property and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in attempts to address the criminal activities taking place there. Thus, the evidence undermines Petitioners’ claim that their persecutors are so closely connected to the government that intervention would be ineffective or impossible. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners fail to establish that, had the Colombian authorities been informed of the threats made against them, the government would have been either unwilling or unable to offer protection. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (determining that the evidence was lacking to show the government was unable or unwilling to provide protection, particularly where the applicant refrained from contacting police based on the belief that the persecutors were "part of the government").

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have already not met the criteria for asylum, we will nonetheless briefly consider their last contention concerning the practicality and reasonableness of relocating safely elsewhere within Colombia.

The government can overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution, and thereby bar asylum, if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming such fear were established, the evidence in the record sustains the determination that Petitioners could reasonably resettle in another region of Colombia. Petitioners’ family members and relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment within the criminal justice sector. Importantly, Petitioners resided without incident with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota for a period of three weeks before choosing to depart for the United States. Furthermore, the individuals who issued threats to Petitioners merely instructed them to vacate their neighborhood, not to leave the country altogether. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners may sufficiently reduce any risk of future persecution by reasonably and safely relocating to another location within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, the record contains ample evidence to uphold the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they consequently also fail to satisfy the more stringent criteria for withholding of removal. Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, the petition for review is denied. Petitioners contend that relocation within their country is not feasible, asserting that “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (claiming that those family members might harbor political views adverse to the government). However, we cannot grant asylum based on conjectural hardships. Petitioners further argue that internal relocation is impracticable due to administrative and economic constraints, deficiencies in the country’s judicial system, and their persecutors’ connections with the president of Colombia. Nonetheless, these arguments have been insufficiently developed in their briefing, and, in light of the findings discussed above, their assertions are unpersuasive.
