Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

We have jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and hereby DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any purported persecution was connected to a statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian authorities are unwilling or unable to provide protection. Additionally, the evidence does not demonstrate that Petitioners are unable to relocate safely elsewhere within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, together with his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte and their two minor children, are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, they have jointly maintained membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and were supporters of Federico Gutierrez during his campaign for president in 2022. During that period, Petitioners canvassed local neighborhoods door-to-door to solicit support. Around late May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening phone calls once or twice a week pertaining to his political activity with the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates within the Liberal Party were also subjected to threats. In early June 2022, a fellow party member, after reporting threats made against him, was abducted and killed. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted with their campaigning efforts.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and was a law student during 2022, the period when these events occurred. He served as an intern in the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted individuals who were victims of domestic violence, extortion, and other criminal offenses. Acting as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo seeking his assistance with a property conflict. She informed him that her tenant had failed to pay rent for over a year and requested that Jimenez speak with the tenant about both returning the property and making restitution. Jimenez went to the property in an attempt to facilitate a resolution; however, he did not succeed, as the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused any discussion of settlement. The woman elected not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, instead hoping that the mere involvement of a lawyer would persuade the tenant to vacate voluntarily.

Upon reviewing the client’s property title, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the premises was the client’s former spouse. Although the ex-husband had held ownership previously, title to the property was conveyed to the client through a divorce settlement. Jimenez further found that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also referred to as “Don Rafa,” who is a prominent figure leading a group of narco-trafficking guerrillas with political ties to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While at the property, Jimenez noticed that the building’s third floor was secured and closed off by order of the health department. Continued inquiry by Jimenez revealed that authorities had shuttered the property due to its use as a short-term rental for migrants and as a site supporting narco-trafficking operations and prostitution. The location had additionally been linked to incidents involving kidnapping and homicide. Attempts by law enforcement to execute searches and seizures at the property were reportedly unproductive. Jimenez was advised by a local police officer to avoid the premises and refrain from becoming involved.

Later that same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing brochure at his factory, which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez paid no attention to this threat and threw the brochure away. However, in the following month, while he was working at the furniture factory during the night, two armed men confronted him and ordered him to leave the area. One of these individuals accused Jimenez of acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and held him responsible for the property’s searches and seizures. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and, from that point on, took extra precautions by vigilantly supervising their children’s trips to and from school.

Approximately two months following the incident involving the death threat, the identical two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were away. They physically attacked one of the factory's employees and brandished a firearm while demanding information regarding Petitioners’ whereabouts. Once more, the men asserted that Petitioners acted as “snitches for the police” and had political connections. They reiterated their threat to kill Petitioners if they appeared again, specifying that Don Rafa had issued this directive. The employee informed them that Petitioners were traveling, after which the men departed without inflicting further harm.

A few days later, Jimenez saw a man wrap his arm around his younger son's shoulder as the child was leaving school. When the boy tried to get away, the man covered his mouth and instructed him to "run to your dad." Jimenez rushed over, shoved the man, and his son ran to the family car. After this event, the family stayed at Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area of Bogota for three weeks, during which time they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer informed him that nothing could be done and stated that police officers were part of the issue. The officer advised Jimenez that his family should leave. Accordingly, Petitioners followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without authorization by an immigration officer—either through admission or parole—the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded to the accuracy of DHS's factual assertions. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Subsequently, Petitioners submitted applications for asylum and for withholding of removal, designating the minor children as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries associated with Jimenez’s asylum application.

Drawing from the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte as well as other evidence within the record, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners’ applications. Despite determining that the Petitioners were credible, the Immigration Judge found that they did not demonstrate: (1) their experiences qualified as past persecution; (2) a connection to a protected ground under the statute; (3) that internal relocation was not a reasonable option; and (4) that the government of Colombia was unwilling or unable to protect them from those who persecuted them. The Immigration Judge further denied their claims for relief under the CAT. The Petitioners sought review, but the BIA upheld the decision and dismissed the appeal. In addition, the BIA rejected the Petitioners’ arguments that the Immigration Judge had infringed on their right to counsel and thereby violated their due process rights.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA's decision, which rejected their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made mistakes in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) they suffered past persecution; (2) such persecution was based on their political opinion; and (3) they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution which the government is either unwilling or unable to prevent. Petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or the adverse ruling on their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, we possess jurisdiction to examine final removal orders, which may encompass a combined evaluation of asylum applications. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our review of a denial of asylum relief by the Board is restricted to "the administrative record on which the order of removal is based." Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Where a single BIA member has upheld the immigration judge's ruling, our assessment focuses on the Board's decision, but we may also reference the immigration judge's more detailed reasoning underlying the same basis. Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We assess the Board’s factual determinations applying a substantial evidence standard, meaning those findings are deemed conclusive unless the record compels any reasonable adjudicator to reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General has the authority to extend to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for this discretionary relief, an applicant must satisfy the burden of establishing by a preponderance of the evidence that he meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). According to the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who, being outside their country of nationality . . . is either unable or unwilling to return to, and cannot or does not wish to seek the protection of, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)), the court noted that one manner by which an applicant can achieve refugee status is “by demonstrating past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” See Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (internal citation omitted). To establish this, the applicant bears the burden of proving three elements: “(1) the occurrence of one or more incidents constituting persecution; (2) that such persecution was based on one of the grounds specifically protected by statute; and (3) that the persecution was either carried out by the government or by entities or individuals whom the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners claim that, besides the aforementioned threats, they endured physical, psychological, and emotional injuries that constitute past persecution.

Although the INA does not provide a definition of persecution, "we have determined that persecution requires that suffering or harm be imposed on individuals who are distinguished by race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner that is perceived as offensive, and must involve more than mere limitations or threats to one’s life and freedom." Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). "Rather than evaluating each incident separately, we analyze them in totality, since multiple acts taken together may amount to persecution." Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). "The issue of whether a noncitizen has established past private persecution in their country of origin—the principal matter in this case—is a question of fact." Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the repeated threats directed toward them amount to persecution. While threats are taken into account when evaluating the totality of the harm experienced by Petitioners, this Court has previously determined that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, Petitioners admitted during their merits hearing that they did not suffer any physical injury, and likewise acknowledged in their opening brief that they did not experience any enduring physical harm. While it is not necessary for a petitioner to demonstrate physical harm in order to meet the persecution standard, id. at 804, the harm alleged must be egregious and cannot consist only of limitations or isolated threats. See Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask that we take into account the continuing emotional and psychological effects they have suffered. Duarte, for instance, reports difficulties sleeping and resting, and states that her son has developed panic attacks and experiences stress. Although Petitioners have faced difficult situations, the fear caused by the threats they received is not enough to constitute persecution. No matter how physically or psychologically distressing these events may be, our precedent seldom recognizes threats and emotional or psychological suffering, on their own, as sufficient grounds for asylum. See id. (holding that an applicant’s claimed harm—including arrest and detention for 10 hours and threats—did not constitute persecution despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). Furthermore, as Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged during oral argument, our prior decisions have denied asylum even in cases involving more severe forms of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (concluding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” was not persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that being robbed six times and threatened with machetes did not constitute persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Based on these circumstances, we determine that there is substantial evidence to uphold the BIA’s conclusion that, even when considered collectively, the harms alleged by Petitioners do not meet the standard for past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was based on grounds protected by statute, specifically their collective political opinion and their status as a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not presented any substantive argument regarding the definition of their proposed social group or demonstrated their inclusion within such a group. Instead, their nexus argument centers on the claim that Don Rafa persecuted them due to their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is confined to assessing whether Petitioners can show a connection between the claimed persecution and their political opinion. In particular, they allege that Don Rafa and his associates targeted them because of their involvement in campaigning for a candidate opposing the sitting president of Colombia. “To demonstrate persecution on the basis of political opinion, an applicant must show that political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). Thus, a petitioner’s political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to some other motive for harm” or play “a minor role” in their prior mistreatment or anticipated future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is well established that a persecutor may act out of several motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nonetheless, even in mixed-motive scenarios, one protected ground must be “at least one central reason” for the harm. Id. Accordingly, Petitioners must establish that their political opinion was, or would be, at least a central motivation for the persecutors’ actions against them.

Petitioners have not met their burden of proof, as there is considerable evidence supporting the determinations of the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were targeted chiefly because Jimenez posed a threat to Don Rafa's income and safety, rather than due to Petitioners’ political opinions. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners had not been dissuaded from continuing their political activity by threatening phone calls or the death of another campaign worker. Moreover, Petitioners have not identified any evidence within the record implicating Don Rafa in those earlier, politically-motivated threats. Instead, it was only after Jimenez became involved in a property conflict with Don Rafa and Don Rafa learned of Jimenez’s investigation into his rental property that the threats intensified. On one occasion, an individual accused Jimenez of working for the prosecutor's office and held him accountable for searches and seizures conducted at Don Rafa’s property. Later, when Petitioners' employees were threatened by the same individuals, they explicitly stated that they had been sent by Don Rafa.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not make a mistake in concluding that the primary reason for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s association with Don Rafa both professionally and personally, and not from Petitioners’ political opinions. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that any political opinion held by Petitioners played only a minor and secondary role compared to the principal motive for the harm suffered.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to prove past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future, partly because they believe the Colombian government is either incapable of or unwilling to provide them protection. They claim the individuals responsible for the persecution maintain close ties to, and cooperation with, the Colombian government, which, in their view, renders safe internal relocation impossible.

The inquiry into whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “examines evidence concerning a government’s actions in response to an applicant’s past persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. This analysis typically relies on two key points: “(1) whether the government intervened to stop or attempted to halt the persecutors . . ., and (2) whether the government extended protection to the applicant, such as through placing them in protective custody.” Id.

Embedded within this analysis is the question of whether the government had awareness of the claimed persecution and was afforded a chance to respond. Id. at 861–62. “In the absence of such notice, it remains unclear how the police might have responded or whether government assistance would have been provided.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “an essential consideration in the analysis is whether the applicant informed the government of prior persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting may undermine an assertion that the government was powerless or unwilling to address the actions of the persecutors,” this alone is not always determinative. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can still meet the government protection element by establishing that filing a police report would have been pointless or would have subjected the applicant to danger. Id.

There is, once more, considerable evidence in the record backing the determinations of both the BIA and the IJ. The attempts by Petitioners to seek assistance from government authorities were scant to nonexistent. Both Jimenez and his father are, or have been, government employees in Colombia, yet neither of them ever reported misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Although Petitioners indicated in their testimony that they had discussions with two individual police officers who warned them not to involve themselves in trouble, the record lacks any indication that these comments represent the stance of the Colombian government itself. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). Consistent with the IJ’s findings, there is no evidence that Petitioners attempted to seek support outside of their immediate locality or through Jimenez’s professional contacts within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from government authorities would have been both ineffective and hazardous, due to their persecutors' alleged strong ties and affiliation with the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this assertion. The government was informed of the disputed property and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in response to the ongoing criminal activities there. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ claim that their persecutors maintain a close association with the government, or that the authorities would be unwilling or incapable of intervening. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Additionally, Petitioners have not demonstrated that the Colombian government was, or would be, either unable or unwilling to offer protection if notified of the threats against them. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding there was inadequate evidence that the government could not or would not protect the applicant, especially where the applicant chose not to approach police because he believed the persecutors were government-affiliated).

Therefore, we concur that Petitioners are unable to demonstrate a well-founded fear of future persecution necessary for asylum eligibility.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not satisfied the criteria necessary for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining contention concerning whether safe and reasonable internal relocation within Colombia is possible.

The government can overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution, and thereby defeat the asylum claim, if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming such fear exists, the evidence in the record substantiates the determination that they could feasibly relocate to another region within Colombia. Petitioners’ family members and other relatives remain in Colombia without incident, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment in the criminal justice sector. Significantly, Petitioners were able to reside safely with Jimenez’s mother in a rural area near Bogota for three weeks before electing to move to the United States. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that the individuals who issued threats advised Petitioners to leave the neighborhood but did not insist they depart the country. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners have the ability to lessen any risk of future persecution by relocating safely and reasonably within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, the record contains ample evidence to uphold the BIA’s determinations that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they are likewise unable to satisfy the more rigorous criteria needed for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners suggest that internal relocation is not always viable since “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (contending these relatives may also oppose the government politically). Nonetheless, we cannot award asylum on the basis of such speculative difficulties. Petitioners further argue that internal relocation would pose substantial administrative and economic hardships, highlight perceived inadequacies in the country’s judicial system, and emphasize that their alleged persecutors possess connections to the president of Colombia. However, Petitioners provide only cursory discussion of these points, and, considering the analysis set forth above, these arguments are unpersuasive.
