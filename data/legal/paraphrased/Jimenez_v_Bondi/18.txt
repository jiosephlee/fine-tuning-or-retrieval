Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The Petitioners, who are citizens and nationals of Colombia, submitted claims seeking asylum, withholding of removal, and protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were denied by the immigration judge, a decision which was subsequently affirmed by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, Petitioners challenge only the BIA’s denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to our jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any persecution claimed was connected to a statutorily protected ground, or that the government of Colombia is unable or unwilling to provide assistance. Additionally, the record does not support Petitioners’ contention that they are unable to safely relocate elsewhere in Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, along with his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte and their two minor children, originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and was employed at a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, all members of the family have participated in the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez during his campaign for the Colombian presidency in 2022. Petitioners canvassed local neighborhoods, soliciting support door-to-door. Beginning near the end of May 2022, Jimenez began to receive one or two threatening phone calls per week linked to his affiliation with the Liberal Party. Other party leaders and associates faced similar threats. In June 2022, despite having notified authorities of the intimidation, one of their colleagues was abducted and murdered. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their political campaigning.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and was enrolled as a law student in 2022, when the pertinent events occurred. He also served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted victims of crimes such as domestic violence, extortion, and other offenses. Acting as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo seeking assistance with a dispute over her property. She reported that her tenant had not paid rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez contact the tenant to discuss repayment and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to resolve the matter by visiting the property and seeking to negotiate a settlement, but his efforts were unsuccessful; the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and declined any negotiation. The client chose not to initiate a formal eviction process and instead wished for the tenant to relinquish the property upon realizing a lawyer was involved.

Upon reviewing the title to the client's property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was actually the client's former spouse. While the ex-husband had once held ownership of the property, ownership was transferred to the client through a divorce settlement. Jimenez further found out that the tenant’s name was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, who is also known as “Don Rafa,” and is recognized as a leader among narco-trafficking guerrillas with political affiliations to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While at the property, Jimenez noticed that the health department had cordoned off the third floor. After additional investigation, Jimenez found that authorities had shut down the property because it was being used for daily rentals to migrants as well as functioning as a hub for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The location was also connected to allegations of kidnapping and murder. Multiple attempts by law enforcement to conduct searches and seizures on the property had apparently not succeeded. A local police officer advised Jimenez to avoid the property and to refrain from getting involved.

Later in the same month, Jimenez found a menacing pamphlet at his factory that read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” He ignored the warning and threw away the pamphlet. However, the following month, as he was working a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals approached Jimenez, threatened him with firearms, and ordered him to vacate the area. One of them accused Jimenez of acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures conducted at the location. After these individuals departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began vigilantly overseeing their children’s travel to and from school.

Approximately two months following the previous death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were absent from town. They attacked one of the factory's employees and, brandishing a firearm, insisted on learning Petitioners' whereabouts. Once more, the men accused Petitioners of being "snitches for the police" who were involved in politics. They reiterated their threat to kill Petitioners should they reappear, specifying that this directive originated from Don Rafa. The employee informed them that Petitioners were away traveling, after which the men departed without inflicting further harm.

A few days after, Jimenez observed a man placing his arm around his younger son's shoulder as the child was leaving school. When the boy attempted to break free, the man covered the boy’s mouth and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried to his son’s side and pushed the man away, allowing the son to run to Jimenez’s car. In response to the incident, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for three weeks, during which they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to departing, Jimenez informed a police patrol officer about the events that had occurred. The officer replied that nothing could be done and explained that members of the police force were part of the issue. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave. As a result, Petitioners left.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without official admission or parole from an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded to the factual statements made by DHS. The Immigration Judge deemed them inadmissible and ordered removal to Colombia. Petitioners sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children participating as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Drawing upon the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with other evidence contained in the record, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ found Petitioners credible, the IJ determined that they had not demonstrated: (1) that the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a protected statutory ground; (3) that internal relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option; and (4) that the Colombian authorities were either unable or unwilling to offer protection from their persecutors. Additionally, the IJ denied their requests for relief under the Convention Against Torture. Petitioners subsequently appealed, but the Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA likewise rejected Petitioners’ arguments that their right to counsel had been violated and found no due process violation by the IJ.

II. Discussion

The petitioners challenge the BIA’s order rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. Their contention is that the BIA erred in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was on the basis of their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution, as well as that the government would be unable or unwilling to protect them. The petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or their claim of a due process violation.

A. Standard of Review

Ordinarily, our authority encompasses the review of final removal orders, which may involve a combined assessment of asylum applications. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). The scope of our examination of the Board’s denial of asylum is confined to "the administrative record on which the order of removal is based." Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). When a single BIA member upholds the immigration judge’s ruling, our review targets the BIA’s decision itself, though "we may look to the IJ’s more thorough reasoning addressing the identical issues." Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

The Board’s factual determinations are reviewed according to the substantial-evidence standard, under which such findings are deemed final unless the evidence compels any reasonable adjudicator to reach the opposite conclusion. See Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a form of relief that the Attorney General has the discretion to extend to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). An individual seeking a discretionary asylum grant must establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he qualifies as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). The term “refugee” under the INA refers to any individual who, being outside their country of nationality, is either unable or unwilling to return to that country and unable or unwilling to seek its protection due to past persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)), it is explained that one method by which an applicant can be granted refugee status is by demonstrating past persecution, which establishes a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. As stated in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted), the applicant bears the burden of establishing three requirements: "(1) an occurrence, or occurrences, amounting to persecution; (2) that occurred due to one of the protected grounds listed in the statute; and (3) was carried out either by the government itself or by entities the government cannot or will not restrain." Id. (citing Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

Although the INA does not provide a definition of persecution, “we have determined that persecution involves the imposition of suffering or harm on individuals who are distinguished by race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner considered objectionable and that persecution must involve more than mere limitations or threats to one’s life or liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Additionally, “rather than examining each event separately, we assess the events in their entirety because the aggregated impact of several occurrences may rise to the level of persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). “Determining whether a noncitizen has demonstrated prior private persecution in their native country—which is the principal matter in this case—is a factual question.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the series of threats directed at them amount to persecution. While we take threats into account when evaluating the total effect of Petitioners’ mistreatment, our precedent states that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Notably, Petitioners admitted during their merits hearing that they did not suffer any physical injury, and acknowledged in their opening brief that they did not sustain any enduring physical harm. While physical injury is not a prerequisite for meeting the persecution standard, id. at 804, the harm endured must be severe, rising above simple restrictions or occasional threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask us to take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological effects they have suffered. Duarte, as an illustration, claims she is unable to sleep or relax, and that her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and heightened stress. Although Petitioners have faced regrettable situations, apprehensions stemming from threats they received do not meet the standard for persecution. Even if certain incidents have been physically taxing or caused psychological disturbance, our court has seldom, if ever, granted asylum solely on the basis of threats combined with emotional or psychological suffering. See id. (concluding that harm claimed by an applicant—such as a ten-hour detention and threats—failed to constitute persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). During oral argument, Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged that our precedent has denied asylum in matters with even more severe patterns of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (determining that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not reach the threshold for persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that “threats with machetes and being robbed six times” was inadequate for persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Considering all of these circumstances, we hold that the record amply supports the BIA’s determination that, even when considered collectively, the harm experienced by Petitioners does not satisfy the definition of past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners contend that the harm they experienced was due to grounds protected by statute, specifically their collective political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners did not present any substantive arguments concerning the definition of their particular social group, nor did they demonstrate that they belong to such a group. Instead, Petitioners’ nexus argument centers solely on alleged persecution by Don Rafa motivated by their political opinion. As a result, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners can show a sufficient connection between their asserted persecution and their political beliefs. Petitioners claim they suffered harm from Don Rafa and his associates as a consequence of their involvement in the campaign of an opponent to Colombia’s current president. “To establish persecution on the basis of political opinion, the applicant must show that political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, Petitioners’ political opinion must not be merely incidental, subordinate, tangential, or superficial in relation to other motivations for the harm, nor may it play only a minor role in their past persecution or in the risk of future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is acknowledged that a persecutor might act based on multiple motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even in situations where mixed motives are present, at least one of the protected grounds must serve as a central reason for the persecution. Id. Therefore, Petitioners have the burden of proving that their political opinion was, or would be, at least one central motive for the actions taken or to be taken against them by the persecutors.

Petitioners do not meet this requirement because ample evidence supports the findings of the BIA and IJ that the primary reason Petitioners were targeted was Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than their political beliefs. Before Jimenez’s confrontation with Don Rafa, Petitioners continued their political involvement despite menacing phone calls and the fatality of a fellow campaign participant. Petitioners have not cited any evidence in the record showing Don Rafa’s involvement in those earlier threats associated with politics. In reality, Petitioners only began facing increased threats after Jimenez became entangled in a property conflict with Don Rafa and after Don Rafa learned that Jimenez was investigating the property he rented. On one occasion, an individual threatening Jimenez accused him of being connected to the prosecutor’s office and responsible for searches at Don Rafa’s property. Furthermore, when these same individuals later threatened the Petitioners’ employees, they indicated they were acting on Don Rafa’s behalf.

Given these circumstances, the BIA acted within its discretion in concluding that the chief impetus for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s business and personal dealings with Don Rafa, rather than Petitioners’ political opinions. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even in the absence of proof of past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future, partly due to the alleged incapacity or unwillingness of the Colombian government to provide protection. They claim that those responsible for the persecution are closely connected with the Colombian government, thereby making internal relocation unsafe.

The "unable-or-unwilling" inquiry examines evidence regarding how the government responded to any previous persecution faced by the applicant. Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In assessing a government's response to persecution, two categories of information are particularly significant: (1) whether authorities intervened or made efforts to halt the persecutors, and (2) whether the government took measures to protect the applicant, such as placing the individual in protective custody. Id.

Part of this evaluation involves determining if the government was aware of the purported persecution and had a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. The court noted, “Without such notice, it is impossible to ascertain how authorities would have responded or whether governmental assistance would have been offered.” Id. (altered citation). Thus, “an essential consideration in this analysis is whether the applicant notified the government of prior persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting the persecution can weaken an argument that the government was either unwilling or unable to protect against the persecutors,” this omission does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, the applicant may still meet the governmental protection criterion by showing that reporting to police would have been ineffective or posed a significant risk. Id.

There is ample evidence in the record to uphold the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. Petitioners made only minimal attempts, if any, to seek government assistance. Both Jimenez and his father are, or have been, employed by the Colombian government. Nevertheless, neither chose to report any misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners stated that they spoke with two individual police officers, who advised them to avoid trouble; however, no evidence suggests that this reflects the official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ also noted that nothing in the record demonstrates that Petitioners pursued assistance beyond their local area or by utilizing Jimenez’s relationships in law enforcement.

Petitioners suggest that seeking assistance from the government would have been both ineffective and risky, as their alleged persecutors were purportedly closely connected to the Colombian authorities. However, the record does not support this claim. The Colombian government was cognizant of the contested property and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in an effort to address the criminal activity occurring there. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors were in close association with the government and that authorities would either be unwilling or incapable of intervening. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners are unable to establish that the Colombian authorities were, or would have been, unable or unwilling to provide protection if they had been informed of the threats made against Petitioners. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding that there was not enough evidence to demonstrate the government was unable or unwilling to offer protection, especially where the applicant chose not to contact police based on the belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not met the criteria necessary for asylum, we turn briefly to their remaining contention concerning whether it would be practical and reasonable for them to relocate safely elsewhere within Colombia.

The government can overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thereby defeat the asylum claim—by proving, through a preponderance of the evidence, either: “(1) a ‘fundamental change’ in the relevant circumstances has occurred, eliminating the applicant’s well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to a different part of his or her country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming such fear exists, the evidence in the record substantiates the conclusion that reasonable internal relocation within Colombia is feasible. Petitioners’ family members and relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment with the criminal justice system there. Significantly, Petitioners resided without incident with Jimenez’s mother in a rural area near Bogota for three weeks prior to their decision to move to the United States. Additionally, it is important to note that the individuals who threatened Petitioners merely instructed them to leave the neighborhood and did not demand their departure from the country as a whole. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners have the ability to alleviate any risk of future persecution by relocating safely and reasonably to another region within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA's determinations that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they also do not satisfy the more stringent criteria necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we reject the petition for review. Petitioners contend that relocation is not feasible because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (noting these relatives might themselves hold political views contrary to the government). Nevertheless, asylum cannot be granted based upon such speculative difficulties. Petitioners further claim that internal relocation is impractical due to administrative and economic hardships, inadequacies in the country's judicial system, and because their alleged persecutors have connections to the president of Colombia. However, these arguments are raised only in a cursory manner and, in light of our preceding analysis, do not suffice to alter the outcome.
