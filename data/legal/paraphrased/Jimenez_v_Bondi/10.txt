Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Petitioners, who are citizens and nationals of Colombia, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were denied by the immigration judge, and this denial was subsequently affirmed by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, Petitioners contest only the Board's denial of their claims for asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any claimed persecution was based on a protected ground as defined by statute, or that the Colombian authorities are either unable or unwilling to offer them protection. Furthermore, the evidence does not support the assertion that Petitioners are unable to relocate to another part of Colombia safely.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to their move to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, they have all been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez during the 2022 Colombian presidential campaign. Petitioners actively canvassed local communities, soliciting votes in favor of their candidate. Starting in late May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening phone calls on a weekly or biweekly basis in connection with his role in the Liberal Party. Similar threats were directed at other leaders and members of the Liberal Party. In early June 2022, despite reporting previous threats, a fellow colleague was abducted and killed. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their campaigning activities.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and was a law student in 2022 when the events in question occurred. During this period, he also served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted victims of crimes such as domestic violence and extortion. Acting in his provisional legal role, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo who sought his assistance with a dispute regarding her property. She informed Jimenez that the tenant residing on her property had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested that he speak to the tenant about reimbursing her and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to reach a resolution by visiting the property, but his efforts were unsuccessful; the tenant refused to negotiate and told Jimenez not to “look for trouble.” Ultimately, Jimenez’s client chose not to move forward with formal eviction proceedings, preferring instead that the tenant would relinquish the property after learning that an attorney had become involved.

Upon reviewing the title for the client’s property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was actually the client’s former spouse. While the ex-husband had been the prior owner, ownership was transferred to the client as part of their divorce proceedings. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also referred to as “Don Rafa,” who is recognized as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization with political ties to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While at the property, Jimenez noted that the third floor had been closed off by order of the health department. Continuing his investigation, Jimenez found out that the property was shuttered because it was operating both as a day-rental facility for migrants and as a hub for narco-trafficking and prostitution activities. Additionally, the location had been connected to reports involving kidnapping and homicide. Efforts by law enforcement to search and seize property had apparently not succeeded. A nearby police officer advised Jimenez to keep away from the premises and to avoid entangling himself in related matters.

Later in that same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory bearing the message, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez chose to ignore this threat and threw the brochure away. However, the following month, while he was working at the furniture factory during nighttime hours, Jimenez was approached by two armed individuals. These men threatened him at gunpoint, instructing him to leave the area. One accused Jimenez of acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures conducted at the location. After the individuals departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and subsequently took extra precautions by vigilantly overseeing their children’s travels to and from school.

Approximately two months following the prior death threat, the same individuals returned to the furniture factory when Petitioners were not present in town. On this occasion, they attacked an employee at the factory and brandished a firearm while inquiring about Petitioners’ whereabouts. Once more, the men remarked that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They reiterated their intention to kill Petitioners upon sight, asserting that this directive originated from Don Rafa. The employee informed them that Petitioners were away traveling, after which the men departed without further harming anyone.

Several days after, Jimenez observed a man wrap his arm around his younger son’s shoulders while the child was leaving school. The individual covered the boy’s mouth as the latter attempted to break free and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez then rushed to his son’s aid, shoved the man aside, and his son ran to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area outside Bogota for a period of three weeks, during which time they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer responded that nothing could be done and added that law enforcement officials themselves were contributing to the issue. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave the area. Petitioners complied with this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without admission or parole by an immigration official, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. Each Notice alleged that the Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded to the factual allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge found Petitioners inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children proceeding as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte along with other evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge denied Petitioners’ applications. Despite finding the Petitioners credible, the IJ determined that they did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm suffered constituted past persecution; (2) that there was a nexus to a statutorily protected ground; (3) that internal relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option; or (4) that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to offer protection against their alleged persecutors. The IJ further denied Petitioners’ applications for relief under the Convention Against Torture (CAT). Petitioners subsequently appealed, but the Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also rejected Petitioners’ assertions that the IJ had infringed upon their right to counsel and denied their due process claims.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s order rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in concluding that they failed to prove: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was motivated by their political opinion; and (3) that they have a well-founded fear of future persecution and that the government would be unable or unwilling to protect them. Petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or the dismissal of their due process argument.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, our authority extends to reviewing final removal orders, which may encompass a combined assessment of asylum applications. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our examination of the Board’s rejection of asylum is restricted to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Given that a single BIA member endorsed the IJ’s ruling, our review focuses on the BIA decision, yet “we are not prevented from considering the IJ’s more detailed rationale regarding the same issues.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

Our review of factual determinations by the BIA is governed by the substantial-evidence standard. In applying this approach, we treat the BIA's factual findings as conclusive unless the record demonstrates that no reasonable factfinder could agree with them. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation altered).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may extend to noncitizens who satisfy eligibility requirements.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for asylum as a matter of discretion, the applicant must establish by a preponderance of the evidence that he qualifies as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). According to the INA, a refugee is defined as any individual who is outside their country of nationality and who is either unable or unwilling to return to, or to avail themselves of the protection of, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant may demonstrate eligibility for refugee status by establishing past persecution, which in turn creates a rebuttable presumption that they have a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To do so, the applicant is required to establish three elements: “(1) conduct or occurrences that constitute persecution; (2) a nexus to one of the protected statutory grounds; and (3) that such acts were perpetrated by the government or by entities that the government is unable or unwilling to restrain.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

Although the INA does not provide a definition for persecution, “we have determined that persecution involves the imposition of suffering or harm on individuals who differ based on race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner deemed offensive, and requires more than mere limitations or threats to life and liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). “Rather than evaluating each episode independently, we assess them as a whole, since the aggregate impact of multiple events may rise to the level of persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). “Determining whether a noncitizen has demonstrated past private persecution in their country of origin—the principal issue in this matter—is a question of fact.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the several threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are relevant in evaluating the totality of mistreatment experienced by Petitioners, this court has determined that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Notably, Petitioners acknowledged during their merits hearing that they had not suffered physical harm, and likewise stated in their opening brief that no enduring physical injury occurred. Although physical injury is not required to establish persecution, id. at 804, the harm must nevertheless be egregious and exceed simple restrictions or occasional threats. Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners request that we take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological impacts they have experienced. For instance, Duarte reports suffering from sleeplessness and inability to rest, while her son has developed panic attacks and experiences stress. Although Petitioners have encountered difficult and distressing experiences, the fear caused by the threats they received does not, by itself, meet the threshold for persecution. Events that are physically or mentally distressing do not typically provide sufficient grounds for granting asylum solely based on threats and resulting emotional or psychological harm. See id. (concluding that the claimed harm—including a ten-hour arrest and detention along with threats—did not rise to the level of persecution, even though such experiences were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling"). Furthermore, at oral argument, Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged that our precedents have denied asylum where the underlying harm was even more severe. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” was not persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (concluding that threats with machetes and being robbed six times did not constitute persecution as “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Based on this record, we conclude that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s determination that the cumulative harm alleged by Petitioners does not satisfy the standard for past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced arose from grounds protected by statute—specifically, their common political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not presented any arguments regarding the formation of their claimed social group, nor have they shown that they belong to such a group. Instead, when addressing the nexus requirement, Petitioners claim that Don Rafa persecuted them due to their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners can show a nexus between their purported persecution and their political beliefs. Petitioners claim that Don Rafa and his associates harmed them because they campaigned for a political rival of Colombia’s current president. “To demonstrate persecution based on a political opinion, the applicant must prove that the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, the political opinion in question “must not be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to some other motive for the harm” and must not play “a minor role” in either the harm suffered or the risk of future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). Naturally, it is possible for a persecutor to act based on several motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, in these mixed-motive scenarios, at least one protected ground must constitute a central reason for the harm imposed by the persecutor. Id. Therefore, here, Petitioners are required to prove that their political opinion was or would be at least one central motivation for the actions of their persecutors.

Petitioners have not demonstrated the necessary requirement because substantial evidence underpins the findings of both the BIA and the IJ that Petitioners were mainly targeted due to Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than because of their political beliefs. Before Jimenez's conflict with Don Rafa, the Petitioners disregarded menacing phone calls and the killing of a fellow campaign worker, continuing their political engagements undeterred. Petitioners do not identify any record evidence connecting Don Rafa to those earlier threats tied to political activity. Rather, heightened threats only commenced after Jimenez became embroiled in a property dispute with Don Rafa, and following Don Rafa’s realization that Jimenez was probing into his rental property. In one particular incident, an individual accused Jimenez of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and being accountable for law enforcement searches at Don Rafa’s premises. Subsequently, when threats were made against Petitioners’ employees, the perpetrators explicitly stated they had been sent by Don Rafa.

Given these circumstances, the BIA was correct in concluding that Jimenez’s occupational and personal dealings with Don Rafa, as opposed to Petitioners' political opinions, were the primary motivations for the threats. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, regardless of their inability to demonstrate past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future, partly due to the Colombian government's incapacity or unwillingness to provide protection. They claim that the perpetrators maintain significant alignment and association with the Colombian government, thereby rendering safe internal relocation unfeasible.

The “unable-or-unwilling” inquiry focuses on “evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In assessing this response, “two types of information are especially significant: (1) whether the authorities intervened to halt or attempted to halt the actions of the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the applicant was offered protection by the government, such as by being placed in protective custody.” Id.

A related aspect of this analysis involves determining if the government was aware of the claimed persecution and had the chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. As stated, “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “an important consideration in the analysis is whether an applicant reported prior persecution to the government.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting to authorities can weaken an argument that the government was unable or unwilling to prevent the persecutors,” such a failure does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can meet the government protection standard by proving that submitting a police report would have been pointless or unsafe. Id.

There is, once more, considerable evidence in the record that upholds the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made little, if any, attempt to seek assistance from government authorities. Despite Jimenez and his father having been or presently being employed by the Colombian government, they did not report any instances of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). According to their testimony, Petitioners spoke with two individual police officers who warned them to avoid making trouble. However, there is no evidence indicating that these comments represent the official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As found by the IJ, there is no indication in the record that Petitioners sought government assistance beyond their immediate local area or took advantage of Jimenez’s contacts within law enforcement.

Petitioners contend that seeking governmental assistance would have been both ineffective and unsafe due to their persecutors’ alleged strong ties to the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this assertion. The authorities were aware of the property in question and had initiated multiple search and seizure operations to curb the criminal activity occurring there. Thus, the available evidence undermines Petitioners’ claim that their persecutors are so closely connected with the government that the authorities would not be able or willing to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners fail to demonstrate that the Colombian government was or would have been incapable or unwilling to protect them had it been informed of the threats. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding that there was a lack of sufficient evidence establishing the government's inability or unwillingness to provide protection, especially where the applicant chose not to approach law enforcement due to his belief that the persecutors were associated with the government).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not satisfied the standards required for asylum, we will briefly consider their remaining contention concerning whether safe and reasonable relocation within Colombia is possible.

The government is permitted to overcome an applicant’s presumption of possessing a well-founded fear of future persecution and thereby defeat the asylum request if it can establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; nonetheless, even assuming arguendo that such a fear existed, the evidence of record substantiates the determination that Petitioners have the option of reasonably relocating elsewhere within Colombia. Petitioners’ family members, along with their relatives, remain in Colombia without having suffered harm, and Jimenez’s father maintains employment within the criminal justice sector. Additionally, Petitioners resided without incident for three weeks with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota before ultimately choosing to depart for the United States. It is also noteworthy that the individuals who issued threats directed Petitioners to vacate their neighborhood rather than compelling their departure from the country altogether. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners have the means to avoid any prospective persecution by relocating safely and reasonably to another region of Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, considerable evidence backs the BIA’s determinations that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they inevitably do not satisfy the more stringent criteria for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that internal relocation is not practical because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (contending that those family members could also harbor political opposition to the government). However, we are unable to grant asylum on the basis of speculative difficulties. Petitioners further claim that internal relocation poses unworkable challenges due to bureaucratic and financial obstacles, deficiencies in the judicial system, and the persecutors’ connections with Colombia’s president. Nevertheless, these assertions are only superficially addressed in their briefing and, for the reasons discussed above, are unpersuasive.
