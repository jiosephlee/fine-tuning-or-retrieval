Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Petitioners, who are citizens and nationals of Colombia, sought relief by applying for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge rejected their requests, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld that decision. On appeal, Petitioners challenge solely the BIA’s determinations regarding asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to our jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any such persecution was connected to a ground protected by statute, or that the Colombian authorities are unable or unwilling to offer assistance. Additionally, the record does not support Petitioners’ claim that internal relocation within Colombia would be unsafe for them.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young children originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, they have been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez, a candidate in Colombia’s 2022 presidential race. During the campaign, Petitioners canvassed neighborhoods, seeking support for their preferred candidate. Around late May 2022, Jimenez began receiving weekly, and sometimes biweekly, threatening phone calls related to his Liberal Party activities. Threats were also directed at other leaders and members of the Liberal Party. In early June 2022, despite having alerted authorities about prior threats, one colleague was abducted and killed. Petitioners nevertheless persisted in their political campaigning.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and was a law student during 2022, the period in which the events at issue occurred. Additionally, he served as an intern with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted victims of crimes such as domestic violence, extortion, and others. Acting in his provisional legal capacity, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo who sought assistance with a property-related conflict. She informed Jimenez that she had not received rent payments from her property’s tenant for more than a year and requested that he contact the tenant regarding restitution and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to mediate by visiting the property and negotiating a resolution, but his efforts were unsuccessful—the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and declined to engage in negotiations. The client opted not to pursue formal eviction proceedings, expressing hope that the tenant would relinquish the property after becoming aware of the involvement of a lawyer.

Upon reviewing the client's property title, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the premises was actually the client's former husband. While the ex-husband had been the previous owner, the property was awarded to the client through a divorce settlement. Jimenez further identified the tenant as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, who is also referred to as “Don Rafa,” and recognized as a prominent figure leading a faction of narco-trafficking guerrillas with political ties to Colombia's current president, Gustavo Petro.

When Jimenez inspected the property, he noted that the health department had cordoned off the third floor. His further inquiries revealed that the authorities had closed the building due to its operation as a day-rental location for migrants, and as a center for narco-trafficking activities and prostitution. Additionally, the property had been linked to reports involving kidnapping and homicide. Although law enforcement had carried out several search and seizure attempts, these efforts appeared to have been unsuccessful. A police officer in the area advised Jimenez to avoid the property and refrain from becoming involved in any related disputes.

Later in the same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing pamphlet at his workplace which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez paid no attention to the threat and threw away the pamphlet. However, the following month, while he was working a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted Jimenez at gunpoint and ordered him to move out of the area. One of these individuals accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures that had taken place at the premises. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and, from that point forward, began to closely supervise their children’s journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were away from town. On this occasion, they attacked an employee at the factory and brandished a firearm while demanding the whereabouts of Petitioners. Once again, the men accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They reiterated their intent to kill Petitioners if they were encountered again, specifying that the directive originated from Don Rafa. The factory employee informed them that Petitioners were traveling, after which the men departed without causing further harm.

Several days after the previous incident, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around the shoulders of his younger son as the boy was leaving school. The individual covered the boy's mouth as he attempted to get away, instructing him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried to his son and shoved the individual, allowing the boy to run toward Jimenez’s car. Following this event, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area of Bogota for three weeks; during this time, they decided to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the events to a police patrol officer. The officer responded that no action could be taken and that law enforcement officers themselves were part of the problem. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without lawful admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued separate Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners acknowledged the accuracy of DHS’s factual allegations. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children proceeding as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with other evidence presented, the Immigration Judge denied Petitioners’ applications. Despite determining that Petitioners were credible witnesses, the IJ held that Petitioners did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered constituted past persecution; (2) a connection to a protected statutory ground; (3) that internal relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option; or (4) that the Colombian authorities were unable or unwilling to offer them protection from their alleged persecutors. The IJ further denied their claims for relief under the CAT. Petitioners subsequently appealed, but the BIA upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. Additionally, the BIA rejected Petitioners’ assertions that the IJ had infringed upon their right to counsel, finding no due process violation.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s order rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA incorrectly determined that they failed to demonstrate: (1) they suffered past persecution; (2) such persecution was based on their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution where the government would be unable or unwilling to offer protection. Petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or the rejection of their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Our jurisdiction typically extends to reviewing final orders of removal, which may encompass a consolidated evaluation of asylum petitions. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our examination of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is restricted to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since a single BIA member affirmed the immigration judge’s ruling, our review is directed at the BIA’s determination, though we “may also look to the IJ’s more extensive reasoning on those same issues.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We assess the BIA’s factual determinations pursuant to the substantial-evidence standard. This standard dictates that the BIA’s factual findings are binding unless the evidence in the record would obligate any reasonable adjudicator to reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation altered).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General has the authority to grant to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for this discretionary relief, the applicant must establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). The INA characterizes a refugee as: any individual who is located outside the country of his or her nationality . . . who cannot or does not wish to return to, and cannot or does not wish to seek the protection of, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant can qualify for refugee status by demonstrating past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To do so, the applicant must establish three requirements: “(1) the occurrence of one or more incidents that constitute persecution; (2) that such persecution is on account of a statutorily protected ground; and (3) that the acts were carried out by the government or by entities the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not provide a definition for persecution, yet “we have determined that establishing persecution necessitates that suffering or harm be imposed on individuals who are different (due to race, religion, or political opinion) in a manner considered objectionable and must involve more than mere limitations or threats to personal liberty or life.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). “Our analysis does not treat each event in isolation; rather, we evaluate them as a whole, since the aggregate impact of several events may rise to the level of persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). “The question of whether an individual has demonstrated prior private persecution in their country of origin—which is the primary issue here—is a factual matter.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the numerous threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are relevant in evaluating the totality of mistreatment experienced by Petitioners, this court has previously determined that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, during the merits hearing, Petitioners admitted they did not suffer any physical harm, and their opening brief similarly acknowledges the absence of any lasting physical injury. Although physical harm is not a prerequisite for establishing persecution, id. at 804, the harm endured must be egregious and exceed mere limitations or isolated threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners also request that we take into account the enduring emotional and psychological impacts they have experienced. Duarte, for instance, asserts that she suffers from an inability to sleep or find rest, and that her son has begun to experience panic attacks and stress. Although the Petitioners have undoubtedly faced difficult situations, the apprehensions resulting from the threats they received do not meet the threshold for persecution. Regardless of how physically taxing or psychologically disturbing certain incidents might be, our precedent rarely—if ever—recognizes asylum claims grounded solely on threats or emotional and psychological distress. See id. (holding that alleged harms, such as a 10-hour arrest and detention coupled with threats, did not constitute persecution, even though they were described as “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged during oral argument, our precedent has denied asylum in cases featuring more egregious instances of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (determining that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not amount to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that threats involving machetes and six robberies fell short of persecution since “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Taking these facts and the surrounding circumstances into account, we conclude that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s determination that the harm suffered by Petitioners, even when considered collectively, does not constitute past persecution under the definition set forth in the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to reasons protected under statute—specifically, their collective political opinion and their membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners did not present arguments addressing the existence of their claimed particular social group nor did they show that they belong to such a group. Instead, their nexus argument is premised on the assertion that Don Rafa persecuted them on account of their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners have demonstrated a nexus between the alleged persecution and their political opinion. In particular, Petitioners claim that Don Rafa and his associates harmed them because they participated in the campaign of a political opponent to the current president of Colombia. “To show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, the political opinion held by Petitioners must not be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” nor may it “play a minor role” in the harm previously suffered or feared in the future. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is understood that a persecutor may act based on several motivations. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even when multiple motives are present, one protected ground must constitute a central reason for the persecution. Id. Accordingly, Petitioners must show that their political opinion was, or will be, at least one central reason their persecutors targeted or will target them.

Petitioners have not met their burden to show otherwise, as ample evidence exists to uphold the BIA and IJ’s determination that Petitioners were chiefly targeted due to Jimenez’s impact on Don Rafa’s financial interests and security, rather than on account of their political beliefs. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners persisted in their political involvement despite receiving threatening calls and after the death of a fellow campaign participant. Petitioners also fail to cite any evidence in the record indicating that Don Rafa was responsible for those early threats associated with political activity. The escalation of threats only occurred after Jimenez became entangled in a property conflict with Don Rafa and after Don Rafa learned Jimenez was probing into his rental property. During one such incident, an individual accused Jimenez of working with the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches and seizures on Don Rafa’s premises. Later, when threats were directed at Petitioners’ employees, the perpetrators stated they were sent by Don Rafa.

Given these circumstances, the BIA correctly determined that the central reason for the threats related to Jimenez’s professional and personal disputes with Don Rafa, not Petitioners’ political views. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even in the absence of demonstrating past persecution, they nonetheless possess a well-founded fear of persecution in the future, partly due to the Colombian government’s alleged inability or unwillingness to provide protection. They maintain that the individuals responsible for their persecution have significant connections and affiliations with the Colombian government, which, according to petitioners, precludes the possibility of safe internal relocation.

The “unable or unwilling” inquiry evaluates how a government responded to the alleged past persecution experienced by the applicant. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. The analysis gives substantial weight to two categories of evidence regarding the government's response: (1) whether the authorities intervened to stop or made efforts to stop the persecutors, and (2) whether the government provided protection to the applicant, including measures such as placing the applicant in protective custody. Id.

An integral aspect of this analysis involves determining if the government was aware of the claimed persecution and had a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. As noted, “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” such a failure does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, the applicant may still meet the government protection requirement by establishing that attempting to report to the police would have been futile or put the applicant at risk. Id.

Once more, the record contains ample evidence that underpins the conclusions reached by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners made little to no genuine attempt to solicit assistance from the authorities. Both Jimenez and his father have past or present employment with the Colombian government; despite this, neither took steps to report any misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners indicated in their testimony that they spoke with two police officers who advised them to avoid causing trouble, but there is no evidence indicating that this view represents the stance of the Colombian government as a whole. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). Consistent with the IJ’s determination, the record lacks evidence that Petitioners sought governmental assistance beyond their immediate locality or leveraged Jimenez’s connections within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both pointless and hazardous due to the close connection between their persecutors and the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this claim. The authorities were already aware of the disputed property and had undertaken multiple searches and seizures to address the criminal activities taking place. Thus, the evidence actually undermines Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors were closely linked with the government and that the authorities would have been unable or unwilling to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners are unable to demonstrate that the Colombian government was, or would have been, unable or unwilling to protect them if informed about the threats. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding that there was not enough evidence to conclude that the government was unable or unwilling to protect the applicant, especially since the applicant chose not to contact law enforcement based on a belief that the persecutors were affiliated with the government).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have already not met the necessary criteria for asylum, we will briefly consider their remaining contention about whether internal relocation within Colombia is both possible and reasonable.

The government may overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thereby defeat the asylum claim—if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence either that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming they had done so, the evidence supports the conclusion that reasonable internal relocation within Colombia is available to them. Petitioners’ family members and relatives remain unharmed in Colombia, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment in the criminal justice system. Additionally, Petitioners lived without incident with Jimenez’s mother in the rural area outside Bogota for three weeks prior to their decision to move to the United States. It is also significant that the individuals who threatened Petitioners instructed them only to leave the neighborhood, rather than to depart the country altogether. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners can avoid any risk of future persecution by relocating to a different region within Colombia where it would be both safe and reasonable for them to reside.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence underpins the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they are also unable to satisfy the more stringent criteria for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners argue that return is not feasible because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (noting that those relatives might also hold political views opposed to the government). However, asylum cannot be granted based on such speculative adversities. Petitioners further contend that relocation would be impractical due to bureaucratic and economic obstacles, deficiencies in the country’s judicial system, and because their persecutors are connected to Colombia’s president. Yet, these assertions are only superficially addressed in their briefing, and, in light of our conclusions above, do not persuade the Court.
