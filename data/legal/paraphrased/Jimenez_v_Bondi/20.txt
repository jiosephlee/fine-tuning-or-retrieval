Title: Jimenez v. Bondi  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit  
Date: October 7, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-9573  
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, submitted requests for asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. Their requests were rejected by the immigration judge, and that decision was upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, petitioners challenge only the BIA’s rejection of their asylum and withholding of removal applications.

Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we have jurisdiction and accordingly DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not shown they experienced persecution, that any harm was connected to a protected statutory ground, or that Colombian authorities are either unable or unwilling to offer them protection. Furthermore, the evidence does not establish that petitioners would be unable to safely relocate to another region within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to their move to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, they have collectively been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and lent their support to Federico Gutierrez during his 2022 campaign for the presidency of Colombia. During that period, Petitioners engaged in canvassing activities, soliciting votes in residential neighborhoods. Beginning at the end of May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls, typically once or twice per week, concerning his participation in the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates within the Liberal Party similarly received threats. In June 2022, one colleague, despite having previously reported threats made against him, was kidnapped and killed. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their campaign efforts.

Jimenez held a provisional license to practice law and, during 2022 when the incidents in question occurred, he was a law student. Additionally, he served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where his work included assisting victims of crimes such as domestic violence, extortion, and others. Acting in his capacity as a provisional attorney, he was approached by a woman from San Bernardo in June 2022, who sought assistance regarding a dispute over property. She informed Jimenez that her tenant had failed to pay rent for more than a year and asked him to discuss with the tenant the issues of repayment and vacating the property. Jimenez subsequently went to the property to attempt to negotiate a resolution, but his efforts were unsuccessful—the tenant told Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused to engage in negotiations. The client opted not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, instead expressing hope that the tenant would relinquish possession after learning of legal involvement.

Upon reviewing the client’s property title, Jimenez determined that the tenant residing there was, in reality, the client’s former husband. The property, though once owned by the ex-husband, had been transferred to the client through their divorce settlement. Further inquiry by Jimenez revealed the tenant’s identity as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also referred to as “Don Rafa,” who is recognized as a leader within a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization that politically supports Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While onsite, Jimenez noticed that the health department had sealed off the property’s third floor. Delving deeper, Jimenez found out that authorities had shut down the premises due to its use as a day-rental for migrants as well as a hub for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The property was further associated with accounts of kidnapping and murder. Several attempts by law enforcement to conduct searches and seize evidence appeared to be unsuccessful. Jimenez was cautioned by a local police officer to keep away from the property and to avoid becoming involved.

Later in that month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his factory that read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” He paid no heed to this warning and disposed of the brochure. However, the following month, while working a night shift at the furniture factory, two individuals confronted Jimenez at gunpoint and ordered him to leave the area. One of these individuals labeled Jimenez a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures that had occurred at the location. After these men departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to carefully supervise their children’s travel to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same pair of individuals returned to the furniture factory during Petitioners' absence from the area. During this visit, they attacked an employee of the factory and brandished a firearm while insisting on learning Petitioners’ whereabouts. Once more, the men accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They issued another threat to kill Petitioners if they appeared again, specifying that the directive was given by Don Rafa. The employee explained that Petitioners were away, leading the men to depart without inflicting further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed a man placing his arm around the shoulders of his younger son as the boy was leaving school. The man then covered the child’s mouth with his hand while the boy attempted to get away, instructing him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez rushed to his son's aid and shoved the man, enabling his son to run toward Jimenez’s car. Following this incident, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area of Bogota for three weeks; during that time, they chose to depart for the United States.

Prior to departing, Jimenez reported the incident to a patrolling police officer. However, the officer responded that there was nothing that could be done, further adding that police officers were contributing to the issue. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners then followed this advice and left.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without lawful admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded to the accuracy of the allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte as well as the additional evidence presented, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ determined that the Petitioners were credible witnesses, the IJ ultimately held that they had not demonstrated: (1) that the harm they experienced rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to any ground protected by statute; (3) that internal relocation within Colombia was not reasonably feasible; and (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to offer them protection from their alleged persecutors. The IJ further denied the Petitioners’ claims for relief under the CAT. Petitioners appealed this decision, but the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld the IJ’s ruling and dismissed their appeal. Additionally, the BIA rejected the Petitioners’ assertions that the IJ had infringed upon their due process right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s order rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA incorrectly concluded that they failed to prove: (1) prior persecution; (2) prior persecution based on political opinion; and (3) a reasonable fear of future persecution that the government would be unable or unwilling to prevent. Petitioners do not contest the adverse ruling on their CAT protection claim or their due process argument.

A. Standard of Review

Our authority typically extends to reviewing final removal orders, which encompasses the ability to consider consolidated asylum claims as well. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our evaluation of the Board's denial of asylum is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since a single BIA member affirmed the immigration judge’s decision, our analysis is directed to the BIA’s ruling; however, we may “consult[] the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We assess the BIA’s factual determinations under the substantial-evidence standard. Pursuant to this standard, such findings are deemed conclusive unless the evidence in the record “compels” any reasonable adjudicator to reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a form of relief that is granted at the discretion of the Attorney General to noncitizens who are eligible.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for this discretionary relief, the applicant must establish, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he qualifies as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). The Immigration and Nationality Act specifies that a refugee is “any person who is outside any country of such person’s nationality . . . who is unable or unwilling to return to, and is unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.”

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)), the court observed that one path to demonstrating refugee status is “through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (internal citation omitted). To establish this, the applicant must satisfy three criteria: “(1) a specific act or acts that constitute persecution; (2) the persecution is based on one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) the persecution is carried out by the government or by actors whom the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (citing Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

The petitioners claim that, aside from the aforementioned threats, they experienced physical, psychological, and emotional injury that amounts to past persecution.

Although the INA does not provide a specific definition of persecution, “we have determined that persecution involves causing suffering or harm to those who differ—by race, religion, or political opinion—in a manner that is considered offensive and that surpasses mere restrictions or threats to life or liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). “Incidents are not evaluated in isolation; instead, we examine them in their entirety, as the combined impact of several incidents may rise to the level of persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). “Determining whether a noncitizen has established past private persecution in their country of origin—which is the principal matter at issue here—is a question of fact.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (quoting Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the numerous threats directed at them constitute persecution. While threats are taken into account when evaluating the overall effect of the mistreatment endured by Petitioners, precedent dictates that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Importantly, Petitioners admitted at their merits hearing that they had not suffered any physical injury, and likewise acknowledged in their opening brief that they did not sustain any enduring physical harm. Although physical injury is not a prerequisite for establishing persecution, id. at 804, the harm suffered must be serious and go beyond simple limitations or occasional threats, see Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners request that we take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological trauma they have suffered. For instance, Duarte claims that she experiences sleeplessness and an inability to rest, while her son has developed panic attacks and experiences stress. Although Petitioners have encountered unfortunate events, fears resulting from threats they received do not suffice to meet the standard of persecution. Even when certain incidents may cause significant physical or psychological distress, this court has seldom, if ever, recognized eligibility for asylum based solely on threats together with emotional or psychological injury. See id. (determining that the applicant’s claimed harm—including a ten-hour period of arrest and detention and threats—did not constitute persecution, despite their being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As Petitioners' counsel acknowledged during oral argument, precedent in our circuit has denied asylum claims under even more severe factual scenarios involving harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (“four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not constitute persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (where threats with machetes and being robbed six times fell short of persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

In view of these facts and circumstances, we conclude that there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that the cumulative harm suffered by Petitioners does not satisfy the definition of past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to reasons protected by statute—specifically, their alleged political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have neither presented argument nor shown proof regarding the definition of their asserted social group or their belonging to such a group. Instead, their argument regarding the nexus requirement rests solely on their claim that Don Rafa persecuted them due to their political beliefs. Consequently, our analysis is restricted to determining whether Petitioners can show the requisite connection between their purported persecution and their claimed political opinion. More precisely, Petitioners argue that the harm they sustained from Don Rafa and his associates was a result of their participation in the campaign of a candidate who opposed the sitting president of Colombia. Under the standard for demonstrating persecution based on political opinion, “the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). In other words, Petitioners’ political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” or play only “a minor role” in either past mistreatment or anticipated future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized, of course, that a persecutor may be driven by more than one motive. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, in these so-called mixed-motive cases, at least one protected ground must constitute a central reason for the harm inflicted. Id. Therefore, Petitioners here must establish that their political opinion was or will be at least one central reason the persecutors targeted or may target them.

Petitioners do not meet the required showing, as considerable evidence underpins the BIA's and IJ's determinations that Petitioners were mainly targeted because Jimenez jeopardized Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than due to Petitioners’ political beliefs. Before Jimenez encountered issues with Don Rafa, Petitioners persisted in their political engagement despite receiving intimidating calls and experiencing the death of a fellow campaign participant. Petitioners fail to identify any proof in the record indicating Don Rafa’s involvement in the initial threats linked to politics. In reality, it was only after Jimenez became embroiled in a property disagreement with Don Rafa and Don Rafa discovered Jimenez’s investigation into his rented property that the threats escalated. On one occasion, a man accused Jimenez of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and being responsible for the execution of searches and seizures on Don Rafa’s property. Subsequently, when these same individuals threatened Petitioners’ employees, they explicitly said Don Rafa had sent them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not make a mistake in concluding that the principal motivation for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s occupational and personal association with Don Rafa, instead of Petitioners’ political opinion. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that Petitioners’ political opinion played only a minor and incidental role compared to the principal basis for the harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to demonstrate past persecution, they nonetheless possess a well-founded fear of being persecuted in the future, partly due to the Colombian government's lack of ability or willingness to protect them. They claim that the persecutors maintain close connections and associations with the Colombian government, which undermines the possibility of safe internal relocation.

The “unable-or-unwilling” standard examines “evidence regarding the government’s reaction to persecution suffered by the applicant.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Specifically, “two categories of information are particularly important in assessing how a government responds to persecution: (1) whether the authorities intervened or attempted to intervene to stop the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the applicant was afforded protection by the government, for example, through placement in protective custody.” Id.

This assessment also involves determining whether the government was informed of the purported persecution and had a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. “Absent such notice, it is impossible to determine how the police might have acted or whether government assistance would have been forthcoming.” Id. (citation modified). Consequently, “whether an applicant notified the government of prior persecution constitutes an important consideration in this inquiry.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not reporting incidents to authorities may weaken an argument that the government was incapable of or unwilling to prevent the persecutors’ actions,” such an omission does not invariably defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can meet the requirement of seeking government protection by establishing that lodging a complaint with authorities would have been useless or posed a significant risk. Id.

Once more, the record contains ample evidence supporting the determinations made by the BIA and IJ. The Petitioners' attempts to obtain assistance from governmental authorities were negligible, if they occurred at all. Both Jimenez and his father have current or past employment with the Colombian government. Despite this, neither individual reported any misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners stated that they spoke with two specific police officers who advised them against causing trouble; however, there is no indication that these remarks represent the official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). The IJ observed that the record lacks evidence that the Petitioners sought protection beyond their own neighborhood or by utilizing Jimenez’s connections in law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both futile and dangerous, given that their persecutors allegedly maintain close ties and associations with the Colombian government. However, the record does not substantiate this claim. The authorities were cognizant of the property involved and had undertaken multiple searches and seizures in an attempt to curb the continuing criminal activity at that location. Thus, the evidence contradicts Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors are closely affiliated with the government and that the government would be either incapable of or disinclined to prevent the persecutors' actions. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners are unable to demonstrate that the Colombian government was, or would be, incapable or unwilling to offer them protection if it had been made aware of the threats they faced. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (determining that there was a lack of sufficient evidence showing government inability or unwillingness to protect, particularly where the applicant refused to contact authorities based on a belief that persecutors were “part of the government”).

Therefore, we concur that Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of future persecution as required for asylum eligibility.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not satisfied the criteria necessary for asylum, we will briefly consider their remaining contention about whether it is both possible and reasonable for them to relocate to a safer area within Colombia.

The government may overcome the applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of persecution—and thereby preclude an asylum grant—if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances so that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by moving to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; nevertheless, assuming arguendo that they had, the evidence in the record substantiates the conclusion that reasonable internal relocation within Colombia is available to them. Petitioners’ family members and relatives remain in Colombia without suffering harm, and Jimenez’s father is still employed in the criminal justice field. Significantly, Petitioners resided safely for three weeks with Jimenez’s mother in rural Bogota prior to their decision to depart for the United States. Furthermore, the individuals who issued threats against Petitioners only directed them to leave the neighborhood rather than the country itself. Accordingly, we conclude that any risk of future persecution can be sufficiently addressed by Petitioners’ ability to safely and reasonably resettle elsewhere in Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA's determination that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution under the asylum criteria, they cannot satisfy the more stringent requirements necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (quoting Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that relocation within their home country would not guarantee safety, as "the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety." Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (suggesting that relatives might also oppose the government politically). However, asylum cannot be granted solely on the basis of such speculative difficulties. Petitioners further argue that relocation is infeasible due to administrative and economic challenges, deficiencies in the country’s judicial system, and because their persecutors allegedly have connections to the Colombian president. Nonetheless, these contentions are presented in a cursory fashion, and considering our conclusions above, Petitioners’ arguments are insufficient.
