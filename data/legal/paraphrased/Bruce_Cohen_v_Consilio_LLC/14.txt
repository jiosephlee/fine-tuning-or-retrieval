Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in similar positions for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in Consilio’s favor, after which Cohen appealed. We partly affirm the judgment, and in part we vacate and remand the case.

I.

Consilio operates as a national provider of legal document review services and maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to work as an hourly document reviewer, holding a law license. In July 2019, Consilio informed Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers via email that, starting in August 2019, it would implement a revised premium overtime policy. Under this new policy, overtime premium pay was discontinued and it was made clear that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio on his own behalf and for a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not compensate him and similarly situated workers for overtime hours as required. Cohen contended that these alleged failures constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested relief in the form of overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers, satisfying the overtime wages and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Included in these payments, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 in overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, they were unable to reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen maintained he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the statutory provisions did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that "the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We examine the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio under a de novo standard, considering all evidence and reasonable inferences in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing the motion. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when the record reveals no disputed material facts and the moving party is entitled to prevail as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We turn first to Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is entitled to seek “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes individuals to file civil actions for redress due to violations of specified portions of the MPWA, including § 181.101, and establishes employer liability for compensatory damages and any additional suitable relief when such violations occur. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, employees can obtain civil penalties or damages, provided that these remedies are specified in the particular section of the MPWA that the employer is accused of breaching. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen claims that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and asserts that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allows employees to recover penalties based on average daily wages. However, § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner in connection with imposing penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties become available only after the Commissioner has “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If the wages are not paid within ten days after the service of this demand, then “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, any funds obtained by the Commissioner “must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees themselves, the statute only provides that they may “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it so chooses, the capacity to grant an employee who has suffered harm the statutory authority to seek penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee, upon making a demand, may recover due wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (containing a similar provision for employees who voluntarily leave their positions); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s decision to act in one statute but not in another demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a certain aim when it intends to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Based on our determination that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 resides exclusively with the Commissioner, we find no error in the district court's ruling. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that § 181.101(a) “confines the right to enforce a penalty to the [Commissioner], with any amounts recovered to be provided to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him the full amount of overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he remains eligible for statutory penalties. Both parties concur that, contrary to overtime wages and liquidated damages, any statutory penalties awarded are to be paid to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. At this point in the litigation, we are significantly concerned about whether we have jurisdiction to address this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of their payment to the government, can provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who have been harmed or are at risk of harm from ongoing illegal activity, since these penalties may prompt defendants to cease present violations and serve as a deterrent against future infractions. Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, a plaintiff is required to establish standing for every individual claim asserted and for each specific type of relief requested. Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously stated, the necessary personal stake that constitutes standing at the beginning of litigation must persist for the case’s duration to avoid mootness. Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could be rendered moot if later developments made it absolutely clear that the purportedly improper conduct is not reasonably likely to happen again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, “it is a well-established principle that ‘a defendant’s voluntary abandonment of the disputed practice does not eliminate a federal court’s authority to rule on the practice’s legality,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages, but they disagreed as to whether Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy continues to exist. The ongoing existence of Consilio's policy could allow Cohen's claim for civil penalties to proceed. See id. at 186. On the other hand, should Consilio's policy have been discontinued, and it is "absolutely clear" that the alleged misconduct will not recur, Cohen's MFLSA claim may indeed be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Additionally, federal courts do not possess a "license to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]" Id. at 192. For these reasons, we vacate the district court’s entry of summary judgment regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess its own jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the notice required by statute regarding a change to its overtime policy, and therefore he seeks penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts the district court “included little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that he received all wages owed by Consilio, and he fails to identify any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this statute. Lacking any substantial argument advocating for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his argument is limited to asserting a right to “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” While his initial complaint identified certain specific types of injunctive relief, Cohen later shifted his position in his summary judgment briefing by requesting different relief—namely, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, for understandable reasons, did not address this new request. Consequently, this matter is not correctly before us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (stating that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties under Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). He potentially does, but distinctions exist between the penalties in Laidlaw and those provided under the MFLSA. Neither side addressed this issue in their briefs. Nevertheless, I also set aside the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may be moot, and therefore I agree with vacating the judgment and sending the matter back. On remand, the district court may initially address the question of mootness. Id. at 180.
