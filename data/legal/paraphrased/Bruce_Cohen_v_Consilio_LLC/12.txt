Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed this action asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in similar positions with overtime pay. The district court entered summary judgment in Consilio's favor, prompting Cohen to appeal. We partially affirm the district court's decision, and in part, we vacate and remand.

I.

Consilio operates as a national company specializing in legal document review and maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen commenced his employment with Consilio in 2018, serving as a document reviewer licensed to practice law and paid on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email to Cohen and other similarly-situated hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota, notifying them of the implementation of a revised overtime pay policy set to take effect in August 2019. Under this updated policy, Consilio discontinued premium pay for overtime hours and expressly stated that Cohen and other affected employees would be compensated “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not pay overtime compensation due to him and other employees in similar positions. Cohen claimed these failures constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. The relief Cohen requested included unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen's MFLSA claim. Within this sum, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 as overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen "has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit" and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." The issue of penalties, however, remained unresolved between the parties. Cohen contended he was eligible to receive $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and another $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio maintained that the statutory provisions did not authorize Cohen to recover such penalties.

After Consilio made the payments, the parties agreed that the quickest and most efficient way to proceed was for the Court to first consider and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion regarding the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before moving forward with any additional discovery or litigation. Both Cohen and Consilio recognized that if the Court were to grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, all claims would be eliminated and the matter would be completely resolved.

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We examine the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio de novo, considering all evidence and any reasonable inferences that can be drawn therefrom in the manner most favorable to the party opposing the motion. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate if the record presents no genuine dispute regarding any material fact, and the party seeking judgment is entitled to it as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen's claim under the MPWA. The parties dispute whether Cohen is entitled to pursue "average daily wage penalties" pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specific MPWA sections, including § 181.101, and that if an employer “is found to have violated” such a section, it becomes “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, an employee may obtain civil penalties or damages, but only to the extent they are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer is accused of breaching. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and asserts that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” grants employees the right to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) specifically mentions only the Commissioner in relation to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, such penalties may be imposed only when the Commissioner first “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Should wages remain unpaid for ten days after service of this demand, the statute allows the commissioner to “charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to the employee, the statute only preserves the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (“[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, the capacity to grant an employee the statutory authority to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a terminated employee, upon making a demand, can seek recovery of unpaid wages or commissions and is authorized to "charge and collect a penalty"); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who voluntarily leave employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that the Legislature’s decision to act in one statute, but not another, demonstrates that it "knows how" to achieve a specific objective when desired (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Having determined that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties pursuant to § 181.101 lies exclusively with the Commissioner, we find that the district court acted properly. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (clarifying that under § 181.101(a), "the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee").

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Notwithstanding this agreement, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that, in contrast to overtime pay and liquidated damages, any statutory penalties awarded are to be remitted to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this point in the litigation, we harbor significant doubts about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, despite being remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” since such sanctions may prompt defendants to halt ongoing violations and act as a deterrent against future infractions. Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously discussed, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties may be rendered moot if later developments make it definitively certain that the purportedly wrongful conduct is not likely to occur again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, as established law provides, “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its summary judgment motion, both parties had agreed that Cohen received all due overtime wages and liquidated damages; however, there remains disagreement as to whether Consilio’s improper overtime policy continues to exist. Should the policy persist, Cohen might still have a viable claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has eliminated the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the challenged conduct will not reoccur, then Cohen’s MFLSA claim may be moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts, furthermore, are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess its own jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen's claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the notice mandated by statute regarding changes to its overtime policy, entitling him to penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court “included little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that Consilio has paid him all amounts due, and he fails to cite any statutory provision that permits civil penalties under this section. Lacking any substantive argument for reversal, we decline to set aside the district court's dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he maintains that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint identified particular types of injunctive relief, but he later shifted his position in summary judgment proceedings, asking instead for the rescission of the 2019 policy. The district court—understandably—did not address this revised request. Consequently, the matter is not properly before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that, under Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000), Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties. While it is possible that standing exists, there are distinctions between the penalties at issue in that case and those provided by the MFLSA. The parties did not address this matter in their briefs. Additionally, I set aside the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I also acknowledge that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could be rendered moot; thus, I agree with the decision to vacate the judgment and remand the proceedings. Upon remand, the district court may address the question of mootness as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
