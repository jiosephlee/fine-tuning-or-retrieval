Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal that decision. We affirm the judgment in part, but also vacate it in part and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates across the country as a legal document review firm and maintains offices in Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen was hired by Consilio to perform document review as a licensed attorney compensated on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio informed Cohen and other Minnesota-based licensed attorney document reviewers via email that, starting in August 2019, it would implement a revised premium overtime policy. This updated policy removed overtime premium compensation and specified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio on his own behalf and for a proposed class, asserting that Consilio neglected to compensate him and other similarly situated employees with the overtime wages owed. Cohen claimed that Consilio’s conduct was in violation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime pay and liquidated damages asserted under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Out of these funds, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 in overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties reached an impasse regarding penalties. Cohen maintained he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted summary judgment for Consilio on each of the three claims. Cohen now appeals this decision.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio, considering the record and any reasonable inferences in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). The standard for summary judgment requires that it be granted only when there are no disputed issues of material fact and the party moving for judgment is entitled to prevail as a matter of law. See Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central issue between the parties concerns whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes individuals to file a civil action to address violations of certain MPWA sections, including § 181.101, and stipulates that an employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” if found in violation of any such provisions. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee may be awarded civil penalties or damages, provided such remedies are specified “in the section” of the MPWA that the employer is accused of violating. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, asserting that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” empowers employees to pursue penalties calculated by average daily wage. However, § 181.101(a) refers exclusively to the Commissioner regarding such penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may be imposed only when the Commissioner has initially “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Following this, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding the role of the employee, the statute merely preserves the option to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and further providing that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

It is significant that the Minnesota legislature demonstrated, when it so desired, the ability to grant an aggrieved employee a statutory entitlement to seek penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a terminated employee who makes a demand may recover unpaid wages or commissions, and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same rights for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that the legislature’s decision to act in one statute but not in another demonstrates its knowledge and ability to pursue a particular aim when it chooses (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court’s decision contains no error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that, pursuant to § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has fully paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should additionally recover statutory penalties. Both parties concede that statutory penalties, as opposed to overtime wages and liquidated damages, are remitted to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this juncture, we harbor substantial doubt as to our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of the fact that they are remitted to the government, can nonetheless “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” since such sanctions might “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously explained, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if later circumstances made it abundantly clear that the conduct in question could not reasonably be anticipated to occur again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)), although it “is a firmly established principle that ‘a defendant’s voluntary abandonment of a contested practice does not strip a federal court of authority to decide the practice’s legality,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties concurred that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, the existence of Consilio’s allegedly improper overtime policy remains contested. Should Consilio’s policy still be operative, Cohen may still have a valid claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio’s policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that such conduct will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may become moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the case so the district court may consider its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s MWTA argument, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the statutory notice required for a change in its overtime policy, claiming entitlement to penalties as a result. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court devoted “little to no discussion” to this issue. However, Cohen admitted that Consilio has paid him all compensation owed, and he fails to cite any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this law. Absent any substantial argument supporting reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole contention is that he is owed “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint sought particularized injunctive remedies, but he later shifted his position during summary judgment briefing, instead requesting withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, for understandable reasons, did not address this altered request. Consequently, this matter is not suitably presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (stating that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s order concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the matter for additional proceedings. All other aspects are affirmed. KOBES, Circuit Judge, writing separately in concurrence.

I am not persuaded that Cohen has standing to pursue statutory penalties as contemplated in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Although he may have standing, the statutory penalties at issue in that case differ from those under the MFLSA. The parties did not address this issue in their briefs. Additionally, I would vacate the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may be moot, therefore I concur in vacating the judgment and remanding. Upon remand, the district court may address the issue of mootness as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
