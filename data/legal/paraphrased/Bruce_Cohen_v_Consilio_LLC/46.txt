Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed this action claiming that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not provide overtime compensation to him and others in comparable positions. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals. We uphold the decision in part, but in part we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm on a national scale and maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen was employed by Consilio beginning in 2018 in the role of an hourly licensed attorney responsible for document review. In July 2019, Consilio emailed Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers based in Minnesota to announce the implementation of a revised premium overtime policy set to take effect in August 2019. Under this new policy, overtime premium pay was discontinued, and it was made clear that Cohen and other affected employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not compensate him and other similarly positioned employees for overtime work. Cohen contended that Consilio’s conduct breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief the recovery of overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers licensed as attorneys in Minnesota, to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought by Cohen under his MFLSA claim. Out of this sum, Cohen individually was paid $3,225.21 as overtime wages, along with $462 representing liquidated damages. The parties reached a stipulation confirming that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and further, that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties remained in disagreement with respect to the issue of penalties. Cohen contended that he was due $172,080 in penalties based on average daily wage under the MPWA, $42,000 as penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and another $42,000 under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio asserted that the relevant statutes do not authorize the imposition of penalties in favor of Cohen.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court subsequently granted summary judgment in favor of Consilio on each of the three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We examine the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio using a de novo standard, considering the record and all reasonable inferences therefrom in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when there exists no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central issue between the parties is whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes a person to “bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specific MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101, and further specifies that if an employer “is found to have violated” any such provision, the employer is subject to “liability for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, the statute allows for the recovery of civil penalties or damages by an employee, provided that those remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer is alleged to have breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and asserts that the "plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]" permit employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, the text of § 181.101(a) refers only to the Commissioner with respect to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties become available solely when the Commissioner has first "serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee." Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If the employer does not pay the wages within ten days following service of this demand, "the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." Id. Additionally, all "[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned." Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees, the statute limits them to “prosecut[ing] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (providing “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated that it could grant an aggrieved employee an explicit statutory right to pursue penalties when it so intends. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that when a discharged employee makes a demand, that employee may recover unpaid wages or commissions, and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same remedy for employees who leave their employment voluntarily); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (clarifying that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 rests solely with the Commissioner, the district court did not make an error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that, under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

With respect to his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen contends that he should receive statutory penalties as well. The parties do not dispute that any such statutory penalties, in contrast to overtime pay and liquidated damages, must be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture in the case, we harbor significant reservations as to our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even if directed to the government, can “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs injured or at risk of injury due to ongoing unlawful behavior,” since such penalties may “motivate defendants to halt ongoing violations and discourage future transgressions.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must establish standing for each claim asserted and for every type of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously discussed, “[t]he necessary personal stake that is required at the outset of the lawsuit (standing) must persist throughout the case’s duration (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if later developments made it unequivocally certain that the purportedly wrongful conduct could not be reasonably expected to happen again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)), though “it is a well-established principle that ‘a defendant’s voluntary halt of the contested behavior does not strip a federal court of jurisdiction to rule on the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties stipulated that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, the status of Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy is still contested. Should Consilio’s policy still exist, Cohen may continue to have a valid claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio’s policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” there is no prospect of the misconduct recurring, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could become moot. Id. at 189. Moreover, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. For these reasons, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand so the district court may address its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not furnish the notice required by statute regarding a change to its overtime policy, and therefore he is owed penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court offered minimal, if any, analysis of this issue. However, Cohen acknowledged by stipulation that he was paid all amounts due from Consilio, and he fails to cite any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this section. Without a substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of the claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he maintains only that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen asked for specific types of injunctive relief, but subsequently, in his summary judgment briefing, he changed his request and instead sought the rescission of the 2019 policy. The district court accordingly did not address this latter request. Therefore, the matter is not correctly before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the case back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm. Circuit Judge Kobes files a special concurrence.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties under Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Perhaps he does, but the penalties at issue there are distinguishable from those available through the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this question in their briefs. However, I also would set aside the district court's determinations concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for statutory penalties under the MFLSA could be moot, thus I concur in vacating the judgment and remanding. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to address the issue of mootness as a preliminary matter. Id. at 180.
