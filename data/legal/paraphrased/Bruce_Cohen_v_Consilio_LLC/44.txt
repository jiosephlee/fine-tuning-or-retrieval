Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed a suit asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions for overtime work. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen’s appeal. We uphold the judgment in part, but in part we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review company with a national presence, maintaining offices within Minnesota. Cohen started his employment with Consilio in 2018, serving as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota via email about the implementation of a new premium overtime policy set to take effect in August 2019. This revised policy discontinued the payment of overtime premium wages, specifying instead that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio had not compensated him and others similarly situated for overtime work. Cohen claimed that these practices constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief the recovery of unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive measures.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages claimed under Cohen’s MFLSA action. Within these disbursements, Cohen himself was paid $3,225.21 in overtime compensation and $462 for liquidated damages. The parties agreed in their stipulation that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and also stipulated that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, they were unable to reach consensus concerning penalties. Cohen claimed entitlement to penalties amounting to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the MWTA. Consilio, conversely, contended that the relevant statutes did not authorize the imposition of penalties for Cohen.

After Consilio made the required payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio further “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court proceeded to grant Consilio’s motion for summary judgment as to all three claims. Cohen has filed an appeal.

II.

We undertake a de novo review of the district court’s decision to grant summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering the record and any reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central disagreement between the parties is whether Cohen can recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified sections of the MPWA, including § 181.101. It further establishes that, if an employer “is found to have violated” any such provision, the employer is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee is entitled to pursue civil penalties or damages provided that such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA the employer is accused of breaching. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, contending that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allows employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, the text of § 181.101(a) mentions penalties only in relation to actions by the Commissioner. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In particular, penalties may be assessed solely after the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, if the employer fails to remit wages within ten days of receiving such demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). For the employee’s part, the statute preserves only the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, the ability to grant employees who have been wronged a statutory right to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that a terminated employee, upon making a demand, is entitled to recover both unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (granting similar rights to employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that the Legislature’s decision to act in one statute but not another indicates it “knows how” to achieve a particular result when it chooses, citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010)). Since we hold that § 181.101 limits the authority to seek daily average wage penalties to the Commissioner, the district court’s decision was not erroneous. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both sides concur that statutory penalties, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, are to be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture in the litigation, we have significant doubts about whether we possess jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of whether they are remitted to the government, can “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are harmed or face the threat of harm resulting from ongoing illegal actions,” since such penalties may “motivate defendants to cease current infractions and dissuade them from engaging in future misconduct.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff must establish standing with respect to each claim asserted and for every type of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously indicated, “[t]he necessary personal stake required at the outset of litigation (standing) is also required to remain throughout the proceedings (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments demonstrate “with absolute clarity that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)); however, the established rule holds that “‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio moved for summary judgment, the parties stipulated that Cohen had received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages; however, there is an ongoing dispute about whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy is still operative. Should Consilio's policy persist, Cohen's claim for civil penalties could remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio’s policy has been rescinded and it is “absolutely clear” the wrongful behavior will not happen again, then Cohen’s MFLSA claim might indeed be moot. Id. at 189. Moreover, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand so the district court may assess whether it has jurisdiction.

C.

We turn now to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the statutorily mandated notice regarding a modification to its overtime policy, and thus he should receive penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court offered “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen has admitted that he has been fully paid all amounts Consilio owes him, and he fails to cite any statutory basis for awarding civil penalties under the cited statute. Without any substantive argument for overturning the district court’s decision, we decline to set aside the order dismissing this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole contention is that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint identified particular types of injunctive relief, but he subsequently altered his position in his summary judgment briefing, seeking the rescission of the 2019 policy instead. The district court, quite reasonably, did not address this request. Therefore, this matter is not correctly presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court's order as it pertains to Cohen's claim arising under the MFLSA and send the matter back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the district court's decision. KOBES, Circuit Judge, writing separately.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While he may in fact have such standing, the penalties considered in that case are not identical to those available under the MFLSA. This issue, however, was not briefed by the parties. Nonetheless, I also vacate the district court’s decision concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s pursuit of MFLSA statutory penalties could be rendered moot; therefore, I concur in the decision to vacate the judgment and remand the proceedings. Upon remand, the district court has the authority to address the issue of mootness as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
