Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not properly compensate him and a class of similarly situated workers for overtime hours. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen appeals that decision. We partly affirm, but also vacate and remand in certain respects.

I.

Consilio, which operates as a legal document review firm across the country and maintains offices in Minnesota, hired Cohen in 2018 to serve as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly-situated hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota via email of its intent to implement a revised premium overtime policy effective August 2019. Under this updated policy, Consilio discontinued premium overtime compensation and specified that Cohen and his colleagues would be compensated “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both individually and on behalf of a putative class, contending that Consilio failed to pay overtime compensation that was due to him and other similarly situated workers. Cohen asserted that Consilio’s conduct was in contravention of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen sought as relief the recovery of unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Included in these disbursements, Cohen individually obtained $3,225.21 for overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties jointly stipulated that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and further agreed that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties remained at an impasse concerning penalties. Cohen asserted his entitlement to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the relevant statutes did not authorize the recovery of penalties by Cohen.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that "the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation." Both Cohen and Consilio "acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved."

The district court proceeded to grant summary judgment in Consilio's favor on all three claims. Cohen now brings this appeal.

II.

Our review of the district court's summary judgment in favor of Consilio is conducted de novo, considering the record and all reasonable inferences therefrom in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted where there are no genuine disputes regarding any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim brought under the MPWA. The parties disagree about whether Cohen can recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specific sections of the MPWA, which include § 181.101, and further provides that if an employer “is found to have violated” any of those provisions, the employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. The statute also allows an employee to obtain civil penalties or damages, but only if such penalties or damages are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is claimed to have been violated. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and contends that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) explicitly assigns the authority regarding penalties solely to the Commissioner. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may only be imposed if the Commissioner has first “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Should the employer fail to make payment of wages within ten days following the service of such demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, the statute requires that any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees themselves, the statute specifically preserves only the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (“[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and provides “a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated, when it so intended, that it could provide an employee with a statutory right to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that when a discharged employee makes a demand, that employee can recover owed wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (applying similar provisions for employees who quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s choice to act in one statute but not another indicates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a given outcome when it so decides” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 lies exclusively with the Commissioner, the district court committed no error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that under § 181.101(a), “the right to pursue a penalty is reserved to the [Commissioner], with any recovered amounts being directed to the employee”).

B.

Regarding Cohen's MFLSA claim, he has agreed that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that statutory penalties, as distinguished from overtime wages and liquidated damages, are to be paid to the Commissioner and not directly to Cohen. Given the current posture of the case, we harbor significant doubts about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, notwithstanding that they are remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” because such penalties may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously noted, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments made it entirely certain that the disputed conduct could not reasonably be expected to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, it is a firmly established principle that “‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both sides acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages, but there remained a contested issue regarding the continued existence of Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy. Should that policy persist, Cohen might still maintain a viable claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. On the other hand, if Consilio’s policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that such wrongful acts will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. In light of the above, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the case so the district court can determine whether it retains jurisdiction.

C.

We turn now to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the notice required by statute regarding changes to its overtime policy, and thus he seeks penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court gave “little to no discussion of this claim.” Nevertheless, Cohen conceded that he received all compensation owed by Consilio, and he fails to cite any statutory provision granting civil penalties under this law. As Cohen does not present any substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s decision dismissing his claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint asked for specific types of injunctive relief, but he later shifted his position during summary judgment proceedings, instead requesting withdrawal of the 2019 policy. As a result, the district court did not address this new request, which was reasonable. Accordingly, this matter is not properly presented to us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[N]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court's decision on Cohen's claim under the MFLSA and remand for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm. Circuit Judge KOBES files a special concurrence.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as interpreted in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible Cohen does have standing, distinctions exist between the penalties considered in Laidlaw and those present under the MFLSA. Neither litigant addressed this point in their briefs. Additionally, I would vacate the district court's order concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s claim for MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, and thus I agree to vacate the judgment and remand the matter. Upon remand, the district court may address the question of mootness before proceeding further. Id. at 180.
