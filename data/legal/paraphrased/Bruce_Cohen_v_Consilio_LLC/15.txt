Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not provide overtime pay to him and other employees in comparable positions. The district court ruled in favor of Consilio on summary judgment, prompting Cohen to appeal. We partially affirm the district court’s decision and, in part, vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm with a national presence and maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen commenced employment with Consilio in 2018, working as a licensed attorney document reviewer compensated on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email to Cohen and other similarly situated hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota, notifying them of the implementation of a revised premium overtime policy effective August 2019. This updated policy discontinued overtime premium compensation and specified that Cohen and the other employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated legal action against Consilio, bringing claims on his own behalf and on behalf of a proposed class. Cohen asserted that Consilio did not pay him and other similarly situated employees the overtime compensation to which they were entitled. Cohen alleged that these actions constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested remedies including payment of owed overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within this amount, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 as overtime pay and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and further that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, no agreement was reached regarding the issue of penalties. Cohen asserted entitlement to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and another $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] prior to conducting any additional discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio further “acknowledge[d] that, should the Court grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, there would be no remaining claims and the case w[ould] be completely resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision to grant summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering the evidence and all reasonable inferences derived from it in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute over material facts and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen's claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree as to whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 allows a person to “bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified portions of the MPWA, which include § 181.101. It further establishes that if an employer is determined to have committed such a violation, the employer becomes “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee is entitled to seek civil penalties or damages, but only to the extent these remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that was breached by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permit employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner in connection with penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). Notably, penalties can be imposed only after the Commissioner has “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Following this, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees, the statute grants only the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and provides “a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated that, when it so chooses, it can expressly authorize an aggrieved employee to pursue statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that, after a terminated employee makes a demand, the employee can recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who voluntarily leave); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that when the Legislature acts in one statute but not another, it demonstrates that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a certain result if it intends to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). As we hold that only the Commissioner is permitted to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, it follows that the district court’s ruling was not erroneous. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that under § 181.101(a), “the power to seek a penalty is confined to the [Commissioner], with any recovered amounts payable to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has conceded that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, any statutory penalties assessed are directed to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this juncture in the case, we harbor substantial doubt about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can "provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are harmed or face harm as a result of ongoing unlawful conduct," since these penalties may "motivate defendants to cease current violations and serve as a deterrent against future infractions." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, "a plaintiff must establish standing for each individual claim advanced and for each specific form of relief requested." Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as we have stated, "[t]he necessary personal stake that must be present when litigation begins (standing) is required to persist for the entire duration of the litigation (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could become moot if later developments make it indisputable that the contested conduct is not likely to occur again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, “it is well established that the fact that a defendant voluntarily ends the disputed activity does not strip a federal court of jurisdiction to assess whether the conduct was lawful,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its summary judgment motion, the parties reached an agreement that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, there remains a dispute as to whether Consilio’s noncompliant overtime policy continues to exist. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen might still have a viable claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio’s policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that such misconduct will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may indeed be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Additionally, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s entry of summary judgment concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter for the district court to evaluate its own jurisdiction.

C.

We turn now to Cohen’s MWTA claim, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the notice required by statute concerning changes to its overtime policy, and therefore he is entitled to penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court offered “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen has admitted that he received all compensation that Consilio owed him, and he has failed to identify any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties under this statute. Because Cohen provides no substantial argument for reversal, we decline to set aside the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he maintains solely that he deserves “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” In his original complaint, Cohen requested particular types of injunctive relief, but subsequently altered his position and, during summary judgment briefing, sought different relief by asking for the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, quite reasonably, did not address this. Consequently, the matter is not appropriately before this court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Although it is possible he does, distinctions exist between the penalties at issue in that case and those permitted under the MFLSA. This matter was not addressed by either party in their briefs. I also set aside the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s claim for statutory penalties under the MFLSA may be moot, and thus concur in the decision to vacate the judgment and remand. Upon remand, the district court has the discretion to consider the mootness issue as a threshold matter. Id. at 180.
