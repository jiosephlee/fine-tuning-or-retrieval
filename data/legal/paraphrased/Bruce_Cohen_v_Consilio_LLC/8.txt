Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not provide overtime compensation to him and other employees in comparable positions. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, and Cohen has appealed that decision. We partially affirm the district court’s ruling and partially vacate and remand the case.

I.

Consilio, which operates across the country and maintains offices in Minnesota, employs legal document reviewers. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 as an hourly licensed attorney tasked with document review. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota by email of an upcoming change: starting in August 2019, a new overtime pay policy would take effect. This policy removed the provision for overtime premium pay, specifying instead that Cohen and the other affected employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked, regardless of the number of hours.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both on his own behalf and as a representative of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not provide him and other employees in similar positions with the required overtime compensation. According to Cohen, these actions constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief as forms of remedy.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages claimed under Cohen’s MFLSA cause of action. Cohen, individually, was paid $3,225.21 as overtime and $462 as liquidated damages within these amounts. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties did not reach a consensus regarding penalties. Cohen contended he should receive $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MWTA. In response, Consilio maintained that the statutory provisions did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that "the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation." Cohen and Consilio further "acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved."

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering the evidence and any reasonable inferences drawn from it in the manner most favorable to the party opposing the motion. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central issue between the parties is whether Cohen is entitled to recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of certain MPWA sections, including § 181.101, and further specifies that an employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” if found to have committed such a violation. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, an employee is permitted to obtain civil penalties or damages, but only if they are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is allegedly breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and contends that the "plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]" permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) references only the Commissioner in relation to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, the statute permits penalties solely if the Commissioner initially “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Afterward, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, the statute specifies that any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to the employee, the statute only allows for the “prosecut[ion] [of] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated its capacity to grant employees a statutory entitlement to recover penalties if it so intended. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a terminated employee, upon making a demand, may receive unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing similar rights for employees who voluntarily leave their positions); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that when the Legislature takes action in one statute but remains silent in another, it demonstrates the Legislature’s ability to achieve a particular result if it so chooses” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties pursuant to § 181.101, the district court’s decision was correct. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that § 181.101(a) “restricts the authority to enforce a penalty to the [Commissioner], and any funds recovered are to be paid to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that any statutory penalties awarded—unlike overtime pay and liquidated damages—are to be paid to the Commissioner rather than Cohen. At this point in the litigation, we have significant doubts about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can "provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury due to ongoing unlawful conduct," as such penalties can "motivate defendants to cease present violations and serve as a deterrent against future infractions." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, "a plaintiff is required to establish standing for every claim presented and for every form of relief requested." Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously stated, "[t]he necessary personal stake required at the outset of the action (standing) must persist throughout the litigation (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments made it definitively certain that the purportedly wrongful conduct would not be likely to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, as the Court has long recognized, “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages; however, the question of whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy is still active remains contested. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen may still maintain a claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” the wrongful behavior will not resume, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter for the district court to assess its own jurisdiction.

C.

We turn next to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give him the notice required by statute regarding a change to its overtime policy, and therefore he seeks penalties. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court “included little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that Consilio paid him all amounts due, and he does not identify any statutory provision that provides for civil penalties under this law. As Cohen offers no substantial argument to support overturning the district court’s dismissal of this claim, we decline to disturb that decision. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” In the original complaint, Cohen asked for certain particular types of injunctive relief, but later altered his request during summary judgment proceedings, seeking instead the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, understandably, did not address this new form of relief. Therefore, this matter is not appropriately presented to us for review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Although it is possible he does, distinctions exist between the penalties addressed in that matter and those provided under the MFLSA. Neither side has submitted briefing on this specific issue. Additionally, I vacate the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s claim for MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot; thus, I concur with vacating the judgment and remanding. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to address the mootness issue initially. Id. at 180.
