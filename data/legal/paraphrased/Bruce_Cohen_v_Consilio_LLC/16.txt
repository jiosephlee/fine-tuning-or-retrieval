Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed this action, asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not pay him and other comparable employees overtime wages. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, after which Cohen appealed. We uphold the decision in part, but also vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio operates across the country as a legal document review provider and maintains offices in Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen was hired by Consilio as an hourly attorney engaged in document review. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly situated hourly attorney document reviewers in Minnesota via email that, starting in August 2019, a revised overtime policy would take effect. This updated policy discontinued premium pay for overtime and specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation for all hours worked “at [their] base pay rate.”

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both in his own capacity and as a representative of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio failed to provide overtime compensation owed to him and other employees in similar circumstances. Cohen contended that Consilio’s conduct violated the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested, as relief, payment of overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, as well as injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within this total, Cohen himself was paid $3,225.21 as overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. Additionally, the parties agreed that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit" and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." The matter of penalties remained unresolved. Cohen maintained that he was eligible for $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MWTA. By contrast, Consilio contended that the governing statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that "the most expedient and efficient way to proceed [would be] for the Court to consider and rule on Defendants’ summary judgment motion regarding [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] prior to conducting any additional discovery or litigation." Both Cohen and Consilio "recognized that, should the Court grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, all claims w[ould] be extinguished and the matter w[ould] be fully concluded."

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We examine the district court’s decision to award summary judgment to Consilio de novo, considering the record and any reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when there are no genuine disputes regarding any material fact, and the party seeking summary judgment is entitled to prevail as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central point of contention between the parties is whether Cohen may recover “average daily wage penalties” as authorized by §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that a person may initiate a civil action to address violations of specified MPWA provisions, such as § 181.101, and that an employer found in violation is subject to “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Furthermore, an employee may obtain civil penalties or damages, provided those remedies are explicitly “provided for in the section” of the MPWA under which the employer’s alleged violation falls. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, maintaining that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to pursue penalties calculated on their average daily wage. However, § 181.101(a) makes mention solely of the Commissioner in relation to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, such penalties can be imposed only after the Commissioner has “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, if the employer fails to pay wages within ten days following service of the demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With regard to employees, the statute only allows them to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, that it is capable of granting an employee who has been wronged a statutory right to seek penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that after a discharged employee makes a demand, that employee is entitled to recover any unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same right for employees who have quit or resigned); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s affirmative action in one statute and omission in another indicates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to effectuate a given purpose when it so desires” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we have determined that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties pursuant to § 181.101, the district court’s decision was not mistaken. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that, under § 181.101(a), “enforcement of a penalty is confined to the [Commissioner], with any monies recovered ultimately provided to the employee”).

B.

With respect to his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen asserts that he remains eligible to receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that such statutory penalties—contrary to overtime wages and liquidated damages—are designated to be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this point in the litigation, we harbor substantial doubts about whether we possess jurisdiction to address this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of their payment to the government, can "provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who suffer injury or face the threat of injury due to ongoing illegal conduct," since such penalties can "motivate defendants to cease ongoing violations and discourage them from future infractions." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought." Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously noted, "[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could become moot if later developments made it unmistakably certain that the claimed wrongful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “[i]t is well established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a disputed activity does not strip a federal court of its authority to rule on the legality of that activity,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages; however, there is an ongoing dispute regarding whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains effective. Should the policy continue to exist, Cohen might still have a viable claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the alleged misconduct will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we vacate the district court’s order granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter for the district court to assess whether it possesses jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s MWTA claim, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the statutorily mandated notice regarding a modification of its overtime policy, entitling him to penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court offered “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen has conceded that he has received full payment from Consilio for all amounts due, and he does not identify any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this statute. Because he fails to provide any substantive argument for overturning the decision, we decline to disturb the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen's request for injunctive relief, he maintains that he deserves “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” In his original complaint, Cohen asked for certain specified injunctive measures, but later revised his position during the summary judgment phase, instead seeking withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, reasonably, did not address this change. Therefore, this matter has not been duly presented for our consideration. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that, under Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000), Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties. While it is possible that standing exists, notable distinctions arise between the penalties addressed in Laidlaw and those provided by the MFLSA. The parties did not provide briefing on this point. Additionally, I vacate the district court’s determinations concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may be moot, and accordingly, I concur in vacating the judgment and remanding the matter. Upon remand, the district court may elect to resolve the mootness issue as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
