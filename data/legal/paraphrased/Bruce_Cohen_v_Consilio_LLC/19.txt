Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed this action against his employer, Consilio, LLC, contending that Consilio did not compensate him and other employees in a similar position for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, and Cohen has appealed that decision. We affirm some aspects of the district court’s ruling, but vacate and remand other parts for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio, a legal document review firm operating across the country and with locations in Minnesota, hired Cohen in 2018 to serve as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly situated Minnesota hourly licensed attorney document reviewers via email of an upcoming change: beginning in August 2019, the company would implement a new premium overtime policy. Under this new policy, overtime premium pay was discontinued, and it was made explicit that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour they worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio on his own behalf and on behalf of a proposed class, claiming that Consilio did not provide overtime wages that were due to him and other employees in similar roles. Cohen asserted that Consilio’s conduct constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen sought overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief as potential remedies.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers licensed as attorneys in Minnesota to satisfy overtime wages and liquidated damages claimed under Cohen’s MFLSA action. Of this amount, Cohen received $3,225.21 attributed to overtime compensation, and $462 designated as liquidated damages. The parties jointly agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and further that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” The issue of penalties, however, remained unresolved. Cohen maintained he was due $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, in addition to $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and another $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the statutes in question did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that "the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation." Both Cohen and Consilio "acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved."

The district court subsequently granted summary judgment in favor of Consilio on each of the three claims. Cohen has filed an appeal.

II.

Our review of the district court's summary judgment in favor of Consilio is conducted de novo, considering the evidence and any reasonable inferences drawn from it in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when there is no dispute as to any material fact and the moving party deserves judgment as a matter of law. See Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is entitled to seek “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101, and that if an employer “is found to have violated” such a section, it will be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, an employee is permitted to obtain civil penalties or damages, provided such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is alleged to have been violated by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and asserts that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) refers exclusively to the Commissioner in connection with penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalty provisions become applicable only if the Commissioner initially “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). For employees, the statute grants only the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, its capacity to grant an employee who has suffered harm the statutory authority to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that after discharge, an employee can, upon request, recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (containing the same provision for employees who voluntarily leave employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (clarifying “that legislative action in one statute and inaction in another signals that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a specific result when it so desires” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Given our determination that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 rests solely with the Commissioner, we find no error in the district court’s ruling. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that enforcement of a penalty under § 181.101(a) “is reserved to the [Commissioner], with any penalty recovered being remitted to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has fully paid him the overtime wages and liquidated damages due to him under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he is further entitled to statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that any statutory penalties assessed, as opposed to overtime wages and liquidated damages, are to be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this juncture, we harbor significant doubts concerning our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, may “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are harmed or face harm from continued unlawful activity” because such penalties have the potential to “motivate defendants to stop their ongoing violations and serve as a deterrent against future infractions.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must establish standing for every claim advanced and for every type of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously set forth, “[t]he necessary personal interest required at the start of a lawsuit (standing) must persist throughout the duration of the action (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could become moot if later developments made it evident that the challenged conduct was not reasonably likely to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, it is firmly established that "a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice," id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio submitted its motion for summary judgment, both parties concurred that Cohen had received full payment for all owed overtime wages as well as liquidated damages; however, whether Consilio continues to maintain the allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains a contested issue. Should Consilio's policy still persist, Cohen's pursuit of civil penalties could yet proceed. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is "absolutely clear" that such misconduct will not happen again, Cohen's MFLSA claim may become moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts do not have the “license to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court's summary judgment ruling regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio failed to provide the notice required by statute regarding changes to its overtime policy, thereby entitling him to statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court gave his claim “little to no discussion.” However, Cohen agreed that he was paid everything Consilio owed him, and he does not cite any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this law. Lacking any substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole contention is that he should receive "an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy." Initially, in his complaint, Cohen asked for specific types of injunctive relief, but subsequently shifted his position during summary judgment briefing to request a different remedy—the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court appropriately did not address this new request. Consequently, the matter is not properly before this court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the case for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the decision. Circuit Judge Kobes writes separately in concurrence.

I am not persuaded that Cohen has standing to pursue statutory penalties under Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). He might, but there are distinctions between the penalties in that case and those permitted under the MFLSA. The parties did not address this issue in their briefs. Additionally, I set aside the district court’s determination regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree there is a possibility that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, so I support vacating the judgment and remanding the case. The district court may, on remand, resolve the mootness question as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
