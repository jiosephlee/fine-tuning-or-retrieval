Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in similar positions with overtime wages. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals that decision. We partially affirm the district court’s ruling, but in part, we vacate and remand the case.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm with a national presence and maintains offices within Minnesota. Cohen commenced his employment with Consilio in 2018, serving as a licensed attorney document reviewer compensated on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email to Cohen and other similarly situated Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers, notifying them that, starting in August 2019, a revised premium overtime policy would take effect. Under this updated policy, overtime premium pay was discontinued, and it was specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, contending that Consilio did not provide the overtime compensation owed to him and other employees in comparable positions. Cohen asserted that Consilio’s conduct constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief as remedies for these alleged violations.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages requested under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Of this sum, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 for overtime and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties were unable to reach an agreement regarding penalties. Cohen contended he was owed $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio maintained that the statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, both sides agreed that the “most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio further “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering the evidence and all reasonable inferences that can be drawn from it in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate where there are no disputed issues of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree on whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. According to § 181.171, “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specific MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101, and stipulates that if an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, the employer is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, employees are permitted to recover civil penalties or damages, provided those remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA at issue. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allows employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, the text of § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner with respect to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties are available solely after the Commissioner has “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Following this, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees, the statute permits them only to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, its ability to grant a statutory right for employees to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee, upon making a demand, may obtain unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same remedy for employees who voluntarily leave their jobs); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that when the Legislature takes action in one statute but not in another, it demonstrates that the Legislature “knows how” to achieve a certain result when it intends to (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court did not make an error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that § 181.101(a) “limits the authority to enforce a penalty to the [Commissioner], and any recovered funds are provided to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen contends that he is further entitled to statutory penalties. The parties concur that, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, any statutory penalties awarded would be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this juncture in the litigation, we are seriously concerned about our jurisdiction to resolve this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” since such sanctions may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously discussed, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments make it unmistakably certain that the purportedly unlawful conduct is not likely to occur again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, as established law dictates, “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages. Nonetheless, a dispute persists regarding whether Consilio continues to maintain the challenged overtime policy. Should this policy still exist, Cohen’s request for civil penalties may continue to present a live controversy. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that the alleged misconduct will not happen again, Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA may be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts do not possess “license[] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio violated statutory requirements by failing to provide notice regarding changes to its overtime policy, and therefore he seeks penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court inadequately addressed this claim, contending there was “little to no discussion” of it. However, Cohen conceded that Consilio paid him all wages due, and he has not cited any statutory provision permitting civil penalties under this statute. Since Cohen has not offered any substantive argument supporting reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of the claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his argument is limited to seeking “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen asked for particular forms of injunctive relief, but subsequently altered his position in his summary judgment briefing by seeking different relief—namely, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, for understandable reasons, did not address this issue. Therefore, the matter is not properly before this court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that he does, distinctions exist between the penalties at issue in that decision and those provided under the MFLSA. Neither side addressed this matter in their briefs. Nevertheless, I also vacate the district court’s decision regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may be moot, and therefore I agree with the decision to vacate the judgment and remand the case. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to address the mootness issue as a threshold matter. Id. at 180.
