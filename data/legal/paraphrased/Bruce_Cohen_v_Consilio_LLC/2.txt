Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, contending that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in similar positions for overtime work. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, after which Cohen appealed. We partially affirm the district court’s decision, but in other respects, we vacate and remand.

I.

Consilio, which operates as a legal document review firm across the United States and maintains offices in Minnesota, employed Cohen in 2018 as an hourly attorney responsible for document review. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers via email that it would implement a revised overtime pay policy starting in August 2019. Under this revised policy, overtime premium pay would be discontinued, and it was specified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, acting both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, claiming that Consilio did not provide overtime compensation that was due to him and to other employees in similar positions. Cohen asserted that these alleged actions constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers licensed as attorneys in Minnesota, intended to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s claim pursuant to the MFLSA. Cohen individually received $3,225.21 as overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties were unable to reach consensus regarding the issue of penalties. Cohen maintained that he was eligible to receive $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment in favor of Consilio, assessing the record and any reasonable inferences in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is appropriate where there exists no genuine dispute regarding any material fact and the party moving for summary judgment is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. According to § 181.171, “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of certain sections of the MPWA, including § 181.101, and an employer “is found to have violated” such a provision, it is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, employees may be awarded civil penalties or damages, provided that such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is alleged to have been violated. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allows employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, the text of § 181.101(a) refers only to the Commissioner with respect to the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties can be imposed only after the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If, after such demand, wages are not paid within ten days, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With regard to employees, the statute only allows them to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated, when it chose to, its ability to grant a statutory right to employees seeking penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that a discharged employee may, upon demand, recover unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (applying the same rule for employees who voluntarily quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Having determined that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 lies exclusively with the Commissioner, we find no error in the district court’s decision. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that, pursuant to § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages to which he is entitled under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that such statutory penalties, as opposed to overtime pay and liquidated damages, are owed to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture, we are seriously concerned about whether we possess jurisdiction to resolve this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” since they may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously discussed, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments made it entirely certain that the disputed conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nonetheless, it is firmly established that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both sides agreed that Cohen had received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages; however, the question of whether Consilio continues to maintain its improper overtime policy is still contested. Should Consilio's policy persist, Cohen may still pursue civil penalties. See id. at 186. On the other hand, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the misconduct will not happen again, then Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter so the district court may assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the notice required by statute regarding changes to its overtime policy, entitling him to penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court offered minimal analysis of this particular claim. However, Cohen has conceded that he has received all wages owed by Consilio, and he does not cite any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties under this section. Since Cohen has failed to offer any substantial argument in favor of overturning the district court’s decision, we decline to disturb the order dismissing his claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his argument is limited to asserting a right to “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, his complaint asked for certain specific injunctive remedies, but subsequently, during summary judgment briefing, he shifted and requested alternative relief—namely, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, reasonably, did not address this new request. Therefore, the matter is not appropriately before this court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as set forth in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible he does, the nature of the penalties in that precedent differ from those permitted under the MFLSA. Additionally, neither party has addressed this issue in their briefing. Nevertheless, I also vacate the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may be moot, and therefore concur with the decision to vacate the judgment and remand the matter. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to resolve the mootness issue as a preliminary matter. Id. at 180.
