Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, asserting that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not provide overtime compensation to him and other employees in comparable positions. The district court entered summary judgment in Consilio’s favor, prompting Cohen to appeal. We affirm the decision in part, but vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio operates across the country as a legal document review firm and maintains locations in Minnesota. Cohen commenced employment with Consilio in 2018 as a licensed attorney document reviewer, compensated on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio informed Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers based in Minnesota by email that, beginning August 2019, it would implement a revised premium overtime policy. Under this revised policy, premium pay for overtime work was discontinued, and it was specified that Cohen and the other affected employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both individually and as a representative of a prospective class, contending that Consilio failed to compensate him and other similarly situated employees for overtime work. According to Cohen, Consilio’s conduct was in violation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive remedies.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages claimed under Cohen’s MFLSA cause of action. Of that sum, Cohen himself received $3,225.21 in overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit” and further stipulated that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties did not reach agreement with respect to penalties. Cohen asserted claims for $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. By contrast, Consilio maintained that the statutes do not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed “that the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court proceeded to grant Consilio’s motion for summary judgment as to all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision to grant summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering the evidence and any reasonable inferences that may be drawn from it in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is appropriate when there are no disputed material facts and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central disagreement between the parties concerns Cohen’s entitlement to “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes “[a] person [to] bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101, and further provides that if an employer “is found to have violated” such a provision, it is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, the statute allows an employee to recover civil penalties or damages, but only if the penalties or damages are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer is alleged to have breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allows employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, the text of § 181.101(a) refers only to the Commissioner with respect to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, the provision makes penalties available only after the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If wages remain unpaid ten days after this demand, then “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. In addition, the statute states that “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees themselves, the statute solely permits them to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (indicating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated its capability to grant a statutory right for an employee to recover penalties when it intends to do so. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that when a discharged employee makes a demand, that employee is entitled to recover any unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing similar rights for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s choice to act in one statute and not another demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a certain purpose if it chooses to” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Because we find that daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 may be pursued only by the Commissioner, the district court’s decision stands. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that under § 181.101(a), “authority to enforce a penalty resides solely with the [Commissioner], and any recovered penalty is paid to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen agreed that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed to him under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that any statutory penalties awarded—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are to be paid to the Commissioner, rather than to Cohen. At this juncture in the case, we harbor significant doubt as to our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of whether they are remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” since these penalties might “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously stated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments made it unmistakably certain that the alleged misconduct would not likely happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)); however, it is an established principle that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both sides agreed that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, the continued existence of Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy remains a contested issue. If that policy is still being enforced, then Cohen may maintain a valid claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has abandoned the policy and it is “absolutely clear” the offending behavior will not repeat, then Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts, moreover, are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. For these reasons, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment as to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the case back for the district court to assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the notice mandated by statute when altering its overtime policy, allegedly entitling him to statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court offered “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen admitted in a stipulation that he received the full compensation owed by Consilio and fails to cite any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this statute. Lacking a substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen's request for injunctive relief, he contends that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint sought certain specified forms of injunctive relief; however, during summary judgment briefing, he shifted his position and instead asked for the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court did not address this altered request, which is understandable. Consequently, this matter is not appropriately before us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the matter back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the district court’s judgment. KOBES, Circuit Judge, writing a special concurrence.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that Cohen does have such standing, the penalties in Laidlaw and those provided by the MFLSA are not identical. The parties have not addressed this issue in their submissions. Additionally, I also set aside the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for statutory penalties under the MFLSA could potentially be moot and therefore join in setting aside the judgment and returning the case for further proceedings. Upon remand, the district court is free to address the mootness issue as a threshold matter. Id. at 180.
