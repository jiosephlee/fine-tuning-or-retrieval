Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not provide him and other employees in comparable positions with overtime compensation. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen has appealed that decision. We affirm the judgment in part, while vacating and remanding it in part.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm with a national presence and maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen was employed by Consilio starting in 2018 as an hourly document reviewer who is a licensed attorney. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based licensed attorney document reviewers working on an hourly basis, notifying them of an upcoming change to its premium overtime policy, effective August 2019. This revised policy discontinued overtime premium compensation and stated explicitly that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive payment "at [their] base pay rate" for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen filed a lawsuit against Consilio, both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not pay him and other similarly situated workers the overtime compensation they were owed. Cohen claimed that Consilio’s conduct violated the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. As relief, Cohen requested payment of overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers, satisfying the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within this sum, Cohen individually was paid $3,225.21 as overtime pay and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has received payment for all overtime wages he claims are owed in this action,” and that “Cohen does not contest that he has also received all liquidated damages he asserts are due to him under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties remained at odds regarding penalties. Cohen maintained that he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, in addition to $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that these statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made the payments, both parties agreed and stipulated that the most direct and efficient course of action would be for the Court to consider and rule on Defendants’ motion for summary judgment regarding the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before proceeding with any additional discovery or litigation. Cohen and Consilio further recognized that if the Court were to grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, then all claims would be disposed of and the matter would be completely resolved.

The district court proceeded to grant summary judgment in favor of Consilio on each of the three claims. Cohen now appeals that decision.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering the record and all reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). As noted in Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)), summary judgment is appropriate when the record reveals no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. There is disagreement between the parties about whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “A person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of designated sections of the MPWA, including § 181.101, and further establishes that if an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, it is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, an employee may obtain civil penalties or damages, provided those remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer is claimed to have breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that the "plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]" permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) references only the Commissioner with regard to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) ("When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to express legislative intent and must be enforced as written." (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties are available solely if the Commissioner first "serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee." Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, "if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." Id. In addition, any "[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned." Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees, the statute only allows for the "prosecut[ion of] a claim for wages." Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that "[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages" and that "[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times").

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it so desires, the ability to authorize an employee with a statutory right to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that, after being discharged, an employee who makes a demand may obtain unpaid wages or commissions in addition to being able to “charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (establishing similar provisions for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that when the Legislature acts in one statute but not another, it demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to effectuate a specific aim if that is its intent” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we hold that only the Commissioner is permitted to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, we find no error by the district court. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that, per § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is restricted to the [Commissioner], with any amounts recovered being paid to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages to which he is entitled under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that any statutory penalties awarded would go to the Commissioner, rather than Cohen, unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages. At this point in the case, we have significant reservations about whether we possess jurisdiction to rule on this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of being remitted to the government, can "provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who suffer or are at risk of suffering harm as a result of ongoing unlawful behavior," since these penalties might "motivate defendants to stop existing violations and serve as a deterrent against future infractions." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously discussed, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments made it unmistakably evident that the purportedly unlawful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to happen again. Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “it is well established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of the challenged conduct does not strip a federal court of its authority to rule on the legality of that conduct,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed to him. However, they continued to disagree over whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy was still active. Should Consilio’s policy remain effective, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties could proceed. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the improper conduct will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could become moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts lack authority to maintain jurisdiction in cases where one or both sides evidently no longer possess an ongoing stake. Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess whether it retains subject matter jurisdiction.

C.

We turn now to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not give the notice required by statute when it altered its overtime policy, entitling him to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court gave “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen stipulated that Consilio has paid him all amounts owed, and he has not identified any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this law. Lacking any substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

With respect to Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he deserves “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, his complaint included requests for particular forms of injunctive relief, but he subsequently shifted course and, in his summary judgment briefing, sought a different remedy—specifically, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. Not surprisingly, the district court did not address this revised request. Accordingly, this matter is not properly before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the case back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm the district court’s order. KOBES, Circuit Judge, specially concurring.

I am not persuaded that Cohen has standing to pursue statutory penalties as described in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). He might have standing, but the nature of the penalties in that decision differs from those offered by the MFLSA. This issue was not addressed by either party in their briefs. Additionally, I also vacate the district court’s order as it pertains to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur in the decision to vacate the judgment and remand the case because Cohen’s claim for MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot. Upon remand, the district court has the discretion to address the issue of mootness first. Id. at 180.
