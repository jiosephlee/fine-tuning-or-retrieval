Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, contending that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not compensate him and other employees in similar positions for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal. We uphold the district court’s decision in part, but in part we vacate the judgment and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review company on a national level, maintaining offices within Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to serve as a document reviewer; he is a licensed attorney who was paid by the hour. In July 2019, Consilio emailed Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers working in Minnesota to inform them that a new premium overtime policy would be implemented in August 2019. According to this policy, overtime premium compensation would be discontinued, and it was clarified that Cohen and other affected employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated legal proceedings against Consilio, both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, contending that Consilio did not provide overtime compensation required for him and other similarly situated workers. Cohen asserted that Consilio’s conduct breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. As relief, Cohen requested unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive measures.

Consilio made payments totaling $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime wages and liquidated damages sought under Cohen's MFLSA claim. Of these amounts, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 in overtime pay and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties agreed and stipulated that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” as well as that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties reached an impasse regarding penalties. Cohen asserted that he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio maintained that the statutory provisions did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made the payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court subsequently granted Consilio’s summary judgment motion on all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court's decision granting summary judgment to Consilio, considering the evidence as well as any reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when no genuine disputes concerning material facts exist and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree as to whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that a person may file a civil lawsuit “seeking redress for violations” of designated portions of the MPWA, which include § 181.101, and further provides that an employer found to be in violation of any such provision shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, employees are permitted to seek civil penalties or damages, provided those remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is alleged to have been breached by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen claims that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and contends that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allows employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, under § 181.101(a), only the Commissioner is mentioned in connection with the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may be assessed solely if the Commissioner first “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If, after service of such a demand, “payment of wages is not made within ten days,” then the commissioner may “charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees themselves, the statute preserves solely the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (providing that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated that it possesses the ability to grant a statutory right to seek penalties to employees who have suffered harm, should it choose to do so. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that a terminated employee, upon making a demand, can recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (granting the same rights to employees who voluntarily leave their employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s decision to act in one statute but not another demonstrates its awareness of how to achieve a particular result when desired” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Given our determination that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 rests solely with the Commissioner, we find no error in the district court’s ruling. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that § 181.101(a) “restricts the power to enforce a penalty to the [Commissioner], with any proceeds ultimately disbursed to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has fully paid him all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he remains eligible to receive statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that statutory penalties, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, would be awarded to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture, we harbor significant reservations about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can “provide relief to citizen plaintiffs injured or at risk of injury due to ongoing illegal activity,” since such penalties can “motivate defendants to cease ongoing violations and act as a deterrent against future infractions.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff is required to establish standing for each claim asserted and for every type of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as we have noted, “[t]he necessary personal stake present at the outset of litigation (standing) must persist for the duration of the case (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if later circumstances made it indisputable that the alleged wrongful conduct would not be reasonably expected to happen again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, “it is a well-established principle that ‘a defendant’s voluntary discontinuation of the contested conduct does not strip a federal court of jurisdiction to decide the practice’s legality,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties had reached an agreement that Cohen had received all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages, yet a dispute persists over whether Consilio’s noncompliant overtime policy is still active. Should Consilio’s policy remain in effect, Cohen might continue to maintain a claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been terminated and it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Additionally, federal courts do not possess “license … to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the matter to the district court for it to assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not provide the required statutory notice regarding a modification to its overtime policy, thereby entitling him to penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court devoted insufficient attention to this claim in its discussion. Nonetheless, Cohen agreed that he received all compensation Consilio owes him, and he does not identify any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties in these circumstances. Because he has not presented a substantive argument to justify overturning the district court, we decline to disturb the order of dismissal for this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he is owed “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint asked for specific types of injunctive relief, but he subsequently altered his position during summary judgment briefing to instead request withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court accordingly did not address this new request. As a result, the matter has not been properly presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court's judgment concerning Cohen's MFLSA claim and return the matter for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the district court's decision. Circuit Judge KOBES, writing separately, concurs.

I remain unpersuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as articulated in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible he does, distinctions exist between the penalties addressed in that precedent and those established under the MFLSA. This topic was not addressed by either party in their submissions. Additionally, I concur in vacating the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree with the possibility that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could be rendered moot, and therefore join in the decision to vacate the judgment and remand for further consideration. Upon remand, the district court may resolve the mootness issue first. Id. at 180.
