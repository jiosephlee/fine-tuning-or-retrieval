Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed suit contending that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not pay him and other employees in comparable positions the required overtime wages. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, leading Cohen to appeal. We partially affirm, but also vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm on a national scale, maintaining locations in Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen was hired by Consilio as a licensed attorney to review documents on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other Minnesota hourly licensed attorney document reviewers via email that, beginning in August 2019, a new premium overtime policy would take effect. Under this revised policy, overtime premium pay was discontinued, and it was clarified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation at their "base pay rate" for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated litigation against Consilio for himself and a proposed class, asserting that Consilio had not compensated him and other similarly situated workers for overtime wages owed. He contended that Consilio’s conduct contravened the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested relief in the form of overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy overtime compensation and liquidated damages claimed under Cohen's MFLSA action. Within this sum, Cohen himself was paid $3,225.21 as overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit” and further stipulated that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties were unable to reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen contended he was owed $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MWTA. Consilio maintained that these statutes did not authorize the imposition of penalties in Cohen’s case.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that the “most expedient and efficient” course was for the Court to first adjudicate Defendants’ summary judgment motion as to the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims prior to conducting further discovery or litigation. Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d]” that should the Court grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, there would be no claims left, resulting in full resolution of the action.

The district court granted summary judgment for Consilio on each of the three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

Our review of the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio is conducted de novo, considering the record and all reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Under this standard, summary judgment is appropriate when no dispute exists regarding any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen's claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree on whether Cohen can recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Under § 181.171, a person may initiate a civil lawsuit for violations of designated sections of the MPWA, among which is § 181.101. This section further states that an employer who is determined to have breached such a provision will be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, employees may seek civil penalties or damages if they are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that has allegedly been breached. The statute provides: “An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that "the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]" allows employees to pursue average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) explicitly mentions only the Commissioner in relation to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties become available only if the Commissioner initially "serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee." Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If the employer does not pay wages within ten days following the service of that demand, "the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." Id. In addition, any "[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned." Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). The statute, as regards the employee, limits the employee to the right to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages." Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

It is significant that, had it chosen to do so, the Minnesota legislature was capable of granting employees a statutory right to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee, upon demand, may receive unpaid wages or commissions and also "may charge and collect a penalty"); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who have quit or resigned); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting "that the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another demonstrates the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a specific purpose if that is its intent" (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Based on our determination that only the Commissioner possesses authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court acted appropriately. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that, per § 181.101(a), "only the [Commissioner] is authorized to enforce a penalty, and any funds collected are awarded to the employee").

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged that Consilio has paid all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages as required by the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen asserts he should additionally receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, statutory penalties, if assessed, are awarded to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this point in the litigation, we harbor significant doubts about our jurisdiction to resolve this matter.

Even when civil penalties are paid to the government, they can nevertheless "provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who suffer injury or are threatened with harm due to ongoing illegal behavior," as such sanctions may "motivate defendants to cease current violations and discourage future infractions." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing with respect to every claim he brings and for every specific remedy he requests." Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously discussed, "[t]he necessary personal stake required at the outset of the case (standing) must persist throughout the course of the action (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could be rendered moot if it is made ‘absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur’ due to later developments,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)), but “‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio submitted its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, there remains a dispute over whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy continues to exist. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties may remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” there will be no recurrence of the wrongful behavior, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may, in fact, be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to resolve the issue of its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, wherein he asserts that Consilio neglected to provide the notice of its change in overtime policy required by statute, which he argues entitles him to penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court offered minimal discussion regarding this issue. Nevertheless, Cohen agreed that he was paid in full by Consilio and has not cited any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this law. Because Cohen presents no substantive argument in favor of reversing the district court’s dismissal of this claim, we decline to overturn its decision. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen sought particular specified injunctive remedies, but subsequently altered his request during summary judgment proceedings to seek the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, reasonably, did not address this revised request. Consequently, this matter is not appropriately before us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court's decision concerning Cohen's MFLSA claim and send the matter back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the district court’s judgment. Circuit Judge Kobes writes separately in concurrence.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as set forth in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). It is possible that he does have standing, though the penalties in Laidlaw differ from those provided by the MFLSA. The parties did not address this issue in their briefs. I also vacate the district court’s determinations on attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. Since Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, I concur in vacating the district court’s judgment and remanding the matter. Upon remand, the district court may address the question of mootness as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
