Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in similar positions with overtime pay. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal. We partly affirm the district court’s decision, but also vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio operates as a national legal document review firm and maintains offices within Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to serve as a document reviewer, working on an hourly basis as a licensed attorney. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based licensed attorney document reviewers who were paid hourly, notifying them of a forthcoming premium overtime policy effective August 2019. This updated policy removed the provision for overtime premium compensation and specified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, claiming that Consilio did not compensate him and other similarly situated employees for overtime work. Cohen asserted that Consilio's conduct constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen sought as relief the payment of overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Of that sum, Cohen individually was paid $3,225.21 as overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties reached an impasse regarding the issue of penalties. Cohen maintained that he was owed $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Conversely, Consilio contended that the statutory framework did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision to grant summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering all evidence and the reasonable inferences drawn from it in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when no genuine dispute of material fact exists and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specific sections of the MPWA, among them § 181.101, and that where an employer “is found to have violated” such a section, the employer shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, employees may obtain civil penalties or damages, but only if those remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer is alleged to have violated. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permit employees to pursue penalties calculated on the average daily wage. However, § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner with respect to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties become available solely after the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If the employer fails to pay the wages within ten days after the demand is served, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With regard to employees, the statute allows only for the “prosecut[ion of] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it so chooses, its ability to grant a statutory right to an aggrieved employee to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee, upon making a demand, can recover unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who voluntarily leave their employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s decision to act in one statute and not in another indicates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a specific goal when it intends to do so” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Therefore, as we determine that § 181.101 confers the authority to seek daily average wage penalties solely upon the Commissioner, we hold that the district court did not err. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that under § 181.101(a), “the authority to pursue a penalty is confined to the [Commissioner], and any penalty funds recovered are paid to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he is additionally entitled to statutory penalties. Both parties concur that, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, any statutory penalties assessed would be owed to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture, we are significantly concerned about our jurisdiction to address this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of their payment to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” since these penalties have the potential to “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously noted, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if later developments made it unmistakably certain that the supposed unlawful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to happen again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)), but “it is a well-established principle that ‘a defendant’s voluntary discontinuance of a contested action does not strip a federal court of authority to resolve the lawfulness of that action,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties reached an agreement that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, there is still disagreement as to whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy continues to be in effect. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen’s request for civil penalties could remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio’s policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that the alleged misconduct will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could potentially be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts, moreover, do not have “license[] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment with respect to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess whether it has jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not provide the notice of a change in its overtime policy as required by statute, entitling him to statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court “included little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all amounts owed, and he does not cite any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this statute. As Cohen offers no substantial argument for reversing the dismissal, we decline to disturb the district court’s ruling. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint sought particular types of injunctive remedies, but he later altered his position in his summary judgment briefing to request different relief, namely, withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, understandably, did not address this revised request. Therefore, this matter is not properly before us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties under Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that standing exists, distinctions arise between the penalties addressed in that decision and those provided for in the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this question in their briefing. In addition, I vacate the district court’s decisions concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may now be moot, so I concur with the decision to vacate the judgment and remand for further proceedings. Upon remand, the district court may address the mootness issue as a threshold matter. Id. at 180.
