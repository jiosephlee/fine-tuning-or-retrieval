Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action contending that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not pay overtime compensation to him and other employees in comparable positions. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, and Cohen now appeals that decision. We uphold the judgment in part, but in part, we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review company with a national presence and maintains offices in Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen was employed by Consilio in the role of an hourly, licensed attorney performing document review. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly situated licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota via email that a new overtime compensation policy would take effect in August 2019. Under this new policy, overtime premium compensation would be discontinued, and Consilio further specified that Cohen and other employees would receive compensation for all hours worked solely “at [their] base pay rate.”

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, representing himself as well as a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not compensate him and other similarly situated workers for overtime wages to which they were entitled. According to Cohen, these alleged failures constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief as remedies.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought in connection with Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Out of these amounts, Cohen individually was paid $3,225.21 for overtime and an additional $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, they reached an impasse concerning penalties. Cohen contended that he was eligible for $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 under the MWTA. Conversely, Consilio maintained that the applicable statutes did not allow Cohen to recover such penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that the quickest and most efficient way to proceed would be for the Court to consider and rule on Defendants’ motion for summary judgment regarding the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims prior to conducting any additional discovery or litigation. Both Cohen and Consilio recognized that a complete grant of Defendants’ summary judgment motion would dispose of all claims, thereby fully resolving the case.

The district court subsequently granted summary judgment in favor of Consilio on all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court's summary judgment decision in favor of Consilio, considering the facts and all reasonable inferences drawn from them in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). As stated in Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)), summary judgment is warranted if no genuine dispute as to any material fact exists and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

A.

We begin by considering Cohen's claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree over whether Cohen is entitled to pursue "average daily wage penalties" pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that "[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations" of designated MPWA provisions, among them § 181.101, and further indicates that an employer "is found to have violated" such a provision, the employer becomes "liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief." Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee may recover civil penalties or damages, but only if such remedies are "provided for in the section" of the MPWA that the employer allegedly breached. Id. ("An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.").

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to pursue average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner in relation to imposing penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). The statute actually allows for penalties only if the Commissioner has initially “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). After such demand, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, the law specifies that all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). For employees, the statute only preserves the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, had it so desired, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated its capacity to grant a statutory right to aggrieved employees to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that, upon request by a terminated employee, that employee is entitled to not only unpaid wages or commissions but also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same rights for employees who have resigned or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s choice to act in one statutory context but not another demonstrates the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a given legislative aim when it so wishes” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Based on our determination that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, we find no error in the district court’s decision. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that under § 181.101(a), “the authority to enforce a penalty is reserved to the [Commissioner], with any penalty collected to be paid to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has fully paid him any overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that, unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages, any statutory penalties assessed would be paid to the Commissioner and not to Cohen himself. At this juncture, we are seriously concerned about our jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Even when civil penalties are remitted to the government, they can still “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who suffer or are at risk of injury due to ongoing unlawful actions,” since such penalties might “prompt defendants to stop present violations and discourage future infractions.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must establish standing as to every claim asserted and for every specific remedy sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously noted, “[t]he necessary personal stake that must be present when the action begins (standing) must persist for the duration of the case (mootness).” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if subsequent developments made it certain that the allegedly unlawful conduct was not reasonably likely to happen again. Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “it is firmly established that a defendant’s voluntary discontinuation of the contested practice does not strip a federal court of jurisdiction to rule on the legality of the practice.” Id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages; however, there remains a dispute regarding whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy is still being enforced. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties may still be permissible. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that the improper actions will not reoccur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could indeed be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Moreover, federal courts lack authorization “to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. For these reasons, we set aside the district court’s order granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess its subject matter jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen's claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the statutory notice required for a change to its overtime policy, thereby entitling him to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen notes that the district court "included little to no discussion of this claim." However, Cohen has acknowledged via stipulation that he has received all compensation due from Consilio, and does not identify any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties in these circumstances. Absent any substantive argument supporting reversal, we decline to upset the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen's request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint requested particular types of injunctive relief, but he later altered his position during summary judgment briefing by seeking a different remedy—namely, withdrawal of the 2019 policy. As a result, the district court understandably did not address this revised request. Accordingly, this matter is not appropriately before us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s order with respect to Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and return the case for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm. Circuit Judge KOBES files a special concurrence.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen has standing to pursue statutory penalties as described in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Perhaps standing exists, yet there are notable distinctions between the penalties addressed in that decision and those authorized by the MFLSA. The parties did not present arguments on this point. In addition, I would vacate the district court’s determination concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I also recognize that Cohen’s pursuit of MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, thus I concur with vacating the decision and remanding the matter. Upon remand, the district court may address the potential mootness issue as a threshold matter. Id. at 180.
