Case Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 24-2079  
Opinion by Circuit Judge Kelly

Bruce Cohen filed this action, asserting that Consilio, LLC—his employer—did not provide overtime compensation owed to him and other employees in comparable positions. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal. We uphold the judgment in part, but also vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio, a company providing legal document review services across the country, operates out of Minnesota as well. In 2018, Cohen was hired by Consilio as a licensed attorney document reviewer, paid on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and similarly situated hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota by email that a new policy concerning overtime pay would take effect in August 2019. Under this revised policy, premium pay for overtime hours was discontinued, and Consilio made clear that both Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both for himself and as a representative of a proposed class, claiming that Consilio neglected to compensate him and other similarly situated employees for overtime work. Cohen asserted that these actions were in breach of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested relief in the form of unpaid overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive measures.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorneys performing document review work, to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen's MFLSA claim. Of this sum, Cohen received $3,225.21 representing overtime pay and an additional $462 for liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen "has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit," and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." However, they were unable to reach consensus regarding statutory penalties. Cohen maintained he was eligible for $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and another $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. By contrast, Consilio took the position that the relevant statutes did not authorize the imposition of penalties in Cohen's case.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed and stipulated that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio both “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court proceeded to grant summary judgment in favor of Consilio on all three asserted claims. Cohen now brings this appeal.

II.

We examine the district court's summary judgment in favor of Consilio with fresh review, considering the evidence and any reasonable inferences drawn therefrom in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when there exists no genuine dispute concerning any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties dispute whether Cohen is entitled to pursue "average daily wage penalties" as provided under §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of certain parts of the MPWA, which include § 181.101, and that if an employer “is found to have violated” such a section, the employer is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, an employee may obtain civil penalties or damages if such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is allegedly breached by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and contends that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover penalties in the form of average daily wages. However, § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner in connection with the authority to impose penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may be assessed solely where the Commissioner has first “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If wages remain unpaid within ten days after the Commissioner serves the demand, then “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). The statute grants employees only the express authority to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and conferring on employees “a substantive right ... to the payment of wages ... as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, if the Minnesota legislature intended, it demonstrated the capacity to grant employees a statutory right to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a terminated employee, upon making a demand, may recover unpaid wages or commissions and "may charge and collect a penalty"); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same remedy for employees who voluntarily leave employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting "that the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a particular result if that is its intention" (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since our interpretation is that under § 181.101, only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties, the district court’s decision was not mistaken. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that under § 181.101(a), "the power to enforce a penalty is confined to the [Commissioner], and any sums obtained are distributed to the employee").

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has conceded that Consilio has already paid him all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages pursuant to the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen contends that he remains eligible to receive statutory penalties. Both sides concur that statutory penalties, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, are to be paid to the Commissioner rather than Cohen himself. At this point in the proceedings, we harbor significant doubts about whether we possess jurisdiction to resolve this matter.

Civil penalties, though remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” because they may serve to “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously discussed, “the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could become moot if later developments made it definitively certain that the conduct in question was not likely to recur, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “it is well established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a disputed action does not strip a federal court of its authority to rule on the legality of the conduct,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties conceded that Cohen had received payment for all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages. However, the ongoing existence of Consilio’s overtime policy that allegedly violated the law remains contested. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties could still be valid. See id. at 186. On the other hand, if the policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that Consilio will not reengage in the challenged conduct, then Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA could potentially be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Moreover, federal courts do not possess “license to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the matter to the district court for an assessment of its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not furnish the notice required by statute regarding a modification to its overtime policy, thereby entitling him to statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court offered minimal discussion of this particular claim. However, Cohen agreed that he has received all compensation owed by Consilio, and he does not cite any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties for violations under this statute. As Cohen has not provided a substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s order dismissing his claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, his complaint sought particular injunctive remedies, but he later shifted his position in summary judgment filings, asking instead for withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, understandably, did not address this latter request. Consequently, this argument is not appropriately before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the case back for further proceedings on that matter. In all other respects, we affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring in part.

I am not persuaded that Cohen satisfies the requirements for standing to pursue statutory penalties as outlined in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). It is possible that he does, but there are distinctions between the penalties discussed in that case and those permitted under the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this issue in their briefs. Additionally, I would set aside the district court’s determinations on attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may be moot, and therefore concur in the decision to vacate the judgment and remand. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to address the mootness issue first. Id. at 180.
