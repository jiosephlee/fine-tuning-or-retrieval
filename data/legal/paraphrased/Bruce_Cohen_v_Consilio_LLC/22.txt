Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and a group of comparable employees for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals that decision. We affirm the judgment in part, but in part we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio, a legal document review firm with a national presence and locations in Minnesota, employed Cohen starting in 2018 as a licensed attorney document reviewer paid hourly. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers by email that, beginning in August 2019, it would implement a revised overtime premium policy. This updated policy did away with overtime premium pay and specified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour they worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio on his own behalf and for a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not pay him and other similarly situated employees the overtime wages to which they were entitled. Cohen contended that Consilio's conduct contravened the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen’s requested relief included overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive measures.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers licensed as attorneys in Minnesota, to satisfy the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under Cohen’s claim pursuant to the MFLSA. Within these disbursements, Cohen himself was paid $3,225.21 as overtime wages and an additional $462 as liquidated damages. The parties agreed that Cohen "has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit," and further, that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." However, they could not reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen maintained he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio both “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court subsequently granted Consilio summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We undertake a de novo review of the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio, assessing the record and any reasonable inferences in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is appropriate when the evidence reveals no genuine dispute regarding any material fact and the party moving for summary judgment is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen's claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree over whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” as authorized by §§ 181.171 and 181.101. According to § 181.171, “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified sections of the MPWA, such as § 181.101, and states that if an employer “is found to have violated” one of those provisions, it “will be liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee can seek civil penalties or damages, provided that such relief is “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer allegedly breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and asserts that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” grants employees the authority to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) refers solely to the Commissioner in relation to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties become available only after the Commissioner has “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). After this demand, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). The statute, as it relates to employees, limits them to “prosecut[ing] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated that, when it intends, it can grant employees a statutory entitlement to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that an employee who has been discharged may, after making a demand, recover not only unpaid wages or commissions but also “may charge and collect a penalty”); see also id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same remedy for employees who voluntarily quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Having determined that daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 are enforceable solely by the Commissioner, we find no error in the district court’s judgment. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that statutory penalties, as distinguished from overtime wages and liquidated damages, would be payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this juncture in the case, we harbor significant doubts as to our jurisdiction to resolve this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can “provide relief to citizen plaintiffs who have suffered or are at risk of harm due to ongoing illegal activity,” since such penalties may “prompt defendants to cease their current violations and dissuade them from engaging in future offenses.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must establish standing for every claim presented and each type of remedy requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Further, as previously set forth, “[t]he necessary personal interest that must be present when litigation is initiated (standing) is required to persist throughout the entire litigation (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties may become moot if later developments demonstrate conclusively that there is no reasonable expectation of the alleged wrongful conduct recurring, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, as established precedent holds, “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received full payment for all overtime wages and liquidated damages. However, whether Consilio continues to maintain the overtime policy alleged to be in violation remains a contested issue. Should Consilio’s policy still be in effect, Cohen’s request for civil penalties could remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the challenged policy and it is “absolutely clear” that such conduct will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s order granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not provide the mandatory statutory notice of a modification to its overtime policy and asserts that this entitles him to statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court failed to adequately discuss this claim. However, Cohen agreed that he has been fully paid all sums owed to him by Consilio and has not identified any statutory provision that permits civil penalties under this section. Since Cohen does not present a substantive argument in favor of reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of his claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen's request for injunctive relief, his argument is limited to asserting that he should receive "an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy." Initially, Cohen's complaint requested certain specified injunctive remedies, but he later altered his position during summary judgment briefing by seeking a different form of relief—specifically, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, reasonably, did not address this newly requested relief. Consequently, this matter is not properly before this court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that "[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs"); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the matter back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the district court’s decision. Circuit Judge Kobes writes separately in concurrence.

I am not persuaded that Cohen satisfies the standing requirements to pursue statutory penalties as set out in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). He may have standing, but distinctions exist between the penalties considered in that precedent and those provided by the MFLSA. The parties did not address this issue in their briefs. Additionally, I would set aside the district court's orders concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I also concur that Cohen’s claim to MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, and therefore, I agree with vacating the judgment and remanding. Upon remand, the district court may address the mootness issue as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
