Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions with overtime pay. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal. We partially affirm, but also vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio, a legal document review firm operating across the country and maintaining offices in Minnesota, hired Cohen in 2018 as an hourly attorney to review documents. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other hourly, licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota via email that a revised overtime compensation policy would take effect in August 2019. Under this new policy, overtime premium payments would be discontinued, and it was clarified that Cohen and the other employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio on his own behalf and for a proposed class, contending that Consilio did not pay overtime wages allegedly owed to him and others in similar positions. Cohen claimed that Consilio's conduct constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief the award of overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers in order to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages requested based on Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA. Of this amount, Cohen himself received $3,225.21 in overtime pay and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties jointly agreed that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and further stipulated that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, they were unable to resolve their disagreement regarding penalties. Cohen contended that he was eligible to recover $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, as well as $42,000 in penalties under both the MFLSA and MWTA. Consilio, conversely, maintained that the relevant statutes did not authorize an award of penalties to Cohen.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed by stipulation that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio both “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We examine the district court's summary judgment in favor of Consilio using a de novo standard, considering the record and any reasonable inferences from it in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). According to Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)), the entry of summary judgment is appropriate when there exists no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central disagreement between the parties is whether Cohen is entitled to “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of certain MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101. When an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, it becomes “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Furthermore, an employee may recover civil penalties or damages so long as they are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer allegedly breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and contends that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to pursue penalties calculated on the basis of average daily wages. However, § 181.101(a) identifies only the Commissioner as having authority over penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, such penalties become available only if the Commissioner first “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Following this demand, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees, the statutory language expressly limits them to the ability to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (providing that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated, when it so chose, that it could grant a statutory right to employees to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that, upon demand, a discharged employee may recover unpaid wages or commissions and additionally “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same right for employees who quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). As we interpret § 181.101 to mean that only the Commissioner is authorized to seek daily average wage penalties, the district court’s decision was not mistaken. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages required under the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen maintains that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that, contrary to overtime wages and liquidated damages, statutory penalties—if imposed—are awarded to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture, we harbor significant doubt about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of whether they are remitted to the government, can “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs harmed or facing harm as a result of ongoing illegal activity” since such penalties may “motivate defendants to cease existing violations and dissuade them from future infractions.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff is required to establish standing for every claim they assert and for each type of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously discussed, “[t]he essential personal stake needed at the outset of a lawsuit (standing) must persist throughout its duration (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments demonstrate beyond doubt that the conduct at issue is not likely to occur again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, it is a firmly established principle that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its summary judgment motion, both parties agreed that Cohen had received all overtime pay and liquidated damages owed; however, whether Consilio continues to maintain the overtime policy in question remains contested. Should Consilio’s challenged policy still be in effect, Cohen could potentially maintain a viable claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that the unlawful behavior will not resume, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Moreover, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to address whether it retains jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not give him the notice required by statute regarding a modification to its overtime policy and asserts that this omission entitles him to penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court for providing "little to no discussion" of this issue. Nevertheless, Cohen agreed that he had been paid all amounts owed by Consilio, and he does not identify any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties for such a violation under this statute. In the absence of any substantive argument advocating for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole contention is that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” In his initial complaint, Cohen asked for particular types of injunctive relief, but later, during summary judgment proceedings, he shifted to seeking alternative relief by asking for withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court did not address this newly requested relief, which is understandable. Accordingly, this matter is not appropriately before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as outlined in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible he does, there are distinctions between the penalties awarded in that case and those permissible under the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this issue in their briefing. Additionally, I vacate the district court’s decisions concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may have become moot, and therefore concur in vacating the judgment and remanding the matter. Upon remand, the district court may first address the question of mootness. Id. at 180.
