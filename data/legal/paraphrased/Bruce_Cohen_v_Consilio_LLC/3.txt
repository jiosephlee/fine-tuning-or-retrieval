Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed this action asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions with overtime pay. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals that decision. We partially affirm, but also partially vacate and remand.

I.

Consilio, a legal document review firm operating across the country and maintaining offices in Minnesota, employed Cohen starting in 2018 as a licensed attorney document reviewer on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio informed Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota via email that it would be implementing a revised premium overtime policy beginning in August 2019. Under this updated policy, overtime premium pay would be discontinued, and it was clarified that Cohen and the other affected employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both individually and as a representative of a proposed class, contending that Consilio had not compensated him and other similarly situated employees for overtime work performed. According to Cohen, these actions contravened the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief the payment of overtime wages, an award of liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers, addressing the overtime pay and liquidated damages sought in connection with Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Cohen himself received $3,225.21 in overtime compensation and $462 in liquidated damages as a portion of these funds. The parties jointly agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and further stipulated that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties were unable to resolve their disagreement regarding penalties. Cohen asserted entitlement to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Consilio, on the other hand, maintained that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that "the most expedient and efficient way to proceed [would be] for the Court to address and rule on Defendants’ summary judgment motion regarding [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before engaging in any additional discovery or litigation." Both Cohen and Consilio "recognize[d] that if the Court were to grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, all claims w[ould] be eliminated and the case w[ould] be completely concluded."

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We examine the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio using a de novo standard, considering the evidence and all reasonable inferences drawn therefrom in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is warranted when there are no genuine disputes as to any material facts and the party seeking judgment is entitled to it as a matter of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central issue between the parties is whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” as outlined in §§ 181.171 and 181.101. According to § 181.171, “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified provisions of the MPWA, including § 181.101. It further provides that if an employer “is found to have violated” any such provision, the employer becomes “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, the statute allows for recovery of civil penalties or damages by an employee, provided these are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer is accused of breaching. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and asserts that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner in connection with the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties become available exclusively if the Commissioner initially “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). After this, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. In addition, “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees, the statute solely preserves the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated, when it chose, the ability to grant statutory authority to an employee to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that when a terminated employee makes a demand, that employee is entitled to recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same right for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that § 181.101 authorizes only the Commissioner to pursue daily average wage penalties, the district court's decision was not erroneous. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that § 181.101(a) “limits the ability to enforce a penalty to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he is additionally entitled to statutory penalties. Both parties concur that such statutory penalties—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are owed to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture in the litigation, we harbor significant reservations about our jurisdiction to address this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” as such sanctions can “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Further, as previously stated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if later developments made it completely certain that the allegedly wrongful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to occur again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)), but “[i]t is firmly established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of the disputed conduct does not strip a federal court of authority to rule on the legality of that conduct,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties agreed that Cohen had received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages. However, there is still disagreement regarding whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy is currently in effect. Should the policy persist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties could remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that Consilio’s improper conduct will not reoccur, Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA might be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts do not have the “license to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess its subject-matter jurisdiction.

C.

We turn next to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, wherein he contends that Consilio did not furnish the statutory notice required when it changed its overtime policy, and consequently asserts his entitlement to penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court offered “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that he received all wages owed by Consilio and has not identified any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this statute. Because he presents no substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his argument is limited to asserting a right to “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint asked for specific injunctive remedies, but he later shifted his position in summary judgment briefing to seek different relief—namely, withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, for understandable reasons, did not address this new request. Therefore, the matter is not properly before this court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). It is possible that he does, but distinctions exist between the penalties considered in that decision and those authorized by the MFLSA. The parties did not address this issue in their briefing. Nevertheless, I also set aside the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s pursuit of MFLSA statutory penalties could potentially be moot, so I join in vacating the judgment and sending the case back to the lower court. On remand, the district court may address the mootness issue as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
