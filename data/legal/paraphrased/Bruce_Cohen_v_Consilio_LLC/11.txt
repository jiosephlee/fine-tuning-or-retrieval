Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed this action, contending that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, leading Cohen to seek appellate review. We partially affirm the district court’s decision, and partially vacate and remand the case for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a national legal document review firm and maintains locations within Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen was hired by Consilio as an hourly paid attorney for document review. In July 2019, Consilio emailed Cohen, along with other Minnesota-based hourly attorney document reviewers, to notify them of an upcoming premium overtime policy, scheduled to take effect in August 2019. This revised policy removed overtime premium compensation and specified that Cohen and the other employees would be compensated “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both on his own behalf and for a proposed class, claiming Consilio did not pay overtime wages that were due to him and to employees in similar positions. Cohen asserted that Consilio’s conduct violated the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief the recovery of overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, as well as injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers in response to overtime wage and liquidated damage demands asserted under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Of that sum, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 for overtime wages and an additional $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed through stipulation that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and also that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties were unable to reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen contended he was owed $172,080 in penalties calculated as average daily wages under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and another $42,000 pursuant to the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio maintained that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover such penalties.

After Consilio made the payments, the parties agreed that "the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation." Cohen and Consilio both "acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved."

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering the record and any reasonable inferences drawn therefrom in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted if there is no dispute over any material fact and the moving party is entitled to prevail as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin with Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central dispute between the parties concerns whether Cohen can recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes a person to file a civil lawsuit for relief if certain MPWA provisions, including § 181.101, are violated, specifying that employers found in violation “shall be liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee may obtain civil penalties or damages only if they are specifically “provided for in the section” that the employer allegedly breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, asserting that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allow employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) solely mentions the Commissioner in connection with penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties become available only after the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If wages are not paid within ten days following that demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). As for employees, the statute only allows them to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, its ability to grant employees who have been wronged a statutory right to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that a terminated employee, upon making a demand, may obtain unpaid wages or commissions in addition to having the right to “charge and collect a penalty”); see also id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who have quit or resigned); cf. Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s choice to act in one statute while remaining silent in another indicates it ‘knows how’ to achieve a particular legislative goal when it intends” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Therefore, given our determination that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 belongs solely to the Commissioner, the district court did not commit error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that under § 181.101(a), “the right to enforce a penalty is reserved for the [Commissioner], and any recovered penalty is paid to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has conceded that Consilio has paid him all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages in accordance with the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that such statutory penalties—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—must be paid to the Commissioner and not to Cohen. At this point in the litigation, we harbor significant reservations about our authority to rule on this matter.

Civil penalties, even when directed to the government, may "provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who have been harmed or face harm from ongoing illegal activities" because these penalties can "motivate defendants to stop existing violations and discourage future breaches." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, "a plaintiff is required to establish standing for every claim they assert and for each type of remedy sought." Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously stated, "[t]he necessary personal stake that must be present when the lawsuit is initiated (standing) is required to persist for the entire duration of the case (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could become moot if subsequent developments rendered it entirely certain that the challenged conduct was not likely to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, it is established law that a federal court retains jurisdiction to rule on the legality of a practice even if the defendant has voluntarily discontinued the conduct at issue, id. (citing City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio moved for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all overtime compensation and liquidated damages owed, though whether Consilio continues to maintain its noncompliant overtime policy is a contested issue. Should that policy persist, Cohen may still have a viable civil penalty claim. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio’s policy has been discontinued and it is "absolutely clear" the wrongful acts will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could indeed be moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts do not have the “license to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we vacate the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand so the district court may assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We turn next to Cohen’s MWTA claim in which he contends that Consilio did not give him the notice of a change to its overtime policy as required by statute, entitling him to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court failed to adequately consider this claim. Nevertheless, Cohen agreed that Consilio paid him everything he was owed, and he does not cite any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties for this alleged violation. Since Cohen offers no substantive argument for overturning the district court’s dismissal of this claim, we decline to disturb the district court’s decision. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he deserves “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen requested specific types of injunctive remedies, but he later altered his position during summary judgment briefing by seeking different relief—specifically, withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court—understandably—did not address this new request. Consequently, this matter is not appropriately before us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that standing exists, the statutory penalties at issue in that case differ from those provided by the MFLSA. Neither side has addressed this issue in their briefs. Furthermore, I also set aside the district court's decision regarding attorney's fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I acknowledge that Cohen's pursuit of MFLSA statutory penalties could potentially be moot; therefore, I concur in vacating the judgment and remanding. Upon remand, the district court may give priority to resolving the mootness question. Id. at 180.
