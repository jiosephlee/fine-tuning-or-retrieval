Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions for overtime work. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals that decision. We affirm part of the district court’s judgment and, in part, vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm across the United States, maintaining offices in Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen commenced employment with Consilio, serving as a licensed attorney document reviewer compensated on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email communication to Cohen and other Minnesota hourly licensed attorney document reviewers, notifying them of the implementation of a revised premium overtime policy effective August 2019. Under this revised policy, overtime premium pay was discontinued, and it was clarified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio for himself and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio failed to pay overtime wages that were due to him and other employees in similar circumstances. Cohen asserted that Consilio’s conduct constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen sought compensation for overtime, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and also requested injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other attorney document reviewers licensed in Minnesota to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within this amount, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 in overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen "has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit," and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." However, the parties remained in disagreement with respect to penalties. Cohen maintained he was eligible to receive $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that these statutes did not authorize the imposition of penalties in Cohen's favor.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [was] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before engaging in further discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio further “acknowledge[d] that, should the Court grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, there w[ould] be no remaining claims and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

Our review of the district court’s summary judgment decision in favor of Consilio is de novo, and we consider the evidence—as well as any reasonable inferences arising from it—in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted where no genuine dispute of material fact exists and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central disagreement between the parties concerns Cohen’s eligibility to recover “average daily wage penalties” as set forth in §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes individuals to initiate a civil action to seek remedies for violations of specified MPWA provisions, among them § 181.101. Under this statute, an employer that is determined to have breached such provisions “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, the statute allows employees to seek civil penalties or damages, provided such remedies are explicitly included within the particular MPWA section the employer is accused of violating. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, contending that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allows employees to pursue average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) specifically references only the Commissioner with respect to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). Penalties are not available unless the Commissioner initially “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If wages are still unpaid within ten days after this demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. All “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees, the statute limits them to “prosecut[ing] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it intends to do so, that it can grant employees a statutory entitlement to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (noting that after making a demand, a terminated employee is permitted to recover owed wages or commissions and "may charge and collect a penalty"); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that "the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so" (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Therefore, since we find that under § 181.101, the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties is reserved exclusively to the Commissioner, the district court's decision was not incorrect. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that, pursuant to § 181.101(a), "the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee").

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged that Consilio has paid him all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should receive statutory penalties in addition. Both parties concur that any such statutory penalties would be payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen, distinguishing them from overtime wages and liquidated damages. At this procedural juncture, we harbor substantial doubt concerning our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of their payment to the government, can still “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” since such penalties may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, it is established that “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if subsequent developments made it entirely certain that the supposedly wrongful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to occur again, Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “it is well settled that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties reached an agreement that Cohen had received full payment of all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages; however, there remains an unresolved issue concerning whether Consilio’s noncompliant overtime policy is still being enforced. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties could still proceed. See id. at 186. On the other hand, if Consilio has rescinded its policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the prior wrongful conduct will not resume, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts do not have “license[] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s order granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the case for the district court to assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the notification regarding a change in its overtime policy as mandated by statute, and thus, he seeks statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court’s decision “included little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that he received all compensation owed by Consilio and fails to identify any statutory basis authorizing civil penalties under the cited statute. As Cohen does not present a substantive argument to support reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint sought particular forms of injunctive relief, but he later shifted positions during summary judgment briefing by seeking different relief—specifically, revocation of the 2019 policy. The district court, for understandable reasons, did not address this request. As a result, this matter is not properly before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen has standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that Cohen may have standing, there are distinctions between the penalties considered in that case and those available under the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this question in their briefs. Additionally, I vacate the district court’s decisions regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s claim for statutory penalties under the MFLSA could be moot, and therefore agree with vacating the judgment and remanding the matter. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to address the issue of mootness in the first instance. Id. at 180.
