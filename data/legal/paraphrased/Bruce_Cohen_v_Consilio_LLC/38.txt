Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed suit, claiming that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not compensate him and other comparable workers for overtime hours. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal. We partly affirm the district court’s decision, but in part we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm with a national presence, including locations in Minnesota. Cohen started his employment with Consilio in 2018, serving as a document reviewer who was both licensed as an attorney and paid on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly situated attorney document reviewers in Minnesota by email that a new overtime policy would be implemented starting in August 2019. Under this revised policy, the company discontinued premium pay for overtime and specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation "at [their] base pay rate" for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated litigation against Consilio on his own behalf and for a proposed class, claiming that Consilio failed to provide him and other similarly situated workers with overtime compensation they were owed. Cohen contended that Consilio’s conduct breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested, as relief, payment of overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime wages and liquidated damages sought in Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within these payments, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 as overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties reached an impasse regarding penalties. Cohen contended he was eligible for $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In response, Consilio asserted that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio summary judgment as to all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering all evidence and any reasonable inferences from that evidence in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when there are no genuine disputes as to any material facts and the moving party deserves judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We turn first to Cohen's claim under the MPWA. The central issue between the parties is whether Cohen is entitled to pursue "average daily wage penalties" pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that "[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations" of specified portions of the MPWA, such as § 181.101, and further specifies that an employer "is found to have violated" one of these provisions will be "liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief." Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, the statute permits an employee to recover either civil penalties or damages, provided those remedies are "provided for in the section" of the MPWA that the employer is alleged to have breached. Id. ("An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.").

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to claim average daily wage penalties. However, the text of § 181.101(a) exclusively mentions the Commissioner in connection with the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). Penalties may be imposed only if the Commissioner initially “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If wages are not paid within ten days following this demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Any funds recovered by the Commissioner “must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees, the statute restricts them to “prosecut[ing] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, had the Minnesota legislature intended, it was capable of granting employees a statutory right to seek penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee, upon making a demand, can recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties pursuant to § 181.101 is reserved exclusively to the Commissioner, the district court acted appropriately. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (indicating that under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid him all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that such statutory penalties—contrary to overtime wages and liquidated damages—are designated for the Commissioner and not for Cohen. At this point in the case, we are significantly concerned about whether we possess jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, despite being payable to the government, can nevertheless "provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are harmed or face the threat of harm from ongoing unlawful actions," since such penalties might "motivate defendants to cease ongoing violations and discourage them from future infractions." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to assert and for each type of relief sought." Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously stated, "[t]he required personal interest which must be present when the litigation is initiated (standing) must persist during the entirety of the lawsuit (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if later developments made it indisputably clear that the alleged wrongful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to happen again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “it is a well-established principle that ‘a defendant’s decision to voluntarily stop the contested behavior does not remove a federal court’s authority to rule on whether the practice was legal,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties had reached agreement that Cohen had received all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages. However, there remains a contested issue regarding whether Consilio continues to maintain the overtime policy alleged to be in violation of the law. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties could still proceed. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the objectionable conduct will not occur again, Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA could become moot. Id. at 189. Additionally, federal courts do not have authority to exercise jurisdiction over cases in which “one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Consequently, we set aside the district court’s order granting summary judgment regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter for the district court to address the question of its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not furnish the notice mandated by statute concerning a change in its overtime policy, and that consequently he is owed penalties. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court gave minimal, if any, attention to this claim in its discussion. Nevertheless, Cohen acknowledged by stipulation that he has received all compensation due from Consilio, and he fails to cite any statutory provision that permits civil penalties in connection with this statute. Lacking any substantial argument to challenge the judgment, we decline to overturn the district court’s decision dismissing this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he maintains that he is entitled to “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint requested particular forms of injunctive relief, but he subsequently altered his position during summary judgment briefing, seeking instead to have the 2019 policy withdrawn. The district court, for understandable reasons, did not address this newly framed request. Consequently, this matter has not been properly presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision with respect to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the matter for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the district court’s judgment. KOBES, Circuit Judge, writing separately in concurrence.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties based on Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible he does, the penalties considered there differ from those provided under the MFLSA. Neither side addressed this topic in their briefs. Additionally, I vacate the district court’s determination on attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for statutory penalties under the MFLSA could now be moot; accordingly, I join the decision to vacate the judgment and remand. Upon remand, the district court may address the issue of mootness as a threshold matter. Id. at 180.
