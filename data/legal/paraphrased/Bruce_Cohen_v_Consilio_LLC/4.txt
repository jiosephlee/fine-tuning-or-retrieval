Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not provide overtime compensation to him or other employees in comparable positions. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, and Cohen now appeals that decision. We uphold the judgment in certain respects, but in other respects, we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a national legal document review firm and maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen started his employment with Consilio in 2018, serving as a licensed attorney document reviewer on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers via email that, beginning in August 2019, a revised premium overtime policy would take effect. Under this new policy, overtime premium compensation was discontinued, and Consilio specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio on his own behalf and that of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio had not paid him and other similarly situated employees the overtime compensation to which they were entitled. Cohen claimed that Consilio’s conduct was in violation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief the payment of overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Of this sum, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 for overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and further stipulated that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, they reached an impasse regarding penalties. Cohen asserted a right to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and another $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that the most effective and prompt way to proceed was for the Court to address and rule on Defendants’ motion for summary judgment concerning the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims prior to engaging in additional discovery or further litigation. Cohen and Consilio both recognized that, should the Court grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, there would be no remaining claims and the matter would be completely resolved.

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering all evidence and reasonable inferences therefrom in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is warranted where there exists no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the party moving for summary judgment is entitled to prevail as a matter of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree over whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes individuals to file a civil action seeking remedies for breaches of specified MPWA provisions, including § 181.101, and further states that when an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, it shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, employees may obtain civil penalties or damages, but only if such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA at issue. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allows employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) refers exclusively to the Commissioner in relation to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties can only be imposed after the Commissioner has “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Should wages remain unpaid ten days after such service, the statute authorizes the commissioner to “charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). The statute, with respect to employees, preserves solely the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, if the Minnesota legislature intended, it was capable of granting an aggrieved employee a statutory right to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that, upon demand by a discharged employee, that individual may recover both unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (offering the same right to employees who voluntarily leave employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s choice to act in one statute but not another indicates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a particular purpose when it chooses to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we find that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court’s decision was not in error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that § 181.101(a) confines “the ability to enforce a penalty” to the Commissioner, with any penalties collected being remitted to the employee).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen contends that he is further entitled to statutory penalties. The parties concur that, unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages, any statutory penalties awarded would be remitted to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this point in the litigation, we harbor significant doubt about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a result of ongoing unlawful conduct,” as these penalties can “encourage defendants to cease present violations and serve to deter future infractions.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff must establish standing for every claim pursued and for each specific form of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously stated, “[t]he necessary personal interest (standing) required at the outset of litigation must persist for the duration of the action (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could become moot if later developments made it unmistakably certain that the purportedly wrongful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to occur again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “it is well settled that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its summary judgment motion, both sides acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, the parties continue to dispute whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy persists. Should Consilio’s policy still exist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties could remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that Consilio will not engage in similar misconduct again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may indeed be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts, moreover, do not have the “license to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment with respect to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter so the district court may assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, wherein he asserts that Consilio did not provide the required statutory notice regarding a change in its overtime policy, and that he is consequently entitled to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court offered minimal or no analysis of this claim. However, Cohen has agreed that he received all compensation owed by Consilio, and he fails to identify any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties pursuant to this statute. In the absence of substantive argument supporting reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s decision to dismiss this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his argument is limited to asserting that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint sought particular injunctive remedies, but he later shifted his position during summary judgment briefing, seeking withdrawal of the 2019 policy instead. The district court, understandably, did not address this revised request. Consequently, the matter is not appropriately before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as set forth in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that he does, there are distinctions between the penalties at issue in that decision and those under the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this matter in their briefs. Furthermore, I also vacate the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s pursuit of MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, and therefore I join in vacating the judgment and remanding the proceedings. Upon remand, the district court has the discretion to address the mootness issue as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
