Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not provide overtime compensation owed to him and other employees in comparable positions. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, from which Cohen now appeals. We uphold the district court's decision in part and reverse and remand in part.

I.

Consilio operates as a national legal document review firm and maintains locations in Minnesota. Cohen started his employment with Consilio in 2018, serving as a document reviewer and licensed attorney compensated on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly situated Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers via email that, beginning in August 2019, it would implement a revised overtime compensation policy. Under this new policy, the company discontinued the payment of overtime premiums and specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both individually and as a representative of a proposed class, claiming that Consilio did not pay him and other employees in similar situations the overtime wages they were due. Cohen asserted that this conduct breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested relief in the form of unpaid overtime, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction.

Consilio paid a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers licensed as attorneys in Minnesota to satisfy the overtime wages and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Of that amount, Cohen himself received $3,225.21 as overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties could not reach a consensus concerning penalties. Cohen claimed entitlement to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, as well as $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the relevant statutes do not allow Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made the required payments, the parties agreed and stipulated that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court proceeded to grant Consilio's summary judgment motion on all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We examine the district court’s decision to award summary judgment to Consilio under a de novo standard, assessing the evidence, along with any reasonable inferences that can be drawn, in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when no genuine dispute exists as to any material fact, and the party requesting summary judgment has shown entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree over whether Cohen is permitted to recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101, and further specifies that when an employer “is found to have violated” such a provision, the employer shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Employees may additionally obtain civil penalties or damages, but only if such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is said to have been violated. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen claims that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and contends that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to pursue penalties equal to their average daily wages. However, § 181.101(a) speaks only of the Commissioner in the context of imposing penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, the statute authorizes penalties solely when the Commissioner first “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, should wages not be paid within ten days after such service, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees, the statute solely preserves the option to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, its ability to grant an employee harmed by a violation the statutory authority to seek penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee, upon demand, may recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same remedy to employees who voluntarily quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue penalties based on daily average wages pursuant to § 181.101, there was no error by the district court. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid him all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages in accordance with the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen contends that he should receive statutory penalties as well. Both parties concur that, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, statutory penalties—if imposed—are to be paid to the Commissioner rather than Cohen. At this juncture in the litigation, we harbor significant reservations about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of their payment to the government, can “provide relief to citizen plaintiffs who suffer or face harm as a result of ongoing illegal actions” since such sanctions may “motivate defendants to cease present violations and deter them from future misconduct.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff is required to establish standing for each claim asserted and for each type of remedy that is requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously set forth, “[t]he necessary personal stake that must be present when the lawsuit is filed (standing) must persist for the duration of the case (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments made it unmistakably evident that the alleged misconduct is not reasonably likely to happen again, Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, “it is well established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary discontinuation of a disputed practice does not remove a federal court’s authority to decide the legality of that conduct,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its summary judgment motion, both parties stipulated that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; nonetheless, the ongoing existence of Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy is contested. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen may still maintain a claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the misconduct will not be repeated, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Moreover, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter so that the district court may consider its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio violated statutory requirements by not providing notice regarding changes to its overtime policy, thereby entitling him to statutory penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court for offering “little to no discussion of this claim.” Nevertheless, Cohen agreed that he has been paid in full for all sums Consilio owed him, and he fails to cite any provision in the statute permitting the imposition of civil penalties under these circumstances. As Cohen does not offer substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, the complaint specified certain types of injunctive relief, but Cohen later altered his request, seeking different relief—namely, the rescission of the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing. The district court, for good reason, did not address this request. Consequently, this matter has not been properly presented to us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the case for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the district court’s judgment. Circuit Judge Kobes files a special concurrence.

I remain unpersuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as described in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that standing exists, distinctions can be drawn between the penalties in that matter and those under the MFLSA. The standing issue was not addressed in the parties’ briefing. Additionally, I concur in setting aside the district court’s determinations on attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could be rendered moot, supporting the decision to vacate the judgment and remand the case. Upon remand, the district court may appropriately address the mootness issue as a preliminary matter. See id. at 180.
