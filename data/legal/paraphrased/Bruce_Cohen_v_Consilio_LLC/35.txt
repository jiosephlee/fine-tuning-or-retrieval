Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not pay him and other employees in comparable positions overtime compensation. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen’s appeal. We affirm the district court’s decision in part and vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review company with locations across the United States, including in Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen started his employment with Consilio as a licensed attorney performing document review work on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio emailed Cohen and other similarly situated hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota, informing them that it planned to implement a new overtime pay policy starting in August 2019. Under this new policy, premium pay for overtime would be discontinued, and it was made clear that Cohen and the other employees would receive compensation "at [their] base pay rate" for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both for himself and as a representative of a proposed class, contending that Consilio did not provide overtime compensation owed to him and other similarly situated employees. Cohen claimed that Consilio’s conduct was in violation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen sought as relief the payment of overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers licensed as attorneys in Minnesota, satisfying the demanded overtime compensation and liquidated damages under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Cohen individually received payments consisting of $3,225.21 in overtime wages and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties entered into a stipulation acknowledging that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and agreeing that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties were unable to reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen contended he was owed $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Consilio responded that these statutes did not allow for an award of penalties to Cohen.

After Consilio made the payments, both parties agreed that the most straightforward and effective way to proceed would be for the Court to address and resolve Defendants’ summary judgment motion concerning the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims prior to engaging in any further discovery or litigation. Cohen and Consilio further recognized and agreed that if the Court granted Defendants' summary judgment motion in its entirety, all claims would be extinguished, thereby fully concluding the matter.

The district court subsequently granted summary judgment in favor of Consilio on each of the three claims. Cohen now appeals that decision.

II.

Our review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment in favor of Consilio is conducted de novo, considering the record and all reasonable inferences drawn therefrom in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when there exists no genuine dispute concerning any material fact and the party seeking summary judgment is entitled to prevail as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. There is disagreement between the parties regarding whether Cohen is permitted to pursue "average daily wage penalties" pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of particular provisions of the MPWA, including § 181.101. In cases where an employer “is found to have violated” such a provision, the employer is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee is entitled to recover civil penalties or damages, but only if those remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer allegedly contravened. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and asserts that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” entitle employees to average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) specifically mentions the Commissioner, and not employees, in connection with penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, imposition of penalties is contingent upon the Commissioner first “serv[ing] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Should the wages remain unpaid for ten days after the demand is served, then “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Concerning the employee's role, the statute expressly grants only the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated that, if it chooses, it possesses the ability to grant a statutory right to an aggrieved employee to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that a terminated employee, upon demand, may obtain unpaid wages or commissions and may also “charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (establishing the same rights for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that when the Legislature takes action in one statute and not in another, it “shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a particular result if it desires to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since our determination is that only the Commissioner has authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, there was no error by the district court. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (clarifying that under § 181.101(a), “enforcement of a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], and any amounts recovered are directed to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen acknowledged by stipulation that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages to which he is entitled under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should additionally receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that any statutory penalties, unlike overtime compensation and liquidated damages, are awarded to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this point in the litigation, we harbor substantial doubts as to our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or face the threat of injury resulting from continued illegal activity” by defendants, as such sanctions may “motivate defendants to cease ongoing infractions and dissuade them from repeating such actions in the future.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff is required to establish standing for each individual claim brought and for every type of relief pursued.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously stated, “[t]he necessary personal stake (standing) that is required at the onset of litigation must persist throughout the duration of the case (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could be rendered moot if later developments made it entirely clear that the purportedly improper conduct could not reasonably be expected to occur again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “it is firmly established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary discontinuance of the challenged conduct does not strip a federal court of its authority to rule on the legality of that conduct,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both sides acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages; however, there is still disagreement regarding whether Consilio’s overtime policy that allegedly violates the law is ongoing. Should Consilio’s policy still exist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties may be sustainable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio’s policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” the challenged conduct will not resume, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could indeed be moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s order granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand so the district court may assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the required statutory notice regarding a modification to its overtime policy and therefore he is owed penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends the district court gave minimal or no analysis to this claim. Nonetheless, Cohen has admitted through stipulation that he has received all compensation due from Consilio, and he does not cite any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties under this section. Since he offers no substantive argument advocating reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he maintains solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen requested particular forms of injunctive relief, but subsequently altered his position, instead seeking withdrawal of the 2019 policy in his summary judgment brief. The district court, for understandable reasons, did not address this changed request. Consequently, this matter is not properly before us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the case for additional proceedings; in all other respects, we affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, writing separately in concurrence.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties according to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Perhaps he does, but the penalties discussed in Laidlaw differ from those available under the MFLSA. The parties did not address this issue in their briefs. I further vacate the district court’s decision concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could potentially be moot; thus, I agree with the decision to vacate the judgment and remand. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to address the mootness issue initially. Id. at 180.
