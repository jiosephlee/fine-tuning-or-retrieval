Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals. We uphold the district court's decision in part, but also vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm with locations across the country, including Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen was hired by Consilio to serve as an hourly attorney document reviewer. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other hourly attorney document reviewers in Minnesota by email that, starting in August 2019, it would implement a revised overtime policy. Under this policy, overtime premium pay was discontinued, and it was made clear that Cohen and the other affected employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both on his own behalf and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not pay him and other similarly situated employees the overtime wages to which they were entitled. Cohen contended that these alleged failures constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. As relief, Cohen requested payment of overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers, covering both the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within this amount, Cohen himself received $3,225.21 for overtime work and an additional $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties remained at odds regarding the issue of penalties. Cohen maintained that he was owed $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the statutes in question did not authorize the recovery of penalties by Cohen.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed by stipulation that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] prior to any additional discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio both “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, there w[ould] be no remaining claims and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio, considering the record and all reasonable inferences drawn from it in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is warranted where there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes “[a] person [to] bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of designated sections of the MPWA, such as § 181.101, and specifies that if an employer “is found to have violated” any such provision, it shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. The statute also allows an employee to obtain civil penalties or damages, provided that such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that was allegedly breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, asserting that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to pursue penalties based on average daily wages. However, § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner in relation to the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may be assessed only if the Commissioner first “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Following such service, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. In addition, “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees, the statute specifically allows only for the “prosecut[ion] [of] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (noting that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated that it can grant employees a statutory right to seek penalties when it chooses to do so. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that, after a discharged employee makes a demand, the employee is entitled to recover unpaid wages or commissions and may also “charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who have quit or resigned); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s enactment of a provision in one statute but not in another indicates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a specific result when it intends to” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court acted properly. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that § 181.101(a) “limits the authority to enforce a penalty to the [Commissioner], with any funds recovered going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen acknowledged that Consilio has already paid him all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that such statutory penalties, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, are remitted to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this point in the litigation, we are substantially concerned about our jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Even when civil penalties are directed to the government, they can still “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” as these penalties may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously stated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments make it indisputably clear that the conduct alleged to be unlawful is not likely to occur again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, as has long been established, “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties concurred that Cohen had received all due overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, the issue of whether Consilio continues to maintain its unlawful overtime policy is still contested. Should Consilio's policy still be operative, Cohen’s request for civil penalties might remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the improper conduct will not occur again, then Cohen’s MFLSA claim could indeed be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment with respect to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand so that the district court may address the issue of its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not provide the notice mandated by statute regarding a modification to its overtime policy, thereby entitling him to statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court offered minimal, if any, analysis of this issue. However, Cohen agreed that he had received all compensation due from Consilio and does not cite any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this law. Given the absence of substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint sought particular types of injunctive relief, but he subsequently shifted his position during summary judgment briefing by seeking the withdrawal of the 2019 policy instead. The district court, for understandable reasons, did not address this new request. Accordingly, the matter is not properly before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties under Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). It is possible that he does, but distinctions exist between the penalties at issue in that matter and those provided by the MFLSA. The parties did not address this question in their briefs. Additionally, I vacate the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may be moot, and thus concur with the decision to vacate the judgment and remand the matter. Upon remand, the district court may consider the issue of mootness as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
