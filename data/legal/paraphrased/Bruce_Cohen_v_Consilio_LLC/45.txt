Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, asserting that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not provide overtime compensation to him and other employees in similar positions. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen has appealed this decision. We affirm the judgment in part, while in part we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio, a national legal document review firm that maintains offices in Minnesota, hired Cohen in 2018 to serve as an hourly document reviewer with a license to practice law. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other Minnesota-based licensed attorney document reviewers by email that it would implement a revised overtime pay policy effective August 2019. This updated policy removed the provision for overtime premium compensation and stated explicitly that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for each hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated litigation against Consilio, both individually and as a representative of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not provide him and other similarly situated workers with required overtime compensation. Cohen contended that such conduct breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested, as relief, overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorneys serving as document reviewers, fulfilling the demands for overtime compensation and liquidated damages made under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within these distributions, Cohen himself obtained $3,225.21 in overtime pay along with $462 allocated as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed by stipulation that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, consensus could not be reached regarding penalties. Cohen contended that he was owed $172,080 as penalties for average daily wage violations under the MPWA, as well as $42,000 in penalties under both the MFLSA and MWTA. Consilio, on the other hand, asserted that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover any penalties.

After Consilio made the required payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted summary judgment in Consilio’s favor on all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering all evidence and any reasonable inferences therefrom in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). The standard for granting summary judgment is met when there exist no genuine disputes as to any material facts and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin with Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree over whether Cohen is entitled to recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified parts of the MPWA, including § 181.101, and that if an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, the employer is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. The statute also allows an employee to seek civil penalties or damages, as long as such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that was allegedly breached by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, contending that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, the text of § 181.101(a) refers only to the Commissioner in relation to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). Penalties under this section may only be imposed once the Commissioner has “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). As for employees, the statute only preserves their right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated, when it intended, that it was capable of granting an employee a statutory entitlement to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that, upon request, a terminated employee may recover owed wages or commissions and additionally “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (containing similar provisions for employees who voluntarily separate from employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). As we determine that under § 181.101 only the Commissioner has authority to seek daily average wage penalties, the district court's decision was not mistaken. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that pursuant to § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has conceded that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages owed pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he should receive statutory penalties as well. Both sides acknowledge that statutory penalties, in contrast to overtime pay and liquidated damages, are awarded to the Commissioner and not to Cohen. Given the current posture of the case, we have substantial doubt as to our jurisdiction to adjudicate this question.

Civil penalties, regardless of being remitted to the government, may still “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” since they can “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously discussed, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments made it entirely certain that the challenged conduct could not reasonably be expected to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nonetheless, as established precedent provides, “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (citing City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages. Nonetheless, the issue of whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy is still in effect remains unresolved. Should the policy continue to exist, Cohen may still have a valid claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has rescinded the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the misconduct will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Moreover, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we vacate the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to determine whether it possesses jurisdiction.

C.

We turn now to Cohen's claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the statutory notice required concerning a change to its overtime policy, and therefore, he argues that he should receive penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court for providing “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that he received every payment owed by Consilio, and he does not identify any statutory basis authorizing civil penalties under this provision. As Cohen fails to present a substantive argument supporting reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he maintains solely that he deserves “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint asked for particular types of injunctive remedies, but he later altered his position in the summary judgment briefing to instead seek the rescission of the 2019 policy. The district court, unsurprisingly, did not address this revised request. Consequently, this matter is not properly presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s order concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the matter for additional proceedings. All other aspects of the judgment are affirmed. KOBES, Circuit Judge, writing separately in concurrence.

I remain unpersuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as described in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that such standing exists, distinctions do exist between the penalties considered in Laidlaw and those provided under the MFLSA. Neither party addressed this standing issue in their briefs. Additionally, I set aside the district court’s determination regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that the question of mootness regarding Cohen’s claim for MFLSA statutory penalties is a live issue, thus I join in vacating the district court’s judgment and sending the case back. Upon remand, the district court has discretion to address the mootness issue first. See id. at 180.
