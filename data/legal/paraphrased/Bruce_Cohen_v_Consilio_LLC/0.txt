Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, contending that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in similar positions for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal. We uphold the judgment in part, but in part we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a national provider of legal document review services and maintains offices within Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to serve as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers based in Minnesota by email of its forthcoming implementation of a revised premium overtime policy effective August 2019. This revised policy removed overtime premium compensation and specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio failed to provide overtime compensation due to him and other employees in similar positions. Cohen contended that Consilio’s conduct constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested, as relief, payment for overtime, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime pay and liquidated damages requested under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Of this amount, Cohen himself received $3,225.21 as overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties jointly acknowledged that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit," and further agreed that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." However, the parties did not reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen maintained that he was eligible for $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Consilio, for its part, asserted that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover such penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [was] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, should the Court grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio, assessing the evidence and any reasonable inferences that can be drawn from it in the manner most advantageous to the nonmoving party. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when no genuine dispute exists regarding any material fact and the moving party merits judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is permitted to pursue “average daily wage penalties” as authorized by §§ 181.171 and 181.101. According to § 181.171, “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specific MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101, and if an employer “is found to have violated” any such provision, it is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee may recover civil penalties or damages, but only if they are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is alleged to have been breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen claims that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, contending that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permit employees to pursue average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) mentions penalties solely in relation to actions taken by the Commissioner. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may be imposed only after the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). After such a demand is served, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees, the statute grants only the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, if the Minnesota legislature intended, it clearly possessed the ability to grant a statutory right to an employee to seek penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee, upon request, may recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same right for employees who voluntarily leave their jobs); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s inclusion of a provision in one statute, but not in another, demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a specific result when it intends to” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that the authority to seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 is vested exclusively in the Commissioner, the district court did not make a mistake. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that, under § 181.101(a), “the power to enforce a penalty rests only with the [Commissioner], and any funds collected are distributed to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages to which he is entitled under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should receive statutory penalties as well. Both parties concur that any statutory penalties awarded are directed to the Commissioner, rather than to Cohen himself, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages. At this juncture in the litigation, we have significant reservations about whether we possess jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of being remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” because these penalties may serve to “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Additionally, as previously stated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if later developments made it unmistakably certain that the purportedly improper conduct would not reasonably be anticipated to happen again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)), but it is “firmly established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary discontinuation of the disputed conduct does not remove a federal court’s authority to rule on the lawfulness of that conduct,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties stipulated that Cohen had received all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages. However, the ongoing existence of Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains a contested issue. Should Consilio’s policy still be operative, Cohen’s request for civil penalties could remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the challenged conduct will not be repeated, then Cohen’s MFLSA claim may be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts do not have “license to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s order granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess whether it possesses jurisdiction.

C.

We turn now to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not provide the notice of an overtime policy change as mandated by statute, and thus he seeks penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court offered minimal discussion regarding this issue. Nevertheless, Cohen has agreed that he has received all compensation owed to him by Consilio, and he does not cite any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties under this section. Because Cohen does not advance a substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s decision dismissing this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his argument is limited to seeking “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen requested certain particular forms of injunctive relief, but later, during summary judgment briefing, he altered his position and instead asked for the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, quite reasonably, did not address this new request. Therefore, this matter is not appropriately before us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as discussed in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that he does, the penalties at issue in that precedent differ from those provided under the MFLSA. The parties did not address this matter in their briefs. Additionally, I set aside the district court’s determination regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s pursuit of MFLSA statutory penalties could be rendered moot, and thus agree with the decision to vacate the judgment and remand the matter. Upon remand, the district court may consider the mootness issue initially. Id. at 180.
