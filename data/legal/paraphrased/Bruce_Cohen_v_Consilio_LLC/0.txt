KELLY, Circuit Judge.

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, contending that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable roles for overtime work. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now challenges that decision on appeal. We partially affirm the district court’s ruling, while also vacating and remanding certain aspects.

I.

Consilio, a legal document review firm operating across the country and with a presence in Minnesota, employed Cohen as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer starting in 2018. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other Minnesota hourly licensed attorney document reviewers via email of an impending change to its overtime compensation structure set to take effect in August 2019. Under this updated policy, the company discontinued premium pay for overtime and specified that Cohen and other affected employees would henceforth receive compensation for all hours worked at their standard base pay rate.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated legal proceedings against Consilio, representing both himself and a proposed class, contending that Consilio did not provide overtime compensation owed to him and other employees in comparable roles. Cohen asserted that this conduct amounted to violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. As forms of relief, Cohen requested payment for overtime, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, as well as injunctive measures.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers, which covered both the overtime pay and liquidated damages claimed under Cohen’s MFLSA action. Of this sum, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 as compensation for overtime work and an additional $462 as liquidated damages. Both parties agreed that Cohen “has received payment for all overtime wages claimed in this litigation” and further acknowledged that “Cohen does not contest that he has also received all liquidated damages he claims are due under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties reached an impasse regarding the assessment of penalties. Cohen asserted he was eligible for $172,080 in penalties calculated from the average daily wage under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MWTA. Conversely, Consilio maintained that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made the required payments, both sides agreed that the quickest and most efficient course of action would be for the Court to address and rule on Defendants’ summary judgment motion concerning the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before proceeding with any additional discovery or litigation. Cohen and Consilio further recognized that, should the Court fully grant the Defendants’ summary judgment motion, there would be no remaining claims and the matter would be completely resolved.

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.1

II.

We undertake a de novo review of the district court’s decision to award summary judgment to Consilio, assessing the record and all reasonable inferences in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted where no legitimate disputes exist regarding material facts and the moving party is entitled to prevail as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central issue between the parties is whether Cohen is entitled to seek “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specific sections of the MPWA, including § 181.101. If an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, the employer becomes “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1.2. During oral argument before this court, Consilio essentially acknowledged violations of both the MPWA and MFLSA, clarifying that the only matter in dispute concerning these statutes is the imposition of penalties. Furthermore, § 181.171 specifies that an employee may obtain civil penalties or damages, provided these are “set forth in the section” of the MPWA that the employer is accused of breaching. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, contending that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]”3 permits employees to claim penalties based on their average daily wage. However, § 181.101(a) specifically assigns the authority over penalties to the Commissioner. Referencing Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015), the Minnesota Supreme Court observed: “When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to reflect legislative intent and must be enforced as written.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001)). Notably, the imposition of penalties is contingent upon the Commissioner initially “serv[ing] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Only after this process, and if the wages remain unpaid ten days following the demand, may the Commissioner “charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. The statute allows for employees to bring claims for violations of section[] . . . 181.101 . . . directly to district court. . . . Employers found in violation are liable for compensatory damages as well as additional remedies, including but not limited to injunctive relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. §181.171, subd. 1. Specifically, the commissioner of labor and industry, or their representative, is authorized to deliver a demand for payment on an employee’s behalf, and “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages . . . and a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings at the same rate or rates for each day beyond the ten-day limit following the demand.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a); see also id. § 181.171, subd. 1. (emphasis in original). Moreover, any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). As for employees themselves, the statute restricts their role to the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a), which states that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and affirms that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times.”

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it so chooses, its capacity to grant statutory authority to employees to recover penalties. For example, Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) provides that a terminated employee, upon making a demand, may not only recover unpaid wages or commissions but also has the ability to "charge and collect a penalty"; a similar provision is made in § 181.14, subd. 2 for employees who voluntarily leave their positions. Furthermore, in Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022), the court emphasized that when the Legislature takes explicit action in one statute but not another, it indicates awareness and deliberate choice in legislative drafting, citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010). On this basis, given our interpretation that the statutory framework allows only the Commissioner to pursue daily average wage penalties pursuant to § 181.101, we find no error in the district court’s decision. While Milner discusses penalties under § 181.101(a), its commentary on the MPWA simply notes that although employees may bring actions to enforce § 181.101(a) violations and are entitled to receive penalties collected on their behalf, it is the Commissioner who is vested with exclusive authority to enforce the penalties under § 181.101(a), and any amounts obtained are to be paid out to the employee. See Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged that Consilio has already paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages due to him under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he remains entitled to statutory penalties. Both parties concur that such statutory penalties—unlike overtime compensation and liquidated damages—are designated for payment to the Commissioner, rather than to Cohen.6 At this juncture, we harbor substantial doubt about our jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, although remitted to the government, can nonetheless serve to “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” by potentially motivating offenders to halt present violations and discouraging recurrence in the future. Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, it remains essential that “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously discussed, “the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could be rendered irrelevant if later developments established with certainty that the contested conduct is not likely to happen again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “[t]he general principle holds that ‘a defendant’s voluntary discontinuation of the disputed action does not remove the authority of a federal court to rule on the action’s legality,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)). Furthermore, as clarified in Milner, civil penalties under subdivision 7 are “to be remitted to the Commissioner,” not to individual employees, Milner, 748 N.W.2d at 616, with the opinion further specifying that such penalties are paid to the Commissioner rather than to the employee under the relevant statutory framework. Id. at 618.

Before Consilio submitted its motion for summary judgment, both parties concurred that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, a factual disagreement persists regarding whether Consilio continues to enforce the contested overtime policy. Should Consilio’s policy remain effective, Cohen may still maintain a valid claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been rescinded and there is “absolutely clear” assurance that the misconduct will not happen again, Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA may be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts, moreover, are not authorized to “retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Thus, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for further proceedings to assess whether jurisdiction exists.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the legally required notice regarding a modification to its overtime policy, thus entitling him to statutory penalties pursuant to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen takes issue with the district court, contending that it gave “little to no discussion of this claim.” Nevertheless, Cohen conceded that he has received all wages due from Consilio and cites no legal provision authorizing civil penalties under the statute at issue. Since Cohen presents no substantial argument for overturning the district court’s decision, we decline to overturn its dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (finding an issue waived where it is unsupported by argument or legal authority). In the proceedings below—whether on appeal, in response to the motion for summary judgment, or in seeking to amend or vacate the judgment—Cohen failed to develop this argument. See InfoDeli, LLC v. W. Robidoux, Inc., 136 F.4th 792, 802 n.8 (8th Cir. 2025) (observing that “fail[ure] to adequately develop an argument in support of alleged error amounts to abandonment” and that waiver occurs when a party does not follow the requirements of Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 28(a)(9)(A)). Nonetheless, we are first required to consider our jurisdiction.

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole contention is that he deserves “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint articulated certain precise injunctive remedies, but subsequently, he altered his approach during summary judgment briefing, asking instead for the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, reasonably, did not address this new request. Consequently, this matter is not appropriately presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen has standing to pursue statutory penalties under Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). It is possible that he does, yet there are distinctions between the penalties addressed in that case and those prescribed by the MFLSA. Neither side addressed this matter in their briefs. Additionally, I also set aside the district court’s decisions regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may now be moot, so I concur with vacating the existing judgment and sending the case back for further proceedings. On remand, the district court may initially resolve the mootness issue. Id. at 180.
