Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, contending that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not provide overtime compensation to him and other employees in comparable positions. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals that decision. We affirm the judgment in part, but vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio operates as a national legal document review firm and maintains offices within Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to serve as an hourly paid, licensed attorney document reviewer. In July 2019, Consilio emailed Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers located in Minnesota to notify them of an upcoming change: starting in August 2019, a new premium overtime policy would take effect. This policy discontinued the payment of overtime premium wages and specified that Cohen, along with other similarly situated employees, would receive compensation "at [their] base pay rate" for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both for himself and on behalf of a proposed class, contending that Consilio failed to pay him and other similarly situated employees overtime wages to which they were entitled. Cohen claimed these actions constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested relief in the form of overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought by Cohen under his MFLSA claim. Within this amount, Cohen individually was awarded $3,225.21 for overtime and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties jointly agreed that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties did not reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen contended he should recover $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties based on the MFLSA, and another $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In opposition, Consilio maintained that the statutes at issue did not authorize an award of penalties to Cohen.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court subsequently granted summary judgment in Consilio’s favor on all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

Our review of the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio is conducted de novo, considering the evidence and any reasonable inferences from it in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment should be granted only when there are no genuine disputes regarding any material facts and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree on whether Cohen is permitted to recover “average daily wage penalties” according to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes an individual to “bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified sections of the MPWA, among them § 181.101, and provides that an employer who “is found to have violated” such a provision is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee can obtain civil penalties or damages, but only if such penalties or damages are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA at issue. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen claims that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and contends that "the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]" allows employees to pursue average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) explicitly mentions only the Commissioner in relation to the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties can only be assessed if the Commissioner initially "serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee." Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, "if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." Id. Any "[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned." Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees, the statute only expressly preserves the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (providing that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it has so chosen, the ability to grant a statutory right to aggrieved employees to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that an employee who has been discharged and makes a demand can recover unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing similarly for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that when the Legislature acts in one statute and not another, it demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to pursue a desired outcome if that is its intent” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, there was no error by the district court. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (clarifying that § 181.101(a) “limits the authority to seek a penalty to the [Commissioner], with any recovered penalties distributed to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due to him under the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that any statutory penalties awarded would be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen, unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages. At this point in the case, we are seriously concerned about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can "provide relief to citizen plaintiffs harmed or at risk of harm due to ongoing illegal actions," since such sanctions may "motivate defendants to halt their current infractions and prevent future violations." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, "a plaintiff must show standing for each claim advanced and for every type of remedy requested." Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Further, as previously discussed, "[t]he necessary personal stake present at the outset of the case (standing) must persist for the suit’s duration (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could potentially be rendered moot if later developments made it definitively apparent that the alleged wrongful conduct was not likely to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, “it is well established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not strip a federal court of its authority to rule on the legality of that practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had already received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages; however, the question of whether Consilio’s improper overtime policy is still being enforced is unresolved. Should the policy remain in effect, Cohen may still have a valid claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued its policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the alleged unlawful behavior will not be repeated, then Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. For these reasons, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess whether it has jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the mandatory statutory notice regarding a change to its overtime policy, entitling him to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court for purportedly offering “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that he received all compensation due from Consilio, and he fails to identify any provision in the statute that permits an award of civil penalties. As Cohen does not advance any substantive argument in favor of reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s order dismissing this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint included certain specified requests for injunctive relief, but he later shifted his position at the summary judgment stage, seeking different relief—namely, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, understandably, did not address this new request. Consequently, the matter is not properly presented to us on appeal. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the matter back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm the judgment. Circuit Judge Kobes files a special concurrence.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as outlined in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). It is possible he does, but the penalties at issue there differ from those provided by the MFLSA. The parties did not address this argument in their briefs. Additionally, I would also set aside the district court’s determination on attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s pursuit of statutory penalties under the MFLSA could potentially be moot; therefore, I concur with the decision to vacate and remand the case. Upon remand, the district court should have the opportunity to address the mootness question as a threshold matter. Id. at 180.
