Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed this action claiming that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not provide overtime compensation to him and a group of comparable employees. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals that decision. We affirm some portions of the judgment, but vacate and remand others.

I.

Consilio, which operates as a national legal document review firm and maintains locations in Minnesota, hired Cohen in 2018 as a licensed attorney to conduct document reviews on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota by email that a revised premium overtime policy would take effect in August 2019. Under this new policy, Consilio discontinued payment of overtime premiums and specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated litigation against Consilio both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, contending that Consilio had not compensated him and other similarly situated employees for overtime work. Cohen asserted that these failures breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. As relief, Cohen requested recovery of unpaid overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers, in satisfaction of the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within this sum, Cohen individually was paid $3,225.21 as overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties entered a stipulation stating that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and further that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties reached an impasse regarding penalties. Cohen maintained he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, in addition to $42,000 in MFLSA penalties and $42,000 in MWTA penalties. Consilio countered that the applicable statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Consilio on each of the three claims. Cohen has appealed this decision.

II.

We assess the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio under a de novo standard, considering all evidence as well as reasonable inferences drawn therefrom in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is warranted when there exists no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the party moving for judgment is entitled to it by operation of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties dispute whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. According to § 181.171, “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of certain MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101, and an employer who “is found to have violated” any of those provisions is subject to “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Moreover, employees are permitted to recover civil penalties or damages provided that those remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is alleged to have been violated by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, asserting that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to pursue penalties based on the average daily wage. However, § 181.101(a) addresses penalties only in relation to the Commissioner. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, the statute provides for penalties only after the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If, after ten days from service of this demand, the wages have not been paid, then “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. The statute further specifies that “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to the employee’s role, the statute authorizes only the ability to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (noting “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, if the Minnesota legislature intended to do so, it clearly possessed the means to grant statutory authority for an aggrieved employee to seek penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a terminated employee, upon making a demand, may recover owed wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing a similar remedy for employees who quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but not in another indicates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a specific goal if that is its desire” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Accordingly, because we find that only the Commissioner is authorized to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, we hold that the district court acted properly. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that § 181.101(a) “restricts enforcement of a penalty to the [Commissioner], and any monies collected are paid to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he is still eligible to receive statutory penalties. The parties concur that, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, any statutory penalties assessed are owed to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this juncture in the litigation, we are significantly concerned about whether we have jurisdiction to address this matter.

Civil penalties, even where payable to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” as such penalties may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously articulated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if later developments made it abundantly clear that the alleged wrongful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to happen again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, “it is firmly established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary ending of the disputed conduct does not strip a federal court of authority to decide whether the practice was lawful,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties stipulated that Cohen had received all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages, though whether Consilio continues to maintain an unlawful overtime policy remains contested. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties could still proceed. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio’s policy has ended and it is “absolutely clear” that the alleged unlawful behavior will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may indeed be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment with respect to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to consider whether it retains subject matter jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen's claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio failed to give the notice of an overtime policy change required by statute, thereby entitling him to statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court gave minimal attention to this claim in its discussion. However, Cohen acknowledged through stipulation that he was paid in full by Consilio, and he fails to cite any statutory provision permitting the recovery of civil penalties under this law. As Cohen has not presented a substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen's request for injunctive relief, he asserts solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” In the initial complaint, Cohen asked for certain specific types of injunctive relief, but later revised his position in his summary judgment filings to instead seek withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, understandably, did not address this new request. Consequently, this matter is not properly presented for our consideration. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision as it relates to Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and return the matter for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the decision. Circuit Judge KOBES, concurring specially.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen has established standing to pursue statutory penalties as interpreted in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that he does have standing, I note that there are distinctions between the penalties considered in that matter and those authorized by the MFLSA. The question was not raised in either party’s briefing. In addition, I concur in vacating the district court’s orders on attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that the claim by Cohen for statutory penalties under the MFLSA could potentially be moot; accordingly, I support the decision to vacate the judgment and remand. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to address the mootness issue at the outset. Id. at 180.
