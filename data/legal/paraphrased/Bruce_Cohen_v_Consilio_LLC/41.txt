In this case, Bruce Cohen sued his employer, Consilio, LLC, asserting that Consilio did not compensate him and others similarly situated for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals. We partly affirm the district court’s decision, but in part, we vacate and remand the case for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review company with a presence across the country, including offices located in Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to work as a document reviewer, compensated on an hourly basis as a licensed attorney. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly situated Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers via email of its upcoming premium overtime policy change, effective August 2019. The revised policy ended the provision of premium overtime pay, specifying instead that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation for all hours worked “at [their] base pay rate.”

In August 2020, Cohen initiated litigation against Consilio both individually and as a representative of a proposed class, contending that Consilio failed to compensate him and other similarly situated employees for overtime work performed. Cohen asserted that Consilio’s conduct breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested relief including payment for overtime, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and an injunction.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers licensed to practice law in Minnesota, satisfying the demands for overtime compensation and liquidated damages under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within these sums, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 for overtime and an additional $462 for liquidated damages. Both sides further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties failed to reach consensus on the issue of penalties. Cohen contended that he was entitled to penalties amounting to $172,080 under the MPWA, $42,000 pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the MWTA. Conversely, Consilio took the position that the relevant statutes did not authorize the imposition of penalties in Cohen’s favor.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that the quickest and most efficient way to proceed was for the Court to first consider and rule on Defendants’ summary judgment motion regarding the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims, prior to conducting any further discovery or litigation. Both Cohen and Consilio recognized that if the Court granted Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, there would be no claims left pending and the case would be fully resolved.

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Consilio on all three claims, and Cohen now appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering all evidence and reasonable inferences in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing the motion. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute regarding any material fact and the moving party deserves judgment as a matter of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree over whether Cohen is entitled to seek “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. According to § 181.171, “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specific sections of the MPWA, which includes § 181.101, and if an employer “is found to have violated” one of those sections, the employer shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee is permitted to obtain civil penalties or damages, provided these remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA at issue. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, asserting that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” enables employees to recover penalties calculated on their average daily wage. However, § 181.101(a) refers exclusively to the Commissioner when addressing penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). The statute in fact conditions penalties upon the Commissioner first serving “a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). After that, if wages remain unpaid ten days following such service, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. In addition, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Concerning the employee, the statute preserves solely the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (providing that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and establishes “a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated its ability to grant employees the statutory authority to recover penalties when it chooses to do so. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that, upon demand following discharge, an employee may recover both unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (granting the same right to employees who have quit or resigned); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Having determined that § 181.101 allows only the Commissioner to pursue daily average wage penalties, we find no error by the district court. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that, pursuant to § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

With respect to his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged by stipulation that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he remains eligible to recover statutory penalties. Both parties concur that such statutory penalties, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, are owed to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture in the litigation, we are seriously concerned as to whether we possess jurisdiction to address this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of their payment to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” as such penalties may serve to “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously noted, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments establish conclusively that the challenged conduct is not likely to occur again, Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, “[i]t is well settled that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties agreed that Cohen had received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages; however, a dispute persists regarding whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy continues to be in effect. Should Consilio’s policy still exist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties could remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that the alleged wrongful practice will not resume, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may well be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Moreover, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. For these reasons, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the notification required by statute regarding a modification in its overtime policy, thereby entitling him to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court “included little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that he received every payment Consilio owed him, and he has not cited any statutory provision granting civil penalties for violations of this statute. As Cohen has failed to present a substantive argument for reversing the district court, we decline to disturb its dismissal of the claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his argument is limited to asserting that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint sought particular types of injunctive remedies; however, he later shifted his position in his summary judgment filings, seeking the withdrawal of the 2019 policy instead. The district court, quite reasonably, did not address this change. Accordingly, this issue is not appropriately presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court's order with respect to Cohen's MFLSA claim and return the case for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the decision. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring in part.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as set out in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Cohen may, but the nature of the penalties in that case and those set forth in the MFLSA differ. The parties did not address this issue in their briefing. Additionally, I would vacate the district court’s determinations concerning attorney's fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s pursuit of MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot and thus join in vacating the judgment and remanding the action. On remand, the district court may, if it wishes, address mootness as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
