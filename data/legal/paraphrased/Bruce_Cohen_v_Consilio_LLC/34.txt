Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, contending that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and a group of comparable employees for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in Consilio's favor, prompting Cohen to appeal. We partially affirm and, in other respects, vacate and remand the district court's decision.

I.

Consilio, which operates legal document review services across the country and maintains offices in Minnesota, employed Cohen starting in 2018 as a licensed attorney document reviewer paid on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio emailed Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota to notify them of an upcoming change, indicating that, beginning in August 2019, a revised premium overtime policy would take effect. Under this new policy, overtime premium pay was discontinued, and the company specified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive payment "at [their] base pay rate" for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio for himself and a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not provide him and other similarly situated employees with the overtime compensation they were due. Cohen asserted that Consilio’s conduct breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested, as relief, recovery of overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive orders.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime wages and liquidated damages sought by Cohen under his MFLSA claim. Within these disbursements, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 designated as overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, there was no consensus regarding the issue of penalties. Cohen maintained that he was entitled to penalties in the amounts of $172,080 as average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 as penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the MWTA. Consilio, on the other hand, contended that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover any penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that the quickest and most effective way to proceed was for the Court to consider and rule on Defendants’ motion for summary judgment regarding the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before engaging in any further discovery or litigation. Cohen and Consilio further agreed that, should the Court fully grant Defendants’ motion for summary judgment, there would be no remaining claims and the matter would be completely resolved.

The district court subsequently granted Consilio’s summary judgment motion as to all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court's summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering the evidence and all reasonable inferences in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when there exists no genuine dispute concerning any material fact and the party requesting summary judgment is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. See Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central disagreement between the parties involves whether Cohen is entitled to recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. According to § 181.171, “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified MPWA provisions, one of which is § 181.101. The statute further dictates that when an employer “is found to have violated” one of these sections, it shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee may receive civil penalties or damages, provided such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that was allegedly breached by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, maintaining that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) solely mentions the Commissioner in connection with penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties are not available unless the Commissioner initially “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. The statute further specifies that “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees, the statute limits them to the ability to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (noting “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, if it so desired, the Minnesota legislature had the capacity to authorize an aggrieved employee to pursue statutory penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that a discharged employee, upon request, is entitled to recover both unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); see also id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (granting the same right to employees who quit or resign); see Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s decision to act in one statutory context but not in another demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a particular aim when it wishes,” citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010)). Since we determine that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 is reserved solely for the Commissioner, the district court’s decision was correct. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that, under § 181.101(a), “the power to enforce a penalty is restricted to the [Commissioner], with any recovered sums to be paid to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages required by the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends he is further entitled to statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that any statutory penalties awarded—unlike overtime wages or liquidated damages—are to be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture, we harbor significant doubt as to our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can “provide relief to citizen plaintiffs who are harmed or face harm due to ongoing unlawful activity,” since such penalties may “motivate defendants to cease present violations and discourage future violations.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff is required to establish standing for each individual claim advanced and for every type of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously discussed, “[t]he necessary personal interest (standing) at the start of litigation must persist through the case’s duration (mootness).” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could become moot if later developments made it completely certain that the supposed misconduct could not reasonably be anticipated to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, it is firmly established that “‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its summary judgment motion, the parties stipulated that Cohen had received full payment for both overtime wages and liquidated damages. However, there remains disagreement over whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy is still operative. Should that policy continue to exist, Cohen may maintain a viable claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that Consilio’s improper conduct will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts, moreover, lack authority to “retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. For these reasons, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment decision on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter for the district court to resolve whether it retains jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give him the notice of an overtime policy change as required by statute, entitling him to penalties. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court “included little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen admitted that he received all compensation Consilio owed him, and he identifies no provision in the statute that would permit civil penalties in this context. Because he advances no substantial argument warranting reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen sought specific types of injunctive relief; however, he later shifted his request in his summary judgment briefing, asking for a different form of relief—the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, as a result, did not address this request. Therefore, this matter is not appropriately before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the matter for additional proceedings. In all other respects, the decision is affirmed. Judge Kobes, writing separately, concurs.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties, as discussed in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While standing may exist, the penalties in that precedent differ from those authorized by the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this particular question in their briefs. Additionally, I set aside the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, thus I concur in vacating the judgment and sending the case back. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to address the issue of mootness as a preliminary matter. Id. at 180.
