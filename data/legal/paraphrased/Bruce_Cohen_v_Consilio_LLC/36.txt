In this action, Bruce Cohen claimed that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals that decision. We affirm the judgment in part, but also vacate and remand in part.

I.

Consilio, a legal document review firm operating throughout the United States, maintains offices in Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen was hired by Consilio as an hourly licensed attorney tasked with document review. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email to Cohen and other licensed attorneys performing hourly document review in Minnesota, informing them of a revised premium overtime policy that would take effect in August 2019. Under this updated policy, Consilio removed overtime premium pay and specified that Cohen and other similarly situated employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated legal proceedings against Consilio, both individually and on behalf of a prospective class, asserting that Consilio had not compensated him and other employees in similar positions for overtime work as required. Cohen contended that Consilio’s conduct was in breach of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. He requested relief including unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive orders.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other attorney document reviewers licensed in Minnesota to satisfy the overtime pay and liquidated damages sought in connection with Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Cohen himself received $3,225.21 as overtime compensation, as well as $462 in liquidated damages from these disbursements. The parties agreed and stipulated that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties remained at odds regarding penalties. Cohen asserted a right to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Conversely, Consilio maintained that the statutes did not authorize Cohen to receive penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that the quickest and most efficient way to proceed would be for the Court to consider and rule on Defendants’ motion for summary judgment regarding the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims prior to conducting any further discovery or litigation. Both Cohen and Consilio recognized that if the Court granted summary judgment for the Defendants in its entirety, no claims would remain outstanding and the case would be completely resolved.

The district court subsequently granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on each of the three claims. Cohen now appeals that decision.

II.

We examine the district court's summary judgment decision in favor of Consilio using a de novo standard, considering the evidence and all reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is warranted when there are no disputes over material facts and the moving party deserves judgment as a matter of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central disagreement between the parties concerns whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” as specified in §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “A person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of selected provisions of the MPWA, which includes § 181.101, and specifies that an employer who “is found to have violated” one of these provisions becomes “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Furthermore, the statute allows an employee to seek civil penalties or damages, provided those remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that has allegedly been breached. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, asserting that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” allows employees themselves to pursue average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) references only the Commissioner with respect to the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties can only be pursued once the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). After that step, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). For the employee's role, the statute limits them to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it so chooses, its ability to grant a statutory right to seek penalties to an aggrieved employee. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee may, upon making a demand, recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same right for employees who voluntarily leave employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). As we determine that the authority to seek daily average wage penalties pursuant to § 181.101 resides solely with the Commissioner, the district court’s ruling stands without error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both sides acknowledge that such statutory penalties—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are to be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this point in the case, we are seriously concerned about whether we have jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, including those remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” as such sanctions may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously noted, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments make it “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur,” as stated in Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, “[i]t is well settled that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties agreed that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, there remains a disagreement over whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy continues to exist. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen might still have a valid claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the improper conduct will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts are not permitted to maintain jurisdiction over matters where at least one party clearly no longer maintains an ongoing interest. Id. at 192. Consequently, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the case to the district court for a determination regarding its jurisdiction.

C.

We turn next to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the notice required by statute concerning a modification to its overtime policy, and contends that he therefore should receive penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court for offering “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that he was fully paid all sums owed by Consilio and does not cite any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this law. In the absence of any substantial argument supporting reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he is owed “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint sought certain particular forms of injunctive relief, but subsequently, in his summary judgment briefing, he altered his position to seek a different remedy—namely, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, quite reasonably, did not address this new request. Therefore, this matter is not properly presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision concerning Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and return the case for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm the district court’s judgment. Circuit Judge KOBES files a special concurrence.

I have reservations about whether Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties under the reasoning in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Although it is possible he has standing, the penalties involved in that case differ from those offered by the MFLSA. Neither side addressed this issue in their briefs. Additionally, I would set aside the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s pursuit of MFLSA statutory penalties could be rendered moot, and therefore I concur with the decision to vacate and remand. Upon remand, the district court may first resolve whether the case is moot. Id. at 180.
