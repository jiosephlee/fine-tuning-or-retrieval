Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal. We partially affirm the district court’s decision and partially vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a national provider of legal document review services and maintains offices within Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to serve as a licensed attorney document reviewer paid on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers announcing that, beginning in August 2019, it would implement a revised policy regarding premium overtime. This updated policy discontinued overtime premium compensation and specified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated legal action against Consilio both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not provide overtime compensation that was allegedly owed to him and other employees in similar positions. Cohen claimed that these practices violated the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen’s complaint requested as relief the recovery of overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio paid a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers, which represented overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Of this sum, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 in overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, they did not reach an agreement with respect to penalties. Cohen maintained that he should receive $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in MFLSA penalties, and $42,000 pursuant to the MWTA. Consilio, by contrast, took the position that the statutory framework did not allow Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that the most efficient and expedient way to proceed would be for the Court to consider and rule on Defendants’ summary judgment motion with respect to the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before engaging in any additional discovery or litigation. Both Cohen and Consilio acknowledged that, should the Court grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, there would be no remaining claims and the action would be fully resolved.

The district court ultimately granted summary judgment in Consilio’s favor on all three causes of action. Cohen has appealed this decision.

II.

We examine the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio under a de novo standard, considering the record and any reasonable inferences drawn therefrom in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). A grant of summary judgment is warranted where no material facts are in dispute and the party requesting judgment is entitled to prevail as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central issue between the parties is whether Cohen is permitted to recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. According to § 181.171, “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of designated MPWA provisions, including § 181.101. This statute further states that if an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, the employer is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. The statute also allows for recovery of civil penalties or damages, but only if the section of the MPWA allegedly violated specifically provides for such relief. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, contending that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permit employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, the text of § 181.101(a) directs authority over penalties exclusively to the Commissioner. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). Specifically, penalties can only be assessed if the Commissioner first “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Following service of such demand, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding the role of the employee, the statute only grants the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (noting “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated, when it so intended, that it could grant an employee harmed by an employer’s actions a statutory right to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee, upon demand, can recover unpaid wages or commissions and may also “charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same remedy for employees who quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s choice to act in one statute but not in another indicates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a particular purpose when it desires” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Given our determination that the authority to seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 rests exclusively with the Commissioner, we find no error in the district court’s decision. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (clarifying that under § 181.101(a), “only the [Commissioner] has authority to enforce the penalty, and any funds recovered are directed to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he is further entitled to statutory penalties. Both parties concur that statutory penalties, in contrast to overtime pay and liquidated damages, are awarded to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this point in the case, we harbor significant doubt about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of whether they are remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” since such penalties may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously stated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could potentially become moot if later developments made it “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, as the Court has long held, “‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties had reached an understanding that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, the continued existence of Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy remained contested. Should Consilio’s policy still exist, Cohen might still pursue civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio’s policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” there is no possibility of the wrongful conduct recurring, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts do not possess the authority “to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Thus, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter for the district court to evaluate its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s MWTA claim, in which he contends that Consilio did not give him the notice required by statute regarding a change in its overtime policy, and therefore, he seeks penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court gave “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen admitted that he received all the compensation Consilio owed him, and he fails to identify any statutory basis for the imposition of civil penalties under this provision. Since Cohen has presented no substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he asserts only that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen demanded particular forms of injunctive relief, but he later altered his position at the summary judgment stage, now asking for the removal of the 2019 policy. The district court, not unreasonably, declined to address this new request. As a result, this matter is not properly presented to us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision relating to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the matter back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm. Circuit Judge Kobes files a separate concurrence.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties, referencing Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). It is possible he does, but distinctions exist between the penalties addressed in Laidlaw and those authorized by the MFLSA. The parties did not present arguments on this matter. Additionally, I would set aside the district court’s determinations concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that there is a possibility that Cohen’s demand for statutory penalties under the MFLSA has become moot, and therefore concur in the decision to vacate the judgment and remand. Upon remand, the district court may, as a preliminary issue, resolve whether the claim is moot. Id. at 180.
