Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed suit asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and a group of comparable employees for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals that decision. We uphold the judgment in part and reverse and remand in part.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm on a national scale and maintains offices within Minnesota. In 2018, Cohen was hired by Consilio as a document reviewer—a position for licensed attorneys paid on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly situated Minnesota-based licensed attorney document reviewers via email about the implementation of a revised premium overtime policy, effective August 2019. This updated policy did away with the overtime premium compensation, specifying instead that Cohen and his colleagues would receive payment "at [their] base pay rate" for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio on his own behalf and for a proposed class, contending that Consilio did not compensate him and other similarly situated workers for overtime wages that were owed. Cohen asserted that this conduct amounted to violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen’s requested relief included unpaid overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive orders.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorneys who worked as document reviewers, satisfying the overtime pay and liquidated damages sought in relation to Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within this amount, Cohen individually was paid $3,225.21 for overtime and received $462 as liquidated damages. The parties jointly agreed that Cohen "has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit," and further stipulated that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." However, the parties were unable to reach consensus on the issue of penalties. Cohen maintained that he was owed $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Conversely, Consilio contended that the statutes in question did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that the “most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio further “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court proceeded to grant Consilio’s motion for summary judgment as to all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering the evidence, as well as any reasonable inferences drawn from it, in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). According to Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)), summary judgment is appropriate where the record reveals no genuine dispute regarding any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

A.

We turn first to Cohen’s claim brought under the MPWA. The parties disagree about whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of certain MPWA provisions, including § 181.101, and further specifies that an employer found to have violated such a provision “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Furthermore, the statute authorizes employees to recover civil penalties or damages, but only if such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA at issue. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and contends that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” grants employees the authority to pursue penalties in the form of average daily wages. However, § 181.101(a) refers solely to the Commissioner with respect to imposing penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may only be sought if the Commissioner initially “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If wages are not paid within ten days following service of that demand, the commissioner then “may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, the statute directs that “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees, the statute only expressly permits them to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (noting “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, if it had intended, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated that it knows how to grant a statutory right to employees to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that when a terminated employee makes a demand, the employee may obtain unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same right for employees who have quit or resigned); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Therefore, as we determine that the authority to seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 resides exclusively with the Commissioner, the district court’s decision was not erroneous. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

With respect to his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has already paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he should also receive statutory penalties. The parties are in agreement that any statutory penalties awarded—unlike the overtime wages and liquidated damages—would be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this juncture, we are significantly concerned as to whether we possess jurisdiction to resolve this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can nonetheless "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct," since such penalties may "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Further, as previously articulated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments establish with certainty that the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be anticipated to happen again. See Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, it is a well-established principle that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice.” Id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, there remains a contested issue regarding whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy is still in effect. Should this policy persist, Cohen’s claim for civil penalties could remain actionable. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has terminated the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that such improper conduct will not resume, then Cohen’s MFLSA claim may indeed be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s order granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the matter for the district court to resolve the question of its jurisdiction.

C.

We turn now to Cohen's MWTA claim, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the notice mandated by statute regarding an amendment to its overtime policy, thus entitling him to statutory penalties. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court for offering "little to no discussion of this claim." Nevertheless, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all amounts he is owed and fails to cite any statutory provision granting civil penalties under this law. Since Cohen presents no substantial argument for overturning the district court, we decline to overturn the order dismissing this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole contention is that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, the complaint set forth particular injunctive remedies, but Cohen subsequently shifted his position in the summary judgment filings by seeking alternative relief—specifically, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. As a result, the district court—understandably—did not address this new request. Consequently, this matter is not properly presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (stating that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision pertaining to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the case back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, filing a special concurrence.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as outlined in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Perhaps he does, yet there are distinctions between the penalties addressed in that matter and those accessible under the MFLSA. This issue was not addressed by either party in their briefing. Nevertheless, I also would set aside the district court’s determination regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s entitlement to MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot and therefore concur in vacating the judgment and remanding. Upon remand, the district court may opt to resolve the mootness issue first. Id. at 180.
