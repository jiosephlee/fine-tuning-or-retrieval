Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2079  
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, asserting that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not provide overtime compensation owed to him and other employees in comparable positions. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen appeals that decision. We partially affirm the district court’s judgment, while in other respects we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a national legal document review firm and maintains offices within Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to serve as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers, notifying them that a new premium overtime policy would be implemented starting August 2019. Under this new policy, overtime premium pay was discontinued and it was clarified that Cohen and the other employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, representing himself and a proposed class, claiming that Consilio did not pay overtime wages owed to him and other similarly situated employees. He asserted that Consilio’s conduct contravened the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief as remedies.

Consilio disbursed $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers in satisfaction of the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within this total, Cohen received $3,225.21 for overtime and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and acknowledged that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, they were unable to reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen maintained that he was eligible to receive $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio asserted that the relevant statutes do not authorize the imposition of penalties in this matter.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that the “most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court subsequently granted summary judgment in Consilio’s favor on all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We examine the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio under a de novo standard, considering the record and any reasonable inferences from it in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when there exists no dispute over any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central disagreement between the parties concerns whether Cohen is entitled to seek “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of designated MPWA provisions, which includes § 181.101, and specifies that when an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, the employer is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, employees may obtain civil penalties or damages as long as these are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that was allegedly breached by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, asserting that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, the text of § 181.101(a) identifies only the Commissioner as the authority to impose penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, such penalties are authorized solely after the Commissioner has “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Should wages remain unpaid ten days after this demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding the role of employees, the statute limits them to the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, its ability to grant employees the statutory authority to pursue penalties. For instance, Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) provides that a terminated employee, upon request, may recover not only unpaid wages or commissions but also “may charge and collect a penalty”; similarly, § 181.14, subd. 2 extends this right to employees who resign or quit. See also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Given our determination that only the Commissioner possesses the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, we find that the district court did not make an error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that § 181.101(a) “limits the ability to enforce a penalty to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds ultimately remitted to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has conceded that Consilio has paid him all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he is further entitled to statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that any statutory penalties awarded—unlike overtime compensation and liquidated damages—are to be remitted to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen personally. At this juncture, we harbor significant doubts as to our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Even when civil penalties are paid to the state, they can nonetheless “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or face threatened injury as a result of ongoing unlawful behavior” because such penalties may “motivate defendants to cease present violations and dissuade them from engaging in future violations.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must show standing with respect to every claim he brings and each type of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously stated, “[t]he necessary personal stake required at the outset of the case (standing) must endure for the entirety of the case (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments demonstrated with certainty that the supposed misconduct was not likely to happen again. See Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, “it is firmly established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of the disputed conduct does not strip a federal court of authority to decide whether the conduct was unlawful,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties stipulated that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, the existence of Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy continues to be contested. Should Consilio’s policy still exist, Cohen may retain a valid claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. On the other hand, if the policy is discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that the challenged behavior will not be repeated, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts do not have “license to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. For these reasons, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the matter to the district court so it may evaluate its jurisdiction.

C.

We turn now to Cohen's claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio neglected to give him notice required by statute regarding a change in its overtime policy, entitling him, he argues, to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen asserts that the district court offered minimal, if any, discussion of this allegation. Nevertheless, Cohen agreed that Consilio paid him all amounts due, and he has not identified any statutory provision granting civil penalties for such an alleged violation. As Cohen has not presented any substantive argument in favor of overturning the district court’s decision, we decline to upset its order dismissing his claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his argument is limited to seeking “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” In his original complaint, Cohen requested certain specific injunctive remedies, but later, in his summary judgment motion, he altered his request to seek rescission of the 2019 policy. The district court did not address this request, which is understandable under the circumstances. Therefore, this matter is not properly presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the case to that court for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the district court’s judgment. KOBES, Circuit Judge, writing separately in concurrence.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). He might have standing, but the penalties involved in that case differ from those offered under the MFLSA. The parties also did not address the matter in their briefs. Additionally, I would set aside the district court’s findings related to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s claim for MFLSA statutory penalties may be moot, so I concur in vacating the decision and sending the case back. Upon remand, the district court may address mootness as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
