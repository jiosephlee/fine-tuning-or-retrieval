Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, contending that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not provide overtime compensation to him or to other employees in comparable positions. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal. We partially affirm the district court’s ruling, and partially vacate and remand the case for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm with a national presence and maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to serve as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. In July 2019, Consilio emailed Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers based in Minnesota, announcing the implementation of a revised premium overtime policy set to take effect in August 2019. Under this revised policy, overtime premium pay would no longer be available, and the company clarified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio on his own behalf as well as on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not provide overtime compensation owed to him and other employees in similar circumstances. Cohen claimed that Consilio’s conduct violated the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers, satisfying the demands for overtime compensation and liquidated damages pursuant to Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Of this sum, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 as overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties were unable to reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen claimed he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, as well as $42,000 in penalties each under the MFLSA and the MWTA. Consilio, in contrast, maintained that the statutes at issue did not authorize the imposition of penalties in Cohen’s favor.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Consilio on each of the three claims. Cohen now appeals this decision.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s summary judgment ruling in favor of Consilio, considering the evidence and all reasonable inferences therefrom in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing the motion. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We turn first to Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree on whether Cohen is entitled to pursue "average daily wage penalties" pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that "[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations" of specified MPWA sections, including § 181.101, and that if an employer "is found to have violated" one of these sections, they shall be "liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief." Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, an employee is permitted to recover civil penalties or damages if, and only if, such remedies are "provided for in the section" of the MPWA that the employer is alleged to have violated. Id. ("An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.").

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and asserts that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover penalties based on average daily wages. However, § 181.101(a) solely refers to the Commissioner in relation to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may be imposed only after the Commissioner has “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Following this demand, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, any “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to the employee, the statute restricts the remedy to the ability to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (noting that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it so chooses, its ability to grant a statutory cause of action to an employee for the recovery of penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that a discharged employee, upon making a demand, can recover not only unpaid wages or commissions but also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (containing a similar provision for employees who voluntarily terminate their employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (observing “that when the Legislature acts in one statutory context but remains silent in another, it evidences that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a specific goal when it intends to” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Because we determine that, under § 181.101, only the Commissioner is authorized to seek penalties based on the employee’s daily average wage, the district court’s decision was not in error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that “the authority to enforce a penalty under § 181.101(a) resides exclusively with the [Commissioner], with any amounts recovered for penalties being payable to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages to which he is entitled under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends he is further eligible to receive statutory penalties. Both sides concur that statutory penalties—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—would be payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen personally. At this point in the proceedings, we have significant doubts about our jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of the fact that they are remitted to the government, can “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” since these penalties may “prompt defendants to halt present violations and act as a deterrent against future violations.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff is required to establish standing for every claim presented and for every form of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously stated, “[t]he necessary personal interest that must be present at the initiation of the lawsuit (standing) is required to persist throughout the duration of the case (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen's request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments make it “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, as the Supreme Court has repeatedly stated, “‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties acknowledged that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, they continue to disagree on whether Consilio's unlawful overtime policy persists. Should Consilio's policy remain in effect, Cohen could still pursue civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has ceased the challenged policy and it is "absolutely clear" the misconduct will not repeat, Cohen's MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not authorized "to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]" Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court's summary judgment decision as it pertains to Cohen's MFLSA claim and remand so the district court may consider its own jurisdiction.

C.

We turn next to Cohen’s MWTA claim, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give the notice required by statute regarding a change to its overtime policy, thereby entitling him to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court offered “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed he has been paid all amounts owed by Consilio, and he does not reference any statutory provision granting civil penalties pursuant to this statute. In the absence of any substantial argument supporting reversal, we decline to disturb the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he deserves "an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy." In his original complaint, Cohen identified particular forms of injunctive relief, but he subsequently shifted his approach in his summary judgment submissions, instead requesting the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, quite reasonably, did not address this new request. Consequently, the matter is not appropriately before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that "[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs"); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the matter back for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the decision. KOBES, Circuit Judge, writing separately in concurrence.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as interpreted in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Although it is possible that he does, the nature of the penalties at issue in that case is distinct from those imposed under the MFLSA. The parties did not address this question in their briefs. I also would vacate the district court’s determinations related to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s pursuit of MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot and, accordingly, I join the decision to vacate the district court’s judgment and remand the matter. Upon remand, the district court has the discretion to address the mootness issue at the outset. Id. at 180.
