Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not pay overtime compensation to him and others who were similarly positioned. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to file an appeal. We partially affirm, but also partially vacate the decision and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio, a company that provides legal document review services throughout the United States, maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to work as a document reviewer, paid by the hour and holding a license to practice law. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers working in Minnesota by email that it would implement a revised overtime pay policy starting in August 2019. This updated policy did away with overtime premium pay and specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, representing both himself and a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not provide the overtime compensation allegedly owed to him and other employees in similar circumstances. Cohen contended that Consilio’s conduct constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested overtime compensation, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief as remedies.

Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers a total of $256,010.01, intended to satisfy the overtime wages and liquidated damages sought in connection with Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA. From this amount, Cohen received $3,225.21 as overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties did not reach consensus concerning the issue of penalties. Cohen maintained he was eligible to recover $172,080 in average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio asserted that the relevant statutes do not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient way to proceed [would be] for the Court to consider and rule on Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any additional discovery or litigation occurs.” Cohen and Consilio “recognize[d] that, should the Court fully grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion, all claims w[ould] be extinguished and the matter w[ould] be completely resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court's decision to grant summary judgment in favor of Consilio, considering all evidence and reasonable inferences in the manner most favorable to the party opposing the motion. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted where no genuine disputes of material fact exist and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree on whether Cohen may recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 allows an individual to initiate a civil lawsuit seeking remedies for breaches of specified MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101, stating that an employer found in violation shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, an employee may obtain civil penalties or damages, provided that such remedies are specified in the particular MPWA section alleged to have been violated. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen asserts that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover penalties based on average daily wages. However, § 181.101(a) refers only to the Commissioner with respect to the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties can only be assessed if the Commissioner first “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). After this service, if the employer fails to pay the wages within ten days, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, the statute specifies that “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to the rights of employees, the statute explicitly preserves only their right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated its ability to grant employees a statutory right to seek penalties when it so intends. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that, upon demand, a discharged employee can recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing identical relief for employees who quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s choice to act in one statutory context, but not another, demonstrates it ‘knows how’ to achieve a particular policy goal when desired” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Based on our determination that only the Commissioner has authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, we find no error with the district court’s ruling. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (stating that under § 181.101(a), “enforcement of a penalty is reserved to the [Commissioner], with any recovered amount payable to the employee”).

B.

Regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim, he has acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages due to him under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he is entitled to statutory penalties as well. Both parties concur that, unlike overtime pay and liquidated damages, statutory penalties—if assessed—must be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture of the case, we have significant doubts about our jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of the fact that they are remitted to the government, may serve to “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” as such penalties can “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously stated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments make it indisputable that the allegedly improper conduct is not likely to reoccur, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, it is firmly established that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties stipulated that Cohen had received all owed overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, the parties continue to dispute whether Consilio maintains the overtime policy alleged to violate the law. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen could still pursue civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is "absolutely clear" that the misconduct will not occur again, then Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts, moreover, are not "license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]" Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for further proceedings to allow the district court to assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen's claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not give him the notice required by statute when it changed its overtime policy, and therefore he seeks penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court “included little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen has agreed that he has been paid in full for all amounts owed to him by Consilio, and he fails to cite any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this section. Since Cohen provides no substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s decision dismissing his claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, his sole argument is that he should receive "an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy." Initially, Cohen’s complaint demanded certain specified injunctive remedies, but he later altered his approach during summary judgment briefing to seek different relief—specifically, the rescission of the 2019 policy. The district court did not, understandably, address this new request. Therefore, this matter is not correctly presented on appeal. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties based on Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). It is possible that standing exists, yet there are distinctions between the penalties at issue there and those authorized by the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this question in their briefs. Additionally, I vacate the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s claim for MFLSA statutory penalties could be rendered moot, and therefore I agree with the decision to vacate the judgment and remand. Upon remand, the district court may address the mootness issue in the first instance. Id. at 180.
