Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen filed this action, asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions for overtime work. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, prompting Cohen to appeal. We uphold the decision in part, but in other respects, we vacate and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio, which operates as a legal document review firm with locations across the country, including Minnesota, employed Cohen starting in 2018 as a document reviewer who is a licensed attorney compensated on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly situated hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota via email that, effective August 2019, a revised premium overtime policy would take effect. Under this updated policy, overtime premium pay would be discontinued, and it was specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio failed to compensate him and other similarly situated employees with owed overtime wages. Cohen claimed these actions constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. The relief requested by Cohen included unpaid overtime, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio provided payments totaling $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers who are Minnesota-licensed attorneys, satisfying the demands for overtime compensation and liquidated damages as alleged in Cohen's MFLSA claim. Within these disbursements, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 for overtime and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties entered into stipulations establishing that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties were unable to reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen contended that he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties according to the MFLSA, and another $42,000 under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio asserted that the statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that the "most expedient and efficient path forward" would be for the Court to address and rule on Defendants' summary judgment motion concerning the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims prior to undertaking any further discovery or litigation. Cohen and Consilio both "acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants' summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved."

The district court proceeded to grant Consilio's summary judgment motion as to all three claims. Cohen has filed an appeal.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision granting summary judgment to Consilio, considering the evidence—as well as all reasonable inferences that can be drawn from it—in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate where the record demonstrates there are no genuine disputes concerning any material fact and the party requesting judgment is entitled to prevail as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin with Cohen’s claim brought under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 allows that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified MPWA provisions, one of which is § 181.101, and mandates that an employer “found to have violated” such a provision “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. The statute further permits recovery of civil penalties or damages by the employee, provided those remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA allegedly violated by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen claims that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, contending that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner when referring to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to reflect legislative intent and must be enforced accordingly.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties can only be imposed if the Commissioner initially “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Following that, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, “any money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). For employees, the statute only expressly authorizes them to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, its ability to grant a statutory right allowing an aggrieved employee to seek penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that a terminated employee may recover unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty” upon making a demand); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (granting the same right to employees who quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “[t]he Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that only the Commissioner is authorized to seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, we find no error in the district court’s judgment. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (clarifying that § 181.101(a) restricts the authority to enforce a penalty to the Commissioner, and any funds recovered are directed to the employee).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen asserts that he should also receive statutory penalties. Both parties concur that any statutory penalties assessed would be owed to the Commissioner, rather than to Cohen, unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages. At this point in the case, we are gravely concerned as to whether we have jurisdiction to rule on this matter.

Civil penalties, despite being remitted to the government, can nevertheless "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct," as such penalties may "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as previously articulated, "[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could become moot if later developments establish beyond doubt that the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nevertheless, as “[i]t is well settled that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,’” the issue may remain justiciable, id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties agreed that Cohen had received all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages; however, there remains a dispute as to whether Consilio continues to maintain the challenged overtime policy. Should this policy persist, Cohen may still pursue his claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has terminated the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the alleged wrongful conduct will not be repeated, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. For these reasons, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment concerning Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter for the district court to assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the notice required by statute regarding its change in overtime policy, arguing he is therefore eligible for penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen takes issue with the district court's decision, stating that the court offered minimal or no analysis on this point. However, Cohen conceded that he received all payments owed to him by Consilio, and he does not cite any statutory provision that grants civil penalties based on the alleged statutory violation. Because Cohen has offered no substantial argument warranting reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen requested certain particular types of injunctive remedies, but he later altered his position during summary judgment proceedings and sought different relief—namely, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, understandably, did not address this new request. Consequently, the matter is not appropriately before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s order relating to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm the district court’s judgment. Circuit Judge Kobes, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as outlined in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). He may have standing, but the statutory penalties at issue there differ from those available under the MFLSA. Neither side addressed this question in their briefs. I also vacate the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, and therefore I join in vacating the judgment and remanding. Upon remand, the district court may first address whether the case is moot. Id. at 180.
