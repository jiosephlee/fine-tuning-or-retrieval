Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in similar roles for overtime work. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now challenges that decision on appeal. We affirm the district court’s judgment in certain respects, but in other respects, we vacate the judgment and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio, which operates as a legal document review firm across the country and maintains offices in Minnesota, hired Cohen in 2018 as an hourly licensed attorney to perform document review tasks. In July 2019, Consilio emailed Cohen and other Minnesota hourly licensed attorney document reviewers to inform them that, beginning in August 2019, a new premium overtime policy would take effect. Under this new policy, overtime premium pay would be discontinued, and it was specified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated litigation against Consilio for himself and for a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not pay him and other similarly situated workers the overtime compensation they were due. Cohen claimed that this conduct constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief the payment of overdue overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime wages and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Of this amount, Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties agreed that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit," and further stipulated that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." However, they remained at an impasse with respect to penalties. Cohen contended he was owed $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio maintained that the statutes did not authorize any penalties for Cohen.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that "the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation." Cohen and Consilio "acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved."

The district court granted Consilio’s summary judgment motion as to all three claims. Cohen now brings this appeal.

II.

We examine the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio under a de novo standard, considering the evidence and any reasonable inferences from it in the manner most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted where there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen's claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree over Cohen’s eligibility to recover “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specific sections of the MPWA, including § 181.101. It further establishes that if an employer “is found to have violated” any such provision, it will be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Moreover, an employee may be awarded civil penalties or damages as long as such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that was allegedly breached by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen claims that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and asserts that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) specifies only the Commissioner in connection to penalty enforcement. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may be imposed solely after the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). For employees, the statute only preserves the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating that “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated that it is capable of granting an aggrieved employee an express statutory right to obtain penalties when it chooses to do so. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that when a discharged employee makes a demand, that individual may recover unpaid wages or commissions and may also “charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s action in one statute but not another indicates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a particular result if it so desires” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Based on our determination that only the Commissioner may pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, we hold that the district court did not err. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (clarifying that under § 181.101(a), “the authority to enforce a penalty is restricted to the [Commissioner], and any penalty funds collected are remitted to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed to him pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he is still entitled to recover statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that any statutory penalties awarded, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, would be payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen personally. At this juncture, we are substantially concerned about our jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties—even when remitted to the government—can “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who have suffered or face harm due to ongoing unlawful actions,” as such penalties may “motivate defendants to cease existing violations and dissuade them from future misconduct.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff is required to establish standing as to every claim asserted and for each type of relief requested.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously noted, “[t]he necessary personal stake that is required at the outset of litigation (standing) must persist for the duration of the case (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties may be rendered moot if later developments make it definitively clear that the supposed unlawful conduct cannot reasonably be anticipated to happen again. Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, it is a well-established principle that a federal court’s authority to rule on the lawfulness of the contested behavior remains intact even if the defendant voluntarily stops the challenged conduct. Id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties reached an agreement that Cohen had received all owed overtime wages as well as liquidated damages; however, whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy continues to exist is still contested. Should Consilio’s policy still be in effect, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties might still be legitimate. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” the challenged behavior will not resume, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could potentially be considered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts, moreover, are not “license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim and send the case back to the district court so it may address the question of its jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s claim under the MWTA that Consilio did not provide the required statutory notice regarding an alteration to its overtime policy, for which he asserts entitlement to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen contends that the district court offered minimal analysis of this issue. However, Cohen agreed that he received full payment owed to him by Consilio and cites no statutory provision authorizing civil penalties for violations of this statute. Absent substantial argument supporting reversal, we decline to upset the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he is owed “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, his complaint sought particular types of injunctive relief, but Cohen subsequently shifted his position and, in his summary judgment filings, asked for different relief—specifically, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, quite understandably, did not address this new request. Therefore, this matter is not appropriately before this court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s order with respect to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the case for additional proceedings. In all other respects, we affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, writing separately in concurrence.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as outlined in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that he does, distinctions exist between the penalties considered in that case and those provided for under the MFLSA. Additionally, the parties did not address this issue in their briefs. I also would set aside the district court’s decision with respect to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, and for this reason, I agree with vacating the judgment and remanding. On remand, the district court may address the mootness issue before proceeding further. Id. at 180.
