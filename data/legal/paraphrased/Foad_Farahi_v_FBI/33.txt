Case Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date of Decision: September 30, 2025  
Docket Number: 23-5243  
Opinion By: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to release the majority of the documents contained in the file, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The court, after directing the FBI to refresh its possibly outdated declarations, found that disclosing the requested documents could impede enforcement actions that were either ongoing or reasonably expected to occur. Accordingly, the court determined that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act mandates that federal agencies provide records to the public upon request, except where a specified statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Under Exemption 7(A), records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” may be withheld if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our court has determined that these enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies are required, when invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information, to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, an Iranian citizen, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 on the grounds of overstaying his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi has sought asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure at different stages. To contest these requests, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and in-person testimony from an FBI agent, who testified to having "documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists." Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI records under FOIA, and the agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might be responsive. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline, Farahi initiated legal action. In the years that followed, the FBI conducted a review of the records, released some documents to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once the review concluded, the FBI filed for summary judgment in October 2019. To support its position, the FBI provided a declaration by David Hardy, who serves as Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration stated, among other things, that the documents withheld were assembled for purposes of law enforcement and that disclosing them could reasonably be expected to disrupt enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or reasonably anticipated. The FBI also sought permission to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not object, and the district court granted the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records at issue were created for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, because a considerable amount of time had elapsed, the court required the FBI to provide an updated evaluation on whether such enforcement actions remained ongoing or could still be reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court found that additional details were necessary to resolve the issue of segregability, though it was unable to explain its reasoning for this necessity on the public docket. Accordingly, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, who at the time served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still in progress. The ex parte declaration from Bender supplied further specifics. Additionally, Bender addressed the district court’s issues related to segregability by offering further information.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court relied on the FBI's assertion "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified its position that non-exempt material could not be reasonably separated from the exempt portions. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with other cases, we consider summary judgment decisions under a de novo standard of review. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The obligation rests on the government to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must establish that the withheld documents were gathered for law enforcement objectives and that their release would likely interfere with enforcement actions that are either ongoing or reasonably foreseen. Refer to id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Because the FBI’s core mission is law enforcement, its assertion of exemption 7 warrants a degree of deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially justified when records pertain to national security matters. The Court has repeatedly affirmed that executive branch determinations in FOIA cases deserve deference where national security is at stake—including, in particular, Exemption 7(A) scenarios involving counterterrorism investigation records. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (“CNSS”).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents “when nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Additionally, “a court may rely on government affidavits that describe with reasonable specificity the reasons why records withheld under a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records the FBI withheld are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” pursuant to U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given the FBI’s status as a law enforcement agency, its judgment regarding this criterion generally merits considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the agency’s statements must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” and also demonstrate a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its burden in this matter. The public Hardy declaration stated that the records withheld were collected “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration offered substantial specifics regarding the scope of the investigation. Additionally, the surrounding circumstances bolster that representation. Notably, in proceedings before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI investigated his alleged connections to one or more groups identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as to a person who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court of offenses linked to terrorism. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Thus, it is clear to us that the FBI has met its obligation to demonstrate that Farahi’s records were assembled for purposes of law enforcement.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the records in question “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent has established that this requirement means the enforcement proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings.” Id. at 1097–98. The government can establish the presence of such proceedings by simply confirming their “existence.” Id. at 1099. “As long as the investigation remains active in gathering evidence for a potential criminal case, and the premature disclosure of such evidence would compromise that case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that disclosure was likely to hinder ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. The public Hardy declaration stated that releasing details about an active investigation could reasonably be expected to disrupt it by alerting the subjects to the extent of the inquiry—an explanation that is broadly recognized in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration specified that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided additional information regarding the investigation. Further, the Bender declaration also indicated that the investigation was ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the elapsed time raises questions as to whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be anticipated. He refers to our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” of claims that investigations are ongoing might be insufficient to justify the use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Further, he argues the Bender declaration’s statement that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is likewise lacking in specificity. We concur with Farahi that, by itself, the Bender declaration is notably brief. Nonetheless, it would be inappropriate to evaluate the Bender declaration separately from its context. As discussed above, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were ongoing through the public Hardy declaration, which verified their existence, as well as through the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial information on the investigation’s nature and scope. The Bender declaration was offered by the government not to newly establish ongoing enforcement proceedings, but rather to confirm that there had been no change during the period its original summary-judgment motion was pending. Additionally, when it denied the initial summary-judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had previously determined that the FBI “seemingly” satisfied its burden to demonstrate ongoing enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Accordingly, the Bender declaration is best understood as supplementing and updating the Hardy declarations rather than operating independently.

The extensive nature of the FBI’s investigation lends credibility to its claim that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specific information contained within Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can reference allegations made by the FBI in Farahi’s immigration case: The FBI asserted that Farahi acted as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, it stated that he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. The FBI further alleged that Farahi was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” including Jose Padilla. Id. He was also accused of providing support to “charities that financed terrorism,” naming the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI’s public statements and its sealed submissions, we find that the FBI has satisfied its responsibility under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the ongoing existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a cursory objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain the basis for the categories it employed to describe the withheld documents. When an agency withholds records by grouping them into categories, the selected groupings must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not provide any detailed rationale as to why the FBI’s categories were overly broad. Consequently, Farahi has waived his arguments on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, the agency is nevertheless required to make "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (an agency is obligated to release "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record"). In its second order, the district court stated that, in Bender's supplemental ex parte submission, the FBI had given a "detailed justification" explaining why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further concluded that this submission met the agency's burden in this regard. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the large number of responsive documents, some information should be separable and subject to release. However, where limited non-exempt content is scattered in isolated lines throughout a document, such content generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The emphasis of FOIA is on the release of information, not merely the dissemination of isolated words written by the Government. Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even if some discrete words or short phrases within the responsive materials could be provided without compromising the FBI’s interests, this fact is not determinative. The district court was correct in finding that no significant, releasable information could be reasonably segregated.

Farahi lastly argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration when conducting its segregability analysis. However, the court was under no obligation to place its analysis of the ex parte declaration on the public record. First, Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, so he is precluded from contesting the court’s use of them. Second, although the use of ex parte declarations may indeed disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this difficulty “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
