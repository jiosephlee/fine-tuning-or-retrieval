Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket: No. 23-5243  
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of the documents contained within the file, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court required the FBI to refresh its declarations, which may have become outdated, and then determined that releasing the sought-after documents could potentially disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Consequently, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public when requested, unless a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if making them public “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that enforcement proceedings under this exemption must either be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information, agencies are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 on the basis that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. To contest these requests, DHS offered both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a FOIA request seeking his FBI file, prompting the agency to identify approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant material. When the FBI failed to respond within the timeframe mandated by statute, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. Over the subsequent years, the agency examined the potentially responsive materials, provided certain records to Farahi, but withheld the majority. After its review was finished, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s declaration, among other points, stated that the documents being withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with then-pending or expected enforcement proceedings. The FBI additionally requested permission to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte; Farahi did not object to this motion, and the district court approved it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records were created for law enforcement purposes and found that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records could hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, because a considerable amount of time had elapsed, the court instructed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation as to whether such enforcement actions were still active or reasonably expected. Additionally, the court found it required additional information to resolve the issue of segregability, though it could not articulate its reasoning on the public record. Consequently, the court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI submitted revised declarations and renewed its request for summary judgment. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who at that time served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender stated that RIDS had contacted the agent managing Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still ongoing. Bender’s ex parte declaration offered further details. Bender also supplemented the record with additional information addressing the district court’s segregability concerns.

The district court then granted the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s assertion “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. After reviewing the ex parte submission, the court also determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt portions of the material could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA litigation, as with other matters, our review of summary judgment decisions is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The responsibility falls to the government to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this requirement, the FBI must establish that the materials withheld were assembled for law enforcement objectives and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. As the FBI is an agency with expertise in law enforcement, its invocation of exemption 7 is accorded deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when the documents at issue pertain to matters of national security. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—a principle applied even in Exemption 7(A) disputes concerning records from counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents “when nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). A court may accept government declarations that explain with sufficient detail why records withheld under a legitimate exemption are not capable of further segregation. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI functions as a law-enforcement agency, its judgment in this regard is generally accorded significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s affidavits must "demonstrate a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,'" as well as a "link between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'" Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its evidentiary burden. The public Hardy declaration indicated that the records in question were gathered "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations." J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration offered extensive specifics regarding the investigation’s character. Additional context supports these assertions: before the district court, Farahi admitted that the FBI had investigated him for associations with one or more groups identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as for his connections to a person who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Accordingly, we are satisfied that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the documents in question “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has interpreted the requirement to mean that such proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” as determined in id. at 1097–98, and the government may meet its burden by simply affirming the “existence” of those proceedings, id. at 1099. The exemption remains applicable “[s]o long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly found that releasing the records could reasonably be expected to disrupt pending or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. The public Hardy declaration set forth that revealing information about an ongoing investigation might interfere with it by alerting the subjects to the investigation’s breadth—an explanation commonly accepted under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration asserted that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte submission provided further details regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration also verified that, as of March 2023, the investigation was ongoing.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines any assertion that enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or reasonably expected. He relies on our observation in CREW that the “vague nature” of claims that investigations are still active may not adequately justify reliance on Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Additionally, he asserts that the Bender declaration’s statement that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was likewise conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, if viewed on its own, the Bender declaration seems notably brief. However, considering the Bender declaration in isolation is not warranted by the context. As previously discussed, the government demonstrated the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their continuation, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which offered substantial information regarding the investigation’s character and extent. The purpose of the Bender declaration was not to independently establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings, but rather to verify that the status had not changed during the pendency of the government’s initial summary judgment motion. Furthermore, when denying the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI “seemingly” satisfied its burden of proving the existence of pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Accordingly, the Bender declaration is best viewed as a supplement to the Hardy declarations rather than as an independent submission.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s underlying investigation lends credibility to its assertion that the inquiry is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the details contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can reference the allegations the FBI made about Farahi during his immigration case: The FBI stated that he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The Bureau further claimed he had associations with Khaled Sheikh Mohamed as well as other identified Al Qaeda members. Id. Additionally, he was reportedly “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. He was also said to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. After reviewing both the FBI’s public filings and its sealed statements, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a cursory objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently justify its delineation of document categories used to support its withholding determinations. When an agency withholds information based on categories, it is required to ensure those categories are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each one. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not specifically articulate why he believes the FBI’s chosen categories are excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his arguments on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request covers records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission as to why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large amount of responsive records, some information must exist that can be segregated and released. However, when non-exempt material is scattered “line-by-line throughout the document,” in minimal amounts, such content generally is not considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As Mead explains, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, although Farahi is right that isolated words or phrases in the records could potentially be released without compromising the FBI’s interests, that argument is immaterial. The district court properly determined that there was no reasonably segregable meaningful information.

Farahi also contends that the district court gave excessive weight to Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no obligation for the court to detail its evaluation of that declaration in the public record. To begin with, Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, so he cannot now contest the court’s use of them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations certainly put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, that issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
