Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to disclose the majority of these documents, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The court directed the FBI to revise any declarations that might be outdated. Upon review, the court determined that making the requested materials public could impede enforcement actions that were ongoing or could be reasonably foreseen. As a result, the court found that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to disclose records to the public upon request unless a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” where release of such information “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption are required to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 on the basis that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi has submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, as well as for voluntary departure. In contesting these requests for relief, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who "documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists." Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a request under FOIA seeking his FBI records. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might be responsive to his request. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the following years, the FBI reviewed the potentially responsive material, released some documents to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once its review was concluded, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, submitting a declaration from David Hardy, who is the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), in support. The Hardy declaration included, among other statements, that the materials being withheld were collected for law enforcement purposes and that disclosing them could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt enforcement actions that were ongoing or likely to occur. The FBI sought permission to file a supplemental Hardy declaration both under seal and ex parte. Farahi did not object to this request, and the district court granted the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records at issue had been created for law enforcement purposes and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that releasing the records might disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable lapse of time, the court instructed the FBI to reexamine whether such enforcement actions remained pending or reasonably expected. Additionally, the court found it required additional details in order to evaluate segregability, noting it could not elaborate on the reasoning for this need in the public record. As a result, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency presented both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who served as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS at that time. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who affirmed that the investigations were still active. The ex parte declaration from Bender offered further specifics. Additionally, Bender supplied more details to address the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. Relying on the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing,” J.A. 138–39, the court further determined—after examining the ex parte submission—that the FBI had sufficiently justified why material subject to exemption could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under the FOIA, summary judgment is subject to de novo review, just as in other types of cases. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government's responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is warranted. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation in the present matter, the FBI must establish that the records withheld were gathered for law enforcement activities and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to impede enforcement actions that are ongoing or expected. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such deference is especially warranted when the records at issue pertain to national security interests. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including specifically in Exemption 7(A) cases involving requests for records concerning counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the portions that are not exempt “are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). In such cases, courts may consider affidavits from the government which demonstrate, with reasonable detail, why records withheld under a valid exemption cannot be further segregated. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI functions as a law enforcement body, its conclusion regarding this criterion is typically accorded significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s statements must demonstrate a logical “‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” along with an explicit “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s determination that the FBI satisfied its burden in this case. The public Hardy declaration described that the withheld documents were created “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration provided significant details concerning the nature of that investigation. This position is further supported by the surrounding circumstances. Before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI had investigated his connections to one or more entities designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as his association with an individual who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and, later, convicted on terrorism-related charges in federal court. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are convinced that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the documents must be such that it "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings," as set forth in U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this standard necessitates the enforcement proceedings be "pending or reasonably anticipated." See CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations are considered "proceedings," id. at 1097–98, and the government can satisfy this requirement by simply affirming the "existence" of such proceedings, id. at 1099. As explained in Juarez, "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that disclosure was likely to hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. The public Hardy declaration described how releasing details about an active investigation might jeopardize it by alerting subjects to the investigation’s scope—an argument commonly accepted under Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration stated that the investigation in question was still "ongoing." J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte submission provided further specifics about the investigation, and the Bender declaration substantiated that the investigation was ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He references our statement in CREW, where we noted that merely asserting in vague terms that investigations continue might not be enough to justify use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Furthermore, he argues that the Bender declaration—by simply stating that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131—is similarly lacking in substantiation. We concur with Farahi that the Bender declaration, viewed in isolation, comes across as notably succinct. However, it is inappropriate to assess the Bender declaration on its own, given the surrounding context. As previously discussed, the government had already demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement actions through the public Hardy declaration—which confirmed such proceedings—and the ex parte Hardy declaration—which described their character and scope in detail. The Bender declaration’s purpose was not to independently establish the existence of pending enforcement activity, but rather to affirm that circumstances remained unchanged while the government’s initial motion for summary judgment was being considered. Additionally, in its earlier decision denying the initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had determined that the FBI appeared to have satisfied its responsibility to show the existence of ongoing enforcement actions. J.A. 122. Accordingly, it is reasonable to view the Bender declaration as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, not as an independent statement.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s initial investigation supports the plausibility of its claim that the investigation is still active. While the substance of Hardy’s ex parte declaration cannot be revealed, it is pertinent to highlight the FBI’s public assertions regarding Farahi during his immigration case: He was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI also alleged that he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. He was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, Farahi allegedly supported “charities that financed terrorism,” including both the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Upon review of both the public and sealed statements from the FBI, we find that the agency has satisfied its responsibility under Exemption 7(A), establishing the continued existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a limited objection, contending that the government insufficiently explained its basis for defining the document categories underpinning its withholding rationale. Agencies that rely on categorical withholdings are required to utilize groupings that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each respective category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any concrete reasons as to why the particular categories formulated by the FBI are impermissibly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his contentions on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency is still obligated to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (stating that “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record” must be disclosed by the agency). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” explaining why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated, as set forth in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court also determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the large number of responsive documents, some information must be both non-exempt and capable of being released. However, when a minimal amount of non-exempt content appears sporadically “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such material generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, while Farahi is correct that certain isolated words or short phrases within the documents could potentially be revealed without compromising the FBI’s interests, this does not affect the analysis. The district court properly found that no substantive, segregable information was available to be disclosed.

Farahi ultimately argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration when evaluating segregability. However, there was no obligation for the court to provide its assessment of that declaration on the public record. Additionally, Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, and thus cannot now dispute the court’s consideration of them. Moreover, although ex parte declarations certainly create disadvantages for FOIA plaintiffs, such challenges “are inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
