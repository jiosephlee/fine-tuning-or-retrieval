Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to release the majority of the documents contained therein, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The court directed the FBI to revise any potentially outdated declarations previously provided. Ultimately, the court determined that disclosing the requested documents might impede enforcement actions that were ongoing or could reasonably be expected. As a result, the court ruled that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded these records from disclosure. We concur with this determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request unless a specific exemption set forth in the statute is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” where releasing such records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that enforcement proceedings under this exemption must either be currently “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on his overstaying his visa. Throughout the course of these proceedings, Farahi submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, Convention Against Torture protections, and voluntary departure at different points. Contesting these forms of relief, DHS provided both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a FOIA request seeking his FBI records. The agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive material. After the FBI did not meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. Over several years, the FBI processed and reviewed the identified materials, released some documents to Farahi, and withheld the majority. When the review was concluded, in October 2019, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment. To support its position, the agency provided a declaration by David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy's declaration stated, among other points, that the withheld documents were gathered for law enforcement purposes and their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with then-current or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The FBI sought permission to submit an additional Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not oppose this request, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the documents in question were assembled for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure could disrupt ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the considerable time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to refresh its evaluation of whether any enforcement actions remained ongoing or could reasonably be anticipated. Additionally, the court found it required further information concerning segregability, for reasons that it was unable to disclose on the public record. Consequently, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI provided revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, who at that time served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In the publicly filed declaration, Bender stated that RIDS reached out to the agent assigned to manage Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. Bender’s ex parte declaration contained additional specifics. Furthermore, Bender supplied further details to address the district court’s issues with respect to segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. It accepted the FBI’s statement “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified its position that no reasonably segregable nonexempt material could be provided. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under the FOIA, summary judgment determinations are reviewed by this Court de novo. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The onus is on the government to demonstrate that an exemption under FOIA is applicable. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this obligation, the FBI is required to establish both that the records being withheld were compiled for purposes of law enforcement, and that their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings which are either ongoing or reasonably foreseen. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such judicial deference is especially warranted in cases involving materials touching upon national security. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—and this includes Exemption 7(A) cases involving the disclosure of counterterrorism investigation records. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to fully withhold records if the non-exempt material is so closely linked with exempt material that they cannot be separated. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are allowed to accept government declarations that detail with sufficient specificity why records withheld under a proper exemption cannot be further divided. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to materials that have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI operates as a law enforcement body, its judgment on this matter is owed significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the agency's affidavits must demonstrate a reasonable "nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties," and must also show there is a "connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s assessment that the FBI met its burden in this matter. The public Hardy declaration stated that the withheld materials were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The Hardy declaration submitted ex parte provided significant additional information regarding the character of that investigation. Further confirmation of this claim is found in the broader context. Farahi himself, in district court proceedings, acknowledged that he had been the subject of FBI investigation due to alleged connections to organizations identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, and associations with a person who was subsequently classified as an enemy combatant and convicted in federal court of terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We have no hesitation in concluding that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s records were assembled for purposes related to law enforcement.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates a showing that disclosure of the documents at issue “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our decisions establish that this requirement is satisfied when such proceedings are either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, active criminal investigations constitute “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government may meet its burden simply by affirming their “existence,” id. at 1099. Exemption 7(A) remains applicable so long as an investigation continues to collect information for a potential future prosecution, and the disclosure of that information would endanger that potential prosecution. Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that disclosure was likely to impede current or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. The publicly filed Hardy declaration stated that releasing information about an active investigation could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt it by alerting the investigation’s subjects to its scope—an established basis under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration confirmed that the relevant investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Further details were provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. The Bender declaration likewise affirmed that as of March 2023, the investigation was ongoing.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be anticipated. He references our observation in CREW that the “vague nature” of claims regarding ongoing investigations may be insufficient to justify Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Additionally, he argues that the Bender declaration’s assertion that the investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. While we acknowledge that, if viewed independently, the Bender declaration does appear notably brief, the relevant context counsels against an isolated analysis. As previously discussed, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were pending through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant details about the investigation’s scope and character. The Bender declaration was not intended to prove from the beginning that enforcement proceedings were pending; rather, it was submitted merely to confirm that no relevant circumstances had changed while the government’s initial summary-judgment motion was pending. Furthermore, by denying the government’s original summary-judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its burden to establish the existence of pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration can be reasonably read as a status update to the Hardy declarations rather than as a separate, self-sufficient submission.

The wide scope of the FBI’s investigation provides a reasonable basis for its claim that the inquiry is still in progress. While the details of Hardy’s ex parte declaration cannot be revealed, the FBI’s public statements regarding Farahi during immigration hearings are notable: it stated Farahi served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. It further alleged connections between Farahi and Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, as well as other identified Al Qaeda operatives. Id. The FBI also maintained that Farahi was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, he was said to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” among them the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. In light of both its public statements and the contents of sealed declarations, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligations under Exemption 7(A), demonstrating the existence of an ongoing “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief challenge, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently substantiate its rationale for how it defined the document categories that support its withholding determinations. When an agency withholds records on the basis of categories, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each, as stated in CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any particular reasons why the categories employed by the FBI were overly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his arguments on this issue. See Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request covers records that are protected, the agency is nonetheless required to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information,” as mandated by U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had furnished a “detailed justification” for the lack of reasonable segregability concerning the protected records, as detailed in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission was sufficient for the agency to meet its burden on the issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive documents, there must exist some information that can be separated and released. However, when minimal non-exempt content is sporadically “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such material is unlikely to be considered “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As stated in Mead, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, while it may be accurate that certain words or brief phrases within the documents could be disclosed without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests, this argument does not alter the analysis. The district court properly determined that there was no meaningful, reasonably segregable information.

Farahi also objects to the district court’s significant reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during the segregability assessment. However, there was no obligation for the court to make its analysis of that declaration part of the public record. Moreover, because Farahi did not contest the district court’s use of ex parte declarations at the time, he cannot now argue against the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, while reliance on ex parte declarations can place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this difficulty “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
