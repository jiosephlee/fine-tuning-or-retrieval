Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket: No. 23-5243  
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of these documents, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court directed the FBI to provide updated versions of previously filed declarations that might have become outdated. Upon review, the court determined that making the requested records public could disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Accordingly, the court found that FOIA Exemption 7(A) justified withholding the documents. We concur with this determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act mandates that federal agencies must provide records to the public upon request, except where a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” provided that releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This court has determined that such proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies asserting a FOIA exemption are required to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002, citing a visa overstay as the basis. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition, DHS submitted documentation and live testimony from an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking his FBI file. In response, the agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive material. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi brought suit. Over several years, the FBI conducted a review of the potentially responsive records, providing some to Farahi while withholding most. When the review concluded, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other claims, stated that the records withheld had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. The FBI further sought permission to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte; Farahi did not contest this request, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the documents at issue were created for law enforcement purposes, and found that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure could hinder ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable lapse of time, the court required the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding whether such enforcement actions were in fact still ongoing or reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court found it necessary to obtain further information to rule on the issue of segregability, though it was unable to elaborate on these reasons on the public record. Consequently, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI presented updated declarations and renewed its summary judgment motion. On this occasion, the agency offered both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had consulted with the agent in charge of Farahi’s case file, who verified that investigative efforts were continuing. Bender’s ex parte declaration included additional detail. Bender also addressed the district court’s prior questions concerning segregability by providing further information.

The district court then granted the FBI’s renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s assertion “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. After considering the ex parte materials, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified its claim that non-exempt information could not reasonably be segregated from exempt material. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under FOIA, as in other matters, we assess summary judgment rulings de novo. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this requirement in the present case, the FBI must establish that the documents withheld were compiled for law enforcement objectives and that making them public would reasonably be anticipated to interfere with current or expected enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Because the FBI is an agency specializing in law enforcement, deference is given to its use of exemption 7. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such deference is especially warranted where the documents involve issues of national security. This Court has "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated," including in cases under Exemption 7(A) relating to records from counterterrorism probes. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire records if the portions that are not exempt “are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). A court is entitled to rely on government declarations that provide with reasonable detail the reasons why material covered by a valid exemption cannot be further separated. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Because the FBI operates as a law-enforcement entity, its judgment on this matter is accorded a significant degree of deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI's statements must show both a logical “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” and a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its burden in this instance. The Hardy declaration submitted on the public record stated that the withheld materials were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The confidential Hardy declaration provided additional specifics concerning the nature of the investigation. Additional context further substantiates this claim. Before the district court, Farahi admitted that the FBI investigated him in connection with one or more entities designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations and with an individual who was later held as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court of terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Accordingly, we are satisfied that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s records were assembled for law-enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that the disclosure of withheld documents “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent has established that this standard is met when enforcement proceedings are either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Under this standard, active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government may satisfy its burden regarding the existence of such proceedings by simply attesting to their “existence.” Id. at 1099. According to Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.”

The district court properly found that releasing the records could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt pending or expected enforcement proceedings. The public Hardy declaration detailed that providing information concerning an ongoing investigation could likely interfere with it by alerting targets to the investigation’s scope—a rationale consistently recognized in Exemption 7(A) matters. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration further indicated that the investigation at issue was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration offered additional detail about the nature of the investigation, while the Bender declaration verified that the investigation was still active as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines the claim that enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated. He relies on the observation made in CREW that a “vague nature” of the assertion that investigations are still in progress may be insufficient to justify use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further argues that the Bender declaration’s statement that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi to the extent that, on its own, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, considering the context, it would be inappropriate to evaluate the Bender declaration in isolation. As detailed above, the government demonstrated the ongoing nature of enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which attested to their continuation, as well as through the ex parte Hardy declaration, which offered extensive details regarding the investigation’s nature and scope. The purpose of the government’s submission of the Bender declaration was not to newly establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings, but rather to reaffirm that the situation had not changed during the time its original summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, when the district court previously denied the government’s summary-judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to show pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as a supplemental update to the Hardy declarations, instead of as an independent document.

The expansive scope of the FBI’s initial inquiry also supports the plausibility of its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although the details of Hardy’s ex parte declaration cannot be revealed, we emphasize the FBI’s public statements regarding Farahi during his immigration case: the agency identified Farahi as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI also asserted that he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members,” id.; “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” including Jose Padilla, id.; and provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” specifically the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation, id. In light of the FBI’s assertions, both public and in sealed filings, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its burden under Exemption 7(A) by establishing that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is ongoing. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a limited objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale in defining the document categories used to support its withholding justifications. When an agency chooses to withhold documents based on specific categories, it is required to define those categories with enough clarity so that a court can assess whether the asserted exemptions are properly applied to each one. See CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reasons why the categories outlined by the FBI were excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has waived his arguments on this matter. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include materials that are protected, the agency is still required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” explaining why the protected records were not reasonably segregable, as described in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the extensive amount of responsive records, some material must be segregable and releasable. However, if only minimal non-exempt content is “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even if Farahi is right that some isolated words or phrases could be released without prejudicing the FBI’s interests, this does not alter the outcome. The district court properly determined that no meaningful information could be reasonably segregated.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration in conducting its segregability review. However, the court was not obligated to detail its consideration of that declaration in the public record. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from now contesting the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, although the use of ex parte declarations does put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
