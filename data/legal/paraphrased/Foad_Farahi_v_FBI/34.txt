Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to release the majority of those documents, leading Farahi to initiate a lawsuit. The court directed the FBI to revise any outdated declarations it had provided. Ultimately, the court determined that disclosing the requested materials might impede ongoing or foreseeably expected enforcement actions. As a result, the court found the documents shielded from disclosure under Exemption 7(A) of FOIA. We concur with this determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide public access to records upon request unless a specific exemption stated by statute applies. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Under Exemption 7(A), materials that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes” are exempt from disclosure if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This court has previously determined that such proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable efforts” to “segregate and disclose nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on the expiration of his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi has submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, as well as voluntary departure. The DHS opposed his requests for relief and presented both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI file pursuant to FOIA. In response, the FBI found approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant materials. After the FBI failed to comply with the statutory timeline for its response, Farahi filed a lawsuit. Subsequently, over several years, the FBI evaluated the possibly responsive documents, released some materials to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once its review was finished, in October 2019, the FBI sought summary judgment. It supported this motion by submitting a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other points, contended that the records being withheld had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosing them could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement actions that were pending or could reasonably be anticipated. The FBI also requested permission to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not object to this request, and the district court granted the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records were assembled for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable lapse of time, the court required the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding the existence of any still-pending or reasonably expected enforcement proceedings. Additionally, the court ruled that further details were necessary to resolve the question of segregability, though it could not elaborate publicly. Consequently, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and once again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency presented both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, who at the time served as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. The ex parte declaration from Bender included further particulars. Additionally, Bender supplied supplemental information to address the district court’s questions related to segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion, giving weight to the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after considering the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why non-exempt material could not reasonably be separated from exempt information. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under the FOIA, summary judgment decisions are reviewed de novo, just as in other types of cases. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government's responsibility to demonstrate that a particular FOIA exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). In this instance, to meet its burden, the FBI must establish that the records it withheld were assembled for the purpose of law enforcement, and that releasing them would reasonably be expected to impede enforcement actions that are either pending or foreseeably imminent. Refer to id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Due to the FBI’s expertise in law enforcement, its invocation of exemption 7 warrants judicial deference. See Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This level of deference is especially warranted in the context of records touching on national security issues. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including in cases under Exemption 7(A) concerning records pertaining to counterterrorism inquiries. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents “when the nonexempt material is so closely connected to exempt material that it cannot reasonably be segregated.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may accept government declarations that explain with sufficient detail why records withheld under a legitimate exemption cannot be further separated. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the FBI's withheld records are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” as set forth in U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI functions as a law-enforcement body, its assessment in this regard is generally entitled to significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s statements must demonstrate a reasonable “‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” along with a “‘connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its obligation in this matter. The public Hardy declaration described the records withheld as being gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration elaborated significantly on the specifics of the investigation. Additional background supports this characterization. Before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI investigated him for connections to one or more organizations identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as for links to an individual subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and later convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully persuaded that the FBI met its burden to demonstrate that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) additionally mandates that disclosure of the documents in question “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” as outlined in id. at 1097–98, and the government can satisfy its burden by merely attesting to the “existence” of such proceedings, see id. at 1099. As articulated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.”

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt current or foreseeable enforcement proceedings. The public Hardy declaration detailed that revealing information related to an active investigation might tip off targets about the investigation’s scope, which is a well-established rationale for withholding under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration asserted that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided further detail on the status of the investigation. The Bender declaration also confirmed that, as of March 2023, the investigation continued to be ongoing.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated. He refers to our observation in CREW that the “vague nature” of claims that investigations are ongoing might not be adequate to justify invoking Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Additionally, he claims that the Bender declaration’s statement that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, when viewed alone, the Bender declaration is notably succinct. However, taking context into account suggests the Bender declaration should not be reviewed in isolation. As discussed above, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings in the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their presence, and in the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial information regarding the investigation’s character and scope. The government introduced the Bender declaration not to initially prove the existence of pending enforcement proceedings, but rather to affirm that circumstances had not changed during the pendency of its initial summary-judgment motion. Furthermore, in denying the government’s original summary-judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden of establishing pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is appropriately viewed as an update to the Hardy declarations, instead of as an independent submission.

The extensive scope of the FBI's foundational investigation lends credibility to its claim that its inquiry is still in progress. While we are unable to reveal the details contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we do observe the FBI’s assertions made publicly regarding Farahi during his immigration case: namely, that he acted as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI also claimed he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, Farahi was purported to be “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Moreover, he was said to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” with specific reference to the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. In light of both public and confidential submissions by the FBI, we find that the agency has satisfied its responsibility under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that there is still a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief challenge regarding the sufficiency of the government’s justification for its categorization of documents underlying its withholding determinations. Under the applicable standard, when an agency withholds records by grouping them into categories, those categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to the respective categories. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any detailed argument as to why the FBI’s particular categories were impermissibly broad. Thus, Farahi has forfeited any claims on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, the agency is nevertheless required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (stating the agency must make available “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” demonstrating that, as presented in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, the protected records were not reasonably segregable. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this showing met the agency’s burden in this respect. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive documents, there must exist at least some information that can be separated and disclosed. However, when the limited non-exempt content is “randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such information generally cannot be considered “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The underlying principle of FOIA emphasizes the release of substantive information rather than merely individual words or phrases recorded by the Government. Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even if, as Farahi argues, the documents might contain isolated words or brief segments that could theoretically be released without compromising the FBI’s interests, that fact is inconsequential. The district court correctly determined that no significant information could reasonably be separated for release.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability assessment. However, the court was not obligated to publicly disclose its evaluation of that declaration. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s use of them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations can certainly disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this difficulty “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
