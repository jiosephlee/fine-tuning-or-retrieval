Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket: No. 23-5243  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of those documents, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The court, after directing the FBI to refresh potentially outdated declarations previously filed, determined that disclosing the requested materials could impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. Accordingly, the court found the records exempt from disclosure under FOIA Exemption 7(A). We concur with this conclusion.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request, unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) specifically relates to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” where the release of such records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that the enforcement proceedings contemplated by this exemption must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Under FOIA exemptions, agencies are required to make "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) ("Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.").

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds of a visa overstay. Throughout the course of these proceedings, Farahi has submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, relief pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition to these requests, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and the in-court testimony of an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI file. The agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially relevant materials. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline to respond, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. For several years following, the FBI reviewed these records, released some portions to Farahi, and withheld the majority. After finishing its review, the FBI submitted a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, supported by a declaration from David Hardy, who serves as Section Chief for the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration stated, among other things, that the documents withheld had been compiled in connection with law enforcement proceedings and their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement matters. The FBI further sought leave to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal; Farahi did not contest this request, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, because a considerable amount of time had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be anticipated. The court further found that additional details were necessary to rule on segregability, citing reasons that could not be disclosed on the public docket. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI provided revised declarations and again moved for summary judgment. On this occasion, the agency filed both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, the then-Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender described that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. Bender elaborated further in his ex parte declaration. Additionally, Bender included supplemental information to respond to the district court’s issues about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Additionally, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified its position that exempt information could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with any other type of case, we assess a district court’s summary judgment ruling anew, without deference. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government's responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption applies. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must establish that the documents withheld were compiled for purposes of law enforcement and that their release could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with enforcement actions that are ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. "Given the FBI’s primary focus on law enforcement, its determination to use exemption 7 merits deference." Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted regarding materials touching on national security issues. This Court has “repeatedly affirmed that deference to the executive branch is required in FOIA cases where national security interests are present”—this includes Exemption 7(A) cases involving access to records from counterterrorism probes. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents “when nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Furthermore, a court may accept government affidavits that demonstrate with reasonable particularity why documents withheld under a valid exemption cannot be segregated further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI functions as a law enforcement agency, its conclusion regarding this requirement is generally accorded significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the agency’s affidavits must demonstrate a reasonable "nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties," and also show a "connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’" Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary obligations in this matter. The public Hardy declaration clarified that the documents being withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration offered significant specifics regarding the character of that investigation. Furthermore, additional circumstances support this claim. Before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him for connections to one or more organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department, as well as for associations with an individual subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court for terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully satisfied that the FBI demonstrated that Farahi’s file was assembled for law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the withheld materials “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This court has determined that this standard necessitates enforcement proceedings to be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For these purposes, ongoing criminal investigations are considered “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government can establish such proceedings merely by confirming their “existence,” id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.” 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that release of the information could reasonably be anticipated to hinder current or expected law enforcement actions. The public Hardy declaration detailed that providing information about an active investigation could reasonably lead to interference by alerting subjects to the investigation's parameters—an explanation that has been broadly accepted in Exemption 7(A) matters. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Furthermore, the declaration affirmed that the relevant investigation was “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided additional details regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration also verified that the investigation was still in progress as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time raises questions regarding whether enforcement actions were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He relies on our observation in CREW that simply making a "vague" claim about ongoing investigations may not be sufficient to justify the use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Additionally, he argues that the Bender declaration’s assertion that investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in substance. While we acknowledge that the Bender declaration, if viewed on its own, appears unusually brief, considering it in isolation would overlook its context. As previously discussed, the government had already demonstrated the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their presence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant information about the investigation's character and breadth. The Bender declaration’s purpose was not to re-establish the existence of pending enforcement actions, but rather to affirm that circumstances had not changed during the pendency of its initial summary judgment motion. Furthermore, when it denied the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to show ongoing proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best understood as supplementing the Hardy declarations, rather than serving as an independent proof.

The wide scope of the FBI’s investigation supports the plausibility of its assertion that the inquiry is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the information contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we observe the FBI’s public allegations during Farahi’s immigration case: Farahi was accused of being “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, he was alleged to be “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. He was purportedly “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, he was said to support “charities that financed terrorism,” among them the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. In light of both the public and sealed declarations from the FBI, we find that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the continued existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a cursory objection, contending that the government failed to sufficiently explain its basis for defining the document categories underlying its withholding decisions. When an agency groups documents for withholding, the categories employed must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each, as stated in CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate a particularized reason for asserting that the FBI’s delineated categories were excessively broad. Consequently, Farahi has forfeited any arguments regarding this issue. See Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, the agency is nonetheless required to make "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (the agency must disclose "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record"). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a "detailed justification" in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission as to why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding this issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large number of responsive documents, some information should be both segregable and subject to release. However, when only minimal non-exempt content is “randomly ‘interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,’” such content is generally not deemed “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, while Farahi correctly asserts that isolated words or snippets within the records might not compromise the FBI’s interests if released, that fact is immaterial. The district court properly found that no significant information could be reasonably segregated for release.

Farahi also asserts that the district court excessively depended on Bender’s ex parte declaration when evaluating segregability. However, there was no obligation for the court to place its assessment of that declaration on the public docket. To begin with, since Farahi did not object to the district court's consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court's reliance on them. Additionally, even though ex parte declarations certainly disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this limitation “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
