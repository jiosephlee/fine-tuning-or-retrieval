Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, as appellant, sought access to his FBI file under the Freedom of Information Act. The FBI declined to release most of the responsive documents, leading Farahi to initiate legal proceedings. The district court directed the FBI to revise certain declarations that might have become outdated. Upon review, the court determined that disclosing the sought records would pose a risk of hindering enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or could be reasonably expected. Consequently, the court found the records were shielded from disclosure by Exemption 7(A) of FOIA. We concur with this determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public when requested, except where a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” in situations where their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedents establish that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When asserting a FOIA exemption, agencies are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. To contest these applications, DHS introduced documents and the in-court testimony of an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi later made a request under the Freedom of Information Act for his FBI file. The FBI identified around 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially responsive. When the FBI failed to respond by the statutory deadline, Farahi filed suit. In the subsequent years, the FBI reviewed the potentially responsive records, releasing some to Farahi but withholding the majority. After completing the review, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration claimed, among other points, that the records being withheld were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to disrupt ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. The FBI also requested permission to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not object to this request, and the district court granted it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the documents had been assembled for purposes of law enforcement and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden of demonstrating that disclosure could disrupt ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable amount of time that had elapsed, the court instructed the FBI to reassess and update its determination as to whether enforcement proceedings were actually ongoing or could still be reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court indicated a need for further information on the issue of segregability, which it explained could not be discussed on the public record. As a result, summary judgment was denied without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI filed updated declarations and renewed its summary judgment motion. In this round, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was then serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public filing, Bender described that RIDS had reached out to the agent in charge of managing Farahi’s file, who verified that investigations were still active. In his ex parte declaration, Bender supplied more specific details. He also elaborated further on the segregability concerns identified by the district court.

Upon consideration, the district court granted the renewed motion for summary judgment. The court accepted the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after reviewing the ex parte materials, the court concluded that the FBI had sufficiently explained why the information exempted from disclosure could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases brought under the FOIA, as with all other cases, our review of summary judgment rulings is de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The obligation falls on the government to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI is required to establish that the documents withheld were created for law enforcement objectives and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to impede enforcement actions that are ongoing or reasonably foreseen. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Since the FBI’s core function pertains to law enforcement, its reliance on Exemption 7 receives deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such judicial deference is especially warranted when the materials involve issues of national security. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—as has specifically been affirmed in matters under Exemption 7(A) concerning requests for documents connected to counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

When nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined with exempt portions,” the government is entitled to withhold records in full. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are permitted to rely on governmental declarations that demonstrate with reasonable specificity the impossibility of further segregating nonexempt information from documents properly withheld under a valid exemption. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to materials that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given the FBI’s status as a law enforcement agency, its assessment on this issue receives substantial deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the agency’s affidavits must “show a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met this requirement. The public Hardy declaration indicated that the documents withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Further, the ex parte Hardy declaration elaborated in detail on the nature of this investigation. Context supports this claim; in proceedings before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated his links to organizations classified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations and his connections to a person later detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are persuaded that the FBI has satisfied its burden in showing that Farahi’s file was assembled for law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that releasing the documents withheld must have a reasonable likelihood of interfering with enforcement proceedings. U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This court has determined that the standard requires the proceedings in question to be either ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, criminal investigations that are still underway qualify as “proceedings.” Id. at 1097–98. The government can meet its burden of demonstrating such proceedings by merely attesting to their continued “existence.” Id. at 1099. As held in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, Exemption 7(A) is satisfied whenever an investigation is active and is collecting evidence for a prospective criminal matter, so long as disclosure at that stage would undermine the case.

The district court properly found that the release of the records in question could be reasonably anticipated to impede enforcement proceedings that are pending or foreseeably imminent. According to the public Hardy declaration, making public certain information about an ongoing investigation could reasonably be expected to cause interference by alerting the investigation’s targets to its boundaries—an explanation that is well established in the context of Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. This declaration further asserted that the investigation at issue was still ongoing. J.A. 49. Hardy's sealed declaration provided additional details regarding the investigation, while the Bender declaration further substantiated that as of March 2023, the investigation continued to be active.

Farahi contends that the length of time that has elapsed creates uncertainty as to whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably have been anticipated. He relies on our observation in CREW that the “vague nature” of claims that investigations are ongoing may be insufficient to justify the use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Further, Farahi asserts that the Bender declaration, which states the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in specificity. We concur with Farahi that, if viewed alone, the Bender declaration appears notably brief. However, it would be inappropriate to evaluate the Bender declaration apart from its context. As outlined earlier, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which attested to their status, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which described the investigation’s character and breadth in detail. The government did not offer the Bender declaration to establish from the outset that enforcement proceedings were pending, but instead to verify that circumstances had not changed during the pendency of its initial motion for summary judgment. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined that the FBI “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that enforcement proceedings were pending. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is properly viewed as a supplement to the Hardy declarations rather than as an independent submission.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s initial investigation lends credibility to its claim that the investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the specific contents of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, it is relevant to reference what the FBI asserted in open court during Farahi’s immigration case: The agency described Farahi as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, he was alleged to be “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. The FBI further stated that Farahi had ties to “multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, he was said to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into account both the FBI’s public statements and its sealed filings, we determine that the Bureau has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the continued existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief challenge, contending that the government did not sufficiently substantiate its categorization of documents used to justify its withholding determinations. Agencies that withhold materials by category are required to use groupings that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to those categories. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi fails to specifically articulate why the categories utilized by the FBI were overly broad. Therefore, Farahi has waived this line of argument. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may cover records that are protected, agencies are still obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring the disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had supplied a “detailed justification” for why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated, as explained in Bender’s supplemental ex parte filing. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding segregation. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the large volume of documents responsive to the request, there must exist some segregable and releasable portions. Nevertheless, when any non-exempt content is merely “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content is generally not considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As the “focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down,” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55, the mere possibility that isolated words or short phrases could be released without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests does not alter the outcome. The district court’s determination that there was no reasonably segregable meaningful information was proper.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there is no obligation for the court to publicly disclose its assessment of that declaration. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from challenging the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, even though ex parte declarations can indeed put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, such difficulties “are inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
