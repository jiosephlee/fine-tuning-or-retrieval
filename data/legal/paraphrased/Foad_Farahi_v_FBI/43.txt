Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to release most of the documents contained in the file, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court ordered the FBI to provide revised declarations in light of possible outdated information previously submitted. Upon review, the court determined that disclosing the requested documents could impede enforcement proceedings that were either ongoing or could reasonably be expected. As a result, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request unless a specifically listed statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) specifically protects from disclosure any records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if releasing those records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that such enforcement proceedings must either be currently “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable efforts” to “segregate and disclose nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him, alleging that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of these immigration proceedings, Farahi has sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, as well as voluntary departure. In opposing his requests for relief, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and the in-court testimony of an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI records. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing information that might be responsive. When the FBI failed to meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. Subsequently, over several years, the FBI examined potentially responsive materials, releasing some documents to Farahi and withholding the majority. After finishing its review, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019. In support of its motion, the agency provided a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other claims, stated that the withheld documents were collected for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with either ongoing or anticipated enforcement actions. The FBI requested permission to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and on an ex parte basis. Farahi did not oppose this request, and the district court granted the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the records had been gathered for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might hinder ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, in light of the considerable lapse of time, the court directed the FBI to revisit its determination as to whether such proceedings remained pending or foreseeable. Additionally, the court determined that further details were needed to rule on segregability, though it could not elaborate on these reasons in the public record. Accordingly, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who at that time served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In the public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, and that agent affirmed that the investigations were still active. The ex parte declaration from Bender contained additional specifics. Bender also supplied further clarification aimed at addressing the district court’s issues concerning segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court relied on the FBI's assurance "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt material could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with other cases, we assess the district court’s summary judgment determination anew. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The obligation to demonstrate that an exemption under FOIA is applicable rests with the government. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this requirement in the instant case, the FBI must establish that the documents withheld were assembled for purposes of law enforcement and that their release could reasonably be expected to impede enforcement proceedings that are either ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given its primary law enforcement role, the FBI’s determination to apply exemption 7 warrants judicial deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when the documents at issue pertain to matters of national security. Our Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—specifically in situations involving Exemption 7(A) and requests for materials related to counterterrorism efforts. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents “when nonexempt material ‘cannot reasonably be segregated from exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). “Courts may accept government affidavits that demonstrate with sufficient detail why documents properly withheld under an exemption cannot be further separated.” Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the FBI’s withheld records are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI serves as a law enforcement body, its assessment regarding this requirement is generally afforded considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s statements must still demonstrate a reasonable “‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” along with a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its burden in this case. The public Hardy declaration described that the withheld documents were gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration further provided substantial specifics regarding the nature of the investigation. Additional context serves to reinforce this assertion. Farahi himself acknowledged in the district court that the FBI conducted investigations concerning his connections to certain entities designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations and to an individual who was later apprehended as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court for terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We therefore have no hesitation in determining that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s records were compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the requested materials “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this requirement means the proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For this purpose, active criminal investigations are considered “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government can establish their existence merely by providing an attestation, id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, Exemption 7(A) is implicated whenever an investigation is ongoing to collect evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution and release of such evidence ahead of time would put that prosecution at risk.

The district court properly determined that the release of the information was likely to interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. The Hardy declaration, available to the public, stated that revealing details about an active investigation could reasonably lead to interference by alerting targets to the investigation’s reach—a rationale widely accepted under Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration specified that the investigation in question was “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy's in camera declaration provided further details regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration also affirmed that the investigation was still underway as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the elapsed time raises questions about whether enforcement proceedings continued to be pending or were reasonably expected. He references our observation in CREW that simply claiming investigations are “ongoing,” without specificity, might not adequately justify relying on Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He further argues that the Bender declaration’s statement that investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in detail. While we concur with Farahi that, on its own, the Bender declaration appears unexpectedly brief, it is inappropriate to assess it apart from its context. As noted earlier, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their presence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial detail about the investigation’s character and breadth. The Bender declaration was submitted by the government not to initially prove that enforcement proceedings were pending, but simply to verify that circumstances had not changed while its first summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, when denying the government’s original summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its obligation to show there were pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Accordingly, the Bender declaration is properly viewed as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent submission.

The extensive scope of the FBI's initial investigation also lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the specific information contained in Hardy's ex parte declaration, we can reference the FBI's publicly stated accusations against Farahi during his immigration case: the agency identified him as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, they claimed he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. The FBI also alleged that he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, he reportedly supported “charities that financed terrorism,” among them the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI’s public statements and the sealed declarations, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by establishing that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is still ongoing. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection that the government failed to sufficiently explain the rationale behind the document categories it used to support its withholding decisions. When an agency withholds records using categorical groupings, it is required to ensure that these categories are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer a particularized reason as to why the categories used by the FBI were excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his arguments on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second ruling, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” within Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission as to why the protected records could not reasonably be segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court also determined that this submission was sufficient to meet the agency’s burden regarding segregation. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of records identified, there must exist portions that could be separated and disclosed. However, when only minimal non-exempt information is sporadically “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such material generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Accordingly, even though Farahi is right that isolated words or phrases might be disclosed without compromising the FBI’s interests, that point is irrelevant. The district court properly determined that there was no reasonably segregable meaningful information.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability analysis. However, the court was not obligated to publicly disclose its analysis of that declaration. First, Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations and therefore cannot now contest the court’s reliance on them. Second, although ex parte declarations unquestionably put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this “problem is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
