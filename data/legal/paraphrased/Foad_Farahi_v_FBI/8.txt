Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to disclose the majority of the documents contained in the file, leading Farahi to initiate legal action. The district court ordered the FBI to refresh declarations that might have become outdated, and, upon review, determined that providing the records could disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. Accordingly, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this conclusion.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request, except where a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” where releasing such records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This court has previously determined that such enforcement proceedings must either be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When an agency withholds material pursuant to a FOIA exemption, it is required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, an Iranian citizen, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002, citing a visa overstay. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security contested his requests for relief by submitting documentation and in-court testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) seeking his FBI file. In response, the agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing material that could be responsive. After the FBI did not meet the statutory timeframe to reply, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. Over several subsequent years, the agency examined potentially responsive records, released some to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once this review concluded, in October 2019, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment. To support its motion, the agency provided a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), who, among other assertions, stated that the records withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to impede then-pending or expected enforcement actions. The FBI sought leave to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte; Farahi did not object, and the district court granted the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records at issue were created for law enforcement purposes and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the lengthy lapse of time, the court directed the FBI to reassess whether such enforcement actions were still ongoing or could be reasonably expected. The court further decided that additional information was necessary to rule on the matter of segregability, though it was unable to specify the reasons for this need on the public record. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI responded by filing updated declarations and renewing its motion. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS at that time. In the public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had consulted with the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, who affirmed that the investigations were still active. Bender’s ex parte declaration included additional details. He also submitted further information addressing the district court’s segregability concerns.

Subsequently, the district court granted the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s representation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Additionally, after examining the ex parte materials, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified its position that exempt information could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA litigation, as with other cases, we examine a summary judgment ruling de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this obligation, the FBI must establish that the documents withheld were assembled for law enforcement objectives and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to impede enforcement actions that are either ongoing or reasonably foreseen. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. As the FBI is an agency primarily engaged in law enforcement, its assertion of Exemption 7 warrants deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deferential approach is especially fitting where the records involve matters of national security. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including in instances involving Exemption 7(A) for records pertaining to counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents “when nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). “A court may rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is restricted to materials “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI functions as a law enforcement body, its judgment in this regard generally warrants significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, its statements must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as demonstrate a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s finding that the FBI satisfied its burden in this case. The publicly filed Hardy declaration specified that the documents withheld were created “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The Hardy declaration filed ex parte offered additional, substantial details about the scope of the investigation. This assertion is further substantiated by the larger context. Before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him in connection with one or more groups the State Department designated as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as in connection with a person who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court of terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully satisfied that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that disclosing the withheld materials “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this clause requires the enforcement proceedings to be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings” within this context, id. at 1097–98, and the government can satisfy its burden by simply attesting to the “existence” of such proceedings, id. at 1099. “As long as the investigation is actively collecting evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and release of the evidence would compromise that prosecution, Exemption 7(A) is triggered.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be expected to disrupt enforcement proceedings that are ongoing or reasonably anticipated. The public Hardy declaration described how revealing details about an active investigation could reasonably risk interference by alerting subjects to the investigation’s parameters—a rationale frequently recognized in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration stated that the relevant investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided additional detail about the investigation. Furthermore, the Bender declaration verified that the investigation continued as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time raises questions about whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could be reasonably anticipated. He cites our observation in CREW that merely asserting the ongoing status of investigations in a “vague” manner might not be adequate to justify invoking Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He further argues that the Bender declaration, which states that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. While we recognize Farahi’s point that the Bender declaration, if viewed by itself, seems notably brief, context indicates that it should not be read in isolation. As previously discussed, the government had already demonstrated the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which attested to those proceedings, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial detail regarding the investigation’s nature and breadth. The government’s purpose in submitting the Bender declaration was not to independently establish the existence of pending enforcement proceedings, but rather to affirm that no material developments had occurred while its original summary judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, in denying the government’s initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to demonstrate the presence of pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is properly understood as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent document.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s initial inquiry also supports the plausibility of its claim that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specific details contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can reference the allegations made publicly by the FBI during Farahi’s immigration case: The agency described him as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. It also asserted that he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. The FBI further maintained that Farahi was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. In addition, he was said to support “charities that financed terrorism,” which included both the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into account the FBI’s public assertions and the contents of its sealed statements, we determine that the agency has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the existence of an ongoing “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief challenge, contending that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale for defining the categories of documents used to support its withholding justifications. Agencies that withhold records by categories must ensure those groupings are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each, as held in CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason why the categories delineated by the FBI were overly broad. Therefore, Farahi has forfeited these arguments. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even where a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to release “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission explaining why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this explanation was sufficient to fulfill the agency’s burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large number of responsive documents, there must exist some information that could be segregated and released. Nevertheless, when minimal non-exempt content is “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content is generally not considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The rationale is that “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, although Farahi is technically correct that some isolated words or phrases might be disclosed without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests, this fact is immaterial. The district court properly determined that no meaningful information could be reasonably segregated for release.

Farahi ultimately argues that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no obligation for the court to disclose its assessment of that declaration on the public record. First, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he cannot now contest the court’s use of them. Second, although ex parte declarations do, admittedly, put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
