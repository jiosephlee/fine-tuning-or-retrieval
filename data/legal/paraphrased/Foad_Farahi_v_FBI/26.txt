Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to disclose the majority of the documents contained therein, leading Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court instructed the FBI to provide refreshed declarations to replace those that might be outdated. The court found that disclosing the sought-after records had the potential to hinder enforcement actions that were ongoing or could reasonably be anticipated in the future. Accordingly, the court determined that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded these records from disclosure. We concur with this determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are generally required to provide records to the public when requested, except where a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that enforcement proceedings under this exemption must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable efforts” to “separate and disclose information that is not exempt.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 on the basis that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. To contest his eligibility for relief, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and the live testimony of an FBI agent, who testified regarding Farahi’s purported "connections to high-level terrorists." Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI file. In response, the agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant records. After the FBI did not meet the FOIA statutory deadline for a response, Farahi brought a lawsuit. Over a period of several years, the agency proceeded to review the materials it had found, released certain records to Farahi, but withheld the majority. When the review was complete, the FBI moved for summary judgment in October 2019. To support its position, the FBI submitted a declaration from David Hardy, who serves as Section Chief for the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The declaration, among other assertions, maintained that the records being withheld had been assembled for use in law enforcement proceedings and that disclosure of those records could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or foreseeable enforcement proceedings. Additionally, the FBI requested to file a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not object, and in October 2020, the district court approved the FBI’s motion.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the documents at issue had been assembled for law enforcement objectives and that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that their disclosure might hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the amount of time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding whether enforcement actions were still ongoing or could still be reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court determined that further details were necessary to resolve the question of segregability, though it was unable to explain its reasoning on the public docket. Accordingly, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency presented both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, at that time serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who affirmed that the investigations were still in progress. Bender’s ex parte declaration supplied additional specifics. Furthermore, Bender offered further information in response to the district court’s inquiries about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court relied on the FBI's assurance "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why any exempt information could not be reasonably separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In Freedom of Information Act cases, as with other matters, we examine the district court’s grant of summary judgment afresh. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s responsibility to demonstrate that a particular FOIA exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). Here, the FBI must establish that the documents it withheld were assembled for law enforcement objectives, and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to impede ongoing or reasonably foreseen enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. As “the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its invocation of exemption 7 warrants deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This level of deference is especially warranted when the information at issue involves national security matters. The Court has “repeatedly emphasized the necessity of deferring to the executive branch on national security grounds in FOIA cases”—including in disputes under Exemption 7(A) relating to documents from counterterrorism inquiries. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents where the nonexempt material cannot be separated from the exempt content because they are "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). The court may accept government affidavits that explain, with sufficient detail, why materials withheld under a proper exemption cannot be further segregated. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered under Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is restricted to materials that have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes," as provided in U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI is recognized as a law-enforcement body, its judgment in this respect receives a considerable degree of deference. See, for example, Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, its submissions must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” and also demonstrate a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's conclusion that the FBI satisfied its burden in this matter. The public Hardy declaration indicated that the documents withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration provided significant details concerning the nature of the investigation at issue. Additional circumstances further support this statement. Before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated his connections to one or more groups designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as to an individual who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and later found guilty in federal court of terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully convinced that the FBI demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for legitimate law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further requires that disclosing the withheld information “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has determined that this standard mandates that the proceedings in question must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations are considered “proceedings.” Id. at 1097–98. The government may establish the existence of such proceedings by simply affirming that they exist. Id. at 1099. “As long as the investigation is ongoing and continues to collect evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and disclosure of that evidence ahead of time would compromise the case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to impede ongoing or expected enforcement actions. The public Hardy declaration stated that making public details of an active investigation could likely hinder its progress by alerting subjects to the extent of the investigation—an often-cited reason for applying Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration affirmed that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided further detail regarding the investigation, and the Bender declaration verified that the investigation remained open as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the passage of time undermines the claim that enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be expected. He cites our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” of claims about ongoing investigations may be inadequate for justifying Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Additionally, he asserts that the Bender declaration’s statement that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is likewise merely conclusory. We concur with Farahi that the Bender declaration, when considered on its own, is notably succinct. However, it is inappropriate to examine the Bender declaration in isolation, given the overall context. As discussed earlier, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were pending through the public Hardy declaration, which asserted their existence, and through the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial information concerning the investigation’s character and breadth. The government presented the Bender declaration not as new proof of the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings, but rather as confirmation that circumstances had not changed while the initial motion for summary judgment was under consideration. Furthermore, when denying the government’s original motion for summary judgment without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its obligation to show that enforcement proceedings were pending. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent submission.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s underlying investigation lends credence to its assertion that the investigation is still in progress. Although we are unable to reveal the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we draw attention to the allegations the FBI made publicly during Farahi’s immigration hearings: The FBI alleged that Farahi served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. It further stated that he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additional claims included his being “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. The FBI also asserted that Farahi supported “charities that financed terrorism,” namely the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Having considered both the FBI’s public statements and the sealed declarations, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the continuation of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, claiming that the government failed to sufficiently justify its selection of document categories that formed the basis for its withholding determinations. When an agency chooses to withhold records by organizing them into categories, it must select groupings that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each particular category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason as to why the FBI’s specified categories were allegedly too broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his arguments on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency is still required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (mandating the disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record” by the agency). In its second ruling, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” for the lack of reasonably segregable material within the protected records in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). Furthermore, the court concluded that this submission met the agency’s evidentiary burden regarding segregation. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the large quantity of responsive documents, there should exist at least some portions that could be separated and released. However, when minor, non-exempt content is dispersed "line-by-line throughout the document," such content generally does not qualify as "reasonably segregable." See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, as articulated in Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Although Farahi is right that certain isolated words or short phrases within the records could theoretically be released without endangering the FBI’s interests, that fact does not alter the analysis. The district court properly found that no significant or meaningful information was capable of reasonable segregation.

Farahi’s final objection is that the district court placed undue weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no obligation for the district court to disclose its assessment of that declaration on the public docket. To begin with, Farahi failed to contest the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, so he is precluded from objecting to the court’s reliance on such submissions. Furthermore, though it is true that ex parte declarations present a disadvantage for FOIA plaintiffs, this challenge “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
