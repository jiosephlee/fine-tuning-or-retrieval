Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act to obtain his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of these documents, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court instructed the FBI to refresh potentially outdated declarations previously provided. Upon review, the court determined that releasing the sought records might disrupt enforcement actions that were ongoing or reasonably expected. As a result, the court ruled that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the documents from disclosure. We concur with this conclusion.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide public access to records upon request, unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to records that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” provided that disclosing such records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that the relevant enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable efforts” to “segregate and disclose nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him because he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, as well as for voluntary departure. Contesting Farahi’s eligibility for relief, the DHS submitted both documentary evidence and in-person testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested access to his FBI records under FOIA. The FBI found approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing information that might be relevant to the request. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for its response, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the years following, the FBI conducted a review of the documents that could be responsive, released some records to Farahi, and withheld the majority. When the review concluded, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its position with a declaration from David Hardy, who serves as Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other assertions, stated that the records withheld were assembled for law enforcement activities and their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or foreseeable enforcement proceedings. Additionally, the FBI requested permission to file a supplemental Hardy declaration both under seal and ex parte. Farahi raised no objection to this request, which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records were assembled for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, because a considerable amount of time had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to refresh its evaluation as to whether such proceedings remained ongoing or could still be reasonably expected. Additionally, the court found it required further information in order to resolve the issue of segregability, but could not specify its reasons on the public docket. Consequently, the court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations in support of its renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who at that time served as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent supervising Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. The ex parte declaration from Bender contained additional specifics. Further, Bender supplied extra information in response to the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion, giving weight to the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Additionally, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt information could not reasonably be separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under the FOIA, just as in all other matters, we conduct a de novo review of summary judgment decisions. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The responsibility to establish the applicability of a FOIA exemption rests with the government. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). Here, for the FBI to meet this obligation, it must demonstrate that the documents withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes, and that disclosing them could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with ongoing or expected enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted for materials connected to national security concerns. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including specifically in Exemption 7(A) cases involving requests for records connected to counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire records if the exempt material cannot be reasonably separated from the nonexempt portions, meaning they are “inextricably intertwined.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Furthermore, courts are allowed to accept government affidavits that explain with reasonable particularity why documents withheld under a legitimate exemption are incapable of further segregation. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI is a law enforcement entity, its judgment regarding this criterion is generally afforded considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s affidavits must demonstrate a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties” and also show a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary burden in this matter. The public declaration by Hardy described that the withheld documents were collected “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Additional details concerning the investigation were provided through Hardy’s ex parte declaration. This finding is further supported by the overall context of the case. During the district court proceedings, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him concerning links to one or more groups designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as for associations with an individual later detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Therefore, we are fully satisfied that the FBI demonstrated that Farahi’s records were compiled for law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the records at issue “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this requirement means the proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations are considered “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government may demonstrate the existence of such proceedings merely by affirming their “existence,” id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, “[s]o long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.” 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt current or foreseeably imminent enforcement proceedings. The public declaration by Hardy outlined that making information about an active investigation public could likely impede the process by alerting subjects to the investigation’s breadth, which is a well-recognized rationale under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration stated that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte statement provided further detail on this investigation. The Bender declaration also affirmed that, as of March 2023, the investigation was still active.

Farahi contends that the elapsed time raises questions about whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could be reasonably expected. He relies on our observation in CREW that merely claiming investigations are "ongoing" in a vague manner may be insufficient to justify invoking Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi also argues that the Bender declaration was similarly conclusory when it stated the investigations "remain ongoing," J.A. 131. We concur with Farahi that the Bender declaration, if considered by itself, seems unusually brief. However, the surrounding circumstances advise against evaluating the Bender declaration on its own. As previously discussed, the government already demonstrated the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings in the publicly filed Hardy declaration and further elaborated on the investigation’s scope and character in the ex parte Hardy declaration. The purpose of the Bender declaration was not to independently prove that proceedings were still pending, but rather to confirm that no circumstances had changed during the pendency of the government’s initial summary-judgment motion. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s earlier summary-judgment motion without prejudice, it already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to show there were pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is most sensibly read as an update to the Hardy declarations, rather than an independent submission.

The expansive scope of the FBI’s underlying investigation lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we observe that the FBI has publicly stated in Farahi’s immigration case that he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI also asserted he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, Farahi was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. He was further implicated in supporting “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI’s public allegations and its sealed submissions, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its evidentiary burden under Exemption 7(A) in demonstrating the existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale for selecting the categories of documents it used to support its withholding decisions. When an agency elects to withhold records by categorizing them, the chosen groupings must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” with respect to each category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not specifically articulate in what way the FBI’s designated categories were too broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his objections on this matter. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had given a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission regarding why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission fulfilled the agency’s burden on this matter. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of documents that are responsive, there should be at least some portions that could be separated and released. However, if any non-exempt material consists only of isolated fragments "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it likely does not qualify as "reasonably segregable." See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, FOIA is concerned with the disclosure of "information, ... not simply words which the Government has written down." Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even if, as Farahi asserts, isolated words or short phrases could be released without compromising the FBI’s interests, this is immaterial. The district court properly found that there was no reasonably segregable meaningful information.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed undue weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability assessment. However, there was no requirement for the district court to publicly document its evaluation of that declaration. Furthermore, Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations and thus cannot now contest the court's reliance on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations certainly disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
