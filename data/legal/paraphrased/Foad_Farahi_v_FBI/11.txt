Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his file maintained by the FBI. The Bureau withheld a substantial portion of these documents, prompting Farahi to initiate a lawsuit. The district court, after instructing the FBI to refresh any possibly outdated declarations it had provided, determined that making the requested documents public could hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Accordingly, the court ruled that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded these materials from disclosure. We concur with this determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request, unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” provided that their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent requires that such enforcement proceedings must either be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When an agency withholds records based on a FOIA exemption, it is required to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, an Iranian citizen residing in the United States since 1993, was placed in removal proceedings by the Department of Homeland Security in 2002 based on a visa overstay. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security opposed these forms of relief, introducing both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi later submitted a FOIA request seeking his FBI records. In response, the agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially containing relevant material. When the FBI failed to meet the deadline imposed by statute for its response, Farahi initiated litigation. Over the ensuing years, the agency examined the responsive records, releasing some documents to Farahi and withholding the majority. Once this process was finished, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, providing a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), which, among other assertions, stated that the withheld records had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could be reasonably expected to interfere with law enforcement activities that were ongoing or reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the FBI requested leave to submit a supplemental declaration from Hardy under seal and ex parte. Farahi did not object to this request, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the records in question had been assembled for law enforcement objectives and determined that the FBI had "seemingly" fulfilled its responsibility to demonstrate that disclosure might hamper ongoing or anticipated enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable lapse of time, the court directed the FBI to provide a revised evaluation as to whether enforcement actions were still underway or likely to be initiated. The court further found it required additional information to rule on the issue of segregability, for reasons that could not be disclosed on the public docket. As a result, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI furnished supplemental declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. On this occasion, the agency supplied both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS at the time. Within his public declaration, Bender indicated that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who verified that investigations were still open. Bender’s ex parte statement included additional detail. He also furnished further information pertinent to the court’s concerns over segregability.

The district court ultimately granted the renewed summary judgment motion. The court accepted as credible the FBI’s assertion “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, based on its examination of the ex parte materials, the court determined that the FBI had satisfactorily justified the non-segregability of exempt content. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA actions, as in other cases, our review of a summary judgment ruling is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The burden rests with the government to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must establish that the documents it withheld were collected for law enforcement purposes and that disclosing them could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with ongoing or likely future enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given the FBI’s focus on law enforcement, courts afford its decision to rely on exemption 7 a measure of deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when the materials at issue involve national security. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—applying this principle to Exemption 7(A) disputes over records pertaining to counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Records may be withheld in full by the government “when nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). A court is permitted to depend on government declarations that demonstrate with adequate specificity why records, withheld under a proper exemption, are not subject to further segregation. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI functions as a law-enforcement agency, its assessment in this area is generally afforded a considerable level of deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s statements must "demonstrate a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’" along with a "link between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’" Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s finding that the FBI satisfied its obligation in this instance. The public Hardy declaration stated that the withheld documents were gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration offered substantial information concerning the nature of that investigation. Additional context reinforces this position. Before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI investigated him for connections to one or more groups that the State Department designates as foreign terrorist organizations, and for relationships with a person later detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully satisfied that the FBI has met its burden to demonstrate that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the documents in question "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent has determined that this condition means the proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, active criminal investigations are considered "proceedings," id. at 1097–98, and the government can establish the existence of such proceedings by simply affirming their "existence," id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, "as long as the investigation is still actively seeking evidence for a potential criminal prosecution, and the early release of that evidence would jeopardize that prosecution, Exemption 7(A) is applicable." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly found that making the records public could reasonably be expected to disrupt ongoing or anticipated enforcement proceedings. The public Hardy declaration detailed that revealing information about an active investigation could reasonably be expected to interfere with that investigation by alerting targets to its extent—a rationale frequently accepted in cases applying Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration additionally asserted that the pertinent investigation was "ongoing." J.A. 49. Further details were set forth in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. Likewise, the Bender declaration confirmed that the investigation was still ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the elapsed time raises concerns about whether enforcement actions were still ongoing or could reasonably be anticipated. He cites our observation in CREW that simply stating, in general terms, that investigations are ongoing may be insufficient to justify invoking Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further asserts that the Bender declaration’s statement that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was also overly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, when viewed on its own, the Bender declaration appears notably brief. However, context indicates it should not be reviewed in isolation. As previously discussed, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement actions in the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which affirmed their existence, as well as in the ex parte Hardy declaration, which described the investigation's nature and breadth in substantial detail. The Bender declaration was not intended to newly establish the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings, but rather to confirm that circumstances remained unchanged while the government's original summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Furthermore, when the district court denied the initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden of showing pending enforcement actions. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is properly viewed as an update to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent submission.

The expansive scope of the FBI’s underlying inquiry lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the information contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can point to the FBI’s public statements in Farahi’s immigration case: the Bureau alleged that Farahi acted as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, Farahi was described as being “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. The FBI further stated that Farahi was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. He also was alleged to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” naming specifically the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into consideration both the FBI’s open and sealed submissions, we find that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) to demonstrate the ongoing existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, claiming that the government failed to sufficiently explain the rationale behind its selection of categories for the documents underpinning its withholding determinations. When an agency groups withheld documents into categories, those categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each, as set out in CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reasons why the FBI’s identified categories were excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his arguments on this point. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include materials that are protected, the agency is still obligated to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that, according to Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, the FBI had presented a “detailed justification” for the position that the protected documents were not reasonably segregable. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this showing was sufficient for the agency to meet its burden on segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of records responsive to the request, there must be at least some information that can be separated and disclosed. However, if only a minimal amount of non-exempt material appears “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” it is unlikely to be “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, while Farahi is correct to observe that some individual terms or expressions within the records could be disclosed without compromising the FBI’s interests, that observation does not resolve the issue. The district court properly found that no information of substance could be reasonably segregated.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no requirement for the court to disclose its assessment of that declaration on the public docket. Additionally, because Farahi did not object to the district court’s receipt of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s reliance on such submissions. Moreover, even though the use of ex parte declarations inherently places FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this is a feature “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
