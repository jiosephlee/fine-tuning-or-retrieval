Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI responded by withholding the majority of the documents, leading Farahi to initiate litigation. The court instructed the FBI to refresh any outdated declarations previously provided. Upon review, the court determined that disclosing the requested documents might disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Accordingly, the court found that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request, unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Under Exemption 7(A), records that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” are protected if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedents establish that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies asserting a FOIA exemption are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds of a visa overstay. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In contesting these forms of relief, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and the in-person testimony of an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi submitted a request under FOIA for access to his FBI records. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially responsive materials. When the FBI failed to meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi initiated litigation. Over the following years, the FBI reviewed the potentially responsive documents, released a portion to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Upon concluding its review, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, supported by a declaration from David Hardy, who serves as Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other assertions, maintained that the withheld documents were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. The FBI further sought to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and on an ex parte basis. Farahi did not object to this request, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question were assembled for law enforcement purposes and found that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the lengthy passage of time, the court directed the FBI to reassess whether enforcement actions were still active or could reasonably be anticipated. Additionally, the court determined that further information was necessary to resolve the issue of segregability, though the reasons for this could not be stated in a public forum. As a result, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI filed updated declarations and renewed its summary judgment motion. This time, the FBI provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was then serving as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In the public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had communicated with the agent overseeing Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. The ex parte declaration from Bender included additional specifics. Bender also supplied more information to address the district court’s segregability concerns.

The district court ultimately granted the renewed summary judgment motion. The court accepted the FBI’s representation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. Further, after reviewing the ex parte materials, the court concluded that the FBI had sufficiently justified that the exempt material could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under FOIA, as with other matters, we conduct a de novo review of a district court’s decision granting summary judgment. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The burden rests on the government to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must establish that the records at issue were assembled for law enforcement purposes, and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given the FBI’s expertise in law enforcement matters, its invocation of exemption 7 is to be accorded deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such deference is especially warranted when the documents relate to national security interests. This Court has repeatedly affirmed the principle of executive deference in FOIA cases where national security issues are at stake, including in matters involving Exemption 7(A) for records concerning counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Records may be withheld in full by the government when non-exempt material is so intertwined with exempt information that segregation is not feasible. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are permitted to rely on government affidavits that explain with adequate specificity why documents subject to a valid exemption cannot be further separated into disclosable and exempt portions. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to materials “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI is recognized as a law enforcement body, its judgment on this matter is owed significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the agency’s declarations must “demonstrate a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as a “link between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its obligation in this instance. The public Hardy declaration indicated that the withheld documents were collected “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration offered significant details regarding the character of that investigation. Additional context further supports this statement. In proceedings before the district court, Farahi himself admitted that the FBI had investigated him for connections to one or more groups classified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as links to an individual subsequently held as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court of terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are firmly convinced that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s file was assembled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the withheld documents “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this standard is satisfied when the proceedings in question are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this exemption, active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings.” Id. at 1097–98. The government can establish the existence of such proceedings by merely attesting to their “existence.” Id. at 1099. “As long as the investigation continues in order to gather evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and the premature disclosure of that evidence would threaten the case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the records could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated. In his public declaration, Hardy stated that releasing information about an ongoing investigation could reasonably be expected to interfere with it by alerting subjects to the breadth of the investigation—an accepted rationale in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Hardy also declared that the relevant investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. In a separate ex parte declaration, Hardy provided further detail regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration likewise affirmed that the investigation continued as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time raises questions as to whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be expected. He relies on our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” to claims that investigations continue may be insufficient to justify invoking Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi also argues that the Bender declaration’s statement that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was similarly lacking in specificity. We acknowledge that, when viewed on its own, the Bender declaration appears notably brief. However, the surrounding circumstances suggest it should not be read in isolation. As previously discussed, the government had already demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were ongoing through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and through the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant details about the investigation’s character and breadth. The Bender declaration was submitted not to independently establish the existence of pending enforcement proceedings, but rather to verify that the situation had not changed while the government’s first summary-judgment motion was pending. Additionally, when it denied the government’s original summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden of showing that enforcement proceedings were pending. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is best understood as a status update to the Hardy declarations rather than an independent submission.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s underlying inquiry also supports the plausibility of its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can reference the FBI’s public statements made during Farahi’s immigration case: The FBI claimed that Farahi served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI further alleged he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, Farahi was said to be “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. He was also described as having supported “charities that financed terrorism,” among them the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI’s public and confidential submissions, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) to establish the ongoing nature of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently justify the manner in which it defined the document categories underlying its withholding determinations. When an agency opts to withhold records by grouping them into categories, it is required to employ categories that are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" to each category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason as to why the different categories the FBI used were excessively broad. Therefore, Farahi has forfeited his arguments regarding this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request covers records that are protected, the agency is still required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (mandating that an agency must provide “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission to explain why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission met the agency’s burden in this respect. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large number of responsive records, there should exist some segregable and releasable information. However, if a small amount of non-exempt content is merely “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content is unlikely to be considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, although Farahi is correct that individual words or phrases could theoretically be disclosed without prejudicing the FBI’s interests, this fact does not affect the analysis. The district court properly determined that no meaningful information could be reasonably segregated.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no obligation for the court to make its assessment of that declaration part of the public record. First, Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations and therefore cannot now contest the court’s use of them. Second, although ex parte declarations may place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this difficulty “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
