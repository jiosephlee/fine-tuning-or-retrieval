Circuit Judge KATSAS authored the Court's opinion. Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI records. The FBI declined to release the majority of these documents, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The court directed the FBI to provide updated versions of its earlier, potentially outdated, declarations. Ultimately, the court determined that making the requested materials public might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Consequently, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded these records from disclosure. We concur with this judgment.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to make records accessible to the public upon request, provided no specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). According to Exemption 7(A), materials “compiled for law enforcement purposes” are protected from disclosure if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that for Exemption 7(A) to apply, the relevant enforcement proceedings must either be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption are also required to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds of exceeding his authorized stay. Throughout the course of these proceedings, Farahi submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, as well as a request for voluntary departure. In opposition to his requests, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and in-person testimony from an FBI agent, who attested to Farahi’s purported associations with prominent terrorists. Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi initiated a FOIA request to obtain his FBI records. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially relevant to his request. When the FBI failed to meet the legal deadline for a response, Farahi filed a lawsuit. In the subsequent years, the agency reviewed the material, releasing certain records to Farahi but withholding the majority. After finishing its review, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019. To support this motion, the agency presented a declaration from David Hardy, who leads the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The declaration claimed, among other points, that the withheld documents had been gathered for law enforcement purposes and that disclosing them could reasonably be anticipated to hinder ongoing or expected enforcement actions. The FBI then requested permission to submit an additional Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte; Farahi did not object to this request, which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the documents in question had been assembled for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records might impede ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, in light of the considerable lapse of time, the court required the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding the status of any current or anticipated enforcement proceedings. Additionally, the court found it necessary to obtain further details concerning segregability, but was unable to articulate its reasoning publicly. Consequently, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI resubmitted updated declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency offered both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, who served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS at the time. Within his public statement, Bender noted that RIDS had reached out to the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, who affirmed that the investigations were still in progress. Bender’s ex parte submission elaborated further. Additionally, he addressed the district court’s prior inquiries about the issue of segregability with expanded information.

The district court subsequently granted the agency’s renewed motion. It accepted the FBI’s assertion “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. After reviewing the ex parte documentation, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt material could not reasonably be separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as in other types of litigation, appellate review of summary judgment is conducted de novo. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The government carries the responsibility of establishing the applicability of a FOIA exemption. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must demonstrate both that the withheld materials were compiled for purposes related to law enforcement and that release of such materials could reasonably be anticipated to impede pending or likely enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given its primary function in law enforcement, the FBI’s invocation of exemption 7 warrants judicial deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when the records at issue concern national security. The Court has repeatedly emphasized deference to the executive branch on FOIA issues involving national security, and this approach applies notably in Exemption 7(A) cases regarding records of counterterrorism efforts. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

It is permissible for the government to withhold documents in full where the nonexempt content is so closely linked with exempt information that segregation is impossible. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may rely on detailed government affidavits that explain, with sufficient specificity, why documents withheld under a valid exemption cannot be reasonably segregated. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” as specified in U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI operates as a law enforcement body, its judgment on this criterion typically receives considerable deference from the courts. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s affidavits or declarations must demonstrate a logical “‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” and further illustrate a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's conclusion that the FBI satisfied its evidentiary burden in this case. The publicly available Hardy declaration detailed that the records withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Additionally, the in camera Hardy declaration provided substantial specifics regarding the investigation's scope. The context surrounding the case further supports this characterization. Notably, before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him based on his connections to one or more organizations classified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as to an individual subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court on terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Accordingly, we are convinced that the FBI has demonstrated that the records concerning Farahi were gathered for legitimate law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) stipulates that disclosure of the requested materials must create a reasonable expectation of hindering enforcement proceedings. U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This court has interpreted the clause to mean that such proceedings must be either ongoing or legitimately foreseen, as articulated in CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings” under this standard, as explained in id. at 1097–98, and the government may establish the existence of such proceedings simply by providing an attestation, id. at 1099. The exemption remains applicable “as long as the investigation is actively collecting evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and the premature disclosure of this evidence would threaten that case,” per Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court appropriately determined that releasing the information would likely disrupt current or foreseeable enforcement proceedings. According to the public Hardy declaration, making details about an ongoing investigation public could alert those under investigation to its breadth, which is a commonly recognized rationale for applying Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration specified that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided further information about the progress of the investigation. The Bender declaration also verified that the investigation was still active as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that as more time passes, the likelihood diminishes that enforcement proceedings remain ongoing or could be reasonably anticipated. He references the court’s observation in CREW regarding the insufficiency of a “vague” assertion that investigations are still active to justify reliance on Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further argues that the Bender declaration, by simply claiming that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was similarly lacking in specificity and therefore merely conclusory. We concur with Farahi to the extent that, viewed alone, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, the broader context makes clear that considering the Bender declaration in isolation would be inappropriate. As previously discussed, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided extensive information concerning the investigation’s character and breadth. The Bender declaration’s purpose was not to independently establish the continued pendency of enforcement proceedings; rather, it served to verify that circumstances had not changed while the original summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, the district court, in denying the government’s initial motion for summary judgment without prejudice, had already indicated that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to show ongoing enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as a supplement to the Hardy declarations rather than as an independent evidentiary submission.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s investigation lends credibility to its assertion that the inquiry is still active. While we are unable to reveal the details contained within Hardy’s ex parte declaration, it is relevant to highlight the FBI’s public statements during Farahi’s immigration case: the Bureau described Farahi as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Furthermore, the FBI asserted that Farahi maintained connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and various other identified Al Qaeda operatives. Id. Farahi was also reported to be associated with “multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, he was said to have provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” among them the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. In light of both public and confidential FBI declarations, we determine that the Bureau has satisfied its obligations under Exemption 7(A), demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is still underway. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, contending that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale for delineating the document categories that formed the basis for its withholding decisions. Under relevant precedent, when an agency opts to withhold documents by grouping them into categories, those categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each, as held in CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer a concrete argument as to why the FBI’s identified categories are excessively broad. As a result, he has forfeited any claims on this issue. See Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include materials that are exempt from disclosure, the agency is nonetheless obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” within Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission explaining why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding this issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the substantial number of responsive documents, there should exist some information that can be separated and released. However, when any non-exempt content appears sporadically—embedded line-by-line within the records—it generally does not meet the standard of being “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As clarified in Mead, the central concern of FOIA is the conveyance of substantive information, not merely the disclosure of isolated words that happen to be present in government documents. Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Although Farahi is correct in noting that it might be possible to release certain words or short phrases from the records without compromising the FBI’s interests, this possibility is irrelevant in this context. The district court properly determined that there was no significant information that could be reasonably segregated and released.

Lastly, Farahi argues that the district court gave undue weight to Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability assessment. However, there was no obligation for the court to publicly disclose its evaluation of that declaration. To begin with, Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, thereby forfeiting any basis to contest the court’s dependence on them. Additionally, although the use of ex parte declarations inevitably places FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this challenge “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
