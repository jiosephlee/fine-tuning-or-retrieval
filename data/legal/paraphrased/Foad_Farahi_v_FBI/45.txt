Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI chose to withhold the majority of these documents, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The district court instructed the FBI to refresh any outdated declarations previously provided. Ultimately, the court determined that disclosing the materials Farahi requested might impede ongoing or foreseeably likely enforcement actions. As a result, the court ruled that the records are exempt from disclosure pursuant to FOIA’s Exemption 7(A). We concur with this determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request, except when a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Pursuant to Exemption 7(A), records that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes” are protected from disclosure if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that such enforcement proceedings must either be ongoing or reasonably foreseen. Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the basis of a visa overstay. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has sought several forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition to these requests, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and in-court testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI records through a FOIA application. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially relevant. When the agency failed to respond within the time mandated by statute, Farahi initiated legal action. Over the following years, the FBI evaluated the possibly responsive materials, released some documents to Farahi, and withheld the majority. After finishing its review, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, offering a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), in support. The Hardy declaration, among other points, maintained that the records withheld were gathered for law enforcement purposes and that disclosing them could be expected to impede law enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or reasonably foreseen. The FBI also moved to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not object, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the summary judgment motion. The court determined that the records in question had been assembled for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, because a considerable amount of time had elapsed, the court instructed the FBI to revisit its evaluation regarding whether such enforcement proceedings remained ongoing or reasonably likely. The court further found that additional details were needed in order to resolve the issue of segregability, but it could not articulate its reasons on the public record. As a result, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, at that time serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, and that this agent had affirmed that the investigations were still in progress. Bender’s ex parte submission contained further specifics. Additionally, Bender offered additional information responding to the district court’s questions concerning segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court relied on the FBI's assurance "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why exempt information could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with other types of cases, we evaluate summary judgment decisions anew, without deference to the prior ruling. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The obligation falls on the government to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must establish that the documents withheld were gathered for purposes related to law enforcement, and that releasing them would likely impede ongoing or reasonably projected enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such judicial deference is even more warranted with materials involving national security interests. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—a principle that applies in Exemption 7(A) cases involving requests for records from counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the parts that are not exempt “are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may base their decisions on government affidavits that detail with reasonable specificity the reasons why materials withheld under a valid exemption cannot be separated further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” as specified in U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given the FBI’s status as a law enforcement entity, its assessment regarding this issue is generally accorded substantial deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s statements must demonstrate a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” and must also show a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary burden in this instance. The public declaration submitted by Hardy stated that the records withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Further, the ex parte Hardy declaration offered substantial information regarding the character of the investigation in question. The broader circumstances further substantiate this point. Notably, during proceedings before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that he was the subject of an FBI investigation due to connections with organizations identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as with a person later detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court of terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Consequently, we are fully persuaded that the FBI has satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that the materials regarding Farahi were compiled for law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further stipulates that disclosure of the documents in question “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has determined that this requirement means the relevant proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this standard, active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” as recognized in id. at 1097–98, and the government can establish the presence of such proceedings by simply attesting to their “existence,” id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.”

The district court properly determined that disclosure was reasonably likely to disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. In the public Hardy declaration, it was explained that releasing information about an active investigation could alert the subjects to the investigation’s scope, which is a commonly accepted rationale for applying Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration also confirmed that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Further details regarding the investigation were provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. Additionally, the Bender declaration verified that as of March 2023, the investigation continued to be ongoing.

Farahi contends that the mere lapse of time undermines the assertion that enforcement actions were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He relies on our observation in CREW, which recognized that “the vague nature” of claims that investigations continue may not, on its own, justify use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further argues that the Bender declaration, which stated that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was similarly conclusory in nature. We concur with Farahi that, viewed alone, the Bender declaration is notably brief. Nonetheless, broader circumstances caution against reviewing the Bender declaration in isolation. As previously discussed, the government demonstrated the existence of enforcement proceedings via the public Hardy declaration, which attested to them, and through the ex parte Hardy declaration, which described the investigation’s nature and breadth in significant detail. The government’s purpose in filing the Bender declaration was not to re-establish the presence of enforcement proceedings from the ground up, but rather to verify that circumstances remained unchanged during the pendency of its original summary judgment motion. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s earlier summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate the existence of pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is most properly viewed as an update supplementing the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent submission.

The scope of the FBI’s initial investigation lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we cannot reveal the information contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we do refer to the FBI’s public statements during Farahi’s immigration case: the Bureau described Farahi as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. He was allegedly “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. The FBI also asserted that he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, Farahi was said to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” specifically naming the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Relying on both the FBI’s public allegations and its sealed statements, we determine that the Bureau has satisfied its obligations under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that an ongoing “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” persists. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, contending that the government failed to sufficiently justify the manner in which it delineated document categories to support its withholding determinations. Agencies that withhold materials according to such categories must employ groupings that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each one. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason why the FBI’s designated categories are purportedly overly broad. Therefore, Farahi has relinquished his arguments regarding this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request covers documents that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring the release of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” through Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission to explain why the protected materials could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding this issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the substantial quantity of responsive documents, there should be some information that is both segregable and subject to release. However, when minimal, non-exempt content appears sporadically “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content is generally not considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is consistent with the principle that “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even if, as Farahi suggests, individual terms or short phrases within the responsive documents could be disclosed without endangering the FBI’s interests, that observation is inconsequential. The district court rightly determined that no significant information could reasonably be segregated for disclosure.

Farahi’s final contention is that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no obligation for the court to detail its examination of that declaration on the public record. Moreover, Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, and thus cannot now fault the court for depending on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations may put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
