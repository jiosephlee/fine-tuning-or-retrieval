Case Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket Number: 23-5243  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of the documents contained in the file, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court directed the FBI to revise any outdated declarations previously provided. Upon review, the court determined that disclosing the requested documents might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Consequently, the court found the records to be exempt from disclosure under FOIA Exemption 7(A). We concur with this determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request, except where a specific statutory exemption applies. U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to records that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” so long as their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies are required, when invoking a FOIA exemption, to make “reasonable steps” to separate and disclose information that is not exempt. U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of these proceedings, Farahi submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposing his requests for relief, the DHS introduced both documentary evidence and in-person testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI records. In response, the FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant information. After the FBI did not meet the FOIA response deadline, Farahi initiated legal proceedings. Subsequently, over a period of several years, the FBI conducted a review of the identified materials, providing certain records to Farahi while withholding the majority. After concluding its review, the FBI filed for summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, who is the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other assertions, stated that the documents being withheld were assembled for purposes related to law enforcement, and their disclosure could reasonably be expected to impede ongoing or anticipated law enforcement actions. The FBI also moved to submit an additional Hardy declaration under seal and on an ex parte basis. Farahi did not object to this request, which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the documents in question had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to demonstrate that disclosure could hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, because considerable time had passed, the court instructed the FBI to update its evaluation as to whether enforcement proceedings remained ongoing or were still reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court determined that further details were necessary to resolve the issue of segregability, for reasons that could not be disclosed on the public docket. Consequently, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved for summary judgment. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, the then-Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender stated that RIDS had consulted with the agent managing Farahi’s file, who affirmed that investigations were still active. Bender’s ex parte submission contained additional specifics. He further supplemented his statements with more information relating to the district court’s inquiries about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s statement “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified that non-exempt material could not be reasonably separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In Freedom of Information Act cases, as with other types of cases, our review of a district court’s summary judgment ruling is de novo. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The burden is on the government to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this requirement, the FBI must establish that the withheld materials were assembled for law enforcement objectives and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with pending or foreseeable enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Because the FBI’s principal function is law enforcement, its assertion of Exemption 7 is generally afforded deference. See Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This judicial deference is especially justified when the records pertain to matters of national security. Our Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including in cases brought under Exemption 7(A) involving counterterrorism investigation records. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the sections that are not exempt “are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are authorized to rely upon affidavits from the government which explain with sufficient specificity why any material subject to a proper exemption cannot be segregated and released. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records the FBI withheld are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to materials that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As a law-enforcement body, the FBI's assessment regarding this requirement warrants a significant degree of deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI must submit declarations that demonstrate a reasonable “‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as a showing of a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its burden in this matter. The public Hardy declaration described the withheld documents as having been assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration elaborated further with significant specifics about the character of the investigation. Additional context further supports this assertion. Before the district court, Farahi himself admitted that he had been investigated by the FBI for connections to one or more groups identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as for links to an individual later held as an enemy combatant and subsequently convicted in federal court for terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully satisfied that the FBI established that Farahi’s file was created for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosing the materials in question “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our court has determined that this requirement is satisfied where such proceedings are either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations are treated as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government may substantiate the existence of such proceedings through attestation alone, id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.”

The district court properly determined that revealing the information could reasonably be anticipated to hinder ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. The publicly filed Hardy declaration stated that releasing details about an active investigation could, by alerting subjects to the breadth of the inquiry, be reasonably expected to disrupt it—an established rationale in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration further asserted that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s in camera declaration provided additional specifics about the inquiry, and the Bender declaration verified that the investigation was still ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi maintains that the lapse of time undermines the likelihood that enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be expected. He relies on our observation in CREW that an agency's “vague” claim that investigations are continuing may not be adequate to justify application of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He also argues that the Bender declaration, which stated the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in specificity. We concur with Farahi that the Bender declaration, viewed independently, is notably brief. However, considering it apart from its surrounding context would be inappropriate. As discussed above, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were pending via the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which described the investigation’s nature and scope in significant detail. The Bender declaration was not intended to independently establish the ongoing nature of enforcement proceedings, but rather to verify that circumstances remained unchanged during the period when the government's initial summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, the district court, when it denied the government’s earlier motion for summary judgment without prejudice, had already indicated that the FBI “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to show enforcement proceedings were pending. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is most reasonably interpreted as a supplemental update to the Hardy declarations, rather than as a freestanding submission.

The wide scope of the FBI’s initial investigation provides support for its claim that its inquiry is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the contents of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can reference what the FBI has stated publicly about Farahi in his immigration proceedings: The FBI alleged that he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. It was also asserted that he had “connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. The FBI further alleged Farahi was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. He was also said to back “charities that financed terrorism,” among them the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Upon reviewing the FBI’s public and sealed statements, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its burden under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the ongoing nature of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, contending that the government failed to sufficiently justify its selection of the document categories it used to describe its withholding rationale. When an agency relies on categorical withholding, it is required to utilize groupings that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each grouping. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any particular reasons why the FBI’s chosen categories were purportedly too broad. Consequently, Farahi has forfeited any arguments pertaining to this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, the agency is still required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (stating that the agency must make available “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission as to why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission met the agency’s obligation on the issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive records, some information must exist that can be separated and released. However, when minimal non-exempt content is scattered randomly “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” it is unlikely that this material constitutes “reasonably segregable” information. Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This follows because “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, although Farahi is right that isolated words or short phrases within the records could technically be revealed without compromising the FBI’s interests, this is not the relevant inquiry. The district court correctly found that there was no meaningful information that was reasonably capable of being segregated.

Lastly, Farahi contends that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration when conducting its segregability analysis. However, the court was under no obligation to include its evaluation of that declaration on the public docket. First, Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, thus he is barred from contesting the court’s dependence on them. Second, even though the use of ex parte declarations undeniably places FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
