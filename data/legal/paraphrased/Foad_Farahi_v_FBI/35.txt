Case Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Case No.: 23-5243  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld a substantial portion of those documents, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court instructed the FBI to revise any potentially outdated declarations it had provided. Subsequently, the court determined that disclosing the requested documents might impede enforcement actions that were ongoing or could be reasonably expected. Accordingly, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) shields from disclosure records that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” provided that releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This court has determined that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information,” pursuant to U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). Additionally, id. § 552(b) mandates that “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on his overstay of a visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and, alternatively, voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security, in opposition to his requested relief, submitted documentary evidence and introduced the in-person testimony of an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a request under FOIA for access to his FBI file. The agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially containing responsive material. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for response, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the following years, the FBI examined these records, released certain materials to Farahi, but withheld the majority. Once the review was finished, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019. The agency relied on a declaration by David Hardy, Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), which, among other statements, maintained that the records being withheld had been compiled for law enforcement purposes, and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to hinder ongoing or anticipated enforcement proceedings. The FBI also sought to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration to the court under seal and ex parte. Farahi did not object to this request, which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the records in question were gathered for law enforcement objectives and that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure of the records could disrupt ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, owing to the considerable amount of time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation as to whether such proceedings remained ongoing or could still be reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court determined that it required further details regarding segregability, though it could not elaborate on the reasons for this on the public record. Accordingly, the court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency presented both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, at that time serving as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Within his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had communicated with the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, who verified that investigative activities were still in progress. Bender’s ex parte declaration set forth additional information. He also supplemented the record with further detail responsive to the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. It relied on the FBI's affirmation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why exempt information could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under the FOIA, as with other matters, we undertake a de novo review of summary judgment orders. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI in this case must establish both that the records it withheld were collected for law enforcement purposes and that their release could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with either ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Given the FBI’s role as a law enforcement agency, courts afford some deference to its decision to claim exemption 7.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such deference is especially justified when records involve national security considerations. This Court has “repeatedly emphasized deference to the executive branch when considering FOIA matters involving national security concerns”—including, in particular, Exemption 7(A) claims related to counterterrorism investigation records. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Records may be withheld by the government in full if the nonexempt sections are so closely connected with exempt information that they cannot reasonably be separated. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). A court is permitted to accept government affidavits that explain with adequate specificity why materials withheld under a proper exemption cannot be segregated further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered under Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) covers only those documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI operates as a law enforcement body, its assessment on this issue generally warrants significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency's statements must “demonstrate a logical ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” in addition to showing a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary burden in this instance. The Hardy declaration submitted for the public record described the withheld materials as having been assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The Hardy declaration submitted ex parte offered substantial detail regarding the character of that investigation. Additional context supports this claim. In the proceedings below, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI conducted an investigation into his connections to one or more organizations classified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as his links to an individual who was later apprehended as an enemy combatant and subsequently convicted in federal court on charges tied to terrorism. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Accordingly, we are fully persuaded that the FBI has established that Farahi’s records were gathered for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that the disclosure of the records being withheld “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has interpreted this language to mean that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this provision, ongoing criminal investigations are included within the definition of “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government may establish the existence of such proceedings merely by asserting that they exist, id. at 1099. Exemption 7(A) is applicable “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be expected to disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. According to the publicly filed Hardy declaration, making details of an active investigation public could alert the subjects to the investigation’s scope, which is a commonly accepted basis for invoking Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration also affirmed that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Further details regarding the investigation were provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, while the Bender declaration corroborated that the investigation was ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time undermines whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or reasonably expected. He cites our statement in CREW that a “vague nature” in claiming ongoing investigations may not be sufficient to invoke Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further argues that the Bender declaration, in simply asserting that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was similarly lacking in detail. While we concur with Farahi that the Bender declaration, if viewed on its own, appears notably brief, the surrounding circumstances suggest it should not be assessed in isolation. As discussed earlier, the government had already demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were ongoing through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which elaborated significantly on the nature and scope of the investigation. The government provided the Bender declaration not to establish anew that enforcement proceedings were pending, but instead to verify that the situation remained unchanged while its initial summary judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, the district court, when denying the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, had previously found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to show ongoing enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, rather than as a self-contained document.

The expansive nature of the FBI’s initial investigation lends credibility to its assertion that the inquiry is still active. Although the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte declaration cannot be revealed, we reference the FBI’s statements made during Farahi’s immigration case: They described him as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. According to the FBI, he had “connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. The Bureau also asserted that he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” including Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, they alleged his support for “charities that financed terrorism,” namely the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI’s public statements and sealed submissions, we find that the Bureau has satisfied the requirements of Exemption 7(A) by establishing the continued existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, contending that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale for how it designated the categories of documents relied upon in justifying its withholdings. In situations where an agency asserts withholdings based on categorical groupings, it is required to select categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each grouping. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not specifically articulate any reasons why the particular categories identified by the FBI are excessively broad. Therefore, Farahi has forfeited these arguments. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring the agency to provide “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had furnished a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission regarding the lack of reasonably segregable material in the protected records. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission fulfilled the agency’s burden in this regard. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large number of responsive documents, there should be at least some information that can be separated and released. However, if only a minimal amount of non-exempt content is “randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” then such information generally does not meet the standard for being “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As explained in Mead, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, although Farahi is technically correct that some isolated words or fragments within the documents could potentially be released without compromising the FBI’s interests, that does not advance his claim. The district court properly determined that there was no reasonably segregable, meaningful information in the records.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability assessment. However, the court was under no obligation to disclose its analysis of that declaration on the public record. Moreover, because Farahi did not object to the court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is not now in a position to dispute the court’s use of them. Additionally, while ex parte declarations undeniably place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
