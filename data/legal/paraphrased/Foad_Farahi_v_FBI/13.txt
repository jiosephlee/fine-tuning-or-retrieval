Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his file maintained by the FBI. The FBI declined to release the majority of these documents, which prompted Farahi to initiate litigation. The court, after directing the FBI to refresh any outdated declarations it had previously provided, determined that disclosing the requested materials had the potential to hinder law enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or could be reasonably foreseen. Consequently, the court ruled that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from release. We concur with this judgment.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public when requested, except where a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to materials “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedents establish that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

In cases where an agency withholds information pursuant to a FOIA exemption, it is required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him based on allegations of overstaying his visa. Throughout his immigration process, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protections under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition to his requests, DHS introduced documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who "documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists." See Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi later filed a FOIA request to obtain his FBI file. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain responsive material. When the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for responding, Farahi initiated litigation. Over the following years, the FBI conducted a review of potentially responsive records, releasing some to Farahi while withholding the majority. After concluding its review, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other assertions, stated that the documents withheld were assembled for purposes of law enforcement and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. The FBI also requested to submit an additional Hardy declaration both under seal and ex parte; Farahi did not object, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. It determined that the records were created for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure could disrupt existing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the elapsed time, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation of whether such proceedings were still pending or reasonably likely. The court also found it necessary to obtain additional details to resolve the issue of segregability, though it stated this could not be addressed publicly. Consequently, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI filed updated declarations and renewed its motion. On this occasion, the FBI submitted both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, then Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In the public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s case file, who affirmed that investigations were ongoing. The ex parte declaration from Bender included further particulars. Bender also offered additional information to address the district court's segregability concerns.

The district court then granted the renewed summary judgment motion. It accepted the FBI's assertion "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. Reviewing the ex parte materials, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt content could not reasonably be separated from the releasable information. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

As with other matters, our review of summary judgment orders in FOIA cases is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The government is tasked with the responsibility of establishing that a claimed FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this requirement in the present case, the FBI must demonstrate both that the records at issue were assembled for law enforcement objectives and that their release would reasonably be expected to disrupt pending or reasonably contemplated enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Since the FBI’s focus is on law enforcement activities, its reliance on exemption 7 is generally afforded deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when the records involve matters of national security. The Court has regularly emphasized deferring to executive determinations under FOIA when national security is concerned, and this applies to Exemption 7(A) cases that deal with counterterrorism investigation records specifically. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Entire records may be withheld by the government “when nonexempt information ‘is inextricably intertwined with exempt material.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are permitted to accept government declarations that provide adequate detail as to why additional segregation of documents withheld under a valid exemption is not feasible. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI functions as a law enforcement agency, its conclusion regarding this requirement warrants a significant degree of deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s declarations must “demonstrate a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” in addition to showing a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its burden in this instance. The publicly filed Hardy declaration specified that the withheld materials were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The in camera Hardy declaration offered substantial further information regarding the investigation’s nature. Additional contextual information further substantiates this position. In proceedings below, Farahi admitted that the FBI had investigated him due to alleged connections with organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department and with an individual who was subsequently classified as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court of terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are satisfied that the FBI met its burden in demonstrating that Farahi’s records were compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that the release of the requested records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has determined that this standard requires that enforcement proceedings are either "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, continuing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings," id. at 1097–98, and the government may satisfy its burden by merely affirming the "existence" of such proceedings, id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that release of the information could reasonably be expected to disrupt enforcement proceedings that are pending or may reasonably be anticipated. In his public declaration, Hardy stated that making information public regarding an active investigation might reasonably result in interference by alerting subjects about the breadth of the investigation, which is a commonly accepted rationale under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration also confirmed that the investigation in question remained "ongoing." J.A. 49. Further details on the investigation were provided in Hardy's ex parte declaration. Additionally, the Bender declaration corroborated that as of March 2023, the investigation was still ongoing.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time raises questions about whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be anticipated. He relies on language from our decision in CREW, in which we noted that a “vague nature” in claims that investigations are ongoing may not be enough to justify Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further claims that the Bender declaration, by stating that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was similarly lacking in specificity. We concur with Farahi that, when viewed in isolation, the Bender declaration seems notably succinct. However, the circumstances advise against examining the Bender declaration separately from the broader record. As noted previously, the government demonstrated the presence of pending enforcement proceedings in the public Hardy declaration, which stated they existed, and in the ex parte Hardy declaration, which offered significant information concerning the investigation’s character and scope. The purpose of the Bender declaration was not to newly establish the existence of enforcement proceedings, but rather to verify that the status had not changed during the pendency of the government's initial motion for summary judgment. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s first summary-judgment motion without prejudice, it had already observed that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to show pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best read as supplementing and updating the Hardy declarations, rather than operating as an independent submission.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s original investigation lends credibility to its assertion that its inquiry is still active. While we are unable to reveal the specifics contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we observe the information that the FBI has placed on the record during Farahi’s immigration case: Farahi was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood,” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2; that he had “connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members,” id.; that he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among whom is Jose Padilla, id.; and that he backed “charities that financed terrorism,” naming the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation, id. Having considered both the public and sealed declarations provided by the FBI, we determine that the Bureau has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is ongoing. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a cursory objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently justify its chosen categories of documents employed to rationalize its withholding actions. Agencies that withhold records based on categories are required to select classifications that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each respective category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). Nevertheless, Farahi does not articulate any particular reasons why the categories designated by the FBI were unduly broad. As a result, Farahi has relinquished these arguments. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency is still required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI, through Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, offered a “detailed justification” for the absence of reasonably segregable nonexempt content within the protected records. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission fulfilled the agency’s obligation regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the extensive amount of responsive material, there must be some information that is both segregable and releasable. Nevertheless, when any non-exempt information appears only “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” it ordinarily will not be “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, although Farahi is right in noting that select words or snippets within the records may potentially be disclosed without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests, this argument misses the mark. The district court properly determined that no meaningful nonexempt content was reasonably segregable from the protected records.

Farahi also contends that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its analysis of segregability. However, the court was not obligated to detail its review of that declaration on the public record. First, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he cannot now contest the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations unquestionably disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, such difficulties “are inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
