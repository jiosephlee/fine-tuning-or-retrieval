Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of documents contained within the file, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court directed the FBI to refresh any possibly outdated declarations previously provided. Upon review, the court determined that disclosing the sought-after records could disrupt enforcement actions that were either ongoing or reasonably expected. Consequently, the court found that these documents were shielded from disclosure pursuant to FOIA Exemption 7(A). We concur with this determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request unless a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” where disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that such enforcement proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are obligated to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on his having overstayed his visa. Throughout different phases of his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition to his requests for relief, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI file through a FOIA submission. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant records. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi initiated litigation. In the years that followed, the FBI conducted a review of the materials, released a portion to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once the review concluded, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, submitting in support a declaration by David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other things, claimed that the documents withheld were compiled for purposes of law enforcement and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or could reasonably be foreseen. The FBI also moved to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration both under seal and ex parte; Farahi did not object to this, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the documents at issue were assembled for law enforcement objectives and determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records might obstruct ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable amount of time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation as to whether enforcement actions were still pending or reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court ruled that it required additional details regarding segregability, though it indicated it could not explain its reasoning on the public docket. Consequently, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved the court for relief. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations by Joseph Bender, who at that time served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender detailed that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s case file, who verified that the investigations were still in progress. Further elaboration appeared in Bender’s ex parte declaration. Additionally, Bender supplied further details intended to address the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion, relying on the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Upon examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why exempt information could not be reasonably separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with all other cases, we undertake a de novo review of summary judgment orders. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The obligation lies with the government to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is justified. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation in the present case, the FBI is required to establish that the withheld documents were created for law enforcement objectives and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to hinder ongoing or expected law enforcement activities. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Due to the FBI’s focus on law enforcement, its reliance on exemption 7 receives deference from the courts. See Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when records touch on matters of national security. Our Court has "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated," expressly including circumstances involving Exemption 7(A) and requests for documents associated with counterterrorism inquiries. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the sections that could be disclosed cannot reasonably be separated from the exempt sections. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Furthermore, courts are allowed to rely on affidavits submitted by the government that adequately explain with specific detail why materials withheld under a proper exemption cannot be segregated further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records the FBI withheld are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to materials "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI functions as a law enforcement agency, its assessment regarding this requirement is owed substantial deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency's affidavits must demonstrate a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” in addition to showing a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its burden in this instance. The public Hardy declaration stated that the withheld materials were created "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations." J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration further provided substantial specifics regarding the character of that investigation. Additional context supports this claim. Before the district court, Farahi himself admitted that the FBI has investigated him concerning connections to one or more organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department, as well as links to an individual who was later apprehended as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted on terrorism-related charges in federal court. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Accordingly, we are convinced that the FBI demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that the disclosure of the withheld materials "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This court has previously determined that the enforcement proceedings at issue must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations are classified as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government can establish the existence of such proceedings merely by providing a declaration attesting to their occurrence, id. at 1099. So long as an investigation is in progress to obtain evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and releasing that evidence prematurely would place the case at risk, Exemption 7(A) is applicable. Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the requested information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or expected enforcement actions. The public declaration by Hardy described how disclosing details about an active investigation might alert subjects to its extent, which is a commonly recognized basis under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration asserted that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Further details regarding the investigation were provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. The Bender declaration also stated that the investigation was ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time creates uncertainty about whether enforcement proceedings were still active or could reasonably be anticipated. He cites language from our opinion in CREW, noting that a “vague nature” in claims of ongoing investigations may not be adequate to justify Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He further argues that the Bender declaration’s statement that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was similarly lacking in detail. We acknowledge that, if viewed by itself, the Bender declaration is notably succinct. However, it would be inappropriate to examine the Bender declaration in a vacuum. As previously discussed, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were ongoing through the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided extensive information about the investigation’s nature and breadth. The Bender declaration was not meant to independently establish that enforcement proceedings were pending, but rather to verify that there had been no changes during the time its original summary judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s initial motion for summary judgment without prejudice, it had already concluded that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to show that enforcement proceedings were ongoing. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as a status update supplementing the Hardy declarations, rather than as a document intended to stand alone.

The expansive scope of the FBI’s underlying inquiry supports the plausibility of its assertion that its investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the information contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we acknowledge the FBI’s public statements in Farahi’s immigration case: Farahi was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. He was alleged to have “connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, he was said to be “associated with several individuals convicted or indicted for terrorism offenses,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, he was claimed to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI’s public and confidential submissions, we find that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A), demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is ongoing. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, contending that the government did not sufficiently explain the rationale behind the document categories it employed to support its withholding determinations. When an agency organizes withheld documents by category, those categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each, as stated in CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). Nevertheless, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason why the categories set forth by the FBI are overly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited any arguments on this issue. See Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request covers records that are protected, the agency is still obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had given a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission for why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further concluded that this submission fulfilled the agency’s responsibility on this issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large number of responsive documents, there must exist some information that could be separated and disclosed. However, if the non-exempt content consists merely of isolated fragments “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such material typically does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The emphasis under the FOIA is on “information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, although Farahi is correct in noting that there may be isolated words or short phrases in the documents that could be released without compromising the FBI’s interests, this fact is not dispositive. The district court properly determined that no significant information could be reasonably segregated for release.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration when assessing segregability. However, there was no obligation for the court to publicly disclose its analysis of that declaration. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from now contesting their use. Additionally, although ex parte declarations undoubtedly put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
