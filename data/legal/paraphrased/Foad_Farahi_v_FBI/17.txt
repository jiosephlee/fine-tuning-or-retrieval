Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI largely withheld the documents contained in the file, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The court directed the FBI to revise any declarations that might have become outdated. Upon review, the court determined that disclosure of the sought-after records could impede enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or could reasonably be expected. Accordingly, the court ruled that the documents were shielded from disclosure by Exemption 7(A) of FOIA. We concur with this conclusion.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public when requested, unless a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and exempts them from disclosure if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that such enforcement proceedings must either be ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold materials, agencies are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the basis of a visa overstay. Throughout the course of these proceedings, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, as well as voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security opposed the requested relief and introduced evidence, including documentary materials and the in-person testimony of an FBI agent who "documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists." Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi later submitted a request pursuant to FOIA seeking access to his FBI records. The FBI located approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing material potentially responsive to the request. Following the FBI's failure to respond within the statutory deadline, Farahi commenced litigation. Over the ensuing years, the agency reviewed these materials, providing certain records to Farahi while withholding the majority. When the review process concluded, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019. To support this motion, the FBI relied on a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). This declaration, among other points, maintained that the records being withheld were compiled for purposes of law enforcement and that their release could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with enforcement actions that were pending or reasonably foreseeable. The FBI also moved to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and for ex parte consideration. Farahi did not object, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the records had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and determined that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure could hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, because a considerable amount of time had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation as to whether such enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. Additionally, the court decided it required additional information concerning segregability, citing reasons that could not be disclosed in a public setting. Consequently, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

Following this, the FBI submitted updated declarations and renewed its motion. On this occasion, the agency filed both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, then Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender stated that RIDS had communicated with the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, who verified that investigations were still underway. Bender's ex parte submission included further elaboration. He also addressed the district court’s inquiries about segregability with additional details.

The district court subsequently granted the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s statement “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Moreover, upon review of the ex parte materials, the court found that the FBI had sufficiently explained why any exempt content could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

As with all cases, we examine grants of summary judgment de novo in FOIA actions. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The responsibility falls to the government to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption applies. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this requirement, the FBI must establish that the records it withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes, and that disclosure could reasonably be foreseen to disrupt pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Since the FBI has a primary focus on law enforcement, its invocation of Exemption 7 is owed deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially appropriate with respect to records relating to national security issues. Our Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated,” notably in Exemption 7(A) matters where records of counterterrorism inquiries are sought. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

When the non-exempt portions of records “are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions,” the government is permitted to withhold the records in full. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are permitted to credit government affidavits providing reasonably detailed explanations as to why documents withheld under a valid exemption cannot be further segregated. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given the FBI’s status as a law enforcement agency, its judgment in this regard is afforded significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the agency’s declarations must demonstrate a logical “‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties’” and establish a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its evidentiary obligation in this instance. The publicly filed Hardy declaration stated that the documents at issue were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Further, the ex parte Hardy declaration offered extensive particulars regarding the investigation’s character. Additional context also supports this representation. Before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI has investigated him for links to one or more organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department, as well as for connections to an individual who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court of offenses connected to terrorism. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We therefore find the FBI has plainly met its burden of establishing that Farahi’s file was assembled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further stipulates that withheld materials may only be protected if their release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that such proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For these purposes, continuing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings," id. at 1097–98, and the government may satisfy this criterion by simply affirming their "existence." Id. at 1099. "As long as the investigation continues with the aim of collecting evidence for a possible future prosecution, and that prospective case would be harmed by the early disclosure of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) is applicable." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly found that disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. The publicly filed Hardy declaration stated that releasing information about an active investigation could reasonably be expected to disrupt it by alerting the subjects to the breadth of the inquiry—a justification that is widely recognized in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration attested to the fact that the pertinent investigation was "ongoing." J.A. 49. Hardy's ex parte declaration provided further detail regarding the investigation, and the Bender declaration confirmed the investigation's ongoing status as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines whether there were still pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. He cites language from CREW stating that merely asserting in vague terms that investigations are ongoing may be inadequate to justify Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Furthermore, he argues that the Bender declaration’s statement that the investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is likewise conclusory. While we concur with Farahi that the Bender declaration, when viewed on its own, is notably brief, the surrounding context makes it inappropriate to assess the Bender declaration in isolation. As discussed above, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed this fact, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial detail regarding the nature and extent of the investigation. The purpose of the government’s submission of the Bender declaration was not to newly establish the existence of enforcement proceedings, but to verify that there had been no changes while its initial motion for summary judgment was under review. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already found that the FBI appeared to have satisfied its obligation to demonstrate the existence of pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is properly viewed as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent submission.

The scope of the FBI’s original investigation further supports the credibility of its claim that the investigation is still active. While the contents of Hardy’s ex parte declaration remain sealed, we can point to what the FBI asserted in open court regarding Farahi during his immigration case: specifically, that he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI also stated that he “was connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. The FBI further claimed that he provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” notably the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Upon review of both the FBI’s public submissions and those filed under seal, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its burden under Exemption 7(A) by establishing the continuing existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a limited objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain the basis for its categorization of documents used in support of its decisions to withhold information. When an agency opts to withhold records based on categories, those groupings must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” for each category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer a concrete reason as to why he believes the FBI’s categories were overly broad. As a result, Farahi has relinquished his arguments regarding this matter. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even if a FOIA request includes documents that are exempt from disclosure, the agency is nonetheless required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (stating agencies are required to provide “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission explaining why segregation of the protected records was not reasonably possible. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission was adequate to satisfy the agency’s burden on the matter. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the sheer quantity of records at issue, there must be some information that is segregable and eligible for release. However, if the non-exempt material is only sporadically “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As noted, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, even if Farahi is correct that isolated words or snippets could theoretically be released without impairing the FBI’s interests, that fact does not alter the outcome. The district court properly found that there was no reasonably segregable, meaningful information.

Farahi’s final argument is that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration when evaluating segregability. However, the court was under no obligation to make its analysis of that declaration part of the public record. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, he cannot now contest the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations unquestionably disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, such a difficulty “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
