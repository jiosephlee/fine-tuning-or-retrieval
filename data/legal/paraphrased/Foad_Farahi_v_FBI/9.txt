Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket: No. 23-5243  
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of documents contained in that file, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court instructed the FBI to provide updated declarations in light of concerns that its prior submissions might be outdated. Upon review, the court determined that disclosure of the records sought could disrupt enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or could be reasonably anticipated. Consequently, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded these records from disclosure. We concur with this determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act mandates that federal agencies make records available to the public upon request, except where a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” provided that releasing such records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that those proceedings must either be ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

In cases where an agency withholds materials based on a FOIA exemption, it is required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, an Iranian citizen, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him because of a visa overstay. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, as well as voluntary departure. In seeking to block relief, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a request under FOIA seeking his FBI records. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive material. Following the FBI’s failure to meet the statutory timeframe for responding, Farahi initiated litigation. Over the ensuing years, the agency reviewed the potentially responsive documents, produced certain materials to Farahi, and withheld a majority. After concluding its review, the FBI submitted a summary judgment motion in October 2019. The agency relied on a declaration from David Hardy, who serves as Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other points, maintained that the withheld materials had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure might reasonably be anticipated to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were then pending or reasonably foreseen. Additionally, the FBI sought leave to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and for ex parte review. Farahi raised no objection to this motion, which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the summary judgment motion and determined that the records in question were created for law enforcement objectives, further finding that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, because a considerable period had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to reassess whether such enforcement proceedings were still active or reasonably likely. The court also found it required additional information regarding segregability, for reasons that could not be discussed in the public record. As a result, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI then provided updated declarations and resubmitted its motion. This time, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, then Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had communicated with the agent assigned to Farahi’s file, who affirmed that investigations remained active. Bender’s ex parte declaration contained further specifics. He also supplied expanded information to address the court’s segregability concerns.

Subsequently, the district court granted the renewed motion, finding persuasive the FBI’s representation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Upon reviewing the ex parte submission, the court also determined the FBI had sufficiently justified why exempted material could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with other cases, our review of a summary judgment ruling is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government's obligation to establish that a FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this requirement, the FBI must demonstrate that the documents it withheld were collected for purposes of law enforcement and that making them public could reasonably be anticipated to obstruct enforcement proceedings that are either ongoing or foreseeable. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given the FBI’s law enforcement expertise, its decision to claim Exemption 7 deserves deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This judicial deference is even more justified for materials involving national security matters. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including, in particular, in Exemption 7(A) cases where plaintiffs seek documents concerning counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold documents in their entirety if “nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may accept government affidavits that demonstrate with sufficient detail why withheld documents subject to a proper exemption cannot be further segregated. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI is recognized as a law-enforcement agency, its judgment regarding this issue deserves substantial deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, its statements must “demonstrate a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary obligations in this case. The public declaration from Hardy stated that the materials withheld were put together “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The Hardy declaration filed ex parte offered significant detail about the nature of that investigation. Additional context reinforces this point. Farahi, in the district court, acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him regarding alleged connections to one or more organizations that the State Department has labeled as foreign terrorist organizations and to an individual who was subsequently held as an enemy combatant and later found guilty in federal court of offenses related to terrorism. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. It is clear to us that the FBI established that Farahi’s file was created for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the requested records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this requirement means such proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government may meet its burden by attesting to the “existence” of those proceedings, id. at 1099. The exemption applies “so long as the investigation continues to collect evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be placed at risk by disclosing that evidence prematurely.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that release could reasonably be expected to impede ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The publicly available Hardy declaration set forth that disclosing information about an active investigation could reasonably be expected to interfere by alerting investigation subjects to the investigation’s scope—a rationale widely recognized in the context of Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration stated that the investigation in question was “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s declaration submitted ex parte provided additional detail concerning the investigation. Similarly, the Bender declaration affirmed that, as of March 2023, the investigation was still in progress.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines the assertion that enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He cites our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” to the claim that investigations are still underway may be insufficient to justify the application of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He also asserts that the Bender declaration’s statement that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that the Bender declaration, on its own, appears notably brief. However, it is inappropriate to review the Bender declaration in isolation, as context is essential. As discussed earlier, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were ongoing in the public Hardy declaration, which attested to this fact, as well as in the ex parte Hardy declaration, which described the investigation’s details and scope extensively. The Bender declaration’s purpose was not to independently establish that enforcement proceedings were pending, but simply to affirm that there had been no changes in status while the initial summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, in its denial of the government’s initial motion for summary judgment without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate the pendency of enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Accordingly, the Bender declaration is reasonably interpreted as a supplement to the Hardy declarations rather than a document standing on its own.

The expansive nature of the FBI's initial investigation supports the plausibility of its claim that the inquiry is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specifics of Hardy's ex parte declaration, we observe that in Farahi's immigration case, the FBI publicly asserted several allegations: Specifically, that Farahi was “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Furthermore, he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. He also purportedly supported “charities that financed terrorism,” such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI's public statements and its sealed declarations, we find that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the existence of an ongoing “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi makes a brief contention that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale for establishing the document categories it relied on to support its withholding determinations. When an agency justifies withholdings by categorizing documents, those groupings must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” with respect to each group. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason why the FBI’s chosen categories were too broad. Therefore, Farahi has waived these arguments. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring the disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). The district court, in its second order, noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” for its position that the protected documents could not be reasonably segregated, as stated in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission was sufficient for the agency to meet its burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi challenges this, contending that, given the substantial volume of responsive documents, there must exist some segregable and releasable content. Nevertheless, if non-exempt material is only “randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” then that material typically would not be considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, as “the focus of the FOIA is information, ... not simply words which the Government has written down,” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55, Farahi’s observation that isolated words or phrases might be released without compromising the FBI’s interests does not change the analysis. The district court properly found that no meaningful information in the documents could reasonably be segregated for release.

Farahi further objects to the district court's heavy reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no requirement for the court to publicly disclose its reasoning regarding that declaration. Moreover, because Farahi did not contest the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he cannot now object to the court's dependence on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations do place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
