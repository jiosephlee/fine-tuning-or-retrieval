Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of those records, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court directed the FBI to refresh any declarations that may have become outdated and ultimately determined that disclosing the requested information could impede enforcement actions that were ongoing or could be reasonably expected. Accordingly, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from release. We concur with this conclusion.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are generally obligated to provide records to the public upon request, except when a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). One such exemption, Exemption 7(A), pertains to materials "compiled for law enforcement purposes," when releasing those records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent requires that these enforcement proceedings be "pending or reasonably anticipated." Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) ("CREW") (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, an Iranian citizen, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on his overstay of a visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. The DHS, in contesting his eligibility for relief, introduced documentary evidence as well as in-person testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI file. In response, the FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain responsive materials. Because the agency failed to meet FOIA’s statutory response deadline, Farahi initiated litigation. In the following years, the FBI reviewed the potentially responsive records, ultimately releasing some materials to Farahi while withholding the majority. After completing its review, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, which was accompanied by a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other statements, asserted that the records withheld had been compiled for law enforcement proceedings and that disclosing them could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement actions that were either ongoing or likely to occur. The FBI then requested permission to submit a supplemental declaration from Hardy under seal and on an ex parte basis; Farahi did not object, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court ruled on the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the documents had been assembled for law enforcement objectives and that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might disrupt ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the substantial time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide a revised evaluation regarding whether any enforcement actions remained pending or could still reasonably be anticipated. Additionally, the court found that it required further details to decide on segregability, which, for reasons that could not be disclosed on the public record, were not shared. Consequently, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, serving at that time as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender indicated that RIDS reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. His ex parte statement contained further specifics. Additionally, Bender supplied supplemental information in response to the district court's reservations about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court relied on the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court found that the FBI had sufficiently justified that the exempt material could not be reasonably separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In matters arising under FOIA, as with other types of cases, our standard for reviewing summary judgment is de novo. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s responsibility to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). In this instance, the FBI must establish both that the withheld documents were assembled for law enforcement objectives and that disclosure would likely cause interference with enforcement proceedings that are ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. Refer to id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Because the FBI is an agency dedicated to law enforcement, we afford its use of exemption 7 a measure of deference. See Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This level of deference is especially warranted when the records concern issues of national security. Our Court has repeatedly underscored the necessity of deferring to the executive in FOIA matters when national security is at stake, including in situations involving Exemption 7(A) requests for records associated with counterterrorism operations. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the portions that are not exempt "cannot be reasonably segregated from the exempt portions." Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). A court is allowed to base its decision on affidavits provided by the government, as long as those affidavits describe with adequate specificity the reasons why further segregation of nonexempt material is not possible for records withheld under a legitimate exemption. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the materials withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI functions as a law-enforcement body, its assessment on this issue is afforded significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI's statements must demonstrate a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” as well as establish a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s finding that the FBI met its burden in this case. The publicly filed Hardy declaration indicated that the records withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration also provided substantial details concerning the nature of the investigation. Further context also reinforces this point: before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had been investigating him due to his connections with one or more organizations designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as his association with an individual subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court for terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We have no hesitation in concluding that the FBI has satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that disclosure of the records in question “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this requirement is satisfied only if such proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For these purposes, we have determined that ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government can meet its burden by merely affirming that such proceedings exist, id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, “so long as the investigation is continuing to collect evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and that case could be endangered by an early release of the material, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that disclosure was likely to interfere with enforcement actions that were either pending or reasonably foreseen. The public declaration from Hardy set forth that releasing details about a continuing investigation could reasonably be anticipated to impede that investigation by alerting the subjects to the breadth of the inquiry, which is a commonly recognized basis under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration affirmed that the investigation in question was “ongoing.” J.A. 49. In a separate ex parte declaration, Hardy provided further details regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration also verified that, as of March 2023, the investigation was still in progress.

Farahi maintains that the mere passage of time undermines whether any enforcement proceedings continued to be pending or could reasonably be anticipated. He refers to our observation in CREW that “the vague nature” of a claim that investigations are still ongoing might be insufficient to justify the application of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He also argues that the Bender declaration, which asserted the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in detail. We concur with Farahi that the Bender declaration, viewed in isolation, appears notably brief. Nevertheless, the surrounding circumstances caution against reviewing the Bender declaration on its own. As discussed previously, the government demonstrated the existence of enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which attested to their pendency, as well as the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial specifics regarding the nature and scope of the investigation. The government offered the Bender declaration not to prove de novo that enforcement proceedings were pending, but rather to affirm that the situation remained unchanged while its initial summary-judgment motion was under review. Furthermore, when denying the government’s initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden in showing that enforcement proceedings were ongoing. J.A. 122. Accordingly, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as an update supplementing the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent submission.

The expansive scope of the FBI’s initial investigation lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we highlight the public allegations made by the FBI against Farahi during his immigration case: The FBI claimed he was “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, the agency asserted that Farahi had connections with Khaled Sheikh Mohamed as well as other recognized Al Qaeda affiliates. Id. He was further alleged to be “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Moreover, Farahi was said to support “charities that financed terrorism,” among them the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. After considering both the publicly available and the sealed statements from the FBI, we determine that the agency has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is ongoing. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently justify its choice of document categories in outlining the bases for withholding. When an agency categorically withholds records, it must select categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not specify in what respect the document categories designated by the FBI are overly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited these arguments. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes documents that are protected, the agency is still required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (mandating the disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second ruling, the district court noted that the FBI, through Bender’s supplemental ex parte filing, had given a “detailed justification” for why the protected documents could not be reasonably separated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the large quantity of records at issue, some information should be both segregable and releasable. However, when minor non-exempt portions are scattered intermittently throughout a document, it is unlikely that such material qualifies as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Indeed, as Mead emphasizes, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, although Farahi is correct that the disclosure of isolated words or phrases within the records might not threaten the FBI’s interests, this does not change the result. The district court properly determined that no significant or useful information could be reasonably segregated for release.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its assessment of segregability. However, no requirement exists for the court to publicly disclose its reasoning regarding that declaration. Moreover, Farahi did not object to the court’s use of ex parte declarations, and therefore lacks grounds to contest the court’s dependence on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations can certainly disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
