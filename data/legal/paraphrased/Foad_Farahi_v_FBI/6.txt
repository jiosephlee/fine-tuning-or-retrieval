Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his file held by the FBI. The FBI declined to disclose the majority of these records, leading Farahi to initiate a lawsuit. The court directed the FBI to revise any declarations that may have become outdated, after which it determined that releasing the records in question might impede enforcement proceedings that were either ongoing or could reasonably be expected. Consequently, the court found that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded these records from disclosure. We concur with this decision.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to make records available to the public upon request, unless a specified statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Under Exemption 7(A), records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” are exempt from disclosure if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When an agency withholds material under a FOIA exemption, it is required to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him based on an overstay of his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security, contesting these requests for relief, submitted both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that potentially contained relevant information. After the FBI failed to respond within the statutory timeframe, Farahi brought suit. In the years following, the FBI reviewed the potentially responsive materials, released some documents to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Upon completing its review, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, supported by a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s declaration stated, among other things, that the documents being withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes, and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. The FBI also requested to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. Farahi did not oppose this request, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records at issue were assembled for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had "seemingly" fulfilled its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records might interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, in light of the considerable time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to reevaluate whether enforcement proceedings remained ongoing or were still reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court found that it required additional information to resolve the issue of segregability, for reasons it was unable to discuss publicly. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI provided updated declarations and renewed its motion. On this occasion, the agency submitted both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, then serving as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender described how RIDS reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who affirmed that investigations were still active. Bender’s ex parte declaration included further specifics. He also supplied further details responding to the district court’s queries about segregability.

The district court granted the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s representation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. After reviewing the ex parte submission, the court found that the FBI had sufficiently clarified why the withheld material could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In Freedom of Information Act matters, as with other cases, our review of a summary judgment decision is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this obligation, the FBI must establish that the documents it withheld were assembled for law enforcement reasons and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to impede enforcement actions that are ongoing or likely to occur. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Given that the FBI is a law enforcement agency, its use of exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when records involve national security matters. This Court has “repeatedly emphasized the need to defer to the executive in FOIA cases where national security issues are at stake”—including, in particular, Exemption 7(A) cases involving requests for records concerning counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents “when the nonexempt information ‘cannot be separated from exempt information.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). “Courts may accept government declarations that describe with adequate detail why material withheld under a valid exemption is not reasonably segregable.” Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is restricted to materials "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI operates as a law enforcement body, its judgment on this issue deserves significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the agency’s declarations must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as demonstrate a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s determination that the FBI met its obligation in this matter. The public Hardy declaration stated that the documents withheld were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The Hardy declaration filed ex parte provided extensive information regarding the character of that investigation. Additionally, the broader record supports this position. Before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him due to alleged connections with one or more organizations that the State Department has designated as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as with an individual later held as an enemy combatant and subsequently convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. There is no question that the FBI satisfied its burden of establishing that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that releasing the withheld materials must present a reasonable expectation of interference with enforcement proceedings. See U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has determined that this standard means the proceedings must be either ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, continuing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government can satisfy its burden by affirming their “existence.” Id. at 1099. “Exemption 7(A) is implicated so long as an investigation is active in gathering evidence for a potential criminal prosecution, and such prosecution could be compromised by premature disclosure of that evidence.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly found that release of the information could reasonably be expected to disrupt ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The public declaration by Hardy set forth that making information about an active investigation public could reasonably be expected to hinder the investigation by alerting the subjects to the investigation’s scope—a rationale widely recognized under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, Hardy’s declaration stated that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy provided further details in an ex parte declaration, and the Bender declaration also confirmed that, as of March 2023, the investigation continued.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time calls into question whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably have been anticipated. He cites our observation in CREW that a "vague nature" to claims of ongoing investigations may not be adequate to justify the use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further asserts that the Bender declaration’s statement that the pertinent investigations "remain ongoing," J.A. 131, was similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, viewed by itself, the Bender declaration appears notably brief. However, the surrounding circumstances caution against evaluating the Bender declaration on its own. As described above, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their existence, as well as through the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant specifics about the investigation’s character and breadth. The Bender declaration was not intended to independently establish that enforcement proceedings were pending; rather, its purpose was to confirm that no changes had occurred during the pendency of the government’s initial motion for summary judgment. Furthermore, when the district court denied the government’s initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined that the FBI had "seemingly" fulfilled its burden to show pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is most reasonably interpreted as an update to the Hardy declarations, rather than as a document to be assessed in isolation.

The wide scope of the FBI’s initial investigation lends credence to its claim that the investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the specific contents of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we do observe the FBI’s public statements about Farahi during his immigration case: The FBI asserted that Farahi was “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. They also alleged he had connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and various other identified Al Qaeda members. Id. The FBI further stated he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, they maintained that he provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into account both the public and sealed declarations submitted by the FBI, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) to demonstrate that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” remains in progress. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a cursory challenge, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently justify its delineation of document categories underlying its withholding rationale. When an agency elects to withhold records by grouping them into categories, it must ensure that those categories are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each respective category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any concrete reason as to why the categories selected by the FBI are purportedly too broad. Consequently, Farahi has forfeited any argument regarding this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request covers records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to provide “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had given a “detailed justification” for its position that the protected records could not be reasonably segregated, as set forth in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission was sufficient to meet the agency’s burden on the issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the sheer quantity of responsive records, there must exist at least some information that is both segregable and releasable. However, where only a minimal amount of non-exempt content is “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such information is generally not considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even though Farahi is right that certain isolated words or phrases in the responsive documents could potentially be released without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests, that point is immaterial. The district court properly found that there was no meaningful information that was reasonably segregable.

Farahi further argues that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration when conducting its segregability assessment. However, there was no obligation for the district court to disclose its evaluation of that declaration on the public docket. Notably, Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, and thus cannot now contest the court’s dependence on them. Additionally, although the use of ex parte declarations can disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
