Case Name: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Case Number: No. 23-5243  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of these documents, leading Farahi to initiate litigation. The court directed the FBI to refresh its possibly outdated declarations, and subsequently determined that disclosing the sought-after records could hinder enforcement proceedings that were either ongoing or could reasonably be expected to occur. As a result, the court found that the records fell within the scope of FOIA Exemption 7(A) and were not subject to disclosure. We concur with this conclusion.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request, except where a specified statutory exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) shields from disclosure those records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This court has determined that such enforcement proceedings must either be ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption are required to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States beginning in 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the ground that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure at different stages. In contesting his eligibility for relief, the Department of Homeland Security introduced both documentary evidence and the testimony of an FBI agent who "documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists." Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a FOIA request seeking his file from the FBI. In response, the agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant materials. When the FBI failed to meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. For the following years, the agency examined the potentially responsive documents, providing some to Farahi while withholding the majority. After the review was finalized, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, relying on a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other assertions, claimed that the documents being withheld had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and that their release could foreseeably impede ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. The FBI subsequently requested permission to file an additional Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal, a motion to which Farahi raised no objection. The district court approved this motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to demonstrate that disclosure might interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the amount of time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding the existence of pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. Additionally, the court found that it required further details to rule on segregability, but could not elaborate on these reasons in the public record. Accordingly, the court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI provided revised declarations and again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, then serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In the public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had communicated with the agent handling Farahi’s file, who affirmed that the investigations were still active. More specifics were included in Bender’s ex parte submission. Additionally, Bender supplied further information to address the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court allowed the renewed motion. It gave weight to the FBI's assurance "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt material could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with all others, we examine the district court’s grant of summary judgment under a de novo standard. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s obligation to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). In order to satisfy this requirement in the present case, the FBI needs to establish both that the documents withheld were created for purposes related to law enforcement and that releasing such documents could reasonably be expected to cause interference with enforcement proceedings that are ongoing or foreseeably upcoming. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. As noted in Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998), “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” This deference is especially warranted for materials that affect national security. Our Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated,” which specifically encompasses Exemption 7(A) cases that involve requests for records on counterterrorism investigations. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Records may be withheld by the government in full where the nonexempt material cannot be separated from the exempt content, as they are "inextricably intertwined." Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are permitted to depend upon affidavits from the government that explain, with reasonably detailed specificity, the reasons that documents withheld under a proper exemption are incapable of further segregation. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the FBI’s withheld records are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given the FBI’s status as a law enforcement agency, its assessment in this respect generally receives significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the agency’s statements must “demonstrate a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary obligation in this matter. The publicly available Hardy declaration indicated that the records in question were collected “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The Hardy declaration submitted ex parte provided further substantial details regarding the character of the investigation. Contextual circumstances further substantiate this representation. Notably, before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI has investigated his connections to one or more groups classified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as his relationship to an individual who was later held as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court for terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully persuaded that the FBI demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that disclosing the requested records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this clause requires that such proceedings are either currently “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations are recognized as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government can satisfy its burden by merely attesting to the “existence” of such proceedings, id. at 1099. “As long as the investigation is still collecting evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and the premature disclosure of this evidence could endanger that case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that release of the information could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are either ongoing or expected. The public declaration by Hardy described that revealing details about an active investigation could likely disrupt the investigation by alerting the subjects to its extent—an accepted rationale in cases involving Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration stated that the relevant investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy's ex parte declaration provided further details regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration also verified that the investigation was ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines any argument that enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be anticipated. He relies on our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” to claims of ongoing investigations may be insufficient to justify the application of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He further maintains that the Bender declaration—stating that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131—was similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, if read by itself, the Bender declaration appears unexpectedly brief. However, it would be inappropriate to analyze the Bender declaration without considering its context. As detailed above, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings in the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which affirmed that such proceedings existed, and in the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial information regarding the scope and nature of the investigation. The Bender declaration was submitted by the government not to independently establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings, but merely to verify that the circumstances had remained unchanged during the period its initial motion for summary judgment was under review. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already found that the FBI “seemingly” satisfied its burden to demonstrate the existence of pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration should reasonably be construed as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent submission.

The expansive nature of the FBI’s initial investigation supports the plausibility of its claim that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the material included in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we observe the public accusations the FBI made against Farahi during his immigration case: Allegedly, he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI claimed he had ties to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other individuals associated with Al Qaeda. Id. Furthermore, it asserted that he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. He was also said to have backed “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the public and confidential declarations from the FBI, we determine that the agency has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi makes a brief objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain its basis for defining the categories of documents used in support of its withholding decisions. When an agency withholds records by grouping them into categories, those categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” for each group. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer a particularized reason as to why the FBI’s identified categories were impermissibly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited these arguments. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even where a FOIA request seeks records that are protected, the agency is nonetheless required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring that the agency disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that, through Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, the FBI had furnished a “detailed justification” regarding why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court then determined that this submission met the agency’s obligation concerning this issue. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the large number of responsive documents, there must exist some portions of the records that can be separated and disclosed. However, when minimal non-exempt information is “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” it generally is not considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The underlying principle is that “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, while it is possible that some individual words or isolated phrases could technically be released without undermining the FBI’s interests, such a possibility is not determinative. The district court properly found that no significant or meaningful information could be reasonably segregated from the exempt material.

Farahi further argues that the district court placed undue weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration when conducting its segregability analysis. However, there was no obligation for the district court to publicly disclose its assessment of the ex parte declaration. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s reliance on them at this stage. Additionally, although ex parte declarations undeniably place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
