Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his file maintained by the FBI. The FBI declined to release the majority of the documents, leading Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court, after directing the FBI to provide updated versions of previously submitted declarations that might have been outdated, determined that making the requested documents public could disrupt law enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or reasonably expected. Consequently, the court found that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded these records from disclosure. We concur with this conclusion.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request, unless a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” where releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This court has determined that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information, agencies are required to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on his having overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and the option of voluntary departure. In contesting these forms of relief, DHS introduced documents and called an FBI agent to testify, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi subsequently filed a FOIA request seeking his FBI file. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially responsive. After the FBI failed to respond within the statutory period, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the years that followed, the FBI conducted a review of these records, releasing some material to Farahi while withholding the majority. Upon finishing its review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other assertions, claimed the withheld documents were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. The FBI also sought leave to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not object to this request, which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question had been assembled for law enforcement purposes, and found that the FBI had "seemingly" fulfilled its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure could hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the considerable time that had elapsed, the court instructed the FBI to update its evaluation as to whether such enforcement actions remained ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. The court further found that it required additional details regarding segregability, citing reasons that could not be articulated in the public record. Consequently, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

Following this, the FBI submitted revised declarations and refiled its motion. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was then serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender indicated that RIDS had communicated with the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. Bender’s ex parte declaration elaborated further. He also supplied more comprehensive information to address the district court’s concerns about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s assertion "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. Based on its examination of the ex parte materials, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt information could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases brought under FOIA, summary judgment determinations are subject to de novo review. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The responsibility rests with the government to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this requirement, the FBI must establish both that the documents in question were gathered for law enforcement objectives and that releasing them would reasonably be anticipated to disrupt enforcement actions that are ongoing or can be reasonably foreseen. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Due to its law enforcement expertise, the FBI's use of Exemption 7 receives judicial deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is even more warranted when national security is involved. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated,” a principle that applies specifically to Exemption 7(A) claims concerning counterterrorism records. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Records may be withheld in their entirety by the government if “nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are permitted to rely on affidavits from the government which demonstrate with reasonable specificity that documents withheld under a legitimate exemption cannot be further segregated. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to records that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, its assessment in this respect is generally owed significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s declarations must “demonstrate a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” along with a “link between an ‘individual or incident and a potential security risk or breach of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its burden in this instance. The publicly filed Hardy declaration clarified that the documents withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The Hardy declaration submitted ex parte provided substantial additional specifics regarding the character of the investigation. Furthermore, the broader context substantiates this claim. Before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI has investigated his connections to one or more organizations designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as links to an individual who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Thus, we are fully satisfied that the FBI has established that Farahi’s file was compiled for law-enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that the release of the withheld materials "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this requirement means such proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For these purposes, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings." Id. at 1097–98. The government may establish the existence of such proceedings merely by stating their "existence." Id. at 1099. "As long as the investigation continues to collect evidence for a possible future criminal case, and the premature disclosure of that evidence would jeopardize the case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly found that release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably foreseen. The publicly available Hardy declaration detailed that revealing information about an active investigation could reasonably be expected to impede it by alerting the subjects to the scope of the inquiry—an often-recognized rationale in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration further stated that the pertinent investigation was "ongoing." J.A. 49. Hardy's ex parte declaration provided additional detail about the investigation, and the Bender declaration verified that the investigation was still ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time undermines whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be expected. He cites our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” in claiming ongoing investigations might not be enough to justify Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi also argues that the Bender declaration, by simply stating that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, viewed alone, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, the context advises against viewing the Bender declaration in isolation. As discussed earlier, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were pending through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant details about the investigation’s character and extent. The Bender declaration was not submitted to establish anew that enforcement proceedings were pending, but rather to affirm that circumstances had not changed while the government’s initial summary judgment motion was under consideration. Furthermore, when the district court denied the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to show pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best understood as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent filing.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s original investigation supports the plausibility of its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the details contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can reference the allegations the FBI made about Farahi during his immigration case: the agency asserted that Farahi served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, the FBI claimed he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Further, he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. The FBI also stated he supported “charities that financed terrorism,” naming the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into account both the FBI’s public and sealed statements, we find that the agency has satisfied its burden under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the existence of an ongoing, “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief challenge, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain the basis for the categories it employed in describing its reasons for withholding documents. When an agency opts to withhold records according to categories, it must define these categories in a manner that is “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each respective category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any particular rationale for why the FBI’s chosen categories are overly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited this argument. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even where a FOIA request covers records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second ruling, the district court noted that the FBI had given a “detailed justification” explaining why it could not reasonably segregate protected records, as set forth in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court found that this submission was sufficient to meet the agency’s burden on this issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, considering the large quantity of responsive documents, there must exist some information that can be separated and released. Nevertheless, when non-exempt content is “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” it is generally not deemed “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The essence of FOIA is about releasing meaningful information, not merely “words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, although Farahi is right that some isolated words or phrases could possibly be revealed without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests, this is ultimately irrelevant. The district court properly determined that no meaningful information could be reasonably segregated for release.

Farahi also asserts that the district court placed undue emphasis on Bender’s ex parte declaration when conducting its segregability assessment. However, the court was under no obligation to include its evaluation of that declaration on the public docket. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from now disputing the court’s use of them. Additionally, although reliance on ex parte declarations certainly places FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
