Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of documents contained within that file, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court, after instructing the FBI to provide updated versions of declarations that might have become outdated, determined that disclosing the requested documents could impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Consequently, the court ruled that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shields the records from disclosure. We concur with this decision.

I

The Freedom of Information Act mandates that federal agencies provide records to the public upon request, except where a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Under Exemption 7(A), records that were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" may be withheld if their release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent requires that such enforcement proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold material, agencies are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, an Iranian citizen, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002, alleging he had overstayed his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. To contest his eligibility for relief, DHS introduced documentary evidence and the in-court testimony of an FBI agent who "documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists." Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi submitted a request under FOIA to obtain his FBI file. The agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially responsive. After the FBI did not meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi filed suit. Over subsequent years, the agency reviewed the records it identified, released some to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once its review was complete, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration stated, among other points, that the withheld documents had been compiled for law enforcement purposes and their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with then-ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. The FBI also moved to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte, a motion that Farahi did not oppose, and which the district court granted in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question had been assembled for law enforcement objectives and that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its responsibility to demonstrate that disclosure could impede ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable lapse of time, the court required the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding whether enforcement actions remained ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. Additionally, the court found it required further details to resolve the issue of segregability, for reasons it could not disclose in the public record. As a result, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI subsequently filed revised declarations and renewed its summary judgment request. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was serving as Acting Section Chief of RIDS at the time. In his public statement, Bender noted that RIDS had reached out to the agent responsible for managing Farahi’s file, and that agent confirmed investigations were still underway. The ex parte declaration from Bender elaborated further. Bender also supplemented the record with additional information in response to the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed summary judgment motion. It relied on the FBI’s assurance “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after considering the ex parte declaration, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified that exempt material could not reasonably be separated from nonexempt portions. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In Freedom of Information Act matters, as with other cases, our review of a district court’s summary judgment ruling is de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s responsibility to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). In order to meet this obligation, the FBI in the present case is required to establish both that the records it withheld were compiled for law enforcement objectives and that their release could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with ongoing or reasonably prospective enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. As “the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its invocation of exemption 7 warrants deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This level of deference is especially warranted where the records concern national security interests. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including in Exemption 7(A) cases where plaintiffs seek counterterrorism investigation records. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Where “nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions,’” the government is permitted to withhold entire records. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). “A court may rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to materials that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI functions as a law enforcement agency, its assessment on this matter deserves significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s statements must demonstrate a logical “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” as well as illustrate a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s finding that the FBI satisfied its evidentiary burden in this instance. The public Hardy declaration stated that the withheld documents were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The sealed Hardy declaration provided substantial information regarding the investigation’s character. Additional context reinforces this conclusion. During district court proceedings, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI has investigated his connections to one or more entities designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as links to an individual who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully persuaded that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s file was assembled for law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that the release of the requested documents “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this requirement means the proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this exemption, active criminal investigations are considered “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government can meet its burden by affirming the “existence” of such proceedings, id. at 1099. “As long as the investigation continues to collect evidence for a potential criminal case in the future, and release of that evidence prematurely would compromise the case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that disclosure was reasonably likely to interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. In his public declaration, Hardy stated that making information about an active investigation public could reasonably be expected to impede that investigation by alerting subjects to its breadth—an accepted basis for applying Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, Hardy affirmed in his declaration that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. His ex parte declaration provided further detail regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration also verified that the investigation continued as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time raises questions as to whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He relies on our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” in claims that investigations are still active may not be adequate to justify use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Additionally, he argues that the Bender declaration’s assertion that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was similarly lacking in detail. We acknowledge that, taken alone, the Bender declaration seems notably brief. However, considering the declaration without context would be inappropriate. As noted earlier, the government had already demonstrated the ongoing nature of the enforcement proceedings in the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which elaborated extensively on the investigation’s character and extent. The Bender declaration was submitted by the government not to independently establish the ongoing nature of the proceedings, but merely to verify that circumstances had not changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending. Furthermore, when denying the government’s initial motion for summary judgment without prejudice, the district court had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate ongoing enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than a freestanding submission.

The wide scope of the FBI’s underlying inquiry lends credibility to its assertion that its investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the contents of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we do observe the FBI’s public allegations concerning Farahi during his immigration case: that he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI further stated that he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. It was also alleged that he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Moreover, Farahi was accused of supporting “charities that financed terrorism,” naming the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering the FBI’s statements in both public and sealed filings, we find that the FBI has satisfied its responsibility under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the existence of an ongoing “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief challenge, contending that the government failed to sufficiently substantiate the bases for the categories it used to justify withholding documents. When an agency withholds records by categories, those groupings must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each, as stated in CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any detailed argument as to why the FBI’s specified categories are overly broad. Thus, Farahi has forfeited any challenge on this issue. See Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request covers records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring the agency to disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, explaining why the protected records could not reasonably be segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court also determined that this submission met the agency’s burden with respect to segregation. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large volume of responsive documents, there must exist some information that can be segregated and released. However, where a minimal amount of non-exempt material appears only “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content typically is not “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As the case law recognizes, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, while Farahi is right that certain words or short phrases in the records might be disclosed without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests, this point does not alter the outcome. The district court properly found that no significant information was reasonably capable of segregation.

Farahi further argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability review. However, the court was under no obligation to publicly disclose its assessment of that declaration. First, since Farahi did not object to the court’s use of ex parte declarations, he is now precluded from contesting the district court’s dependence on them. Second, while it is true that ex parte declarations can disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, such a situation “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
