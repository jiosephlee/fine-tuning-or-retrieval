Case Name: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket Number: 23-5243  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to disclose the majority of those records, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The district court directed the FBI to refresh its prior declarations, which might have become outdated, and subsequently determined that providing the requested documents could impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. As a result, the court found that Exemption 7(A) of FOIA shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request, unless one of the specific statutory exemptions applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Under Exemption 7(A), records that have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes" may be withheld if releasing them "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has previously determined that the enforcement proceedings in question must either be ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information, agencies are required to make "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him based on allegations that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, as well as for voluntary departure. In opposition to his requests for relief, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and in-court testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing information that might be responsive. When the FBI failed to respond within the statutory timeframe, Farahi initiated litigation. In the ensuing years, the agency assessed the records in question, provided some disclosures to Farahi, and withheld the majority of the materials. Once its review was complete, in October 2019 the FBI sought summary judgment. To support its motion, it offered a declaration from David Hardy, who serves as Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other assertions, maintained that the records being withheld were gathered for law enforcement purposes and that their release could be reasonably anticipated to interfere with ongoing or foreseeable enforcement proceedings. The FBI also requested to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and on an ex parte basis. Farahi did not object to this request, and the district court approved it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court ruled on the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question had been prepared for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had "seemingly" fulfilled its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, because a considerable amount of time had passed, the court required the FBI to provide an updated evaluation as to whether such enforcement proceedings were still underway or could still be reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court held that it required further information to resolve the issue of segregability, for reasons it could not disclose in a public setting. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who served at the time as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi's file, who verified that the investigations were still active. Bender's ex parte declaration contained additional details. Furthermore, Bender supplied further information in response to the district court's issues concerning segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion, relying on the FBI’s statement “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Additionally, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt material could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as in all other cases, summary judgment decisions are reviewed by this Court de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The responsibility to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption rests with the government. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To fulfill this responsibility, the FBI in this instance must establish that the documents it withheld were assembled for purposes of law enforcement and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to hinder enforcement actions that are ongoing or reasonably foreseen. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Granting such deference is especially justified when the records touch upon matters of national security. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—notably, in Exemption 7(A) disputes where plaintiffs seek materials connected to counterterrorism inquiries. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents “when nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may accept government affidavits that explain with reasonable specificity why documents withheld under a proper exemption cannot be segregated any further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” as stated in U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI functions as a law enforcement body, its assessment on this issue warrants considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s affidavits must demonstrate a logical “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” as well as show a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's finding that the FBI satisfied its evidentiary obligation in this case. The public Hardy declaration articulated that the withheld documents were gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration provided substantial additional information regarding the specific character of the investigation. Moreover, the context supports this representation. During proceedings in the district court, Farahi himself admitted that the FBI had investigated his connections to one or more entities designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as his relationship to an individual who was later detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Thus, it is clear to us that the FBI has established that Farahi’s records were assembled for legitimate law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) additionally necessitates a showing that disclosing the documents in question “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has determined that this requirement means that such proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. The Court has found that active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings” in this context, id. at 1097–98, and the government can meet its burden regarding such proceedings by merely affirming their “existence,” id. at 1099. “As long as the investigation is still collecting evidence for a possible future criminal prosecution, and disclosure of that evidence prematurely would put such a case at risk, Exemption 7(A) remains applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could likely impede current or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. The public declaration by Hardy clarified that revealing details about an ongoing investigation could hinder it by alerting the investigation’s subjects to its scope—an extensively recognized rationale under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, Hardy’s declaration affirmed that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided further specifics regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration also verified that, as of March 2023, the investigation was ongoing.

Farahi contends that the elapsed time undermines any assertion that enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be anticipated. He references our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” in claims about ongoing investigations may not be enough to justify the use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Further, he claims that the Bender declaration’s statement that the investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in substantive detail. While we concur with Farahi that the Bender declaration, when viewed on its own, is notably brief, considering it without context would be inappropriate. As discussed above, the government had already demonstrated, through the public Hardy declaration—affirming the presence of the enforcement proceedings—and through the ex parte Hardy declaration—which described the investigation’s details and breadth—that enforcement proceedings were pending. The government’s submission of the Bender declaration was not to newly establish that proceedings were pending, but simply to verify that the status had not changed while the original summary-judgment motion remained unresolved. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s initial summary-judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied the requirement to demonstrate pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration should be properly interpreted as a supplemental update to the earlier Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent evidentiary submission.

The expansive scope of the FBI’s underlying inquiry supports the plausibility of its assertion that the investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte statement, we observe the public allegations the FBI made regarding Farahi during his immigration case: that he acted as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI also asserted that he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Further, the FBI linked him to “multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, the Bureau claimed he provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” specifically including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. In light of both the FBI’s public and sealed submissions, we determine that the Bureau has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by establishing the continued existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief contention that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale for defining the categories of documents it relied on to support its withholding justifications. Under the governing standard, when an agency chooses to withhold documents grouped by category, it must ensure that these categories are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" to each one. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any detailed reason or argument as to why the different categories put forward by the FBI should be considered overly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited any arguments on this point. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may cover records that are protected, the agency is nevertheless obligated to undertake "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring the agency to disclose "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record"). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had supplied a "detailed justification" in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission explaining why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission was sufficient to meet the agency’s burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the substantial amount of records that are responsive, there must exist some information that could be separated and disclosed. However, if minor non-exempt portions are scattered sporadically throughout the document “line-by-line,” such portions are unlikely to qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As the court noted in Mead, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, although Farahi is technically correct that isolated words or short phrases could theoretically be released without compromising the FBI's interests, this fact is of no consequence. The district court properly determined that no substantive information could be reasonably segregated for release.

Farahi’s argument that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination is unavailing. There was no obligation for the court to publicly disclose its consideration of that declaration. Moreover, because Farahi did not object to the district court’s decision to consider ex parte declarations, he cannot now dispute the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, though ex parte declarations can put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this is an inherent issue in FOIA cases in which, as in this instance, such declarations constitute the only feasible means of resolving the issue without revealing the very information intended to remain confidential. See Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
