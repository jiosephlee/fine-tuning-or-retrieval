Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of those records, leading Farahi to bring suit. The court, after directing the FBI to provide updated declarations that might have become outdated, determined that disclosing the requested information could disrupt enforcement actions that were ongoing or could be reasonably foreseen. Consequently, the court ruled that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this holding.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request, except when a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Under Exemption 7(A), materials “compiled for law enforcement purposes” may be withheld if their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that such proceedings must either be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, an Iranian citizen residing in the United States since 1993, was placed in removal proceedings by the Department of Homeland Security in 2002 based on an overstay of his visa. During the course of his immigration case, Farahi sought relief through applications for asylum, withholding of removal, Convention Against Torture protections, as well as voluntary departure. In opposition to these requests, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and the live testimony of an FBI agent who had “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking his file from the FBI. The Bureau identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive material. After the FBI failed to reply within the required statutory period, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the years following, the FBI evaluated potentially responsive documents, released some materials to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once its review was complete, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, submitting a declaration from David Hardy, who is the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration stated, among other assertions, that the withheld documents were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with law enforcement proceedings that were either ongoing or reasonably anticipated. The FBI also requested leave to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte; Farahi did not contest this motion, and in October 2020 the district court approved it.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the documents had been gathered for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records might hinder ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the lengthy period that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding whether enforcement proceedings remained ongoing or could still reasonably be anticipated. The court further found that additional details were necessary to resolve the issue of segregability, although it could not specify the reasons on the public record. Accordingly, the court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved for summary judgment. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was at that time serving as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender noted that RIDS reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who affirmed that the investigation was still active. In his ex parte submission, Bender included additional specifics. He also supplied further detail responsive to the district court's questions about segregability.

The district court allowed the renewed motion. It accepted the FBI’s assertion “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why exempt information could not reasonably be separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with all others, we conduct a de novo review of summary judgment decisions. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The responsibility falls on the government to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is warranted. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation in the present matter, the FBI must establish that the documents it withheld were created for the purpose of law enforcement, and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to impede either ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Because the FBI is primarily engaged in law enforcement, its assertion of Exemption 7 is afforded deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted where the records at issue relate to national security concerns. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—and this includes Exemption 7(A) cases involving records pertaining to counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to fully withhold documents when “nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Additionally, courts are allowed to consider government affidavits that explain with adequate specificity why records withheld under a legitimate exemption cannot be segregated further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” as provided in U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI functions as a law enforcement entity, its assessment regarding this requirement is generally owed considerable deference. See, for instance, Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s declarations must demonstrate a reasonable “‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” and must show a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary obligations in this case. The public Hardy declaration stated that the documents withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration provided extensive specifics regarding the character of the investigation. Further, the surrounding circumstances bolster that claim. In proceedings before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI investigated his alleged connections to one or more entities designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as his association with a person later held as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Accordingly, we find it clear that the FBI has satisfied its burden of establishing that Farahi’s file was created for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the withheld materials "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this requirement is satisfied when such proceedings are either "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. We have recognized ongoing criminal investigations as qualifying "proceedings" under this standard, id. at 1097–98, and the government may fulfill its burden by providing a declaration affirming their "existence," id. at 1099. "As long as the investigation is still collecting evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and such prosecution could be compromised by disclosure of that evidence before its time, Exemption 7(A) remains applicable." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or foreseeable enforcement proceedings. The publicly filed Hardy declaration detailed that providing information about an active investigation could be expected to impede it by alerting those under investigation to the extent of the inquiry, which is a commonly recognized rationale in Exemption 7(A) contexts. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration stated that the investigation in question was still "ongoing." J.A. 49. Hardy’s in camera declaration provided further detail regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration also verified that the investigation continued as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the length of time elapsed casts uncertainty over whether enforcement actions were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He cites the language from our decision in CREW noting that a “vague nature” in claims that investigations are still open may be insufficient to justify invoking Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi also argues that the Bender declaration, by stating that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was merely conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, if considered on its own, the Bender declaration appears unexpectedly brief. Nevertheless, in context, examining only the Bender declaration would be inappropriate. As described earlier, the government demonstrated the ongoing status of the enforcement proceedings through the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which affirmed their existence, as well as the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided detailed information about the investigation’s character and breadth. The Bender declaration was not intended to initially establish the continued pendency of enforcement proceedings, but rather to affirm that the situation had not changed during the pendency of the government’s first summary-judgment motion. Furthermore, when the district court originally denied the government’s motion for summary judgment without prejudice, it had already observed that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden regarding showing enforcement proceedings were pending. J.A. 122. Therefore, it is reasonable to construe the Bender declaration as a supplement to the Hardy declarations rather than an independent submission.

The scope of the FBI’s ongoing investigation also supports the plausibility of its assertion that the inquiry is still active. While the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte declaration remain undisclosed, the FBI’s public statements during Farahi’s immigration case can be observed: It characterized Farahi as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI asserted he had connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other individuals identified as Al Qaeda members. Id. He was described as “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, he was said to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” which included the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into consideration both the FBI’s open and sealed declarations, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is ongoing. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a cursory objection, asserting that the government did not sufficiently justify its rationale for delineating the categories of documents underlying its withholding justifications. When an agency chooses to withhold records based on categorical groupings, the established categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each grouping. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi fails to articulate any concrete reason that would render the FBI’s categories excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited any argument related to this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even where a FOIA request covers documents that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to release “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that, through Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” for why the protected materials could not reasonably be segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission met the agency’s burden on this issue. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the large quantity of responsive records, some information must exist that can be separated and disclosed. However, when minor amounts of non-exempt content appear sporadically “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content is generally not considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Indeed, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Consequently, although Farahi is correct that isolated words or short phrases in the records might potentially be released without compromising the FBI’s interests, this fact does not affect the analysis. The district court properly determined that there was no reasonably segregable meaningful information.

Farahi’s objection that the district court overrelied on Bender’s ex parte declaration during the segregability review lacks merit. There was no obligation for the court to detail its assessment of the declaration on the public record. First, since Farahi did not object to the court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s use of them. Second, although ex parte declarations can indeed disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
