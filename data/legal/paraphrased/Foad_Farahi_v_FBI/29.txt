Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of those documents, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court instructed the FBI to revise its possibly outdated declarations, and upon review, determined that disclosure of the documents sought could disrupt enforcement actions that were either ongoing or reasonably expected. Consequently, the court determined that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that conclusion.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to make records available to the public when requested, unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” provided that their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, he was subjected to removal proceedings initiated by the Department of Homeland Security for overstaying his visa. Throughout the course of these proceedings, Farahi sought relief in the form of asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition to these requests, the Department of Homeland Security introduced both documentary evidence and testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI file through a FOIA submission. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain responsive material. After the FBI failed to meet the FOIA statutory response deadline, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the years following, the FBI conducted reviews of the materials, released some records to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once the review was finished, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019. It provided a declaration by David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), which claimed, among other points, that the withheld documents had been compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. Subsequently, the FBI sought permission to submit an additional Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. Farahi raised no objection, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records had been gathered for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure of the records might impede ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable amount of time that had elapsed, the court required the FBI to update its evaluation regarding whether any enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be expected. Additionally, the court found that further details were necessary to resolve the issue of segregability, for reasons that could not be disclosed on the public record. Consequently, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again sought summary judgment. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, serving at that time as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In the public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. Additional specifics were included in Bender’s ex parte declaration. Bender also offered further explanation to address the district court’s issues about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. It relied on the FBI’s affirmation that “the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Additionally, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why material subject to exemption could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA litigation, as with other matters, our review of a summary judgment decision is de novo. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government's responsibility to demonstrate the applicability of any FOIA exemption. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this responsibility, the FBI must establish that the withheld materials were created for law enforcement reasons and that disclosing them would reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or foreseeable law enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. As "the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its invocation of exemption 7 warrants deference." Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when the records at issue relate to national security. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—notably in Exemption 7(A) cases concerning documents pertaining to counterterrorism efforts. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the nonexempt material is so closely connected to exempt material that it cannot be separated, as stated in Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are entitled to rely upon affidavits from the government that describe, with reasonable detail, why the documents withheld under a legitimate exemption cannot be further divided for release. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI functions as a law enforcement organization, its assessment on this question is accorded a significant degree of deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, its statements must demonstrate a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” and also a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary obligation in this instance. The publicly available Hardy declaration detailed that the documents withheld were assembled "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Additionally, the ex parte Hardy declaration provided substantial specifics regarding the character of the investigation in question. The surrounding circumstances reinforce this position. During district court proceedings, Farahi admitted that he had been subject to FBI investigations for potential links to one or more entities labeled as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department, as well as for connections to an individual who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court for offenses tied to terrorism. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Therefore, we are fully persuaded that the FBI demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further stipulates that the disclosure of the documents being withheld must be reasonably likely to disrupt enforcement proceedings. See U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent has interpreted this to mean that such proceedings must be either ongoing or reasonably anticipated. See CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, an ongoing criminal investigation qualifies as a “proceeding,” as clarified in id. at 1097–98, and the government can meet its burden by affirming that such proceedings exist, see id. at 1099. “As long as the investigation remains active for collecting evidence potentially relevant to a future criminal case, and that case could be endangered by releasing such evidence prematurely, Exemption 7(A) remains applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be expected to impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. The public declaration submitted by Hardy stated that making details of an active investigation public could alert subjects to the scope of the inquiry, which is a commonly accepted rationale under Exemption 7(A) cases. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration asserted that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte statement provided additional specifics about the investigation. The Bender declaration also confirmed that the investigation continued to be active as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time undermines whether enforcement actions were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He points to our observation in CREW regarding the “vague nature” of claims that investigations are continuing, arguing such statements may not sufficiently justify invoking Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further argues that the Bender declaration’s statement that investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in detail. We acknowledge Farahi’s point that, viewed by itself, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, the context indicates it should not be examined in isolation. As previously discussed, the government demonstrated the existence of ongoing enforcement actions in the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their pendency, as well as in the ex parte Hardy declaration, which described in detail the nature and extent of the investigation. The purpose of the Bender declaration was not to independently show pending enforcement actions, but to verify that circumstances remained unchanged while the initial summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Furthermore, when the district court initially denied summary judgment without prejudice, it had already observed that the FBI “seemingly” fulfilled its responsibility to prove ongoing enforcement actions, J.A. 122. Accordingly, the Bender declaration is properly viewed as a supplemental update to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent submission.

The expansive scope of the FBI’s original investigation lends credibility to its claim that its inquiry is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the substance of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we observe the FBI’s public assertions made during Farahi’s immigration case: Farahi was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood,” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2; he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members,” id.; he had “links to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla, id.; and he supported “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation, id. Taking into account both the FBI’s open and confidential declarations, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its responsibility under Exemption 7(A) to demonstrate that an actual and ongoing law enforcement proceeding persists. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, contending that the government failed to sufficiently articulate the rationale behind its selection of the document categories used to support its withholding determinations. Agencies that choose to withhold records based on grouped categories must select categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each group. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not specifically explain in what way the FBI’s various categories were excessively broad. As a result, he has forfeited any arguments on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring that "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record" must be disclosed). In its second ruling, the district court observed that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” within Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission as to why the protected records could not reasonably be segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court concluded that this submission fulfilled the agency’s burden regarding this issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large amount of responsive documents, there must exist some portions that can be separated and released. However, when the non-exempt content is limited and “randomly ‘interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,’” such content is generally not considered “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As noted in Mead, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, although Farahi is correct that disclosing isolated words or fragments from the records might not compromise the FBI’s interests, that point does not affect the analysis. The district court properly determined that there was no significant information that could reasonably be segregated and disclosed.

Farahi lastly argues that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability analysis. However, there was no requirement for the court to include a discussion of that declaration in the public record. Additionally, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s use of them. Moreover, although ex parte declarations certainly present challenges for FOIA plaintiffs, that issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
