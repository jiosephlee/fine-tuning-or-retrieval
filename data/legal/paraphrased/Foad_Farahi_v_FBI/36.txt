Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI refused to disclose the majority of the records contained within the file, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court, after instructing the FBI to revise potentially outdated declarations previously filed, determined that releasing the requested materials could impede enforcement proceedings that were either ongoing or likely to be initiated. Accordingly, the court found that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that conclusion.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to individuals upon request unless a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) specifically protects records that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when release of such records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedents establish that these enforcement proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds that he overstayed his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposing Farahi’s requests, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and the in-court testimony of an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI records pursuant to FOIA. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs potentially responsive to the request. When the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for a response, Farahi filed suit. Over the subsequent years, the agency examined the identified records, released some to Farahi, and withheld the majority. After completing its review, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its motion with a declaration by David Hardy, Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other points, contended that the materials withheld had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosing them could reasonably be expected to impede enforcement actions that were pending or could be reasonably foreseen. The FBI also requested leave to submit a supplemental declaration from Hardy ex parte and under seal; Farahi did not contest this request, and the district court approved it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the records in question were assembled for law enforcement objectives and that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records might obstruct ongoing or reasonably foreseen enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, because a substantial amount of time had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding whether enforcement actions were still ongoing or reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court determined that further details were necessary to resolve the issue of segregability, though the reasons for this could not be disclosed on the public record. Accordingly, the court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who served at the time as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS reached out to the agent handling Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. Additional specifics were included in Bender’s ex parte declaration. Bender also supplied further details that addressed the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. It gave weight to the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Additionally, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why exempt information could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In Freedom of Information Act cases, as with all other matters, we undertake a de novo review of summary judgment decisions. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The responsibility lies with the government to demonstrate that a claimed FOIA exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). Here, to satisfy this obligation, the FBI must establish both that the records in question were collected for purposes of law enforcement and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to impede either ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This principle of deference is especially warranted with respect to documents that touch on national security issues. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—this includes in Exemption 7(A) cases involving requests for records connected to counterterrorism investigations. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Records may be entirely withheld by the government when the exempt and nonexempt sections are so closely connected that they cannot be separated. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are permitted to depend on affidavits from the government that detail with reasonable specificity the reasons why records withheld under a valid exemption are not subject to further segregation. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the documents withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to records that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI functions as a law enforcement body, its assessment on this issue is generally owed considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s affidavits must demonstrate a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” along with a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary burden in this case. The public Hardy declaration described the withheld documents as having been gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The Hardy declaration submitted ex parte provided significant specifics regarding the characteristics of that investigation. This statement is further corroborated by the broader context. During proceedings before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him for connections to one or more organizations the State Department has classified as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as links to an individual who was subsequently held as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court for terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Therefore, we are convinced that the FBI satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that Farahi’s file was assembled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the requested documents “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this standard is satisfied when the proceedings in question are either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Ongoing criminal investigations are encompassed within the definition of “proceedings” for these purposes, id. at 1097–98, and the government is permitted to establish the presence of such proceedings by merely asserting their “existence,” id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, “so long as the investigation is still collecting evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and disclosure of that evidence at this stage would endanger the case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.”

The district court properly determined that disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to hinder ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. The publicly available Hardy declaration detailed that revealing information related to an active investigation might reasonably cause interference by alerting subjects to the investigation's extent—an extensively recognized basis for applying Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration further confirmed that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte statement provided additional details regarding the investigation. In addition, the Bender declaration substantiated that the investigation was ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the length of time elapsed raises questions as to whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He cites our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” in claims that investigations continue may not adequately justify reliance on Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further maintains that the Bender declaration, which stated that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was similarly lacking in detail. While we concur with Farahi that, when viewed on its own, the Bender declaration does appear notably brief, the circumstances indicate it should not be examined in isolation. As discussed earlier, the government established that enforcement proceedings were pending through the public Hardy declaration—which confirmed their existence—and the ex parte Hardy declaration—which set out substantial information regarding the investigation’s character and breadth. The Bender declaration was not introduced by the government to independently establish the continued pendency of enforcement proceedings, but rather to affirm that no changes had occurred during the interval when the initial summary-judgment motion was before the court. In addition, when the district court denied the government’s original summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden to demonstrate that enforcement proceedings were still pending. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best understood as a supplement or update to the earlier Hardy declarations, rather than as a freestanding submission.

The expansive nature of the FBI’s original investigation supports the plausibility of its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we observe the allegations the FBI made against Farahi in the public record of his immigration case: he was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI also asserted that he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members,” id., and that he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, it was alleged that he aided “charities that financed terrorism,” specifically the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Reviewing the FBI’s statements made both in public and under seal, we determine that the FBI has fulfilled its obligation pursuant to Exemption 7(A) to demonstrate the existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain the basis for the categories of documents it used in its withholding rationale. Agencies that choose to withhold documents by category are required to delineate categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each respective grouping. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason for concluding that the FBI’s delineated categories were too broad. Therefore, Farahi has forfeited any arguments related to this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even where a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (stating agencies must disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission for the lack of reasonably segregable information within the protected records. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court concluded that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive documents, there must exist portions that can be separated and released. However, when a minimal amount of non-exempt content is scattered “line-by-line throughout the document,” such content generally does not constitute “reasonably segregable” information. Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, even if it is true, as Farahi maintains, that isolated words or phrases could potentially be disclosed without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests, this point is immaterial. The district court properly found that no significant information was reasonably segregable.

Farahi also asserts that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability assessment. However, the district court was under no obligation to detail its analysis of that declaration on the public record. Additionally, Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, and therefore cannot now object to the court’s dependence on them. Moreover, although ex parte declarations certainly place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, such an issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
