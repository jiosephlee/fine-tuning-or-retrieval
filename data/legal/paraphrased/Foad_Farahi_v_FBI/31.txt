Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket: No. 23-5243  
Opinion by Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI responded by withholding the majority of the documents contained within the file, leading Farahi to initiate litigation. The court, after directing the FBI to revise any outdated declarations it had previously filed, determined that disclosing the sought-after documents could impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Accordingly, the court found that the materials were exempt from disclosure under Exemption 7(A) of FOIA. We concur with this determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request unless a specific exemption under the statute applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to materials “compiled for law enforcement purposes” where releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that, for the exemption to apply, enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are obligated to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information,” as set forth in U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him based on his overstay of a visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has pursued requests for asylum, withholding of removal, relief pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition to his requests, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and oral testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a FOIA request to obtain his FBI file. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might be relevant. After failing to respond within the statutory time frame, the FBI was sued by Farahi. In the ensuing years, the agency examined the potentially responsive materials, released some records to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once its review was finalized, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, relying on a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other points, contended that the records being withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to disrupt ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. The FBI also sought permission to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. Farahi did not challenge this request, and the district court approved it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question were gathered for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure could disrupt ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the substantial amount of time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation of whether such proceedings remained pending or were still reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court found it required further details regarding segregability, for reasons that could not be disclosed on the public record. As a result, the court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI provided revised declarations and again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS at that time. In the publicly available declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent handling Farahi’s file, and that agent had verified that the investigations were still active. Bender’s ex parte declaration offered further specifics. He also supplied additional details addressing the district court’s issues related to segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. It relied on the FBI's assurance "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt information could not be reasonably separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

As with other matters, we assess a district court’s summary judgment decision in FOIA actions anew. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The burden rests with the government to prove the applicability of a FOIA exemption. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To fulfill this responsibility, the FBI in this instance must establish both that the materials being withheld were compiled for law enforcement objectives and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with enforcement actions that are ongoing or expected. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is even more fitting for documents relating to national security. Our Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—specifically addressing this principle in cases under Exemption 7(A) involving requests for information tied to counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the nonexempt sections are so closely linked with exempt sections that they cannot be separated. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). In such cases, courts are allowed to depend on affidavits from the government that provide a reasonable level of detail explaining why records withheld under a legitimate exemption are not subject to further segregation. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI functions as a law-enforcement body, its judgment regarding this requirement is generally given considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s affidavits must still “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as demonstrate a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s determination that the FBI met its burden in this matter. The publicly filed Hardy declaration indicated that the documents withheld were gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The sealed Hardy declaration submitted ex parte provided extensive specifics regarding the nature of the investigation. Additionally, the broader context supports this conclusion. Before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that he had been investigated by the FBI for connections to one or more groups designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as for associations with an individual later held as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court of terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully satisfied that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that disclosure of the requested documents "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has interpreted this language to mean that the relevant proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings," id. at 1097–98, and the government is permitted to establish the presence of such proceedings simply by attesting to their "existence," id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, "[S]o long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt pending or reasonably expected enforcement proceedings. The publicly available Hardy declaration stated that providing details about an active investigation could likely impede that investigation by alerting its subjects to the investigation’s extent—an extensively recognized rationale under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration confirmed that the relevant investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration offered further detail about the investigation. Furthermore, the Bender declaration verified that the investigation continued as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time raises questions regarding whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be anticipated. He relies on our observation in CREW that a claim about ongoing investigations, when stated in vague terms, may not be sufficient to justify the use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further argues that the Bender declaration’s statement indicating that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was similarly conclusory. While we recognize Farahi’s point that, on its own, the Bender declaration is notably brief, it would be inappropriate to evaluate the Bender declaration without considering the surrounding context. As previously outlined, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were pending through the publicly available Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial information about the investigation’s nature and breadth. The Bender declaration was submitted by the government not to independently establish the existence of pending enforcement actions, but rather to confirm that the circumstances remained unchanged during the period its initial summary-judgment motion was under consideration. In addition, the district court, when it denied the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to show ongoing enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent submission.

The expansive scope of the FBI’s foundational investigation lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although the contents of Hardy’s ex parte declaration cannot be revealed, we can observe what the FBI has alleged in open immigration court about Farahi: that he acted as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI further asserted that he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, Farahi was allegedly “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, he was said to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” notably including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Reviewing both the FBI’s public statements and its sealed filings, we determine that the agency has fulfilled its obligations under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is still underway. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, arguing that the government failed to sufficiently justify its method for defining the categories of documents utilized to support its withholding determinations. When an agency withholds materials based on categorical groupings, the categories chosen must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each grouping. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reasons as to why the categories selected by the FBI were excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his arguments on this particular issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are exempt from disclosure, the agency is still required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information,” as stated in U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b), which mandates agencies to release “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record.” In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” explaining why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated, as outlined in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court also concluded that this submission fulfilled the agency's burden on this issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive records, there should be some information that can be separated and disclosed. However, when isolated non-exempt content appears sporadically “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content is generally not considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because, as noted in Mead, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Accordingly, even though, as Farahi notes, some words or phrases within the records could theoretically be disclosed without compromising the FBI's interests, that point is immaterial. The district court was correct in determining that no significant or useful information could be reasonably segregated.

Farahi’s final argument is that the district court placed undue emphasis on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability review. However, there was no obligation for the court to publicly disclose its assessment of that declaration. First, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is now barred from contesting the court’s use of them. Second, although the use of ex parte declarations undoubtedly disadvantages FOIA plaintiffs, such a circumstance “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
