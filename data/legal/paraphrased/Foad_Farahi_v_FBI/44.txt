Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to release the majority of those documents, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court directed the FBI to revise declarations that might have been outdated. Ultimately, the court determined that disclosing the materials requested could impede enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or could reasonably be expected. As a result, the court found the documents were shielded from disclosure by Exemption 7(A) of FOIA. We concur with that determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request, except where a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Under Exemption 7(A), agencies may withhold records that were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" when their release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that such enforcement proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies are required, when applying a FOIA exemption, to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him, alleging that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi has submitted applications seeking asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security opposed his requests by submitting both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI file under the FOIA. In response, the agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant material. After the FBI did not meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi brought suit. In the following years, the agency went through the records that might be responsive, released some to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once the review was finished, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, submitting a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), in support. The Hardy declaration, among other points, stated that the withheld materials had been assembled for law enforcement activities and their disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to hinder ongoing or expected enforcement actions. The FBI subsequently requested leave to file an additional Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not oppose this request, and the district court approved it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the summary judgment motion and determined that the records in question had been assembled for law enforcement objectives. The court found that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the extensive lapse of time, the court required the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding the status of pending or anticipated enforcement actions. The court further found that additional details were necessary to rule on segregability but could not disclose its reasoning on the public record. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency presented both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, then serving as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public filing, Bender stated that RIDS had communicated with the agent managing Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still in progress. His ex parte declaration contained additional specifics. Bender also offered further clarification in response to the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court relied on the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Further, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt material could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases brought under the FOIA, as with all cases, our review of summary judgment is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The onus lies with the government to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). In this matter, to meet its obligation, the FBI must establish that the documents withheld were compiled for the purpose of law enforcement and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or anticipated enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is even more warranted when the records at issue concern national security matters. Our Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—a principle that specifically includes situations arising under Exemption 7(A) involving requests for documents related to counterterrorism efforts. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold whole documents “when nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Furthermore, “a court may rely on government affidavits that demonstrate with reasonable specificity why documents withheld under a proper exemption cannot be further segregated.” Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the FBI's withheld records are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to records that have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI operates as a law enforcement agency, its assessment regarding this requirement is generally afforded considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s affidavits must demonstrate both a reasonable "nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties," and a link "between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's conclusion that the FBI satisfied its burden in this matter. The public Hardy declaration indicated that the records withheld were assembled "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations." J.A. 42. The Hardy declaration submitted ex parte offered substantial specifics regarding the character of the investigation. Additional context further substantiates this assertion. Before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI had been investigating him for connections to one or more organizations identified as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department, as well as for his association with an individual who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. There is no question that the FBI met its burden to demonstrate that Farahi’s file was created for law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further stipulates that disclosure of the records at issue must present a reasonable expectation of hindering enforcement proceedings. See U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this standard is met when such proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purpose of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations are deemed “proceedings.” Id. at 1097–98. To satisfy this requirement, the government may merely attest to the “existence” of such proceedings. Id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, “so long as the investigation is still collecting evidence for a potential criminal prosecution, and release of that evidence ahead of time would jeopardize that case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.”

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or foreseeably forthcoming enforcement actions. The public Hardy declaration detailed that revealing details about an active investigation could alert subjects to the investigation’s scope, thereby potentially hindering the process—an explanation broadly recognized as sufficient under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration stated that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided further detail about the investigation, while the Bender declaration also confirmed that the investigation was active as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the length of time involved brings into question whether the enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be anticipated. He relies on our comment in CREW, where we stated that a merely “vague” claim that investigations are still underway may not be enough to justify the use of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He also argues that the Bender declaration, which stated the investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, suffers from being similarly conclusory. We acknowledge Farahi’s point that, taken alone, the Bender declaration seems notably brief. However, the surrounding circumstances indicate it should not be viewed in isolation. As previously described, the government already established the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings through the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which attested to such proceedings, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant detail about the investigation’s character and breadth. The purpose of the Bender declaration was not to independently show that enforcement proceedings were ongoing, but rather to affirm that the situation remained unchanged during the pendency of the original motion for summary judgment. In addition, when the district court previously denied the government's first summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already found that the FBI “seemingly” met the burden of showing ongoing proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration should reasonably be regarded as a supplement to the earlier Hardy declarations rather than as a separate evidentiary statement.

The wide-ranging scope of the FBI’s original investigation supports the credibility of its claim that the investigation is still ongoing. Although we are unable to reveal the information contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we do reference the FBI’s statements made during Farahi’s immigration case: it alleged publicly that Farahi served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, the FBI asserted he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. The Bureau further claimed that he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. Moreover, it was alleged that Farahi supported “charities that financed terrorism,” specifically the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking both the FBI’s publicly available and sealed declarations into account, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is still ongoing. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a limited objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain the basis for the document categories it used to support its withholding determinations. When an agency opts to withhold records by categorizing them, it must select categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each, as established in CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason why the categories designated by the FBI should be considered overly broad. Consequently, Farahi has forfeited any arguments on this point. See Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes documents that are protected, the agency remains obligated to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission regarding the lack of reasonably segregable material in the protected records. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission fulfilled the agency’s obligation on this issue. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the large number of responsive documents, there must exist at least some information that can be separated and disclosed. However, when minimal non-exempt content is scattered “line-by-line throughout the document,” such portions generally do not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because, as emphasized in Mead, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, even though Farahi is right that the FBI could, without jeopardizing its interests, release certain isolated words or fragments, this point does not alter the analysis. The district court appropriately found that there was no reasonably segregable, meaningful information.

Farahi also objects to the district court’s heavy reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration when assessing segregability. However, the court was not obligated to make its assessment of that declaration part of the public record. Moreover, because Farahi did not object to the district court's use of ex parte declarations, he cannot now contest the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations inevitably disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, such disadvantage “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
