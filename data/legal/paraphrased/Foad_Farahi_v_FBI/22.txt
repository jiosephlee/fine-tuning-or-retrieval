Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI refused to disclose the majority of those documents, leading Farahi to initiate legal action. The court directed the FBI to refresh any declarations that might be outdated. Ultimately, the court determined that making the requested materials public could hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. Consequently, the court ruled that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this decision.

I

The Freedom of Information Act mandates that federal agencies disclose records to the public upon request, unless a statutory exemption specifically applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" when releasing them "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that such enforcement proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are obligated to make "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information," as set forth in U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (stating, "Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.").

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on his exceeding the terms of his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has sought asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure at different stages. In contesting Farahi’s requests for relief, DHS offered both documentary evidence and in-court testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI records. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant material. After the agency failed to respond within the statutory timeframe, Farahi initiated litigation. In the years that followed, the agency examined the materials, released some documents to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Upon finishing its review, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019. To support its motion, the agency provided a declaration from David Hardy, who serves as the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other points, maintained that the documents being withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt law enforcement actions that were either ongoing or expected. The FBI sought leave to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not object to this motion, which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court ruled on the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the records in question had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records might disrupt ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the lengthy passage of time, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation as to whether enforcement proceedings continued to be pending or reasonably foreseeable. Additionally, the court determined that it required further information to consider the issue of segregability, though it was unable to detail its reasoning on the public record. Accordingly, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI provided revised declarations and refiled its motion. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, who at that time was serving as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender detailed that RIDS reached out to the agent managing Farahi’s file, who affirmed that the investigations were still active. Bender’s ex parte statement contained additional specifics. Further, Bender supplemented his submission with information responding to the district court’s issues related to segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s verification “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after considering the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why exempt information could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under the FOIA, as with all cases, we undertake a de novo review of summary judgment rulings. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The onus rests on the government to demonstrate the applicability of any FOIA exemption. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this burden, the FBI is required to establish that the information withheld was assembled for law enforcement objectives and that releasing such information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt enforcement proceedings that are either ongoing or reasonably expected. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Deference to the FBI’s judgment is especially warranted when the records pertain to national security matters. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including in cases involving Exemption 7(A) where the requested records relate to counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold complete documents when the nonexempt sections are so closely connected with the exempt material that they cannot be separated. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may accept government affidavits detailing with reasonable particularity the reasons why documents covered by a legitimate exemption cannot be further segregated. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” as stated in U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI serves as a law-enforcement body, its assessment in this context is generally due significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the FBI's statements must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as demonstrate a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary burden in this case. The public Hardy declaration stated that the documents withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Additionally, the Hardy declaration submitted ex parte included significant details regarding the investigation’s character. The broader context serves to reinforce that statement. In district court, Farahi himself admitted that the FBI had been investigating his connections to one or more organizations classified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, and to an individual who was later apprehended as an enemy combatant and eventually found guilty in federal court for terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully persuaded that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s file was put together for legitimate law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that disclosure of the withheld documents "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this language means the relevant proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this provision, active criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings," id. at 1097–98, and the government can establish the existence of such proceedings merely by attesting that they are ongoing, id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. The publicly filed Hardy declaration stated that releasing details about an active investigation could plausibly hinder it by alerting those under investigation to its scope—an explanation commonly accepted under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration affirmed that the investigation in question was still "ongoing." J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte submission provided additional detail about the investigation, while the Bender declaration further verified that, as of March 2023, the investigation continued.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be anticipated. He cites language from our opinion in CREW, where we observed that the “vague nature” of claims that investigations are ongoing may not be enough to justify invoking Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further argues that the Bender declaration’s assertion that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in specificity. We concur with Farahi to the extent that, taken by itself, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, contextual considerations suggest it should not be viewed in isolation. As previously noted, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which confirmed such proceedings, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which offered substantial information regarding the investigation’s character and breadth. The Bender declaration was submitted by the government, not to prove anew that enforcement proceedings were pending, but simply to verify that the situation had not changed during the pendency of the initial summary-judgment motion. Additionally, when denying the government’s initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden of showing enforcement proceedings were pending. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is reasonably interpreted as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent document.

The extensive nature of the FBI’s underlying probe also lends credibility to its claim that the investigation is still active. While we cannot reveal the details of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we point out the FBI’s assertions in Farahi’s immigration case: Farahi was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. He was alleged to be “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, he was said to be “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. He was also accused of aiding “charities that financed terrorism,” such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering the FBI’s statements in both public and sealed filings, we determine that the Bureau has satisfied the requirements of Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is still underway. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a cursory objection, contending that the government failed to sufficiently explain the bases for its chosen categories of documents in support of its withholding rationale. When an agency opts to withhold records grouped by categories, those groupings must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each group. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any concrete reason as to why the categories used by the FBI are excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has relinquished any argument in this respect. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information,” pursuant to U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to provide “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI, through Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, had given a “detailed justification” for why the protected records could not be reasonably separated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court also concluded that this submission was sufficient to meet the agency’s burden on this matter. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the sheer quantity of responsive documents, there must exist some information that is both segregable and eligible for release. However, where minimal, non-exempt content appears sporadically “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content does not ordinarily qualify as “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is consistent with the principle that “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, although Farahi is right in noting that isolated words or fragments from the records might be disclosed without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests, this argument misses the point. The district court properly found that no significant information was reasonably segregable.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration when evaluating segregability. However, the court was under no obligation to disclose its assessment of that declaration on the public record. Furthermore, because Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations undeniably place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this difficulty “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
