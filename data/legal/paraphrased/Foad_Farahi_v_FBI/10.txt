Case Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket Number: 23-5243  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

Appellant Foad Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI records. The FBI withheld the majority of those documents, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court directed the FBI to provide updated declarations to address any information that might have become outdated. Upon review, the district court found that disclosing the materials sought by Farahi could disrupt law enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or could be reasonably foreseen. Consequently, the court determined that FOIA Exemption 7(A) applied to the withheld records, precluding their release. We concur with that determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide public access to their records upon request, unless one of the specific statutory exemptions applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) protects records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that these enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies that withhold information under a FOIA exemption are required to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, an Iranian citizen, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 on the grounds of overstaying his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition, DHS introduced documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive materials. After the FBI did not meet the statutory time frame for response, Farahi filed a lawsuit. In the following years, the FBI reviewed the relevant records, released some to Farahi, and withheld a majority. Once its review was finalized, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, providing a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration stated, among other points, that the withheld documents had been compiled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosing them could reasonably be expected to interfere with law enforcement proceedings that were pending or anticipated. The FBI also sought permission to file an additional Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. Farahi did not object, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records at issue were created for law enforcement purposes and found that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records could hinder ongoing or foreseeable law enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the lengthy amount of time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated analysis regarding whether enforcement actions were still underway or likely in the future. Additionally, the court ruled that further information was necessary to resolve the issue of segregability, though it was unable to elaborate its reasons on the public record. As a result, the court denied summary judgment, but did so without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI offered updated declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. This time, the agency submitted both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, who at the time served as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Within the public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had communicated with the agent managing Farahi’s file, and that this agent verified the investigations were still open. In his ex parte declaration, Bender included additional specifics. Bender also addressed, with more detail, the district court’s segregability concerns.

Upon review, the district court granted the renewed summary judgment motion. The court accepted the FBI’s statement "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. Further, after considering the ex parte filing, the court found that the FBI had sufficiently justified why no reasonably segregable, non-exempt material could be released. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA actions, as in other matters, our review of summary judgment orders is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The responsibility rests with the government to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). Here, in order to meet that requirement, the FBI must establish that the records being withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to impede enforcement actions that are ongoing or reasonably anticipated. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “As a law enforcement agency, the FBI’s determination to invoke exemption 7 is afforded deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such deference is especially warranted for materials that touch upon national security interests. This Court has “repeatedly affirmed the importance of deference to the executive branch in the FOIA arena where national security issues are at stake”—including in the specific context of Exemption 7(A) claims involving requests for records tied to counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents “where the nonexempt material cannot be reasonably segregated from the exempt material because they are closely intertwined.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). “A court may base its determination on government declarations that describe, with reasonable detail, why material withheld under a valid exemption cannot be further separated and disclosed.” Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to information “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given the FBI’s status as a law enforcement entity, its judgment on this issue should be accorded substantial deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the FBI’s affidavits must demonstrate both a logical “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” and also a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s finding that the FBI satisfied its evidentiary requirements in this matter. The publicly filed Hardy declaration indicated that the documents withheld were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Furthermore, the ex parte Hardy declaration offered substantial specifics regarding the investigation’s character. Additional context supports this conclusion. Before the district court, Farahi admitted that he had been investigated by the FBI due to his connections with one or more groups identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, in addition to his associations with an individual who was subsequently classified as an enemy combatant and later convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully persuaded that the FBI has demonstrated that the records concerning Farahi were compiled for law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further stipulates that the release of documents may be withheld if such release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedents clarify that this standard requires the enforcement proceedings to either be pending or reasonably expected in the future. See CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this exemption, active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” as established in id. at 1097–98, and the government can satisfy its burden by simply affirming their "existence," id. at 1099. Thus, "as long as the investigation is still collecting evidence for a potential criminal prosecution and the early release of such evidence would endanger the case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that revealing the records could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or anticipated enforcement proceedings. The publicly filed Hardy declaration set forth that disclosure regarding details of an active investigation may plausibly hinder it by alerting subjects to the investigation’s nature and scope—an explanation widely endorsed in 7(A) case law. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, Hardy stated that the investigation at issue was "ongoing." J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided further description of the investigation, and the Bender declaration verified that the investigation continued as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time calls into question whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably have been anticipated. He cites our observation in CREW that simply asserting investigations are ongoing, without specifics, may be too indefinite to justify Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Furthermore, he maintains that the Bender declaration’s assertion that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in specificity. We concur with Farahi that, if viewed alone, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, proper context weighs against considering the Bender declaration in isolation. As set forth above, the government demonstrated the existence of enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their presence, as well as the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided extensive information about the investigation’s scope and character. The government’s purpose in submitting the Bender declaration was not to newly establish the existence of pending enforcement proceedings, but to verify that circumstances had remained unchanged while the initial summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, in previously denying the government’s first summary-judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its burden of establishing pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is most reasonably construed as an update to the Hardy declarations rather than as an independent, self-contained submission.

The wide scope of the FBI’s original investigation also supports the plausibility of its claim that the inquiry is still active. Although we cannot reveal the substance of Hardy’s ex parte statement, it is relevant to mention the allegations the FBI has made about Farahi in the public record of his immigration case: namely, that he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. He was also “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” including Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, he backed “charities that financed terrorism,” such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. After considering both the FBI’s public disclosures and its sealed statements, we find that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A), demonstrating the ongoing existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief contention that the government failed to sufficiently explain the basis for how it delineated the groups of documents underlying its justifications for withholding. When an agency opts to withhold records by grouping them into categories, the categories must be clearly defined so that a court can assess whether the cited exemptions were correctly invoked for each group. See CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason that the FBI's identified categories are overly broad. As a result, he has forfeited any challenge on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include documents containing protected information, agencies are still obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (agencies required to produce “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission to explain why the records at issue could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission was sufficient to fulfill the agency’s burden on this matter. Id.

Farahi argues that given the extensive number of responsive records, there must be some information that is both segregable and releasable. However, when a minor quantity of non-exempt material appears “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As stated, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even though Farahi is correct that certain words or short phrases in the records might be disclosed without causing harm to the FBI, this is immaterial. The district court properly determined that there was no meaningful information that could be reasonably segregated.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no obligation for the court to set forth its analysis of that declaration on the public record. Moreover, because Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s dependence on them now. Additionally, while ex parte declarations certainly place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, that is an unavoidable issue in FOIA cases where, as in the present matter, an ex parte declaration constitutes the sole means of resolving the dispute without revealing the information sought to be protected. See Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
