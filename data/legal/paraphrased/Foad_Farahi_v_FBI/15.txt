Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to disclose the majority of the documents contained therein, leading Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court directed the FBI to refresh any outdated declarations it had provided. Upon review, the court determined that making the requested records public might impede ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. Consequently, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obliges federal agencies to make records accessible to the public upon request, unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) shields records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" from disclosure if releasing them "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that these enforcement proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When an agency withholds records on the basis of a FOIA exemption, it is required to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on his remaining in the country beyond his visa’s expiration. During the course of the immigration process, Farahi has, at different times, sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and permission for voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security, challenging his eligibility for relief, offered documents and the testimony of an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a request under FOIA seeking his file from the FBI. The agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially relevant materials. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for a response, Farahi brought suit. In the ensuing years, the FBI examined potentially responsive materials, released certain documents to Farahi, but withheld the majority. After concluding its review, in October 2019, the FBI sought summary judgment. It provided, among other materials, a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration stated, inter alia, that the withheld information had been compiled for purposes of law enforcement proceedings and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to impair enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. The FBI also requested permission to file an additional Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal; Farahi did not contest this request, and the district court approved it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records were assembled for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records might impede current or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, acknowledging the extensive lapse of time, the court directed the FBI to reassess whether such enforcement proceedings were in fact still underway or reasonably expected. The court further found that additional information was required to rule on the issue of segregability, though it could not disclose its reasoning on the public docket. For these reasons, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI then submitted revised declarations and renewed its motion. On this occasion, the FBI provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender reported that RIDS had contacted the agent managing Farahi’s file, who confirmed that investigations continued to be active. Bender supplied further specifics in his ex parte declaration. He also supplemented the record with additional details to address the court’s prior concerns about segregability.

Subsequently, the district court granted the renewed motion. The court credited the FBI's representation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court held that the FBI had sufficiently demonstrated that exempt material could not reasonably be separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with other types of cases, we conduct a de novo review of summary judgment rulings. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The obligation to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption rests with the government. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this responsibility in the present case, the FBI must establish that the documents being withheld were prepared for law enforcement purposes and that revealing them could reasonably be expected to disrupt enforcement actions that are ongoing or can reasonably be foreseen. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Since the FBI is principally engaged in law enforcement, its reliance on exemption 7 is to be given deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Deference is particularly warranted when such records involve issues of national security. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated,” a position maintained in Exemption 7(A) cases requesting records regarding counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

If nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined with exempt portions,” the government is permitted to withhold records in full. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are permitted to accept government affidavits that explain with sufficient specificity why materials withheld under a legitimate exemption cannot be further divided or segregated. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI functions as a law enforcement body, its determination in this regard should be given substantial deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s affidavits must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” and also show a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s finding that the FBI has satisfied its evidentiary burden in this case. The public Hardy declaration clarified that the records at issue were collected “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The confidential Hardy declaration went further, providing substantial specifics regarding the investigation’s character. Additional context lends further support to this position. In district court filings, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI was investigating him for associations with one or more organizations that the State Department has designated as foreign terrorist organizations, in addition to connections with an individual later apprehended as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are convinced that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s records were compiled for purposes of law enforcement.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the records at issue “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This court has previously determined that this standard requires that such proceedings be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of the exemption, continuing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government can satisfy this showing by providing sworn statements regarding the “existence” of these proceedings, id. at 1099. The exemption is applicable “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to hinder enforcement proceedings that were either pending or could be reasonably foreseen. The publicly filed Hardy declaration described how providing details about an active investigation could reasonably be expected to hinder the process by alerting those under investigation to its scope, an established basis for withholding under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration stated the relevant investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s sealed declaration further detailed the investigation, and the Bender declaration verified that the investigation was still ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the passage of time raises questions as to whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be anticipated. He relies on our observation in CREW that merely asserting, in vague terms, that investigations continue is not necessarily sufficient to justify Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further claims that the Bender declaration’s statement that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in detail. While we acknowledge Farahi's point that the Bender declaration seems notably brief if considered by itself, the surrounding context indicates it should not be viewed alone. As discussed earlier, the government demonstrated the ongoing nature of enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which elaborated on the specifics and scope of the investigation. The Bender declaration was provided not to reestablish from the beginning that enforcement proceedings were ongoing, but simply to verify that circumstances had not changed during the pendency of its original summary judgment motion. Additionally, when the district court initially denied the government’s summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already noted that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden in demonstrating pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as a supplement to the Hardy declarations rather than as a self-contained submission.

The scope of the FBI’s investigation further supports the plausibility of its assertion that its inquiry is still active. While we are unable to reveal the specifics contained within Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we highlight the FBI’s public assertions concerning Farahi during his immigration case: Specifically, the agency described him as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. It was also alleged that Farahi maintained “connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Moreover, he was said to be “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, he reportedly provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI’s statements made publicly and those found in sealed declarations, we determine that the agency has satisfied its obligations under Exemption 7(A), demonstrating the ongoing nature of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a cursory challenge, contending that the government failed to sufficiently justify the manner in which it delineated the document categories supporting its withholding rationale. When an agency opts to withhold information based on categorical groupings, the categories chosen must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each group. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason why the FBI’s chosen categories were too broad. As a result, Farahi has abandoned any arguments on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may cover records that are protected, the agency is still obligated to make "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring the disclosure of "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record"). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission explaining why the protected records could not reasonably be segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission met the agency’s evidentiary obligation on this issue. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the substantial number of responsive records, there must be at least some information that is both segregable and releasable. However, if a small portion of non-exempt content is simply "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," then that material is unlikely to be "reasonably segregable." Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The rationale is that "the focus of the FOIA is information, ... not simply words which the Government has written down." Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, while Farahi is correct that particular words or short segments within the records at issue might potentially be disclosed without compromising the FBI's interests, this does not establish that any meaningful information is reasonably segregable. The district court properly found that no significant information could be separated and disclosed.

Lastly, Farahi asserts that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no obligation for the court to disclose its consideration of that declaration on the public docket. First, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from disputing the court’s dependence on them now. Second, although ex parte declarations undeniably place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
