Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to release the majority of these records, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court instructed the FBI to revise any declarations that may have become outdated. Ultimately, the court determined that disclosing the documents in question could disrupt enforcement actions that were either ongoing or likely to occur. Consequently, the court found that these documents were exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 7(A). We concur with this determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request, unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Pursuant to Exemption 7(A), records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” may be withheld if their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This court has interpreted enforcement proceedings under this exemption as those that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

In cases where an agency withholds information pursuant to a FOIA exemption, it is required to make "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) ("Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.").

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on his having overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition to these requests, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and in-person testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI records under the FOIA. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant information. Because the agency did not respond within the statutory timeframe, Farahi filed suit. Over several subsequent years, the FBI conducted a review of potentially responsive records, released certain documents to Farahi, and withheld the majority. When its review concluded, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, supporting its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other points, stated that the withheld materials were gathered for law enforcement proceedings and that disclosure would reasonably be anticipated to impede enforcement actions that were ongoing or could be expected. The FBI requested leave to file an additional Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal; Farahi did not object, and in October 2020, the district court approved the motion.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure of the records might obstruct ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the considerable time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to reassess and provide an updated evaluation regarding whether enforcement actions remained active or were still reasonably expected. Additionally, the court found it required further details to resolve the issue of segregability, for reasons it could not disclose publicly. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS at the time. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. The ex parte declaration by Bender offered further specifics. Additionally, Bender supplied additional information to respond to the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion, accepting the FBI's assurance “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Additionally, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified that any exempt information could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under the FOIA, as with all other types of cases, we assess summary judgment decisions anew, without deference. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must establish that the documents being withheld were assembled for the purposes of law enforcement, and that releasing them would reasonably be expected to impede ongoing or anticipated enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given that the FBI is primarily a law enforcement agency, its assertion of Exemption 7 is afforded judicial deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted for documents that affect national security interests. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including, in particular, for Exemption 7(A) cases concerning counterterrorism investigative records. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Records may be withheld in full by the government when the nonexempt material is so closely connected to the exempt material that separation is impossible. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). In such cases, courts may accept government affidavits that explain, with adequate detail, the reasons why records covered by a lawful exemption cannot be further separated. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” pursuant to U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The FBI, functioning as a law enforcement body, is generally afforded significant deference regarding this aspect. See, for example, Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, its supporting statements must “demonstrate a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties’” and also establish a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s assessment that the FBI met its evidentiary obligation in this instance. The public declaration submitted by Hardy described the withheld documents as having been assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte declaration by Hardy provided substantial information about the character of the investigation. Further context strengthens this claim. During proceedings in the district court, Farahi acknowledged that he had been investigated by the FBI for connections to one or more organizations identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as for links to a person who was later detained as an enemy combatant and eventually found guilty in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully convinced that the FBI demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the withheld materials “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has interpreted this to mean that enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings” for purposes of this exemption, id. at 1097–98, and the government may demonstrate the existence of such proceedings by simply attesting to their “existence,” id. at 1099. According to Juarez, “so long as the investigation is still collecting evidence for a potential criminal prosecution, and early disclosure of that evidence would put the case at risk, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to impede current or foreseeable enforcement actions. The publicly filed Hardy declaration detailed that releasing details about an active investigation might interfere with it by alerting targets to the investigation's boundaries—an often recognized rationale in cases involving Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration affirmed that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte statement provided further information about the investigation, and the Bender declaration also confirmed that the investigation was still active as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time calls into question whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be expected. He cites our observation in CREW that a party’s “vague” claim of ongoing investigations may not suffice to justify withholding under Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He further argues that the Bender declaration’s assertion that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We acknowledge that, taken alone, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, proper context suggests the Bender declaration should not be assessed in isolation. As previously discussed, the government showed the existence of pending enforcement proceedings in the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which attested to their continuance, and in the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant specifics regarding the nature and scope of the investigation. The purpose of the Bender declaration was not to independently demonstrate the existence of pending enforcement matters, but simply to confirm that no material circumstances had changed while the initial summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s first summary-judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its obligation to show enforcement proceedings were pending. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best understood as an update to the earlier Hardy declarations, rather than as a freestanding submission.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s initial investigation further supports the credibility of its assertion that the investigation is still active. While the specific details contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration cannot be revealed, we observe that the FBI made several public allegations about Farahi during his immigration case: He was alleged to be “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. Additionally, he was purportedly “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. He was also said to be “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Furthermore, it was asserted that he supported “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into account both the FBI’s publicly disclosed and sealed statements, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is ongoing. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi briefly contends that the government failed to sufficiently support its delineation of document categories used to justify its withholding actions. An agency withholding documents based on categories must designate groups that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” with respect to each category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any particular reasoning as to why the categories the FBI used were excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited any objections regarding this point. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency is nonetheless required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” See U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (mandating disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” within Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission as to why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission adequately met the agency’s burden regarding this issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the substantial quantity of responsive documents, there must exist information that could be separated and disclosed. However, when minimal non-exempt content is scattered sporadically "line-by-line throughout the document," such information is unlikely to be considered "reasonably segregable." See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even if Farahi is right that certain words or brief phrases could theoretically be released without affecting the FBI’s concerns, that point is immaterial. The district court properly determined that no substantive information could be reasonably segregated.

Lastly, Farahi argues that the district court placed undue reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no obligation for the court to present its evaluation of that declaration on the public docket. First, Farahi did not contest the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, and as such, he cannot now object to the court’s use of them. Second, although ex parte declarations may disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
