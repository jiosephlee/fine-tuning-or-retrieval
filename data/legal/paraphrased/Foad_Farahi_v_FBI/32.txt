Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request to the FBI under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his file. The FBI declined to release the majority of the documents, leading Farahi to initiate a lawsuit. The court instructed the FBI to provide updated declarations in case earlier ones were outdated. Ultimately, the court determined that disclosing the requested documents might hamper ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. Accordingly, it found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide access to their records upon public request, except where a specific statutory exemption applies. U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) protects records that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” so long as their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that the referenced proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, an Iranian citizen, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him based on a visa overstay. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi has sought relief through applications for asylum, withholding of removal, Convention Against Torture protection, and voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security opposed these requests by submitting documents and presenting in-person testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a request under FOIA seeking his file from the FBI. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing information that could be responsive. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline to respond, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the ensuing years, the FBI examined the materials it had identified, released some documents to Farahi, and withheld the majority. After its review was completed, the FBI, in October 2019, sought summary judgment. It supported its motion with a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other representations, stated that the records being withheld had been assembled for purposes of law enforcement proceedings and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement actions that were then ongoing or reasonably foreseen. The FBI requested leave to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte; Farahi did not object, and the district court approved the request in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question had been created for law enforcement objectives and that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to demonstrate that disclosing the records might interfere with ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the substantial lapse of time, the court directed the FBI to revise its evaluation regarding whether enforcement actions remained ongoing or could reasonably be anticipated. The court further found that additional details were necessary to resolve the issue of segregability, citing reasons that could not be disclosed publicly. Consequently, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, who at that time served as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent supervising Farahi’s file, who affirmed that the investigations were still active. Bender’s ex parte statement offered further specifics. Additionally, Bender supplied additional information in response to the district court’s inquiries about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. It relied on the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after examining the ex parte submission, the court found that the FBI had sufficiently justified its determination that the exempt material could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases brought under the FOIA, as in all other matters, our review of summary judgment decisions is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government’s responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must establish that the documents it withheld were compiled for law enforcement objectives, and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to hamper enforcement proceedings that are ongoing or likely to occur. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such deference is especially warranted when the records concern national security interests. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including, in particular, in cases arising under Exemption 7(A) that seek materials related to counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire records when the nonexempt sections are so closely connected to the exempt sections that they cannot be separated, as held in Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Additionally, courts may accept government affidavits that describe, with reasonable detail, the reasons why materials withheld under a legitimate exemption are not amenable to further segregation. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the documents withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given the FBI’s role as a law enforcement entity, its conclusion regarding this requirement is generally accorded considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s affidavits must demonstrate a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” in addition to showing a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s determination that the FBI has satisfied its burden in this case. The public Hardy declaration articulated that the materials withheld were gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Further details concerning the character of this investigation were provided in the ex parte Hardy declaration. The larger factual background also reinforces this statement. During proceedings before the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated his connections to one or more organizations labeled as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department, as well as his association with an individual subsequently held as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are convinced that the FBI has met its burden to establish that Farahi’s file was compiled for purposes of law enforcement.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the records at issue must be such that it “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this requirement means that the proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For purposes of this standard, active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings.” Id. at 1097–98. The government may establish the existence of such proceedings simply by affirming their “existence.” Id. at 1099. As explained in Juarez, “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and release of that evidence prematurely would threaten that case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be expected to hinder current or foreseeable enforcement actions. According to the publicly available Hardy declaration, releasing details about an active investigation might alert subjects about the investigation’s breadth, which is a commonly accepted basis for invoking Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration confirmed that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s in camera declaration provided further details about the investigation, while the Bender declaration also verified that the investigation was ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time brings into question whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably have been anticipated. He cites our observation in CREW that simply claiming investigations are still open in a “vague” manner may not be enough to justify Exemption 7(A)’s application. See 746 F.3d at 1098. He also asserts that the Bender declaration’s statement that investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, if viewed on its own, the Bender declaration does seem notably brief. Nevertheless, it would be inappropriate to analyze the Bender declaration in a vacuum, given the broader context. As discussed previously, the government already demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were ongoing through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, as well as the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial information concerning the scope and character of the investigation. The government presented the Bender declaration not to independently establish the existence of enforcement proceedings, but rather to verify that no circumstances had changed during the pendency of its original summary-judgment motion. Additionally, when it denied the government’s first summary-judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had found that the FBI had “seemingly” carried its burden to show enforcement proceedings remained pending. J.A. 122. Accordingly, the Bender declaration is properly understood as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than an independent document.

The extensive scope of the FBI's original investigation also supports the plausibility of its claim that the inquiry is still ongoing. Although we are unable to reveal the details of Hardy's ex parte declaration, we can reference allegations that the FBI made against Farahi during his immigration hearings: Specifically, the FBI described him as "a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood." Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. He was purportedly “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, he was said to be “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. The FBI further asserted that Farahi provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” naming the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Upon review of both the FBI’s public statements and its sealed declarations, we find that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) to demonstrate the ongoing nature of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi briefly contends that the government failed to sufficiently justify its selection of document categories that formed the basis of its withholding determinations. When an agency withholds documents based on categorical groupings, those categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any specific reason as to why the categories identified by the FBI were excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his arguments in this regard. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency is obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (stating that an agency must release “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI, through Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, had offered a “detailed justification” for why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court also concluded that this submission was sufficient to meet the agency’s burden regarding this issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of documents found to be responsive, there must exist portions containing non-exempt information suitable for disclosure. However, if minor amounts of non-exempt content are “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As explained in Mead, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even if Farahi is technically correct that isolated words or snippets could be released without threatening the FBI’s interests, that point is not determinative. The district court properly held that no significant or meaningful information was reasonably segregable.

Farahi ultimately contends that the district court gave undue weight to Bender’s ex parte declaration when conducting its segregability assessment. However, there was no obligation for the court to publicly disclose its evaluation of that declaration. Notably, Farahi did not object to the district court’s receipt of ex parte declarations and therefore cannot now contest the court’s use of them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations certainly disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this issue is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
