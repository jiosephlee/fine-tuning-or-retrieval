Case Name: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Case No.: 23-5243  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of documents contained within the file, prompting Farahi to initiate a lawsuit. The court directed the FBI to revise declarations that might have become outdated, and subsequently determined that disclosing the sought-after records could impede enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or reasonably expected. As a result, the court ruled that the records fell within the protection of FOIA’s Exemption 7(A). We concur with this conclusion.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to make their records available to the public when requested, unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to records that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and whose release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that the enforcement proceedings in question must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has pursued several forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposition to his applications, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who "documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists." Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI file under FOIA. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might be responsive to his request. After the FBI failed to respond within the time required by statute, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the following years, the FBI conducted a review of the responsive materials, providing some documents to Farahi while withholding the majority. After finishing its review, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019. To support this motion, it provided a declaration from David Hardy, who leads its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration stated, among other things, that the materials withheld had been gathered for law enforcement proceedings and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. The FBI also sought leave to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte, a request Farahi did not oppose, and which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question were assembled for law enforcement purposes, and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure could potentially hinder ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to re-evaluate whether enforcement actions remained active or reasonably foreseeable. The court further found that additional details were necessary to resolve the issue of segregability, though it could not specify its reasoning on the public record. Accordingly, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and reinstated its motion. On this occasion, the agency presented both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who at that time served as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent in charge of managing Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still in progress. Bender offered additional information in his ex parte declaration. He also supplied further details specifically aimed at responding to the district court’s inquiries about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. Relying on the FBI's affirmation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing," J.A. 138–39, the court, after considering the ex parte submission, determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt information could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA litigation, as with all other cases, our review of a summary judgment ruling is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The burden rests with the government to establish that a claimed FOIA exemption is justified. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI is required to demonstrate both that the documents in question were compiled for a law enforcement purpose and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are either ongoing or can reasonably be expected to occur. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such judicial deference is especially appropriate for records touching upon issues of national security. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including, in particular, in cases under Exemption 7(A) involving records from counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the nonexempt material cannot be separated from the exempt material because they are “inextricably intertwined.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are entitled to accept affidavits from the government that detail, with sufficient specificity, the reasons why documents withheld under a valid exemption cannot be segregated any further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the documents withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to materials that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI operates as a law enforcement body, its determination regarding this requirement is generally granted considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI’s affidavits must demonstrate a logical “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” and must also show “a connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its burden in this case. The public Hardy declaration clarified that the records at issue were gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration provided substantial information regarding the specific focus of that investigation. Additional context also supports this determination. Before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him for connections to organizations classified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations and for his association with a person who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are convinced that the FBI satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that disclosure of the withheld materials "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that this condition means the proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For these purposes, ongoing criminal investigations are included within the definition of "proceedings," id. at 1097–98, and the government can meet its burden simply by affirming their "existence," id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt current or foreseeable enforcement actions. The publicly filed Hardy declaration stated that revealing details about an active investigation could reasonably be expected to impede it by alerting subjects to the breadth of the inquiry—an extensively recognized ground for withholding under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration asserted that the pertinent investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Further clarification concerning the investigation was provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. The Bender declaration also verified that the investigation was ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time calls into question whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could have been reasonably anticipated. He relies on our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” claim that investigations are ongoing may not be enough to justify withholding under Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi also argues that the Bender declaration’s statement that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is equally conclusory. We acknowledge that, taken by itself, the Bender declaration appears rather brief. However, context indicates that it should not be viewed in isolation. As discussed earlier, the government had demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings in the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided extensive information about the investigation’s nature and scope. The purpose of the Bender declaration was not to independently establish the continued pendency of proceedings, but to verify that no developments had occurred during the period when the initial summary judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, when it denied the government’s initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already observed that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden of showing pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best seen as a supplement or update to the Hardy declarations rather than as an isolated document.

The scope of the FBI’s initial inquiry further supports the credibility of its assertion that the investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the specific information in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we observe what the FBI made public regarding Farahi during his immigration case: He was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. He purportedly “had connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. He was said to be “associated with several convicted or indicted terrorists,” including Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, he allegedly provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” naming the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into account both the FBI’s publicly filed and confidential declarations, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligations under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the continued existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi makes a brief argument that the government failed to sufficiently explain its basis for defining the document categories it used to support its withholdings. When an agency chooses to withhold records by grouping them into categories, it is required to select categories that are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" to each group. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not provide any particular reason why the FBI’s designated categories are overly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his argument on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring the agency to disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had submitted a “detailed justification” within Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission as to why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission fulfilled the agency’s burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive documents, there must exist some portions that can be separated and released. However, when a minimal amount of non-exempt content is randomly “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content typically does not meet the standard for being “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The Freedom of Information Act, after all, is primarily concerned with substantive information rather than isolated “words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, although Farahi accurately observes that certain words or brief phrases might be released without compromising the FBI’s interests, this point does not affect the outcome. The district court properly found that no substantive information could reasonably be segregated for release.

Farahi also contends that the district court placed undue weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, the court was under no obligation to make its evaluation of that declaration available on the public record. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from disputing the court’s dependence on them. Additionally, even though ex parte declarations undoubtedly put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
