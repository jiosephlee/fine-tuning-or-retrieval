Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI declined to release the majority of those records, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The court instructed the FBI to supplement any outdated declarations it had provided. Ultimately, the court determined that disclosing the requested materials could impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Consequently, the court ruled that these records were exempt from disclosure pursuant to Exemption 7(A) of the FOIA. We concur with this finding.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request, unless one of the specific statutory exemptions applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Under Exemption 7(A), records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” are exempt if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that enforcement proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information, agencies are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him based on the expiration of his visa. Throughout the course of these immigration proceedings, Farahi has submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and for voluntary departure. In opposing Farahi’s requests for relief, the Department of Homeland Security introduced both documentary evidence and in-court testimony from an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking his FBI records. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain relevant material. After the agency failed to respond within the time required by statute, Farahi initiated litigation. Over the course of several years, the FBI examined the identified records, provided Farahi with some materials, and withheld the majority. When the review concluded, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, presenting a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s declaration, among other assertions, stated that the documents being withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes and their disclosure could reasonably be expected to impede law enforcement proceedings that were either ongoing or foreseeable. Subsequently, the FBI requested permission to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal; Farahi did not object to this request, and the district court granted it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question had been gathered for purposes of law enforcement and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to demonstrate that disclosure could impede ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, because a considerable amount of time had passed, the court required the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding whether enforcement actions remained pending or reasonably foreseeable. Additionally, the court found it lacked sufficient information to rule on the issue of segregability and explained that its reasons for this could not be disclosed on the public docket. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations authored by Joseph Bender, who served as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS at that time. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had communicated with the agent handling Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. More detailed information was included in Bender’s ex parte declaration. Additionally, Bender supplied further clarification in response to the district court’s questions about segregability.

The district court allowed the renewed motion. It relied on the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified that non-exempt information could not reasonably be separated from exempt material. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In Freedom of Information Act litigation, as with all matters, appellate courts conduct de novo review of summary judgment decisions. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The burden rests on the government to demonstrate that an exemption under FOIA is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this requirement, the FBI must establish both that the documents withheld were assembled for law enforcement objectives and that disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given that the FBI’s primary mission is law enforcement, its determination to assert Exemption 7 is accorded deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such judicial deference is especially warranted when the records concern national security. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated,” and this extends in particular to Exemption 7(A) cases requesting access to documents concerning counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the segments that are not exempt are “inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may depend on affidavits from the government that describe with reasonable specificity the reasons why information withheld under a legitimate exemption cannot be separated further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that are "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI functions as a law enforcement organization, its conclusion in this regard is to be given considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI must support its assertions by showing a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” as well as by demonstrating a link between an "individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law." Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's assessment that the FBI met its evidentiary burden in this case. The public Hardy declaration clarified that the documents in question were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration provided extensive details regarding the specifics of the investigation. Additional context further substantiates that claim. Before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI had investigated his connections with one or more organizations labeled by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as with an individual subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court of terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully satisfied that the FBI established that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the requested records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has determined that this language requires the enforcement proceedings in question to be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings," id. at 1097–98, and the government may establish such proceedings merely by confirming their "existence," id. at 1099. "As long as the investigation remains active in collecting evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and the premature release of such evidence would risk undermining that prosecution, Exemption 7(A) remains applicable." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or foreseeably imminent enforcement proceedings. In the public Hardy declaration, it was stated that releasing details about an active investigation could likely interfere with its progress by alerting the investigation’s subjects to its scope, an established rationale in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration also affirmed that the investigation at issue was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte submission provided further information regarding the status of the investigation, while the Bender declaration likewise indicated the investigation was ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time undermines whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He cites our observation in CREW that merely making a “vague” claim that investigations continue may not adequately justify applying Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Additionally, Farahi claims that the Bender declaration, which stated that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was also lacking in specificity. We acknowledge Farahi’s point that, if viewed alone, the Bender declaration appears notably brief. However, considering the full circumstances, it would be inappropriate to analyze the Bender declaration in isolation. As set forth above, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which affirmed that fact, as well as the ex parte Hardy declaration, which described the investigation’s character and extent in detail. The government did not submit the Bender declaration to newly prove that enforcement proceedings were pending, but rather to verify that the situation was unchanged while its original summary judgment motion was being considered. Furthermore, by denying the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had previously determined that the FBI “seemingly” had satisfied its burden to demonstrate enforcement proceedings were pending. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration should reasonably be interpreted as a supplement to the Hardy declarations rather than as an independent submission.

The expansive nature of the FBI’s initial investigation also supports the credibility of its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the details of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we do observe what the FBI stated in public filings during Farahi’s immigration case: Farahi was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The Bureau also stated he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Furthermore, they alleged he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” naming Jose Padilla among them. Id. The FBI also claimed Farahi provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” specifically referencing the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. After evaluating both the FBI’s public claims and the sealed submissions, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” is still in progress. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi briefly contends that the government insufficiently explained its reasoning behind the categorization of documents used to support its withholding decisions. When agencies choose to withhold documents by grouping them into categories, they are required to make those categories “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to the respective groupings. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason why the FBI’s chosen categories should be considered too broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited any arguments related to this point. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may cover records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring the agency to disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission for why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission was adequate to meet the agency’s burden on that issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large amount of responsive documents, there ought to be some information that can be separated and released. However, where small portions of non-exempt content are scattered sporadically “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content typically does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even assuming, as Farahi argues, that isolated terms or brief expressions might be released without jeopardizing the FBI’s interests, this does not alter the outcome. The district court properly found that no significant or useful information could be reasonably segregated from the materials.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed undue weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability review. However, there was no obligation for the court to detail its consideration of the declaration on the public record. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations certainly place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this is a limitation that “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
