Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of documents from the file, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The court instructed the FBI to refresh any outdated declarations it had provided, and subsequently determined that making the sought records public might impede enforcement actions that were either ongoing or reasonably expected. Consequently, the court found that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act mandates that federal agencies provide records to the public upon request, except when a specified statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) specifically protects records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” from disclosure if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our decisions have determined that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” See Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information, agencies are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds of overstaying his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In contesting these requests, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI file under FOIA. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as possibly responsive. After the agency failed to meet the statutory response deadline, Farahi filed suit. Over subsequent years, the FBI examined the potentially responsive materials, released certain records to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once the review concluded, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, submitting a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s declaration included assertions that the withheld materials had been compiled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to impede either ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The FBI later sought permission to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration both under seal and ex parte. Farahi did not object to this motion, which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records at issue were assembled for law enforcement purposes, and that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the considerable lapse of time, the court directed the FBI to reexamine whether such enforcement actions remained ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. Additionally, the court found it required further details regarding segregability, although it could not explain these reasons publicly. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI provided revised declarations and refiled its motion. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS at that time. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent supervising Farahi’s file, who verified that investigations were still in progress. Bender’s ex parte declaration contained additional specifics. He also offered further information to respond to the district court’s issues related to segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. It accepted the FBI’s assertion “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after considering the ex parte submission, the court found that the FBI had sufficiently justified its position that no reasonably segregable non-exempt material existed. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with other cases, we undertake a de novo review of summary judgment decisions. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The obligation rests with the government to demonstrate the applicability of any FOIA exemption. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this requirement, the FBI must establish that the records it withheld were created for the purpose of law enforcement and that their release could reasonably be anticipated to impede enforcement actions that are ongoing or can be reasonably foreseen. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given the FBI’s law enforcement expertise, its choice to invoke exemption 7 is afforded deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when the materials concern national security. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—a principle that applies equally to Exemption 7(A) cases involving counterterrorism investigative records. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Records may be withheld by the government in full "when the nonexempt material cannot reasonably be separated from exempt material," Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). The court may accept affidavits from the government that explain, with sufficient detail, the reasons why documents withheld under a proper exemption cannot be segregated further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records the FBI withheld are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to materials that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given that the FBI functions as a law enforcement body, its assertion regarding this requirement warrants considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI must provide declarations that demonstrate a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” along with evidence of “a connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its burden in this matter. The public declaration by Hardy stated that the withheld documents were gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte declaration provided by Hardy offered substantial specifics regarding the character of that investigation. This assertion is further substantiated by the surrounding context. During the proceedings in district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him due to alleged associations with one or more organizations labeled as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department, as well as his connection to an individual who was later detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are persuaded that the FBI met its burden of establishing that Farahi’s records were compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that disclosure of the documents in question “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has determined that, for this condition to be met, the enforcement proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of Exemption 7(A), ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government may establish such proceedings simply by attesting to their “existence,” id. at 1099. Exemption 7(A) is applicable “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseen enforcement actions. The public Hardy declaration detailed that revealing details about an active investigation might alert targets to the extent of the inquiry, which is a commonly recognized basis for applying Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration affirmed that the investigation in question was “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Further explanation regarding the investigation was provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. The Bender declaration also verified that, as of March 2023, the investigation was still active.

Farahi contends that the mere lapse of time undermines whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could have been reasonably expected. He references our observation in CREW that the “vague nature” of claims asserting ongoing investigations might not be adequate to justify withholding under Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He also asserts that the Bender declaration’s statement that the relevant investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is likewise conclusory. We acknowledge Farahi’s point that, taken alone, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, the broader context indicates that it should not be evaluated on its own. As discussed earlier, the government already demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which detailed the investigation’s character and breadth. The Bender declaration was introduced not to prove pending proceedings anew but simply to verify that the circumstances had not changed during the pendency of the government’s initial summary-judgment motion. Additionally, in its order denying the initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had found that the FBI “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to show pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, it is reasonable to interpret the Bender declaration as a supplemental update to the Hardy declarations, rather than an independent submission.

The wide scope of the FBI’s investigation supports the plausibility of its claim that the investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the specific details of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can reference the information the FBI presented in Farahi’s immigration case: they alleged he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. They asserted he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. They also claimed he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, he was said to support “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Drawing on both the FBI’s public statements and its sealed declarations, we find that the Bureau has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” remains ongoing. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale in establishing the categories of documents it relied on to justify withholding. When an agency elects to withhold records by grouping them into categories, those categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each respective grouping. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not specifically articulate why the various document categories designated by the FBI are overly broad. Therefore, Farahi has forfeited any arguments related to this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to release “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second ruling, the district court stated that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission as to why the protected documents could not reasonably be separated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission was sufficient to meet the agency’s burden concerning segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the substantial number of records that respond to the request, there must be some information capable of segregation and release. However, when only minimal non-exempt material is “randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The underlying principle is that “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even if Farahi is right that select words or phrases from the records could be disclosed with no adverse impact on the FBI’s interests, that does not advance his position. The district court properly determined there was no reasonably segregable, meaningful information.

Farahi also asserts that the district court placed undue weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration in conducting its segregability review. However, there was no obligation for the court to include its assessment of that declaration in the public record. First, Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, and therefore he cannot now contest the court’s reliance on them. Second, although reliance on ex parte declarations can certainly disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, such circumstances are “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
