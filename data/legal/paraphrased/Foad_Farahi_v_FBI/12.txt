Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Appellant Foad Farahi submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of the records, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court ordered the FBI to revise its earlier, possibly outdated, declarations, and then determined that disclosure of the sought-after records might impede enforcement actions that were ongoing or could reasonably be expected to occur. Accordingly, the court ruled that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request unless a specified statutory exemption justifies withholding them. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) protects records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when release of those records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent holds that enforcement proceedings referenced under this exemption must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

In invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold materials, agencies are required to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 due to an overstayed visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security opposed these applications by presenting documents and in-court testimony from an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request seeking his FBI records. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially relevant. When the FBI failed to respond within the statutory timeframe, Farahi initiated legal action. In the years following, the FBI reviewed the potentially relevant materials, producing some records to Farahi and withholding the majority. After concluding its review, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019. The agency provided a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), which stated, among other things, that the records withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with then-ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. The FBI requested permission to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte; Farahi raised no objection, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records in question were assembled for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records could impede ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the extended amount of time that had passed, the court directed the FBI to reassess whether such enforcement proceedings were still current or could be reasonably foreseen. Additionally, the court decided it required further details to resolve the issue of segregability, citing reasons that could not be discussed publicly. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI then filed updated declarations and renewed its summary judgment motion. In this instance, the agency supplied both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, at the time Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Within his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had communicated with the agent responsible for Farahi’s file, who verified that investigations were still ongoing. His ex parte declaration offered additional specifics. Bender further supplemented the record with information meant to address the district court’s segregability concerns.

Subsequently, the district court granted the renewed summary judgment motion. The court accepted the FBI’s verification “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Following its examination of the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently demonstrated why the exempt information could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under FOIA, our review of summary judgment orders is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The onus rests upon the government to demonstrate that a particular FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To satisfy this obligation, the FBI must establish both that the records at issue were assembled for purposes of law enforcement and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to impair either ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. The FBI’s determinations to invoke exemption 7 receive deference because of its law enforcement focus. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This judicial deference is heightened in matters that touch upon national security. Our Court has repeatedly affirmed that, within the FOIA setting, special deference is owed to executive judgment when national security is a concern, including in the context of Exemption 7(A) where records sought involve counterterrorism efforts. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government may properly withhold documents in full “when nonexempt material ‘is inextricably intertwined with exempt material.’” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are permitted to accept government affidavits that explain, with adequate specificity, why materials withheld on the basis of a legitimate exemption cannot be further separated and disclosed. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is restricted to documents that are "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI functions as a law enforcement entity, its assessment in this context warrants considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s statements must demonstrate a logical "nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties," and must also demonstrate a link between the "individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law." Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's conclusion that the FBI satisfied its obligation in this matter. The public declaration by Hardy indicated that the documents withheld had been gathered "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations." J.A. 42. The ex parte declaration from Hardy elaborated extensively on the nature of that specific investigation. This statement is reinforced by additional context. In district court filings, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI conducted an investigation into his connections with organizations identified as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department, as well as links to a person who was later detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted on terrorism-related charges in federal court. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are convinced that the FBI met its burden of establishing that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that the disclosure of withheld documents “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has previously determined that this requirement means that such proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” See CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, active criminal investigations are recognized as “proceedings,” id. at 1097–98, and the government may satisfy its burden by merely attesting to the “existence” of these proceedings, id. at 1099. “[S]o long as the investigation is still collecting evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and if the early release of that evidence would put the case at risk, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that release could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseen enforcement proceedings. The public Hardy declaration stated that releasing information about a pending investigation could foreseeably impede it by alerting targets to the investigation’s scope, which is a commonly accepted rationale in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration also affirmed that the relevant investigation was “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Additional details about the investigation were provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. Furthermore, the Bender declaration corroborated that the investigation was still active as of March 2023.

Farahi asserts that the elapsed time brings into question whether any enforcement proceedings are still ongoing or can still be reasonably expected. He refers to our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” claim regarding ongoing investigations may not adequately justify invoking Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi further maintains that the Bender declaration's assertion that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, when considered alone, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, the surrounding circumstances caution against evaluating the Bender declaration in isolation. As discussed previously, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed such proceedings, as well as the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial details about the nature and breadth of the investigation. The government’s purpose in submitting the Bender declaration was not to independently prove the pendency of enforcement actions, but to affirm that no circumstances had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion awaited resolution. Additionally, in denying the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already found that the FBI “seemingly” met its burden to demonstrate ongoing enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is properly construed as a supplement to the Hardy declarations rather than as a standalone submission.

The expansive nature of the FBI's underlying inquiry lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we cannot reveal the information contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we reference the public allegations the FBI made against Farahi during his immigration case: According to the government, he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. He had associations with “Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. He was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Further, he was alleged to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into consideration both the FBI's public submissions and confidential filings, we determine that the agency has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) to establish the existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection that the government insufficiently justified its method of defining the categories of documents used to support its withholding decisions. When an agency withholds records by category, it must ensure that the categories are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” with respect to each individual category. See CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any particular reason why the FBI’s designated categories were impermissibly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited his argument on this point. See Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may cover records that are protected, the agency is nevertheless obligated to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had submitted a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte filing explaining why segregation was not feasible with regard to the protected records. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this showing was adequate to meet the agency’s evidentiary obligation on this issue. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the substantial number of records involved, there must exist some information that can be both segregated and released. However, where any isolated non-exempt content appears “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” that material generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Accordingly, while Farahi is correct in asserting that certain words or short phrases could theoretically be released without prejudice to the FBI, this argument is ultimately irrelevant. The district court appropriately found that there was no reasonably segregable information of significance.

Farahi also asserts that the district court gave excessive weight to Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, there was no requirement that the court disclose its analysis of that declaration on the public record. Notably, Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, so he is precluded from contesting the court’s use of them. Additionally, even though ex parte declarations undeniably disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, such disadvantage “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
