Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his file maintained by the FBI. The FBI declined to disclose the majority of the documents, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The district court directed the FBI to revise its declarations to address concerns about their timeliness, and subsequently determined that providing the requested documents could hinder law enforcement actions that were ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. As a result, the court found that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to make records available to the public upon request unless a specified exemption applies. U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) protects from disclosure records that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds of a visa overstay. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposing these applications, DHS relied on documentary evidence and the testimony of an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking his FBI file. The agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially responsive materials. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for a response, Farahi initiated litigation. Over several years, the FBI conducted a review of these records, releasing some to Farahi and withholding the majority. After finishing its review, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, supported by a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other points, stated that the withheld documents were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or anticipated law enforcement proceedings. The FBI also sought to file a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not oppose this request, which the district court approved in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the documents in question had been assembled for law enforcement objectives and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, in light of the substantial lapse of time, the court directed the FBI to reassess whether enforcement actions were still pending or reasonably expected. Additionally, the court decided that it required further details to resolve the issue of segregability, which it was unable to discuss in the public record. As a result, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI filed revised declarations and resubmitted its motion. On this occasion, the agency provided both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, who was serving as Acting Section Chief of RIDS at the time. In his public declaration, Bender indicated that RIDS had communicated with the agent in charge of Farahi’s case file, who affirmed that investigations continued to be active. The ex parte declaration from Bender contained additional specifics. Bender also supplemented the record with further information responding to the district court’s segregability concerns.

The district court ultimately granted the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s representation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Upon reviewing the ex parte submission, the court found that the FBI had sufficiently articulated the reasons why non-exempt material could not reasonably be separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In cases arising under FOIA, our review of a summary judgment decision is conducted de novo. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The burden rests with the government to establish the applicability of a FOIA exemption. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). In this instance, the FBI must demonstrate that the records withheld were created for purposes of law enforcement and that their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given the FBI’s expertise in law enforcement matters, its invocation of exemption 7 is afforded deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This level of deference is especially justified when records concerning national security are involved. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including in cases under Exemption 7(A) relating to requests for counterterrorism investigative records. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold records in full when the portions that are not exempt are “inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may rely on affidavits submitted by the government that explain with reasonable specificity why documents withheld under a legitimate exemption cannot be further segregated. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is applicable solely to documents that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Given the FBI’s status as a law enforcement agency, its judgment in this respect is generally accorded significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nonetheless, the FBI’s statements must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” in addition to demonstrating a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its burden in this instance. The public Hardy declaration described that the records at issue were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The sealed Hardy declaration offered significant information regarding the character of the investigation. Furthermore, the wider circumstances support this representation. Before the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI investigated his connections to one or more organizations that the State Department has identified as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as his association with a person who was later held as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Accordingly, we are fully persuaded that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further stipulates that the release of the withheld materials must be such that it "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has previously determined that this requirement means that enforcement proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For these purposes, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings," id. at 1097–98, and the government may satisfy its burden simply by affirming their "existence," id. at 1099. The exemption remains applicable "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly found that releasing the information could reasonably be expected to hinder enforcement proceedings that are either ongoing or reasonably foreseen. The publicly available Hardy declaration stated that releasing details about an active investigation could reasonably be expected to impede it by alerting targets to the extent of the investigation, a rationale broadly recognized in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. In addition, the declaration confirmed that the investigation in question was still "ongoing." J.A. 49. Hardy's ex parte declaration provided further details regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration also confirmed that the investigation was still ongoing as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time brings into question whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could reasonably be anticipated. He relies on our observation in CREW that a “vague nature” in claims that investigations are ongoing may not be sufficient to justify Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Additionally, he argues that the Bender declaration’s assertion that the investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, when viewed alone, the Bender declaration appears notably brief. However, the circumstances caution against evaluating the Bender declaration in a vacuum. As noted earlier, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were pending through the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant detail regarding the investigation’s nature and breadth. The government’s purpose in submitting the Bender declaration was not to independently prove the existence of pending enforcement proceedings, but rather to verify that the status quo persisted during the pendency of its initial summary-judgment motion. Furthermore, in its denial of the government’s first motion for summary judgment without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its obligation to show pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as a supplement to the Hardy declarations, rather than as a separate document.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s initial investigation supports the credibility of its assertion that the inquiry is still active. While we are unable to reveal the information contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we observe that the FBI has made public certain allegations regarding Farahi during his immigration case: he was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. He allegedly had associations with Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other individuals affiliated with Al Qaeda. Id. Further, he was purportedly “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, the FBI claimed that he provided support to “charities that financed terrorism,” specifically citing the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI’s public statements and its sealed submissions, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) to demonstrate the ongoing existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a cursory challenge, asserting that the government insufficiently explained its rationale for the categories of documents it employed in its withholding rationale. When an agency withholds information based on categories, those groupings must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each, as set forth in CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi fails to articulate any concrete reason as to why the FBI’s identified categories were too broad. As a result, Farahi has waived these arguments. See Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request covers records that are exempt from disclosure, the agency is still obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to release “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI, through Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, had provided a “detailed justification” demonstrating why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding segregation. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive records, there must be at least some segregable and releasable content. However, where minor non-exempt information is “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content typically does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As noted, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, although Farahi is right that some isolated words or phrases in the responsive records could theoretically be disclosed without endangering the FBI’s interests, this argument is ultimately immaterial. The district court properly determined that no meaningful information could be reasonably segregated.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed excessive reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, the court was under no obligation to make its evaluation of that declaration a matter of public record. Additionally, because Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s dependence on them. Furthermore, although ex parte declarations can indeed disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
