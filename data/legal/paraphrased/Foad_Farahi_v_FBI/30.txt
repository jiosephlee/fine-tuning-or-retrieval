Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket: No. 23-5243  
Author: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI responded by withholding the majority of these documents, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The court, after directing the FBI to revise its previously submitted declarations due to possible outdatedness, determined that disclosing the requested materials could impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable law enforcement proceedings. Consequently, the court ruled that FOIA Exemption 7(A) shields these records from disclosure. We concur with this decision.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to disclose records to the public when requested, unless a specifically listed statutory exemption applies. U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to materials “compiled for law enforcement purposes” where their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent has determined that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies, when relying on a FOIA exemption to withhold material, are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him on the grounds that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of his immigration case, Farahi has submitted applications for asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, as well as voluntary departure. To contest his eligibility for such relief, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and the in-court testimony of an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a FOIA request to obtain his FBI records. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as containing information that could be relevant. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory timeframe for responding, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the years that followed, the FBI examined the possibly responsive documents, released certain records to Farahi, but withheld the majority. When the review process was completed, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019. In support of this motion, the FBI provided a declaration by David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy's declaration stated, among other assertions, that the records withheld had been compiled in connection with law enforcement activities, and their disclosure could reasonably be expected to disrupt then-pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. Additionally, the FBI sought permission to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not object to this motion, and the district court approved it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records at issue had been assembled for law enforcement purposes, and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might disrupt ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, in light of the lengthy period that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated analysis regarding whether such enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court decided it required further information to rule on the issue of segregability, stating that the rationale could not be provided in a public forum. Consequently, the court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and again moved for relief. On this occasion, the agency presented both public and ex parte statements from Joseph Bender, at that time serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender noted that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi's file, who verified that investigative efforts were still in progress. The ex parte declaration furnished by Bender contained additional specifics. Bender also supplied further details in response to the district court's questions about segregability.

The district court allowed the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s affirmation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. Furthermore, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt information could not reasonably be separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with all others, our standard for reviewing summary judgment is de novo. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government's responsibility to establish that a FOIA exemption is warranted. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). In order to meet this requirement in the present case, the FBI must demonstrate that the documents withheld were assembled for purposes of law enforcement and that their release could reasonably be anticipated to hinder ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. As the FBI is an agency dedicated to law enforcement, its decision to rely on exemption 7 is entitled to a certain degree of deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such judicial deference is particularly warranted with respect to materials involving national security matters. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including, in particular, in cases invoking Exemption 7(A) and seeking information regarding counterterrorism inquiries. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents if the segments that are not exempt “are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Furthermore, “A court may rely on government affidavits that demonstrate with reasonable specificity the reasons why documents withheld under a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the documents withheld by the FBI are covered under Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI functions as a law enforcement body, its conclusion regarding this issue receives considerable deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI's affidavits must demonstrate a reasonable “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” and must also show a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court's finding that the FBI satisfied its evidentiary obligations in this matter. The public Hardy declaration described that the documents withheld were assembled "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations." J.A. 42. Additionally, the ex parte Hardy declaration provided substantial specifics regarding the nature of the investigation in question. Further supporting this, the wider circumstances reinforce this claim. During the district court proceedings, Farahi himself acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him for alleged connections to one or more organizations labeled as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department, as well as for associations with an individual subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court for offenses linked to terrorism. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Therefore, we are convinced that the FBI fulfilled its responsibility to demonstrate that Farahi’s file was created for legitimate law enforcement objectives.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the materials at issue “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedents establish that this standard requires enforcement proceedings to be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” See CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purposes of this exemption, active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” as held in id. at 1097–98, and the government may satisfy its burden by simply affirming the “existence” of such proceedings. Id. at 1099. According to Juarez, “as long as the investigation remains ongoing in its collection of evidence for a prospective criminal action, and releasing that evidence prematurely would threaten such a case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to impede current or foreseeably upcoming enforcement actions. In the public Hardy declaration, it was detailed that sharing details about an ongoing investigation could plausibly disrupt it by alerting the subjects to the investigation’s breadth—a rationale widely recognized in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration affirmed that the relevant inquiry was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Further explanation of the investigation was provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. The Bender declaration also verified that, as of March 2023, the investigation was still active.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time raises questions regarding whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be expected. He relies on our observation in CREW that an agency's “vague” claim of ongoing investigations may not be enough to justify Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Additionally, he asserts that the Bender declaration’s statement that the investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, if considered on its own, the Bender declaration is notably brief. However, the surrounding circumstances caution against reviewing the Bender declaration in isolation. As previously discussed, the government demonstrated the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings through the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which confirmed their pendency, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant details regarding the nature and breadth of the investigation. The Bender declaration was not intended to independently establish the ongoing nature of enforcement proceedings, but rather to verify that the situation had not changed while the government’s initial summary judgment motion was still before the court. Furthermore, when the district court denied the government’s earlier motion for summary judgment without prejudice, it had already found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden to demonstrate pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration should be viewed as a supplemental update to the Hardy declarations, rather than an independent document.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s initial investigation supports the plausibility of its claim that the investigation is still in progress. While we are unable to reveal details from Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we highlight the FBI’s public assertions regarding Farahi made during his immigration case: the FBI described him as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The agency asserted he had connections with “Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. It further stated he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. The FBI also accused him of backing “charities that financed terrorism,” naming the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into account both the FBI’s public statements and sealed submissions, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the ongoing existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi makes a cursory objection that the government failed to sufficiently justify its chosen categorizations of documents in support of its withholding decisions. When an agency withholds records by grouping them into categories, it is required to select groupings that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each group. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason why the document categories identified by the FBI are allegedly too broad. As a result, Farahi has relinquished any challenge on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, agencies remain obligated to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” within Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission for why the protected materials could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission met the agency’s burden regarding segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the substantial quantity of responsive records, there must be some portions that can be separated and released. However, when only a minimal amount of non-exempt content is scattered “line-by-line throughout the document,” such content generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As emphasized, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, although it is true that isolated words or short passages from the requested files could theoretically be released without prejudicing the FBI, this point is ultimately immaterial. The district court correctly determined that no meaningful information could be segregated in a reasonable manner.

Farahi argues that the district court placed excessive weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration in conducting its segregability analysis. However, there was no obligation for the district court to publicly disclose its reasoning concerning that declaration. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s dependence on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations do put FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, such a circumstance “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
