Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his file maintained by the FBI. The FBI withheld the majority of those documents, prompting Farahi to initiate legal action. The district court, after instructing the FBI to provide updated declarations to replace those that might have become outdated, determined that disclosing the sought-after documents could hinder ongoing or reasonably expected enforcement actions. Accordingly, the court ruled that the records fell within the scope of FOIA Exemption 7(A) and were therefore shielded from disclosure. We concur with that conclusion.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request, except where a specific statutory exemption is applicable. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) pertains to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that such enforcement proceedings must either be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

In asserting a FOIA exemption, agencies are required to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 on the grounds that he had overstayed his visa. Throughout the course of these proceedings, Farahi filed applications for asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, and for voluntary departure. In opposition to his requests for relief, DHS offered both documentary evidence and in-court testimony from an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi later made a FOIA request seeking his FBI file. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive materials. After the FBI failed to respond within the statutory timeframe, Farahi filed suit. Over several subsequent years, the FBI reviewed these materials, provided some disclosures to Farahi, and withheld the majority. Once this review concluded, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, supported by a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s declaration, among other things, maintained that the withheld documents had been gathered for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with then-ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. The FBI requested permission to submit a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte, a motion that Farahi did not contest. The district court granted this request in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the documents at issue had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, in light of the considerable time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to reassess whether any enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court held that it required further information to rule on the issue of segregability, which could not be detailed on the public record. As a result, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI provided updated declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. On this occasion, the agency submitted both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was serving as Acting Section Chief of RIDS at that time. In his public statement, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, who affirmed that investigations were still underway. Bender’s ex parte declaration included further specifics. Bender also supplied additional information responding to the district court’s segregability concerns.

The district court granted the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s representation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. After reviewing the ex parte materials, the court also found that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt portions of the records could not reasonably be segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

We conduct a de novo review of summary judgment orders in FOIA cases, as we do in all other contexts. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The responsibility lies with the government to demonstrate that a particular FOIA exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must establish both that the records at issue were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to hinder enforcement actions that are either ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. As the FBI's primary function is law enforcement, its invocation of Exemption 7 warrants deference. See Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is even more fitting where the records concern matters of national security. Our Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—this includes Exemption 7(A) cases regarding records about counterterrorism probes. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

Records may be withheld in full by the government if the portions not subject to exemption are so connected to exempt material that separation is impossible. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). A court is permitted to accept government affidavits that demonstrate, with reasonable specificity, why materials withheld under a proper exemption are not reasonably segregable. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI functions as a law enforcement agency, its judgment regarding this criterion is entitled to substantial deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI's declarations must "demonstrate a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,'" as well as "a link between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI satisfied its evidentiary obligation in this instance. The public Hardy declaration stated that the withheld materials were collected “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration provided significant specifics regarding the nature of that investigation. Additional context further substantiates this representation. During proceedings in the district court, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated his connections to one or more groups identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as his association with an individual who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court for offenses related to terrorism. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully persuaded that the FBI has met its burden of establishing that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that releasing the requested documents “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedents have interpreted this requirement to mean that the proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Ongoing criminal probes qualify as “proceedings” in this context, id. at 1097–98, and the government may satisfy this standard by affirming the mere “existence” of such proceedings, id. at 1099. According to Juarez, “so long as the investigation continues to collect evidence in anticipation of a possible criminal case, and disclosure of such evidence at this stage would endanger that case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court appropriately determined that release of the information could reasonably be expected to disrupt current or foreseeable enforcement actions. The Hardy public declaration set forth that revealing details of an active investigation could be expected to impede it by alerting targets to the investigation’s parameters, which courts have consistently accepted as a sufficient ground under Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration indicated that the investigation was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s in camera declaration offered further specifics about the probe, and the Bender declaration corroborated that the investigation was active as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the length of time involved undermines whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or could be reasonably anticipated. He cites language from our decision in CREW, noting that merely stating that investigations are "ongoing" may be too vague to justify reliance on Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He also asserts that the Bender declaration’s statement that investigations "remain ongoing," J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. While we concur with Farahi that, viewed alone, the Bender declaration appears unusually brief, the surrounding circumstances caution against reviewing it in isolation. As discussed above, the government demonstrated the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which confirmed their existence, as well as the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial detail regarding the investigation’s scope and nature. The Bender declaration was submitted not to newly prove that enforcement proceedings were pending, but simply to verify that no circumstances had changed while the government’s initial summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Furthermore, when the district court denied the initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined that the FBI "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to show pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is best understood as an update to the earlier Hardy declarations, rather than as a self-contained statement.

The expansive nature of the FBI’s original inquiry lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the details of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we highlight the public claims made by the FBI during Farahi’s immigration case: Farahi was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI asserted he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, Farahi was said to be “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” including Jose Padilla. Id. He was further alleged to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” specifically naming the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering both the FBI’s publicly available and sealed statements, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligations under Exemption 7(A), demonstrating the continued existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, asserting that the government failed to sufficiently explain the basis for its chosen categories of documents when justifying its withholdings. When an agency opts to withhold documents by grouping them into categories, those categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each one. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any detailed argument as to why the FBI’s identified categories were excessively broad. Therefore, Farahi has forfeited his arguments on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second ruling, the district court stated that the FBI had supplied a “detailed justification” within Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission explaining why the protected records could not reasonably be segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission fulfilled the agency’s responsibility on the segregation issue. Id.

Farahi argues that, given the large number of responsive records, there should be some information that is both segregable and releasable. However, when only a minimal amount of non-exempt information is “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” it is unlikely to be “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, while Farahi is correct that isolated words or phrases in the requested documents might theoretically be released without compromising the FBI’s interests, this does not alter the analysis. The district court properly determined that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.

Farahi also contends that the district court gave excessive weight to Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability assessment. However, the court was under no obligation to detail its analysis of that declaration on the public docket. Furthermore, Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, and therefore cannot now contest the court’s use of them. Additionally, while ex parte declarations certainly place FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this difficulty “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
