Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket: No. 23-5243  
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his file held by the FBI. The FBI withheld the majority of these documents, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court directed the FBI to refresh any declarations it had provided that might be outdated, and, upon review, determined that disclosing the sought-after records could disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. Accordingly, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this judgment.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public when requested, except where a specific exemption set out by statute applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) permits withholding of records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that these enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption must also undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him because he had overstayed his visa. During different phases of his immigration case, Farahi sought asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture, as well as voluntary departure. In opposing these requests, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi filed a request under FOIA for his FBI file. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs as potentially responsive to his request. After the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for a response, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. Over the following years, the agency reviewed the relevant materials, releasing some records to Farahi while withholding the majority. When its review was concluded, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019. In support of its motion, the agency submitted a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), who stated, among other things, that the withheld documents were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The FBI also moved to file a supplemental declaration from Hardy under seal and ex parte; Farahi did not object, and the district court granted the request in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. It determined that the documents at issue were assembled for law enforcement purposes and found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosing the records could hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, because a considerable amount of time had passed, the court directed the FBI to update its evaluation regarding whether such enforcement proceedings were indeed still pending or could be reasonably anticipated. The court also found that it required additional information to resolve the issue of segregability, although it could not articulate its reasoning in the public record. Consequently, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

Subsequently, the FBI filed updated declarations and renewed its motion. At this juncture, the agency submitted both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public statement, Bender indicated that RIDS had contacted the agent overseeing Farahi’s case file, who affirmed that investigations were ongoing. Bender’s ex parte declaration included further particulars. Additionally, Bender submitted expanded information to address the district court’s prior concerns about segregability.

The district court then granted the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s representation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Upon reviewing the ex parte materials, the court further determined that the FBI had sufficiently explained the reasons why the material subject to exemption could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with other cases, appellate review of a summary judgment is conducted de novo. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government's responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption applies. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must establish both that the contested records were prepared for law enforcement activities and that revealing them could be reasonably anticipated to hinder either ongoing or foreseeable enforcement actions. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given that law enforcement is the FBI’s primary function, its determination to utilize exemption 7 deserves deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is particularly warranted when the records at issue pertain to national security. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including expressly in instances involving Exemption 7(A) and demands for records concerning counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

When nonexempt material “is inextricably intertwined with exempt portions,” the government may withhold documents in their entirety. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). If the government submits affidavits that provide a reasonable and specific explanation as to why the segregable portions of documents are not reasonably separable from the exempt material, the court may rely on those affidavits. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is restricted to materials that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI is an agency responsible for law enforcement, its conclusions regarding this element are to be given significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI must submit declarations demonstrating both a logical “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” and a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met this obligation in this instance. The publicly filed Hardy declaration stated that the information being withheld was gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. Additional specificity concerning the character of the investigation was furnished in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. The surrounding context reinforces this representation: In the district court, Farahi himself acknowledged that he had been investigated by the FBI due to possible connections with one or more groups labeled as foreign terrorist organizations by the State Department and due to connections with an individual who was later held as an enemy combatant and subsequently convicted in federal court for terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We therefore have no reservation in concluding that the FBI satisfied its obligation to establish that Farahi’s file was assembled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that releasing the withheld documents “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This court has determined that the phrase requires that the relevant proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For the purpose of this provision, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” as explained in id. at 1097–98, and the government is permitted to demonstrate the presence of such proceedings merely by affirming their “existence,” id. at 1099. The exemption is satisfied “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence,” per Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court appropriately determined that making the information public could reasonably be expected to hinder enforcement proceedings that are currently pending or reasonably anticipated. According to the public Hardy declaration, disclosure about an ongoing investigation could hamper it by alerting subjects to the nature and progress of the investigation, a rationale widely recognized in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Further, the declaration confirmed that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Details elaborating on the investigation were provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration. Additionally, the Bender declaration verified that the investigation was still in progress as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time raises questions about whether enforcement actions continued to be pending or could still be reasonably anticipated. He relies on our observation in CREW that a generalized claim that investigations are still active might not sufficiently justify the application of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He further argues that the Bender declaration’s statement that the investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is similarly lacking in detail. We acknowledge Farahi’s point that, taken alone, the Bender declaration seems unusually brief. However, it is important to assess the Bender declaration within its larger context. As noted above, the government previously demonstrated the ongoing status of enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their continuation, as well as the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial information regarding the investigation’s character and breadth. The government did not offer the Bender declaration to independently prove the continuance of enforcement actions, but merely to verify that circumstances remained unchanged during the period when its initial summary-judgment motion awaited resolution. Additionally, when the district court denied the government’s original summary judgment motion without prejudice, it had already determined that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its responsibility to demonstrate pending enforcement activities. J.A. 122. Therefore, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as supplementing and updating the Hardy declarations, rather than as a separate piece of evidence.

The expansive scope of the FBI’s investigation also supports the plausibility of its claim that the inquiry is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specifics contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we reference the allegations the FBI made publicly during Farahi’s immigration hearings: that he served as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. That he maintained connections with Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and additional identified Al Qaeda operatives. Id. That he had associations with several convicted or charged terrorists, such as Jose Padilla. Id. And that he gave support to “charities that financed terrorism,” among them the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. After considering both the public and sealed submissions from the FBI, we determine that the agency has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A), establishing that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” persists. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, asserting that the government failed to adequately explain the basis for its chosen document categories used to justify the withholding determinations. When an agency opts to withhold records by grouping them into categories, those groupings must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each individual category. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason as to why the FBI’s designated categories were excessively broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited these arguments. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request covers records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b), which requires agencies to disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record.” In its second order, the district court stated that the FBI had provided a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission regarding the lack of reasonable segregability for the protected records. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission met the agency’s burden as to segregability. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the substantial amount of responsive records, there must be some information capable of segregation and release. However, as explained by precedent, if the non-exempt material consists merely of a small quantity randomly “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content is generally not considered “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). The purpose of FOIA is to provide “information,” not just “words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Accordingly, while Farahi is correct that isolated words or phrases from the records might technically be disclosed without compromising the FBI’s interests, such disclosures are not material. The district court properly found that there was no reasonably segregable meaningful information.

Farahi also contends that the district court placed undue weight on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability assessment. However, the court was not obligated to disclose its reasoning regarding that declaration on the public docket. Moreover, since Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from disputing the court’s reliance on them. Additionally, although ex parte declarations can admittedly disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, such issues are “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
