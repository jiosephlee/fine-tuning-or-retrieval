Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI withheld the majority of the documents contained therein, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The district court, after instructing the FBI to revise its possibly outdated declarations, determined that disclosing the sought-after records might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. Consequently, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this decision.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide records to the public upon request unless a specific statutory exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) protects from disclosure records that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedents establish that enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

When asserting a FOIA exemption to withhold material, agencies are required to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on a visa overstay. Throughout the course of these proceedings, Farahi sought several forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposing these requests, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent, who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a request under FOIA for access to his FBI file. In response, the agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing information that could be responsive. After the FBI failed to respond within the statutory timeframe, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. Over the ensuing years, the agency conducted reviews of the potentially responsive records, releasing some documents to Farahi while withholding the majority. After its review concluded, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, supported by a declaration from David Hardy, who serves as Section Chief for the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s declaration, among other statements, indicated that the records being withheld were created for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or expected enforcement actions. The FBI also moved to submit an additional Hardy declaration both ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not oppose this request, and the district court approved the motion in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the documents in question were assembled for law enforcement purposes and determined that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, because a considerable amount of time had elapsed, the court required the FBI to provide an updated evaluation regarding the existence of pending or anticipated enforcement proceedings. Additionally, the court decided it required further details to evaluate segregability, although the specific reasons could not be disclosed in the public record. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI subsequently filed updated declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who at the time served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender indicated that RIDS had consulted the agent in charge of Farahi’s file, who verified that investigations were still active. The ex parte declaration from Bender supplied further specifics. Bender also addressed the court’s previous concerns about segregability with additional information.

The district court then granted the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s statement “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. Based on its assessment of the ex parte declaration, the court found that the FBI had sufficiently demonstrated that the exempt material could not be reasonably segregated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with all other cases, we assess summary judgment decisions anew, without deference to the lower court. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is incumbent upon the government to establish the applicability of a FOIA exemption. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this obligation, the FBI must demonstrate that the documents being withheld were gathered for purposes of law enforcement and that their release could reasonably be expected to disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. As the FBI is primarily engaged in law enforcement activities, its invocation of exemption 7 merits deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). Such deference is especially warranted when records relate to matters of national security. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—and this includes Exemption 7(A) cases involving records of counterterrorism probes. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold documents in full when the portions not subject to exemption “are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may depend on government declarations that explain with adequate specificity why the materials withheld under a proper exemption cannot be segregated further. Id.

IV

The district court correctly concluded that Exemption 7(A) protects the records withheld by the FBI.

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents "compiled for law enforcement purposes." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI is a law enforcement agency, its assessment on this issue is entitled to substantial deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency's statements must demonstrate a logical “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” and there must also be a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its burden in this case. The publicly filed Hardy declaration stated that the records in question were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration included substantial specifics regarding the investigation’s character. Additional context further supports this representation. In district court proceedings, Farahi himself admitted that the FBI investigated him in connection with organizations identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as for links to an individual who was later detained as an enemy combatant and ultimately convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully satisfied that the FBI has demonstrated that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further stipulates that records may be withheld if their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent establishes that such proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Active criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings” under this standard, id. at 1097–98, and the government can demonstrate the existence of such proceedings simply by certifying that they exist, id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, “so long as the investigation continues to collect evidence for a potential future criminal prosecution, and premature disclosure of that evidence would threaten the case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.”

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be expected to impede ongoing or anticipated enforcement actions. The publicly available Hardy declaration stated that releasing details about an active investigation could reasonably be expected to disrupt the investigation by alerting the subjects to its scope—an explanation commonly accepted in cases involving Exemption 7(A). J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration specified that the investigation at issue was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided additional details regarding the investigation. The Bender declaration further corroborated that, as of March 2023, the investigation continued.

Farahi contends that the lapse of time undermines whether enforcement actions were still pending or could reasonably be expected. He references our observation in CREW that simply asserting investigations are ongoing in a "vague" manner may not adequately justify reliance on Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. He further asserts that the Bender declaration’s claim that the relevant investigations "remain ongoing," J.A. 131, is similarly conclusory. We concur with Farahi that, viewed alone, the Bender declaration appears unusually brief. However, context suggests it should not be evaluated on its own. As previously discussed, the government demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were pending through the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed their existence, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided substantial information about the investigation’s character and breadth. The Bender declaration was submitted by the government not to independently establish the existence of enforcement proceedings, but to verify that no circumstances had changed while the original summary-judgment motion was under consideration. Additionally, when it denied the government’s initial summary-judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to show pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration should reasonably be interpreted as supplementing the Hardy declarations, rather than as an independent statement.

The wide scope of the FBI’s initial investigation lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the details of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we observe the FBI’s public statements regarding Farahi during his immigration case: According to the FBI, he acted as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. He allegedly “was connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Additionally, he “was linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. He is also accused of supporting “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into account both the FBI’s public and confidential submissions, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the continued existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief challenge, contending that the government failed to sufficiently explain the rationale behind the document categories it used in support of its withholding justifications. When an agency opts to withhold records based on categories, it must select categories that are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" to each one. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (quoting Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason why the FBI’s chosen categories were too broad. As a result, Farahi has waived his objections on this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission for its determination that the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission was sufficient to fulfill the agency’s burden on this matter. Id.

Farahi argues that given the large number of responsive records, some segregable and releasable information must exist. However, if minor non-exempt content is “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” such content generally does not qualify as “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, even though Farahi is right that it may be possible to disclose isolated words or phrases from the records without compromising the FBI’s interests, that fact is irrelevant. The district court properly determined that no meaningful, reasonably segregable information was present.

Farahi also argues that the district court gave undue weight to Bender’s ex parte declaration when determining segregability. However, the court was under no obligation to place its consideration of that declaration on the public docket. Additionally, because Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s reliance on them. Furthermore, although ex parte declarations can disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, such challenges “are inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
