Case Name: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Jurisdiction: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Decision Date: September 30, 2025  
Case Number: No. 23-5243  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking his FBI file. The FBI responded by withholding the majority of these documents, leading Farahi to bring suit. The court, after instructing the FBI to refresh any outdated declarations previously provided, determined that disclosing the sought-after records could disrupt enforcement actions that were ongoing or could reasonably be foreseen. As a result, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with this determination.

I

The Freedom of Information Act obligates federal agencies to provide requested records to the public, unless a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) applies to documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that enforcement proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies are required, when invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information, to undertake “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 based on his overstay of a visa. Throughout the course of these immigration proceedings, Farahi has sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, as well as voluntary departure. To contest Farahi’s eligibility for relief, DHS introduced both documentary evidence and in-person testimony from an FBI agent who "documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists." Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI file under FOIA. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might be responsive. When the FBI did not respond within the timeframe mandated by statute, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the ensuing years, the agency reviewed the documents, disclosed a portion to Farahi, and withheld the majority. After concluding its review, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019. To support its motion, it offered a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other assertions, stated that the materials withheld had been collected for law enforcement purposes, and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings either pending at that time or reasonably anticipated. The FBI also requested permission to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte; Farahi raised no objection, and the district court approved the request in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the records at issue had been assembled for law enforcement purposes and found that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure might impede current or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the substantial amount of time that had passed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation as to whether such enforcement proceedings remained ongoing or reasonably likely. Additionally, the court determined it required further details to rule on the question of segregability, and cited reasons it could not disclose in the public record. Accordingly, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was serving as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS at the time. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent in charge of managing Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. The ex parte declaration furnished by Bender contained further specifics. Additionally, Bender supplied additional information to address the district court’s questions regarding segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. It relied on the FBI’s affirmation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. After considering the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why the exempt information could not reasonably be separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA matters, as with other cases, we conduct a de novo review of summary judgment decisions. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The onus is on the government to demonstrate the applicability of a FOIA exemption. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). In the present matter, the FBI is required to establish both that the records withheld were assembled for law enforcement objectives and that releasing them could reasonably be anticipated to impair enforcement actions that are ongoing or reasonably foreseeable. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Because the FBI is a law enforcement agency, its reliance on Exemption 7 is generally afforded deference. See Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted where the records touch upon national security concerns. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including, in particular, in Exemption 7(A) cases requesting records relating to counterterrorism operations. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to fully withhold records when the nonexempt material cannot be separated from the exempt information because they are “inextricably intertwined.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts are allowed to consider government affidavits that provide a reasonably specific explanation as to why materials withheld under a valid exemption are not capable of further segregation. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that are “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Since the FBI operates as a law-enforcement body, its assessment on this issue is generally given significant deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s declarations must demonstrate a logical “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” in addition to showing a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary burden in this instance. The public Hardy declaration detailed that the withheld documents were gathered “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration elaborated extensively on the specifics of the investigation. This is further substantiated by the overall context. Before the district court, Farahi himself admitted that the FBI had investigated him regarding connections to one or more organizations categorized by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, in addition to associations with a person later apprehended as an enemy combatant and ultimately found guilty in federal court on terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We are fully persuaded that the FBI fulfilled its obligation to demonstrate that Farahi’s file was assembled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further necessitates that disclosure of the withheld documents "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This Court has determined that this requirement is satisfied when the proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For these purposes, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” as noted in id. at 1097–98, and the government may establish the existence of such proceedings by simply attesting to their presence, id. at 1099. “As long as the investigation continues collecting evidence for a possible future criminal prosecution, and premature disclosure of that evidence would endanger the potential case, Exemption 7(A) is applicable.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or foreseeable enforcement proceedings. The public declaration from Hardy described how revealing details about an active investigation could plausibly interfere with that investigation by alerting those under scrutiny to its scope—a rationale commonly recognized in Exemption 7(A) matters. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. The declaration further stated that the pertinent investigation was “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s in camera declaration provided additional detail concerning the investigation. Furthermore, the Bender declaration corroborated that, as of March 2023, the investigation was still ongoing.

Farahi contends that the elapsed time raises questions regarding whether enforcement actions were still pending or could reasonably be expected. He cites our observation in CREW that merely “vague” claims that investigations are ongoing may not be sufficient to justify reliance on Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Farahi also argues that the Bender declaration’s statement that the investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, was conclusory in a similar fashion. While we acknowledge that, standing alone, the Bender declaration is notably brief, the proper context cautions against evaluating it independently. As previously noted, the government demonstrated the existence of pending enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration—which asserted their presence—and the ex parte Hardy declaration—which supplied significant detail concerning the nature and breadth of the investigation. The purpose of the Bender declaration was not to reestablish the pendency of proceedings, but rather to affirm that circumstances had not changed during the period when its initial summary-judgment motion awaited resolution. Further, in denying the government’s initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court already recognized that the FBI had “seemingly” met its obligation to demonstrate pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration is best viewed as providing an update to the Hardy declarations, rather than functioning as an isolated filing.

The extensive scope of the FBI's underlying inquiry further supports the plausibility of its claim that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can refer to the FBI's public assertions regarding Farahi during his immigration proceedings: Farahi was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI also alleged that he had “connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Farahi was further said to be “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. He allegedly supported “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. In light of both the FBI’s publicly available and confidential submissions, we determine that the agency has satisfied its evidentiary requirement under Exemption 7(A) to establish the ongoing nature of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (citing Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief objection, contending that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale for establishing the document categories it relied upon to support its withholding decisions. When an agency elects to withhold documents by grouping them into categories, those categories must be defined with enough specificity to permit judicial determination regarding the appropriateness of the particular exemptions claimed for each grouping. See CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any concrete reason as to why the FBI’s categories were overly broad. Therefore, Farahi has waived any arguments concerning this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Although a FOIA request may include records that are protected, the agency is nonetheless obligated to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (an agency is required to disclose “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” explaining why the protected records could not reasonably be segregated, as set forth in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission met the agency’s burden with respect to this requirement. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive documents, some information must exist that could be separated and disclosed. However, when minimal non-exempt content is scattered throughout a document on a “line-by-line” basis, such material generally does not meet the threshold of being “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). This is because, as emphasized in Mead, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, even if Farahi is correct that certain isolated words or short phrases could potentially be disclosed without compromising the FBI’s interests, such a possibility does not carry weight. The district court properly determined that there was no meaningful information that could be reasonably segregated for release.

Lastly, Farahi argues that the district court placed undue emphasis on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability assessment. However, the court was not obligated to publicly detail its evaluation of that declaration. First, because Farahi did not object to the district court’s consideration of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from contesting the court’s reliance on them. Second, although ex parte declarations admittedly disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
