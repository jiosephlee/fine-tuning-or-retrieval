Case Name: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit  
Date: September 30, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 23-5243  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Katsas

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI largely withheld the contents of this file, prompting Farahi to initiate litigation. The court directed the FBI to refresh any potentially outdated declarations it had filed. Upon review, the court determined that disclosing the records in question might compromise enforcement actions that were ongoing or reasonably expected. Consequently, the court found that FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) shielded the records from disclosure. We concur with that determination.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public when requested, unless a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) permits withholding of documents “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if releasing them “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). This Court has determined that such enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable efforts” to “segregate and disclose nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him based on an allegation that he overstayed his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, as well as voluntary departure. The Department of Homeland Security, in contesting his applications for relief, offered both documentary evidence and the testimony of an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi requested his FBI file through a FOIA request. The FBI identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that might contain responsive materials. When the FBI failed to meet the statutory timeline for a response, Farahi initiated litigation. Over several years, the agency reviewed potentially responsive documents, released some records to Farahi, and withheld the majority. After completing this review, the FBI sought summary judgment in October 2019, relying on a declaration provided by David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration, among other representations, stated that the withheld information was assembled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be anticipated to interfere with enforcement proceedings either then ongoing or reasonably expected. The FBI subsequently sought leave to file a supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. Farahi did not object to this request, and the district court approved it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court ruled on the motion for summary judgment. The court determined that the documents in question were assembled for law enforcement objectives and that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden of demonstrating that disclosure could potentially disrupt ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nevertheless, due to the considerable time that had elapsed, the court directed the FBI to provide an updated evaluation as to whether such enforcement proceedings remained ongoing or were still reasonably expected. Additionally, the court found that it required further details to resolve the issue of segregability, though it could not elaborate on its reasoning in the public record. Accordingly, the court denied the summary judgment motion without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations and reasserted its motion. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who at that time served as the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In the declaration accessible to the public, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent managing Farahi's file, who verified that investigations were still underway. The ex parte declaration from Bender contained additional specifics. Furthermore, Bender supplied further details to address the district court's issues about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion. The court accepted the FBI’s affirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing.” J.A. 138–39. After examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified why material subject to exemption could not reasonably be separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

We conduct a de novo review of summary judgment orders in FOIA actions, just as we do in other cases. Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). It is the government's responsibility to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). For the FBI to meet this requirement, it must establish that the materials withheld were gathered for law enforcement objectives and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably prospective enforcement proceedings. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Given that the FBI is primarily a law enforcement entity, its choice to rely on exemption 7 is to be granted deference. Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This judicial deference is especially warranted when the records pertain to national security matters. The Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—this includes Exemption 7(A) disputes involving records concerning counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire records when the nonexempt material cannot be reasonably separated from exempt sections, as they are “inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). Courts may depend on government declarations that explain with sufficient detail why documents withheld under a valid exemption are not capable of further segregation. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the materials the FBI withheld are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is restricted to records that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). As the FBI functions as a law enforcement body, its conclusion regarding this matter is generally accorded substantial deference. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the FBI's affidavits must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” as well as demonstrate a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its evidentiary burden in this case. The publicly filed Hardy declaration stated that the records in question were assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The in camera Hardy declaration elaborated with significant detail regarding the character of the investigation. This claim is also bolstered by the larger context. Farahi, in district court filings, admitted that the FBI had conducted an investigation into his alleged connections with one or more organizations identified by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as with an individual who was subsequently detained as an enemy combatant and, later, found guilty in federal court on terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. We therefore have no hesitation in concluding that the FBI has demonstrated that the records on Farahi were compiled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosure of the documents at issue "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). Our precedent has established that enforcement proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated" under this standard. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. For these purposes, ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings," as explained in id. at 1097–98, and the government may satisfy this element by attesting to the "existence" of such proceedings. Id. at 1099. As stated in Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59, “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and the early release of that evidence would jeopardize that case, Exemption 7(A) remains applicable.”

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or expected enforcement proceedings. The public declaration of Hardy stated that making details about an active investigation public could reasonably be anticipated to compromise it by alerting those under investigation to its scope, which is a commonly recognized rationale in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration affirmed that the investigation in question was “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration offered further details concerning the investigation. The Bender declaration also verified that the investigation was still active as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the mere passage of time undermines the likelihood that enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably foreseeable. He relies on this court’s observation in CREW that the “vague nature” of a claim regarding ongoing investigations may be insufficient to justify reliance on Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Further, he asserts that the Bender declaration was similarly conclusory, merely stating that the pertinent investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131. We concur with Farahi to the extent that, if read alone, the Bender declaration is unexpectedly brief. Nonetheless, it would be inappropriate to evaluate the Bender declaration in isolation, as context makes clear. As detailed above, the government had already demonstrated that enforcement proceedings were pending through the public Hardy declaration, which affirmed the existence of such proceedings, and the ex parte Hardy declaration, which provided significant information about the investigation’s scope and specifics. The purpose of the Bender declaration was not to independently establish pending enforcement proceedings, but to confirm there had been no developments while the initial motion for summary judgment was pending. Additionally, in its denial of the government’s initial summary judgment motion without prejudice, the district court had already determined that the FBI “seemingly” carried its burden to establish the existence of ongoing enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Thus, the Bender declaration should reasonably be viewed as an update to the Hardy declarations, rather than a separate, stand-alone assertion.

The extensive nature of the FBI's underlying inquiry also supports the plausibility of its assertion that the investigation is still active. While we are unable to reveal the material contained in Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can reference the FBI’s public claims regarding Farahi during his immigration case: he was described as “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The FBI stated he was “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. He was further “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” among them Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, he was said to have supported “charities that financed terrorism,” such as the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Taking into account both the public and confidential statements submitted by the FBI, we determine that the FBI has satisfied its obligation under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the continued existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief challenge, asserting that the government insufficiently explained its rationale for establishing the document categories utilized to justify its decisions to withhold records. In circumstances where an agency withholds records using a categorical approach, it is required to define categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each respective group. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not articulate any particular reason as to why the categories selected by the FBI are overly broad. As a result, Farahi has forfeited any argument on this point. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency is nevertheless obligated to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record”). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had offered a “detailed justification” in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission for why the protected records could not be reasonably segregated. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court determined that this submission fulfilled the agency’s burden regarding segregation. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive records, there should be some information capable of being segregated and disclosed. However, if the minimal non-exempt content is only "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," such material is unlikely to qualify as "reasonably segregable." Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Ultimately, "the focus of the FOIA is information, ... not simply words which the Government has written down." Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Thus, although Farahi is correct that isolated words or brief phrases within the documents could potentially be released without harming the FBI’s interests, this does not alter the outcome. The district court properly found that no meaningful information could reasonably be segregated for release.

Farahi also argues that the district court gave excessive weight to Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability determination. However, the court was not obligated to publicly disclose its assessment of the declaration. Additionally, since Farahi did not contest the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, he is precluded from objecting to the court’s reliance on them. Moreover, although the use of ex parte declarations undeniably puts FOIA plaintiffs at a disadvantage, this issue “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
