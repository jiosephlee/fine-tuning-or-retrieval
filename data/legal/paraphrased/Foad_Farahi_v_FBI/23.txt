Title: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: September 30, 2025
Docket: No. 23-5243
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

Foad Farahi, the appellant, submitted a request under the Freedom of Information Act seeking his FBI file. The FBI refused to disclose the majority of the documents contained in the file, leading Farahi to initiate legal action. The court, after directing the FBI to refresh any declarations that might be outdated, determined that making the requested documents public could hinder ongoing or reasonably foreseeable enforcement proceedings. Consequently, the court ruled that these records were shielded from disclosure by Exemption 7(A) of FOIA. We concur with this conclusion.

I

Under the Freedom of Information Act, federal agencies are obligated to provide records to the public upon request, unless a specific statutory exemption applies. See U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). Exemption 7(A) protects from disclosure records that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if releasing such information “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Id. § 552(b)(7)(A). Our precedent establishes that enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated” for this exemption to apply. Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (“CREW”) (citing Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

Agencies invoking a FOIA exemption to withhold information are required to make “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (“Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”).

II

Foad Farahi, a citizen of Iran, has resided in the United States since 1993. The Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings against him in 2002 because he overstayed his visa. Throughout his immigration case, Farahi has pursued several forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection pursuant to the Convention Against Torture, and the option of voluntary departure. In opposition to his requests, DHS submitted both documentary evidence and in-person testimony from an FBI agent who “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” Farahi v. U.S. Att’y Gen., No. 23-10339, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2 (11th Cir. Feb. 8, 2024).

Farahi made a FOIA request seeking his file from the FBI. The agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs that could potentially contain responsive information. Because the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for a response, Farahi initiated a lawsuit. In the years that followed, the FBI reviewed these records, released some material to Farahi, and withheld a significant portion. After finishing its review, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019, supporting the motion with a declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration included assertions that the withheld records had been gathered for law enforcement purposes and that disclosing them could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were ongoing or could reasonably be foreseen. Subsequently, the FBI sought to file an additional Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal; Farahi did not object to this request, and the district court approved it in October 2020.

In November 2022, the district court considered the motion for summary judgment. The court found that the documents were assembled for law enforcement objectives and determined that the FBI had "seemingly" satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that disclosure could disrupt ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement actions. J.A. 121–22. Nonetheless, due to the considerable lapse of time, the court directed the FBI to provide a revised evaluation regarding the status of any enforcement actions as still pending or anticipated. Additionally, the court found that further details were necessary to resolve issues of segregability, but it could not elaborate on these reasons in the public record. Consequently, the court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI filed revised declarations along with a renewed motion. On this occasion, the agency included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who at the time served as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender stated that RIDS had reached out to the agent handling Farahi’s file, who verified that the investigations were still active. The ex parte declaration from Bender included additional specifics. Furthermore, Bender supplied further information specifically addressing the district court's questions about segregability.

The district court approved the renewed motion, giving weight to the FBI's assertion "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing." J.A. 138–39. Additionally, after examining the ex parte submission, the court determined that the FBI had sufficiently justified that the exempt material could not be reasonably separated. Id. at 139–40.

Farahi appealed.

III

In FOIA litigation, as with other matters, our assessment of a summary judgment ruling is conducted de novo. See Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021). The burden falls on the government to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption is applicable. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). To meet this requirement in this case, the FBI is obligated to establish both that the documents withheld were assembled for law enforcement purposes and that revealing these documents could reasonably be expected to hinder enforcement proceedings which are either ongoing or reasonably foreseen. See id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998). This deference is especially warranted when the records at issue raise national security concerns. This Court has “consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated”—including specifically in Exemption 7(A) cases seeking records related to counterterrorism investigations. Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS).

The government is permitted to withhold entire documents when the non-exempt material is so closely connected with the exempt portions that separation is not feasible. See Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (citing Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). In such situations, a court may accept government declarations that detail with sufficient specificity the reasons why documents covered by a legitimate exemption cannot be segregated further. Id.

IV

The district court properly determined that the records withheld by the FBI are covered by Exemption 7(A).

A

Exemption 7(A) is limited to documents that have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The FBI, being a law-enforcement body, is generally owed substantial deference when making this determination. See, e.g., Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32. Nevertheless, the agency’s supporting statements must demonstrate both a logical “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” as well as “a connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)).

We concur with the district court’s conclusion that the FBI met its burden in this matter. The public Hardy declaration clarified that the withheld documents were created “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42. The ex parte Hardy declaration provided substantial specifics regarding the investigation’s character. Contextual evidence supports this position. Farahi acknowledged before the district court that he had been investigated by the FBI for alleged connections to one or more entities designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, in addition to connections with an individual who was ultimately detained as an enemy combatant and subsequently convicted in federal court on terrorism-related offenses. Pl.’s Mem. Opp. Summ. J., ECF Doc. 39, at 3–5. Accordingly, we are convinced that the FBI has satisfied its obligation to demonstrate that Farahi’s records were assembled for law enforcement purposes.

B

Exemption 7(A) further mandates that disclosing the withheld materials "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B). This court has determined that this language means such proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096. Ongoing criminal investigations are considered "proceedings" under this exemption, id. at 1097–98, and the government can establish this by merely affirming their "existence," id. at 1099. Exemption 7(A) is applicable "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence." Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59.

The district court properly determined that releasing the information could reasonably be anticipated to disrupt ongoing or expected enforcement proceedings. The public Hardy declaration detailed that revealing details about a current investigation could alert subjects to the investigation’s scope, thereby potentially compromising the investigation—a rationale frequently upheld in Exemption 7(A) matters. J.A. 49; see CNSS, 331 F.3d at 929. Additionally, the declaration stated that the investigation in question was still “ongoing.” J.A. 49. Hardy’s ex parte declaration provided further information concerning the investigation, and the Bender declaration also verified that the investigation was still underway as of March 2023.

Farahi contends that the amount of time elapsed calls into question whether enforcement proceedings were still ongoing or could reasonably be anticipated. He points to our observation in CREW that an agency's generalized claim of continuing investigations might not be sufficient to justify the application of Exemption 7(A). See 746 F.3d at 1098. Further, he asserts that the Bender declaration, by stating that the investigations “remain ongoing,” J.A. 131, is likewise conclusory. While we concur with Farahi that, taken by itself, the Bender declaration is notably brief, we are reminded by the broader context not to view it in isolation. As discussed earlier, the government demonstrated the existence of ongoing enforcement actions in the publicly filed Hardy declaration, which confirmed their presence, as well as in the ex parte Hardy declaration, which offered significant specifics regarding the investigation’s scope and character. The Bender declaration was presented not to independently establish that enforcement proceedings were pending, but rather to affirm that the circumstances remained unchanged during the period the government’s initial summary judgment motion was before the court. In addition, the district court, when denying the government’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice, had already indicated that the FBI had “seemingly” fulfilled its obligation to prove the existence of pending enforcement proceedings. J.A. 122. Accordingly, the Bender declaration is best interpreted as a status update supplementing the Hardy declarations, rather than a document intended to stand on its own.

The extensive scope of the FBI’s initial investigation lends credibility to its assertion that the investigation is still active. Although we are unable to reveal the specifics of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, we can reference the FBI’s public statements regarding Farahi during his immigration proceedings: according to the FBI, Farahi was “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood.” Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *2. The agency asserted that he maintained “connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members.” Id. Furthermore, the FBI claimed he was “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” such as Jose Padilla. Id. Additionally, Farahi allegedly supported “charities that financed terrorism,” including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. Id. Considering the FBI’s public and sealed statements, we find that the agency has satisfied its responsibility under Exemption 7(A) by demonstrating the existence of a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1097 (quoting Juarez, 518 F.3d at 58).

C

Farahi raises a brief challenge, contending that the government failed to sufficiently explain its rationale for defining the categories of documents used to support its withholding determinations. When an agency elects to withhold records by grouping them into categories, it is required to use classifications that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each group. CREW, 746 F.4th at 1088 (citing Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). However, Farahi does not offer any detailed argument as to why the different categories specified by the FBI are excessively broad. Consequently, Farahi has waived his objections regarding this issue. Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

V

Even when a FOIA request includes records that are protected, the agency remains obligated to make "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also id. § 552(b) (requiring agencies to disclose "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record"). In its second order, the district court noted that the FBI had given a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable, as set out in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission. J.A. 139 (cleaned up). The court further determined that this submission met the agency’s burden on the issue. Id.

Farahi contends that, given the large quantity of responsive documents, there must be some portions that can be separated and disclosed. However, when only small fragments of non-exempt content are scattered throughout the documents—such as isolated lines here and there—those portions typically are not considered “reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; see also Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023). As Mead observed, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” 566 F.2d at 261 n.55. Therefore, even though Farahi may be right that certain isolated words or brief expressions could possibly be released without affecting the FBI’s interests, that fact is not decisive. The district court properly found that no information of significance was capable of reasonable segregation and release.

Farahi also argues that the district court placed undue reliance on Bender’s ex parte declaration during its segregability review. However, there was no obligation for the court to publicly disclose its assessment of that declaration. Additionally, because Farahi did not object to the district court’s use of ex parte declarations, he is not in a position to now contest the court's dependence on them. Moreover, although ex parte declarations may indeed disadvantage FOIA plaintiffs, such difficulty “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up).

VI

For these reasons, we affirm the district court.

So ordered.
