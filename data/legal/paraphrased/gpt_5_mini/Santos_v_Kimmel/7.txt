Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos challenges a final judgment from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the network responsible for airing Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. Santos initiated the lawsuit after Kimmel, employing various pseudonyms, requested custom videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. Santos completed these requests, after which Kimmel broadcasted the videos on JKL in a satirical segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed Santos’s complaint for failure to state a claim pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, finding that the copyright claims were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and that the state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or insufficiently pleaded. We concur with the District Court’s determination that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright claims, and additionally hold that all of Santos’s state law claims do not sufficiently state a claim. Accordingly, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our analysis encompasses "the facts set forth in the complaint, any exhibits attached thereto, as well as documents referenced within the complaint." Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of "copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry." Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a copyright lawsuit, fair use serves as an affirmative defense that "excuses conduct that would otherwise constitute infringement, enabling courts to avoid strict enforcement of the Copyright Act when such enforcement would suppress the creative expression that the Act aims to foster." Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act directs courts to examine a set of non-exhaustive factors when assessing the existence of fair use: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the “purpose and character of the use,” the analysis centers primarily on the extent to which the use transforms the original work—that is, whether the new work simply replaces the original, or introduces something distinct, with a new aim or altered nature. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This emphasis arises from the principle that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. Kimmel contends that the use lacks transformative character, asserting that he “instigated the creation of the original Works” and therefore is the “designer[]” of their initial function. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the purported infringing use aimed “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he claims that this was also his own purpose when creating the videos. However, the question of whether a secondary use is transformative is determined by the impression of a reasonable observer, rather than the subjective intent of the copyright owner or secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As Santos’s own original pleading concedes, a reasonable observer would perceive the videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” not as a display of willingness to utter absurd statements for monetary gain. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations constitute bad faith, thereby negating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not concur. While the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from that position. Moreover, Santos’s own complaint undermines any suggestion that Defendants aimed to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the allegations depict Defendants as being driven by (sarcastic) commentary and criticism. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Therefore, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first fair use factor weighs heavily in favor of finding fair use.

We are similarly unconvinced by Santos’s objections to the District Court’s consideration of the other relevant factors. In particular, the pertinent copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos in question were published on the same date they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Additionally, it is evident from the allegations in Santos’s complaint that Santos has not experienced the type of market harm contemplated by the fourth fair use factor. As explained, “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Because the determination of fair use requires examination only of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” as set forth in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s ruling that Santos’s copyright claims are precluded by fair use, even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court's decision to dismiss his state law claims alleging breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court rejected the first two claims on the basis that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos, however, argues that his claims are "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, we find it unnecessary to decide the preemption issue, as Santos's state contract claims do not sufficiently plead a cause of action under state law.

We begin by considering Santos’s claim of direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that users such as Kimmel are required to accept and which Santos asserts were violated. According to Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service, there exists a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, any suggestion that the contract extends to third parties must be "so strong as to be practically an express declaration." Id. (quotation marks omitted). Santos has not pointed to any language approximating an "express declaration" that the relevant sections of the Terms of Service apply to him or any other nonparty. Moreover, the Terms of Service include provisions with explicit third-party beneficiary clauses, illustrating that when the contracting parties intended certain terms to be enforceable by third parties, they expressly stated so.

Applying New York law to the implied contract claim, we look at "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to determine "[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms." Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, however, Santos’s complaint does not set forth "in nonconclusory language, ... the essential terms of the parties' ... contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated." Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting to plead the crucial terms of the alleged implied contract breached by Defendants). Additionally, Santos does not adequately claim "an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement" that Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos contests the District Court's dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim on the basis that he did not allege any out-of-pocket loss, which New York law requires. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos has not alleged he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as a result of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Consequently, we affirm the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
