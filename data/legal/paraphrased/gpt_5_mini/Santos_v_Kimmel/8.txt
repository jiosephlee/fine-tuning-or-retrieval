Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos brings this appeal from a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims for copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), which broadcasts Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent, The Walt Disney Company. Santos’s action originated after Kimmel, under various aliases, requested personalized videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. Santos completed these requests, and Kimmel subsequently broadcast the resulting videos on JKL in a satirical segment known as “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court granted dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, determining that the fair use doctrine precluded the copyright claims, and that Santos’s state law causes of action were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. Concluding that the District Court correctly held that fair use bars the copyright claims and that none of Santos’s state law claims states a viable claim, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court's dismissal of Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our analysis includes “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached as exhibits to the complaint, and documents that are incorporated by reference within the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a lawsuit concerning copyright, the affirmative defense of fair use “permits conduct that could otherwise be classified as infringing, thereby enabling courts to refrain from strictly enforcing the Copyright Act when such enforcement would suppress the very creativity the Act seeks to encourage.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate several non-exhaustive criteria to assess whether fair use applies: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” element, the primary consideration is how much the new use transforms the original work—that is, whether the subsequent work simply replaces the original’s purpose, or whether it introduces something novel, with a different intent or function. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach recognizes that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). In some cases, fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” even at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. Kimmel contends that the use lacks transformative character because Kimmel allegedly “instigated the creation of the original Works” and is, therefore, the “designer[]” of their initial purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s conclusion that the infringing use’s objective was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he contends that this was his own original intent in producing the videos. However, whether a secondary use is transformative depends on the perception of a reasonable observer, not the personal intent of the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As Santos himself alleged, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than as evidence of a willingness to say absurd things for money. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations constitute bad faith, thereby negating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not find this argument persuasive. While the Supreme Court in Harper & Row remarked that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from that position. Moreover, the allegations in Santos’s complaint do not support any inference that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint suggests that the Defendants were primarily engaged in (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). We therefore concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first fair use factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We likewise find Santos’s objections to the District Court’s evaluation of the remaining factors to be without merit. For instance, the pertinent copyright registrations confirm that the videos in question were published concurrently with their creation date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, the allegations in Santos’s complaint make it apparent that he has not experienced market harm as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, since “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since resolving “the question of fair use” only requires analysis of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” as set forth in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s rejection of his state law claims, which include breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos contends these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and should not be preempted, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, we need not address the preemption question since Santos’s contract claims under state law do not adequately state a claim for relief.

Turning first to the direct breach of contract claim based on the Cameo Terms of Service, Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that users such as Kimmel must accept and which Santos asserts were violated. Illinois law, which applies to these Terms, recognizes a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). The requirement is that “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, Santos points to no language remotely approaching an “express declaration” indicating that he or any other noncontracting individual is covered by the pertinent sections of the Terms of Service. In fact, the Terms of Service contain explicit third-party beneficiary provisions elsewhere, which shows that the parties expressly allowed third-party enforcement where intended.

Regarding the claim for breach of implied contract, which is governed by New York law, we must look at “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to ascertain “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, Santos’s complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the essential terms of the alleged implied contract that Defendants purportedly violated). Moreover, Santos does not plausibly claim that there existed “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” for Kimmel to follow the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

With respect to Santos’s challenge to the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim for not alleging any out-of-pocket loss, as mandated under New York law, see Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), we concur with the District Court that Santos did not allege any concrete out-of-pocket loss as a result of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we affirm the District Court’s dismissal of that claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
