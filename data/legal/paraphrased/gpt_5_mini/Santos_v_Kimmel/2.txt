Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos appeals the final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law brought against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. The underlying dispute originated when Kimmel, under a variety of pseudonyms, submitted requests for personalized videos from Santos via the Cameo service. After Santos produced and delivered the requested videos, Kimmel broadcast them on JKL in a satirical segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), holding that the copyright claims were foreclosed by the fair use doctrine and that the state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise insufficient. Finding ourselves in agreement that the fair use doctrine precludes Santos’s copyright claims, and additionally determining that all of Santos’s state law claims do not meet the requirements to state a claim, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

Our review of the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) is conducted de novo. See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). In our assessment, we examine “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached as exhibits to the complaint, and documents that the complaint incorporates by reference.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, fair use serves as an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate whether fair use applies by considering the following non-exhaustive criteria: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use,” the analysis centers on the extent to which the use can be considered transformative—specifically, whether the secondary work merely replaces the original’s function or instead contributes new expression, meaning, or message. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This emphasis is based on the principle that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Moreover, a complaint may so clearly establish fair use that a copyright infringement claim can be dismissed at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. asserts that the use lacks transformative character because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and, thus, is the “designer[]” of their intended purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the purported infringing use aimed “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, his contention is that his own intention in creating the videos matched this alleged purpose. However, whether a use qualifies as transformative is determined by the perspective of a reasonable observer, rather than the subjective intentions of the original copyright owner or secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s initial pleading, a reasonable observer would perceive the videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than as expressing a willingness to make absurd statements for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s misrepresentations constitute bad faith, thereby negating any fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are not persuaded by this argument. While it is accurate that the Supreme Court observed in Harper & Row that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from this position. Regardless, Santos’s own complaint undercuts any allegation that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the relevant videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Rather, the allegations depict Defendants as being driven by (sarcastic) commentary and criticism. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). We therefore concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of a fair use determination.

We likewise find no merit in Santos’s objections to the District Court’s evaluation of the other relevant factors. The applicable copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos were published on the same date as their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, Santos’s complaint clearly indicates there has been no market harm as understood under the fourth fair use factor, since “the inquiry is not whether the second work harms the market for the first (for instance, by diminishing its value through parody or criticism), but whether it supplants the original’s market by providing a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since it is sufficient to review “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” in order to resolve “the question of fair use,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further appeals the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court determined that the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos contends that his claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and thus should not be preempted. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, we need not decide this preemption issue because Santos’s state law contract claims do not satisfy the requirements for stating a claim under state law.

We turn first to Santos’s direct breach of contract claim under the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service, which users like Kimmel are required to accept and which Santos claims were breached. Illinois law, governing the Terms of Service, imposes a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). For a contract to benefit third parties, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, Santos points to no language that constitutes anything close to an “express declaration” that would extend the relevant Terms of Service provisions to him or other third parties. In fact, certain sections of the Terms of Service do expressly reference third-party beneficiaries, signaling that when the agreement’s parties intended to permit enforcement by nonparties, they did so unambiguously.

For his breach of implied contract claim, governed by New York law, the analysis focuses on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to assess whether “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos’s pleading does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (failing to identify the essential terms of the supposed implied contract that Defendants are alleged to have violated). Further, Santos does not sufficiently allege facts to support “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would be bound by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos contests the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim, which was based on the failure to allege any actual out-of-pocket damages as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not allege that he incurred any real out-of-pocket loss due to the alleged fraudulent conduct. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
