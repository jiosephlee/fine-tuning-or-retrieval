Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos appeals the entry of final judgment by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his copyright infringement and state law claims brought against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent corporation, The Walt Disney Company. Santos initiated this action after Kimmel, utilizing multiple pseudonyms, requested personalized videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. Santos completed these requests, and the resulting videos were subsequently broadcast on JKL as part of a satirical segment entitled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court granted dismissal of the complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), holding that Santos’s copyright claims were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and finding that the state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise insufficient. We concur with the district court that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright claims, and additionally determine that each of the state law claims does not state a valid claim. Accordingly, we affirm.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court's decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts presented in the complaint, any exhibits appended to the complaint, and documents that are referenced within the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as recorded in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In the context of a copyright lawsuit, fair use operates as an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). To assess whether the defense of fair use applies, the Copyright Act directs courts to weigh several non-exhaustive factors: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use,” the principal inquiry is into how transformative the use is; that is, whether the subsequent work merely replaces the aim of the original, or if it introduces something new, offering an additional purpose or a distinct character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach acknowledges that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). In certain instances, fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” even at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. Kimmel asserts that the use was not transformative on the grounds that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and is therefore the “designer[]” of the work’s initial purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s determination that the purpose behind the accused use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, Santos contends that this also matched his own intended objective in producing the videos. However, the analysis of whether a secondary use is transformative is based on the perspective of a reasonable observer, not the copyright owner’s or secondary user’s subjective motives. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s initial pleading, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than as demonstrating a willingness to make absurd statements for monetary gain. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby undermining the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not share this view. While the Supreme Court did note in Harper & Row that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently pulled back from this position. Moreover, the complaint as pled by Santos does not support the assertion that Defendants aimed to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Rather, the allegations portray the defendants as engaging in (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court's conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of finding fair use.

We likewise find Santos’s objections to the District Court’s treatment of the remaining factors unconvincing. Notably, the pertinent copyright registrations indicate that the videos were made public on the same date they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, from the allegations in Santos’s own complaint, it is evident that Santos has not sustained market injury as defined by the fourth factor of fair use, since “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (internal quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Because only “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” must be reviewed in determining “the question of fair use,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we affirm the District Court’s holding that the doctrine of fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims at the pleading stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further appeals the District Court’s rejection of his state law claims involving breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court dismissed the first two of these claims, reasoning that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos contends that his contract claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and thus fall outside the scope of preemption, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). We do not need to decide the preemption question, as Santos’s state law contract claims independently fail to state a viable claim under state law.

Addressing first the direct breach of contract claim based on the Cameo Terms of Service, we note that Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that users, such as Kimmel, are required to accept and which Santos contends were breached. Illinois law, which applies to the Terms of Service, establishes a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). To overcome this presumption, there must be an “express declaration” or language in the contract making clear it extends to third parties. Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, Santos has not identified anything resembling an explicit statement that the relevant Terms of Service provisions cover him or other nonparties. In fact, certain sections of the Terms of Service do include language specifying third-party beneficiaries, demonstrating that the contracting parties expressly extended rights to third parties when they intended to do so.

With respect to the breach of implied contract claim governed by New York law, the inquiry focuses on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to establish “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this case, Santos’s pleading does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting to set forth the crucial terms of the supposed implied contract allegedly breached by Defendants). Furthermore, Santos does not plausibly assert there was “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would abide by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim on the basis that he failed to allege any out-of-pocket loss, as mandated under New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos did not plead an actual, out-of-pocket loss as a result of the claimed fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we affirm the District Court’s decision dismissing that claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
