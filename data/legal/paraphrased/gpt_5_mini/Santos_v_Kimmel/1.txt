Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos brings this appeal challenging a final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel; American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the network that broadcasts Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”); and ABC’s parent corporation, The Walt Disney Company. Santos’s action stemmed from a series of incidents in which Kimmel, operating under aliases, requested and obtained custom videos from Santos via the Cameo platform, which Santos subsequently recorded. Kimmel later featured these videos on JKL in a recurring segment called “Will Santos Say It?”, in which the videos were ridiculed. The District Court granted dismissal pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, holding that the fair use doctrine shielded defendants from Santos’s copyright claims and finding that his state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise deficient. Concluding that Santos’s copyright claims are indeed foreclosed by fair use and that none of his state law causes of action are adequately pled, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts set forth in the complaint, any documents appended to the complaint as exhibits, as well as documents that are incorporated by reference therein.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations as reflected in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In cases involving copyright, fair use serves as an affirmative defense that “excuses conduct that might otherwise constitute infringement, thereby permitting courts to avoid a strict application of the Copyright Act when doing so would suppress the creative output the Act seeks to encourage.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, the determination of fair use is guided by several non-exclusive factors: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” element, our primary inquiry is whether the use is transformative, meaning whether the new work simply replaces the aims of the original, or instead introduces something novel, serving a further purpose or possessing a distinct character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach recognizes that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). In certain instances, the complaint may so clearly establish fair use that a copyright infringement claim may be dismissed at the pleading stage. See TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. argues that the use is not transformative on the ground that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and is therefore the “designer[]” of their original function. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the alleged infringing use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, he contends that this was also his intent in creating the videos. However, whether a secondary use qualifies as transformative depends on the perspective of a reasonable observer, not the subjective intent of either the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As acknowledged in Santos’s initial allegation, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than a readiness to make absurd statements for monetary gain. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations constitute bad faith, thereby invalidating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not find this argument persuasive. While the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), subsequent decisions have moved away from a strict application of that principle. Moreover, the allegations in Santos’s complaint undermine any suggestion that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the works. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Rather, the complaint describes the Defendants’ actions as motivated by (albeit sarcastic) critique and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We also remain unconvinced by Santos’s objections to the District Court’s consideration of the other fair use factors. Specifically, the pertinent copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos in question were published simultaneously with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, it is evident from the face of Santos’s own complaint that there is no cognizable market harm as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor. The inquiry is not whether the secondary work harms the value of the original through criticism or parody, but rather whether it displaces the market for the original by serving as a direct substitute. Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Because “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” alone are sufficient to resolve “the question of fair use,” see Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims at the pleading stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims, which included breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court had ruled that the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos, however, contends that his claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and should therefore not be preempted, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, we find it unnecessary to resolve the preemption question, as Santos’s state law contract claims do not sufficiently state a claim for relief under applicable state law.

Turning first to the direct breach of contract claim under the Cameo Terms of Service, Santos lacks standing to enforce these terms because he is not a party to the agreement users such as Kimmel must accept, which Santos asserts was breached. Under Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service, there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, any implication that a contract applies to nonparties “must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, Santos fails to point to anything resembling an “express declaration” that would extend relevant provisions of the Terms of Service to him or to other third parties. In fact, the Terms of Service include some provisions that expressly address third-party beneficiaries, making clear that the parties knew how to include such language when desired.

Applying New York law to this claim, we look to “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to determine whether “an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, however, the complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting essential terms of the supposed implied contract allegedly breached by Defendants). Additionally, Santos does not plausibly plead facts suggesting “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos takes issue with the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim, which was rejected due to his failure to plead an out-of-pocket loss as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not allege he incurred any actual out-of-pocket loss as a result of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we affirm the District Court’s dismissal of that claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
