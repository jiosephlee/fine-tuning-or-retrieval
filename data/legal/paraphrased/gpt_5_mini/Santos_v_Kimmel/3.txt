Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos brings this appeal from a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims for copyright infringement and state law violations against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. Santos’s lawsuit stemmed from events in which Kimmel, acting under multiple pseudonyms, requested custom videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. After Santos provided each requested video, Kimmel broadcast the recordings on JKL as part of a satirical segment known as “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the action for failure to state a claim pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, holding that the copyright allegations were precluded by the fair use doctrine and that Santos’s state law claims were either superseded by the Copyright Act or otherwise insufficient. Finding that the District Court correctly determined Santos’s copyright claims to be precluded by fair use and that none of the state law claims were adequately stated, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our analysis encompasses “the facts stated in the complaint, any exhibits attached thereto, and documents that are incorporated into the complaint by reference.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a lawsuit involving copyright, fair use operates as an affirmative defense that “excuses conduct which might otherwise qualify as infringement, enabling courts to prevent strict enforcement of the Copyright Act when such enforcement would inhibit the very creativity the Act aims to encourage.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate several non-exhaustive factors to assess the applicability of fair use: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” factor, our primary consideration is the extent to which the use transforms the original, specifically whether the new work simply replaces the function of the original or instead contributes something novel, serving a distinct purpose or taking on a new character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach is rooted in the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). On occasion, fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. argues that the use was not transformative on the grounds that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and, therefore, is the “designer[]” of the original works’ intended purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the allegedly infringing use was aimed at “comment[ing] on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he contends that this also reflects his own purpose in creating the videos. However, whether a secondary use is transformative is determined from the perspective of a reasonable observer, not by the subjective intention of the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As Santos himself admits in his initial allegation, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than as evidence of a willingness to say absurd things for financial compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s misrepresentations exhibit bad faith, thereby precluding reliance on the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are unpersuaded by this argument. While the Supreme Court did note in Harper & Row that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from this position. Regardless, Santos’s own complaint is inconsistent with any assertion that Defendants aimed to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the allegations suggest that defendants were engaged in (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We also find no merit in Santos’s objections to the District Court’s consideration of the other relevant factors. Specifically, the pertinent copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos in question were published on the same dates they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, it is evident from the face of Santos’s complaint that Santos did not incur market harm as understood under the fourth factor of fair use, since “[t]he inquiry is not whether the secondary work would injure the original’s market (such as by diminishing its value through parody or criticism), but whether it supplants the market for the original by serving as a direct substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he slim chance that authors of creative works would license critical reviews or parodies of their productions means these uses fall outside the idea of a potential licensing market.”).

Given that “the question of fair use” can be resolved by considering “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” as held in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that the fair use doctrine precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos additionally contests the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims alleging breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found the first two of these claims to be preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos contends that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and should therefore not be preempted. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, we find it unnecessary to address the preemption argument because Santos’s state law contract claims do not, in any event, sufficiently allege a viable claim under state law.

Turning first to the direct breach of contract claim based on the Cameo Terms of Service, Santos is not a party to these Terms of Service, which are required to be accepted by users such as Kimmel and which Santos alleges have been breached. Illinois law, the governing law for the Terms of Service, presumes strongly against granting benefits to non-parties to a contract. Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, Santos does not identify any “express declaration” that would suggest the applicable provisions of the Terms of Service are enforceable by him or other non-parties. On the contrary, certain sections of the Terms of Service explicitly include third-party beneficiary provisions, demonstrating that the parties included express language where third-party enforcement was intended.

For the breach of implied contract claim, governed by New York law, the pertinent inquiry is “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” in order to determine “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, however, Santos’s complaint does not set forth, in nonconclusory terms, “the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (failing to allege the material terms of the alleged implied contract the Defendants purportedly violated). Moreover, Santos fails to plausibly plead the existence of “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would abide by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos contests the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim on the grounds that he did not allege any out-of-pocket loss as required under New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos did not plead that he incurred any actual out-of-pocket loss as a consequence of the alleged fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
