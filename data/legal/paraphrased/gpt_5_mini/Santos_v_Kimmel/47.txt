Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, brings this appeal from a final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his claims for copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel; American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the network that broadcasts Kimmel’s late-night program, Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”); and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. The underlying action was prompted after Kimmel, utilizing several pseudonymous identities, submitted requests to Santos for personalized recordings via the Cameo platform. Santos complied and provided each video, which Kimmel later incorporated into televised comedic segments on JKL entitled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the action pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, finding Santos’s copyright allegations precluded by the fair use doctrine, and determining that the state law claims were either superseded by the Copyright Act or were insufficiently pleaded. Since we concur that Santos’s copyright allegations are preempted by fair use, and further find each of the state law claims inadequately stated, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our analysis encompasses "the facts stated in the complaint, any documents annexed as exhibits, and materials that are incorporated by reference." Id. at 111. Furthermore, we may take judicial notice of "copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry." Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a copyright lawsuit, the fair use affirmative defense “excuses conduct that might otherwise amount to infringement, enabling courts to forgo strict enforcement of the Copyright Act in instances where such enforcement would impede the very creativity the statute seeks to encourage.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate several non-exhaustive factors to determine whether the fair use doctrine applies: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the “purpose and character of the use” prong, the main inquiry is the extent to which the use is transformative—specifically, whether the new work simply substitutes for the original or, instead, adds novel elements, serving a different aim or possessing a different nature. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This analysis recognizes that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). In some cases, this can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” on the pleadings. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. Kimmel asserts that the use is not transformative, arguing that he “instigated the creation of the original Works” and therefore is the “designer[]” of their intended purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos, for his part, does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the accused use aimed “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, he contends that this objective aligned with his own when he created the videos. Nevertheless, the determination of whether a secondary use is transformative hinges on the perception of a reasonable observer, rather than the subjective intent of either the copyright owner or the user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As Santos’s initial complaint concedes, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” as opposed to signaling a propensity to utter absurd statements for monetary compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s allegedly false statements are evidence of bad faith, which would negate the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are unpersuaded by this argument. While the Supreme Court did note in Harper & Row that “‘[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,’” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), subsequent decisions from the Court have moved away from that pronouncement. Regardless, Santos’s own complaint undercuts any assertion that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the original videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the complaint characterizes the defendants as being driven by (sarcastic) critique and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court that the first statutory fair use factor weighs decisively in favor of fair use.

We remain unconvinced by Santos’s objections to the District Court’s treatment of the other relevant factors. Notably, the applicable copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos were published simultaneously with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, the allegations in Santos’s complaint make it apparent that Santos has not experienced market harm as described under the fourth prong of fair use, since “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

As our analysis of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” suffices for resolving “the question of fair use,” see Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (internal quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court's decision to dismiss his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the first two of these claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos contends that the contract claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore do not fall within the scope of preemption. See Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, we do not need to address the preemption issue here, since Santos's contract-based claims under state law fail to adequately state a basis for relief as a matter of state law.

We begin by considering Santos’s claim for direct breach of contract under the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself is not a signatory to the Terms of Service agreed to by users such as Kimmel, which Santos contends have been violated. Illinois law, which is controlling over the Terms of Service, provides a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos has not identified anything resembling an “express declaration” that would indicate the relevant terms of the agreement extend to him or other third parties not party to the contract. Furthermore, certain sections of the Terms of Service expressly contain third-party beneficiary provisions, revealing that the contracting parties included such language only when they intended specific terms to be enforceable by outsiders. When analyzing this claim under New York law, we examine “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to determine “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, however, Santos’s complaint fails to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the necessary terms of the purported implied contract at issue). Additionally, Santos does not plausibly claim that “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” existed that required Kimmel to comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos further contests the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim, asserting error in the court’s finding that he did not plead any out-of-pocket loss, a necessity under New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court's determination that Santos did not allege any tangible out-of-pocket loss arising from the alleged fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of the claim.

CONCLUSION

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by Santos and find them unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
