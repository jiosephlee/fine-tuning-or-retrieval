Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos challenges a final judgment from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”)—the broadcaster of Kimmel’s show Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”)—and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. Santos’s lawsuit stemmed from Kimmel, under several assumed identities, requesting personalized videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. Santos complied with these requests, and Kimmel proceeded to broadcast the resulting videos on JKL in a satirical segment called “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court granted dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, determining that the copyright claims were defeated by the fair use doctrine and that Santos’s state law causes of action were either preempted by the Copyright Act or insufficiently pled. We concur that the fair use doctrine precludes Santos’s copyright claims and also find that each of his state law claims fails to state a claim; accordingly, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts stated in the complaint, materials appended to the complaint as exhibits, and documents that are referenced within the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we may properly take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In the context of a copyright lawsuit, fair use serves as an affirmative defense that “excuses conduct which might otherwise qualify as infringement, thereby enabling courts to exercise flexibility under the Copyright Act in circumstances where strict enforcement would suppress the very creativity the statute aims to foster.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act instructs us to evaluate several non-exhaustive considerations in assessing fair use: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” prong, the primary consideration is how much the use is transformative—that is, whether the new work simply replaces the original’s objectives, or whether it introduces new expression, meaning, or message. Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This emphasis is grounded in the notion that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (internal quotation marks omitted). A complaint can set forth facts making fair use “so clearly established . . . as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends the use was not transformative because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and thus should be regarded as their “designer[]” with respect to their initial purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the purpose of the alleged infringing use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he asserts that this was also the objective behind the creation of the videos. However, the question of whether a secondary use is transformative depends on the perspective of a reasonable observer, not on the personal motivation of the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s own earlier statements, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” not a willingness to say absurd things for monetary compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby negating the applicability of the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not accept this argument. Although the Supreme Court in Harper & Row stated that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from this position. Moreover, the allegations in Santos’s complaint are inconsistent with any assertion that Defendants acted with the intent to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the complaint portrays defendants as being driven by (sarcastic) commentary and criticism. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). We therefore concur with the District Court that the first fair use factor weighs heavily in favor of a fair use determination.

We also find no merit in Santos’s objections to the District Court’s treatment of the other relevant factors. The applicable copyright registrations indicate that the videos were published concurrently with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Additionally, the complaint itself demonstrates that Santos did not incur market harm as contemplated under the fourth fair use factor, since the inquiry is not whether the subsequent work harms the original’s market by diminishing its value (such as through parody or critique), but rather whether it supplants the original work’s market by serving as a direct competitor. Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Because our analysis need only focus on “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” when addressing “the question of fair use,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that the fair use doctrine precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at the pleading phase.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s dismissal of his state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos, however, argues that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and should therefore not be preempted, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, we do not reach the preemption question because Santos’s contract claims under state law do not state a claim upon which relief can be granted.

Turning first to Santos’s breach of contract allegation regarding the Cameo Terms of Service, Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that users such as Kimmel must accept, nor does he assert that these terms were breached in a way that binds him. Under Illinois law, which applies to these Terms of Service, there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, Santos fails to identify anything resembling an “express declaration” granting him, or other nonparties, rights under the relevant sections of the Terms of Service. Other sections of the Terms of Service do contain language specifically identifying third-party beneficiaries, suggesting that the contracting parties only intended to provide such rights where explicitly stated.

Under New York law, which governs the implied contract claim, courts examine “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to determine “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, however, Santos’s complaint fails to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the essential terms of the claimed implied contract Defendants are alleged to have breached). Additionally, Santos does not plausibly assert facts showing “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel was obliged to comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also appeals the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim, which was denied due to his failure to allege any out-of-pocket loss as New York law requires. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos has not pleaded any actual out-of-pocket loss as a consequence of the alleged fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Therefore, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
