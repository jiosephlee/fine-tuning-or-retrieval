Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former Congressman, challenges the entry of final judgment by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims alleging copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”)—the network responsible for airing Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”)—and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. The underlying action stemmed from Kimmel, under several aliases, submitting requests through the Cameo service for customized video messages from Santos. After Santos completed the video requests, Kimmel broadcasted them on JKL in a satirical segment known as “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court, invoking Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, dismissed Santos’s complaint for failure to present a cognizable claim, determining the copyright counts were barred by the doctrine of fair use, and finding that the state law claims were either preempted under the Copyright Act or otherwise failed to assert a viable claim. Concluding likewise that Santos’s copyright claims are precluded by fair use and each of his state law counts fails to state a claim, we affirm the District Court’s decision.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s order dismissing Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our assessment encompasses "the factual allegations presented in the complaint, any documents annexed as exhibits thereto, and materials that are referenced within the complaint." Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, the fair use affirmative defense “excuses conduct that could otherwise qualify as infringement, permitting courts to prevent a strict interpretation of the Copyright Act when such application would suppress the creativity the Act seeks to foster.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate several non-exhaustive considerations to ascertain whether the fair use defense applies: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use,” the primary focus is on how transformative the use is—that is, whether the new work simply replaces the original’s objectives or instead brings a novel element, serving a new goal or possessing a distinct character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This emphasis is based on the principle that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” on a motion to dismiss. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. argues that the use cannot be transformative because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and therefore is the “designer[]” of their original intent. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s determination that the allegedly infringing use’s objective was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, his contention is that this was likewise his own original purpose when making the videos. Nonetheless, whether a use is transformative is not decided by the subjective intentions of the copyright owner or the secondary user but is instead based on the perspective of a reasonable observer. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s own initial pleading, a reasonable observer would perceive that the videos expressed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than an inclination to say absurd things in exchange for money. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s intentional misrepresentations indicate bad faith, which would invalidate the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are unpersuaded by this argument. While the Supreme Court did note in Harper & Row that "[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing," id. at 562 (internal quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently moderated this position. Regardless, the allegations set forth in Santos’s complaint are inconsistent with any assertion that Defendants intended to "supplant" Santos’s "commercially valuable right" in the original videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the complaint suggests that defendants acted from a motive of (sarcastic) critique and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of a fair use determination.

We likewise find no merit in Santos’s objections regarding the District Court’s evaluation of the other relevant factors. The pertinent copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos were published contemporaneously with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Additionally, it is apparent from the face of Santos’s complaint that there is no plausible allegation of market harm as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, since “the inquiry is not whether the secondary work might cause damage to the market for the original (such as by diminishing its value through parody or criticism), but rather whether it appropriates the market for the original by serving as a competing alternative.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he improbability that creators of imaginative works would license the critical commentary or parody of their own material eliminates these uses from the concept of a prospective licensing market.”).

Since we are required to examine solely “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” when determining “the question of fair use,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that the doctrine of fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, including at the stage of the pleadings.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims alleging breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos asserts that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, we need not address the preemption question because the state law contract claims, as pleaded, do not adequately state grounds for relief under state law.

We first consider Santos’s claim for direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself is not a signatory to these Terms of Service, which are binding on users such as Kimmel and are the contract Santos claims was violated. Under Illinois law, which governs these Terms, there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, for a contract to benefit third parties, the implication must be so clear that it is essentially an “express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos does not point to anything approaching an “express declaration” showing that the provisions of the Terms of Service were meant to cover him or other parties outside the agreement. In fact, certain other sections of the Terms of Service do explicitly reference third-party beneficiaries, which signals that the parties to the contract intended to permit enforcement by third parties only when they made that intention explicit.

Regarding the relevant law of New York, which applies to this claim, the analysis focuses on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to resolve “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, however, Santos’s complaint fails to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (failing to plead the basic elements of the alleged implied contract the Defendants purportedly breached). Additionally, Santos does not sufficiently claim that there was “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that would bind Kimmel to the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also disputes the District Court’s decision to dismiss his fraudulent inducement claim on the basis that he did not plead any out-of-pocket loss, as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos did not allege that he incurred any actual out-of-pocket damages resulting from the supposed fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos’s additional arguments, we find them unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
