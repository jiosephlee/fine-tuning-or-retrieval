Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced indictment for securities fraud due to their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. According to the superseding indictment, they persuaded their social-media followers to buy securities by posting false information regarding their trading positions and the expected price of certain securities, thus artificially boosting those securities' prices for their own profit. The district court dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it did not adequately allege an offense, reasoning that it accused the defendants only of attempting to withhold potentially valuable economic information, not a conventional property interest. However, finding that the indictment properly alleges both a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government asserts that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight were involved in a securities “pump and dump” operation. Each defendant maintained substantial social media followings on multiple platforms and portrayed themselves as knowledgeable stock traders, regularly sharing their trading actions online. The government claims that, as part of this scheme, defendants would acquire a security and then “pump” its value by disseminating false and misleading information via social media, causing other investors to purchase the security and drive up its price artificially, and then “dump” their own holdings by covertly selling for profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. According to the indictment, the defendants earned $114 million through these actions. The charges include conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and several counts of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight pleaded guilty, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted dismissal, finding the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government appealed in a timely manner.

II

The adequacy of an indictment is reviewed de novo. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment's factual allegations as true and assess if a crime has been alleged. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). For an indictment to be valid, it must: “(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Ultimately, an indictment must set out every essential element of the offense for it to satisfy sufficiency requirements. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute makes it unlawful to engage in any scheme “to defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as to execute schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although there is limited precedent interpreting the securities fraud statute within this circuit, § 1348 incorporates fundamental concepts drawn from the statutes concerning mail and wire fraud. As a result, courts have generally applied analogous interpretations to these statutes’ language, frequently referencing mail and wire fraud precedent when assessing securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Given these established principles, a conviction for securities fraud necessitates (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the acting party’s “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” component involves a significant misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (alteration in original) (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The “intent to defraud” component requires both “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In essence, a person commits securities fraud if he “‘engaged in deception’” with the goal that “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” constituted “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). The defendants admit that the indictment sufficiently alleges deceptive conduct. However, they argue the indictment does not properly charge a scheme or an intent to defraud. They claim the indictment only describes an intent to deprive their followers of important economic information (not money or property) in order to benefit themselves (as opposed to harming their followers). However, considering the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we do not find their arguments persuasive.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to assert any scheme aimed at depriving their followers of a property interest safeguarded by law. Although it is accurate that a fraud conviction cannot rest merely on depriving an individual of valuable economic information, the indictment here alleges more, specifically, a scheme by the defendants to defraud followers out of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court clarified that a conviction for fraud cannot be supported only by depriving someone of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s company had paid a lobbyist to secure government contracts funded by the state. Id. at 309–10. The government’s prosecution relied exclusively on the right-to-control theory of wire fraud. Id. at 310 & n.1. According to the right-to-control theory, the government aimed to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. In essence, under this theory, the victim’s property interest lay in “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Court determined that, because the federal fraud statutes only cover traditional property interests and that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the “right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316. The facts of the present case differ from those in Ciminelli. The indictment does not invoke the right-to-control theory. Instead, it claims the defendants misled their followers via false representations, prompting them to buy securities—thereby relinquishing money or property. The indictment pursues a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” where a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When the Supreme Court considered the fraudulent-inducement theory, it made clear that this approach “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Distinct from the right-to-control theory struck down in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory put forward here “protects money and property.” Id. Thus, because the indictment alleges the defendants fraudulently caused their followers to spend money to purchase securities, it does not conflict with the holding in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants subsequently contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting instead that the indictment claims defendants sought personal gain, rather than to inflict loss upon any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently addressed and rejected a comparable claim, holding that proof of actual economic harm to a victim is not required under the fraud statutes. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In Kousisis, the defendant made false statements to secure contracts with a state department of transportation; the state mandated that contractors work with a disadvantaged business, and Kousisis falsely claimed his company would comply with that provision. After obtaining the contracts, Kousisis only used the disadvantaged business as a nominal intermediary—a “mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When these misrepresentations were discovered, Kousisis was prosecuted based on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which treats as fraud a scenario where a defendant uses a significant misstatement to mislead a victim into forming a contract requiring the transfer of money or property, “regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis challenged this theory on the ground that it sanctioned convictions even where there was no evidence “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, stating that a defendant breaches the fraud statutes by devising a scheme to “obtain” money or property from the victim, regardless of the victim’s resulting financial state. Id. at 1392.

In the present case, the indictment adequately asserts that defendants intended to defraud their followers. It specifies, for instance, that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. As actual economic injury is not an element of the fraud statutes, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not negate the fraud allegations. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (finding liability may attach even if the defendant “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). While defendants assert their objective was personal profit rather than to disadvantage any victim financially, in this context, that argument is immaterial. The indictment asserts that the aim was to obtain money through deceitful inducement of followers into purchasing securities. The allegation that defendants orchestrated a pump-and-dump scheme for personal profit is sufficient to show that their purpose was financial gain. See id. at 1397. The crime of fraud is accomplished once the defendant has caused “the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—regardless of any resultant economic loss to the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s allegations regarding the fraudulent inducement of followers to buy securities are enough to allege harm. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (clarifying that the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” showing of the defendant’s gain being exactly offset by the victim’s loss); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in analogous circumstances).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
