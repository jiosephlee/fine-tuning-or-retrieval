Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants faced charges of securities fraud due to their participation in a "pump and dump" scheme. According to the superseding indictment, they encouraged their followers on social media to buy certain securities by posting false information about their own trading activity and the expected price movement, thereby artificially boosting the prices and enabling themselves to gain financially. The district court dismissed the indictment, finding that it did not allege an actual offense since it only claimed the defendants attempted to withhold potentially valuable economic information from their followers rather than a traditional property right. However, we find that the indictment adequately asserts both a fraudulent scheme and intent to defraud. Therefore, we REVERSE the district court's dismissal of the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight orchestrated a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the allegations, each defendant maintained a significant presence on social media platforms, presenting themselves as expert stock traders and regularly sharing their trading activities online. The government asserts that, as part of their “pump and dump” scheme, the defendants would acquire a security, “‘pump’ the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase the security and artificially increase its price,” and then “dump” the security by covertly selling it at a profit. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment states that defendants collectively realized $114 million in profits from this conduct. Charges included conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and several counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants filed motions to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the motion to dismiss, holding that the indictment did not allege that the defendants intended to deprive victims of any recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal from that decision.

II

We evaluate the adequacy of an indictment on a de novo basis. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept the indictment's factual assertions as true and assess whether it sufficiently alleges an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (quoting United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). To satisfy legal requirements, an indictment should (1) list each essential element of the alleged crime; (2) adequately notify the defendant of the accusations; and (3) afford the defendant protection from subsequent prosecutions for the same offense under the Double Jeopardy Clause. United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). In essence, an indictment’s sufficiency depends on whether it charges every material component of the offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids plans to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” as well as schemes designed “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and thus, “courts have given the terms similar treatment,” frequently referring to mail and wire fraud precedent when evaluating securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Taking those principles into account, the offense of securities fraud requires (1) the existence of a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) that such a scheme was carried out with an “intent to defraud.” Id. at 339. The “scheme to defraud” element necessitates a material misrepresentation that is “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” Id. (modification in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). As for “intent to defraud,” this element means “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In essence, a person is guilty of securities fraud when he “’engaged in deception’” and when “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” constituted “’an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants acknowledge that the indictment properly pleads deception. However, they assert that the indictment fails to allege either a scheme to defraud or an intent to defraud. According to their argument, the indictment only claims they aimed to deprive their followers of valuable economic information (rather than money or property) for their own benefit (rather than to harm their followers). Considering the Supreme Court’s rulings in Ciminelli and Kousisis, we do not agree.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment, asserting it fails to allege any scheme to deprive their followers of a legally protected property interest. They emphasize that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the loss of potentially valuable economic information. However, the indictment at issue here extends beyond that, sufficiently alleging that the defendants devised a scheme to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that depriving a person merely of valuable economic information cannot support a fraud conviction. U.S. at 316. In that case, Ciminelli’s construction firm paid a lobbyist to secure state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The government charged Ciminelli with wire fraud based exclusively on a right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. Under this approach, the government sought to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Thus, the right-to-control theory posits that a victim’s “property” is “the right to control [his] assets.” Id. at 311. The Supreme Court concluded that federal fraud statutes cover only traditional property rights, explaining that “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” and thus, “the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” Id. at 316.

This case differs from Ciminelli. The indictment here does not reference the right-to-control theory. Instead, it asserts that the defendants used false representations to persuade their followers to purchase securities, thereby surrendering money or property. The indictment proceeds under a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. The Supreme Court, in examining the fraudulent-inducement theory, clarified that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory disapproved in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory advanced in this case “protects money and property.” Id. Accordingly, the indictment is consistent with Ciminelli because it alleges that the defendants, through fraud, deprived their followers of a protected property interest by inducing them to purchase securities.

B

Defendants further contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege an intent to defraud, asserting instead that the indictment claims defendants aimed to profit rather than cause harm to any victim. However, the Supreme Court recently addressed and dismissed a comparable contention, finding that fraud statutes do not mandate proof of economic loss to the victim resulting from the defendant’s actions. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In that case, Kousisis misrepresented compliance with a requirement that contractors engage a disadvantaged business in order to secure contracts with a state transportation department. After being awarded the contracts, he utilized the disadvantaged-business subcontractor as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When these misrepresentations were discovered, Kousisis faced prosecution under the “fraudulent-inducement theory,” which posits that a defendant commits fraud by employing a material misrepresentation to induce a victim to enter into a contract and transfer money or property, “regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis challenged this theory, asserting that it permits conviction without any showing that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that a violation occurs where a defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

In the instant case, the indictment properly sets forth that defendants acted with intent to defraud their followers. Among other allegations, it claims defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not require that the victim experience actual economic harm, the fact that defendants’ followers received securities at market value does not eliminate the basis for fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding that a defendant can be liable for fraud even where “he provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants maintain that their objective was to profit, not to inflict harm, but this is not a meaningful distinction under the law in this context. As alleged, defendants’ goal was to acquire money by misleading their followers into buying securities through false statements. The claim that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme for personal profit demonstrates that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. The law provides that “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses,” independent of whether the victim experienced a net loss. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s assertion that defendants fraudulently induced their followers to purchase securities suffices to allege an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that fraud statutes do not demand a “mirror image” relationship in which the victim’s loss exactly matches the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis to analogous circumstances).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
