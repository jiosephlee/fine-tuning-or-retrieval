Title: United States v. Constantinescu
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 2, 2025
Docket: No. 24-20143
Author: Kurt D. Engelhardt, Circuit Judge

The defendants were charged with securities fraud arising from their participation in a “pump and dump” scheme. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants encouraged their social-media followers to buy securities by posting false information about their own trading activities and the likely price movement of the securities, thereby causing the prices to rise artificially and enabling the defendants to gain financially. The district court dismissed the indictment, finding that it did not properly allege an offense, as it claimed only that the defendants aimed to deprive their followers of valuable economic information rather than a conventional property interest. However, because we determine that the indictment adequately sets forth both a fraudulent scheme and the requisite intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s order dismissing the indictment.

I

The government contends that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities scheme. According to the government, each defendant maintained substantial followings on multiple social media platforms, presenting themselves as experienced stock traders and frequently sharing their trading actions online. The government asserts that, in furtherance of their “pump and dump” plan, defendants would acquire a security, “‘pump’ the price of that security by disseminating false and misleading statements about the security on [social media] so as to encourage others to buy and thereby artificially raise its price,” and then “dump” their holdings by covertly selling the security for a gain. Indictment ¶¶ 13–14. The indictment states that defendants collectively gained $114 million from this activity. They were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as several counts of securities fraud in contravention of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2. Knight entered a guilty plea, while the other defendants sought dismissal of the indictment. The district court granted dismissal, finding that the indictment did not adequately allege that defendants had devised a scheme to deprive victims of a recognized property interest. The government filed a timely appeal.

II

We assess whether an indictment is sufficient using a de novo standard of review. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court must accept all factual allegations within the indictment as true and decide if they adequately charge an offense. United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999)). An indictment is required to: “(1) list every prima facie element of the alleged crime; (2) provide sufficient notice to the defendant regarding the accusations; and (3) afford the defendant protection from subsequent prosecutions for the same conduct under the double jeopardy clause.” United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Ultimately, an indictment is adequate only if it alleges every essential element of the offense. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation cleaned up).

III

The securities fraud statute forbids schemes designed to "defraud any person in connection with . . . any security" as well as plans "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security." U.S.C. § 1348. Although “[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” so courts tend to interpret its terms similarly and frequently turn to mail and wire fraud precedent when evaluating securities fraud allegations. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). Taking these principles into account, to prove securities fraud, the government must show: (1) the existence of a "scheme to defraud," and (2) that it was executed with an "intent to defraud." Id. at 339. The requirement of a "scheme to defraud" is satisfied by a material misrepresentation intended "to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived." Id. (alteration in original) (quoting United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)). The "intent to defraud" element necessitates "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." Id. (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712). In essence, securities fraud occurs when a defendant "‘engaged in deception’” and “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)). Defendants admit that the indictment sufficiently alleges deceptive conduct. However, they argue the indictment does not properly set out a scheme to defraud or the intent to defraud. According to them, the indictment only charges that they sought to deprive followers of valuable economic information (but not money or property) to benefit themselves (but not harm their followers). We disagree, given the Supreme Court’s reasoning in Ciminelli and Kousisis.

A

The defendants contend that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment on the grounds that it fails to allege a scheme to deprive their followers of a legally protected property right. Although it is accurate that a conviction for fraud cannot rest solely on the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information, the indictment in this case goes further by appropriately charging that the defendants schemed to defraud their followers of money.

In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court determined that a conviction for fraud may not rest only on depriving someone of valuable economic information. U.S. at 316. In that matter, Ciminelli’s construction company paid a lobbyist in order to secure state-funded contracts. Id. at 309–10. The government’s wire fraud prosecution was based exclusively on the right-to-control theory. Id. at 310 & n.1. This theory permitted the government to “establish wire fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.” Id. at 310. Essentially, this theory treats “the right to control [his] assets” as the victim’s property. Id. at 311. The Supreme Court held that because the federal fraud statutes protect only classic property interests, and because “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” the right-to-control theory is insufficient to support a conviction for fraud. Id. at 316.

This case presents circumstances distinct from Ciminelli. The indictment does not assert the right-to-control theory; instead, it claims that defendants, through misrepresentations, convinced their followers to buy securities—causing them to relinquish their money or property. The indictment is based on a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” whereby a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388. When reviewing the fraudulent-inducement theory, the Supreme Court made clear that it “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.” Id. at 1398 (quotation cleaned up). Unlike the right-to-control theory that Ciminelli disapproved, the fraudulent-inducement theory at issue “protects money and property.” Id. Because the indictment asserts that the defendants fraudulently induced their followers to purchase securities and thereby deprived them of a protected property interest, it does not violate the holding in Ciminelli.

B

Defendants contend that the indictment is deficient because it does not allege that they intended to defraud anyone, asserting instead that their intent was to derive profit from their conduct rather than to inflict harm upon any victims. However, the Supreme Court has recently addressed and dismissed a comparable argument, determining that the federal fraud statutes do not necessitate proof that a victim experienced economic loss as a result of the defendant's scheme. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392. In the Kousisis case, the defendant misrepresented facts to secure contracts from a state department of transportation. These contracts mandated that contractors engage a disadvantaged business as a subcontractor, but Kousisis falsely claimed compliance with this condition. Upon receiving the contracts, Kousisis utilized the purported disadvantaged-business subcontractor merely as “a mere ‘pass-through’ entity.” Id. at 1389. When this misrepresentation was discovered, Kousisis was prosecuted under the "fraudulent-inducement theory," which holds a defendant accountable when “a material misstatement” is used “to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Id. at 1388 (emphasis in original). Kousisis contended that this theory could not be sustained because it allowed for conviction even without proof that “the defendant sought to hurt the victim’s bottom line.” Id. at 1391. The Supreme Court rejected that contention, concluding that the fraud statutes are violated where the defendant “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Id. at 1392.

The indictment in this case sufficiently asserts that defendants intended to defraud their followers. For instance, it charges that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. Since the fraud statutes do not demand actual economic harm, the fact that the followers received securities at market value does not negate the validity of the fraud charges. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1391 (holding that a defendant may still be liable for fraud even where “he provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return”). Defendants maintain they acted with a profit motive, not to injure any victim. Nevertheless, in this context, the distinction is immaterial. The indictment alleges that the objective of defendants’ conduct was to acquire money, and that they achieved this by misleadingly inducing their followers to purchase securities. The allegation that defendants engaged in a pump-and-dump scheme for profit demonstrates that they “had money in mind.” Id. at 1397. “[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—even if there is no net pecuniary loss to the victim. Id. at 1394 n.5. Thus, the indictment’s claims that defendants induced their followers to buy securities through fraudulent means are adequate to allege an injury. See United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (noting that the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” relationship between the victim’s loss and the defendant’s gain); see also United States v. Smith, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104, at *6 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025) (applying Kousisis in an analogous situation).

IV

Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion.
