Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that Consilio, LLC, his employer, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions with overtime pay. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now challenges that ruling on appeal. We uphold the decision in part, but also set aside and return part of the judgment for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm across the country, including a location in Minnesota. Cohen started his employment with Consilio in 2018, serving as an hourly licensed attorney performing document review. In July 2019, Consilio distributed an email to Cohen and other similarly situated hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota, notifying them of a forthcoming change: beginning in August 2019, a revised overtime policy would take effect. Under this updated policy, overtime premium pay would be discontinued, and it was specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated legal action against Consilio both individually and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not provide the overtime compensation allegedly due to him and other employees in similar situations. Cohen claimed that Consilio’s conduct contravened the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive measures.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorneys engaged as document reviewers to satisfy the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought in connection with Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA. Out of this amount, Cohen himself was paid $3,225.21 for overtime and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, there was no consensus on the issue of penalties. Cohen maintained that he should be awarded $172,080 as average daily wage penalties pursuant to the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the relevant statutes did not allow Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made the payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Both Cohen and Consilio “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We consider the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio under a de novo standard, assessing the record and all reasonable inferences in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing the motion. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). “Summary judgment is warranted when there is no dispute of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” as provided in §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified MPWA provisions, which include § 181.101, and further specifies that if an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, the employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, employees may recover civil penalties or damages, provided that these remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is alleged to have been violated. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen claims that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, asserting that the “plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” entitles employees to pursue average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) expressly mentions only the Commissioner in relation to penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, the availability of penalties depends upon the Commissioner first “serv[ing] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If the wages are not paid within ten days after such service, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. In addition, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to the employee, the statute limits them to “prosecut[ing] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, if the Minnesota legislature intended, it demonstrated its ability to grant a statutory right to employees to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that when a terminated employee makes a demand, that individual may recover unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same rights to employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s choice to act in one statute while remaining silent in another demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a desired goal when it chooses” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). As we find that only the Commissioner is authorized to seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court’s decision was not in error. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that under § 181.101(a), “authority to enforce a penalty is restricted to the [Commissioner], and any funds recovered are directed to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has conceded that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen maintains that he is further entitled to statutory penalties. Both parties concur that such statutory penalties—unlike overtime pay and liquidated damages—would be awarded to the Commissioner, and not to Cohen. At this point in the litigation, we have significant doubts as to our jurisdiction to resolve this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of the fact that they are remitted to the government, can still “provide redress to citizen plaintiffs who are harmed or face harm due to ongoing unlawful actions” since such sanctions have the potential to “motivate defendants to cease present violations and dissuade them from future transgressions.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, “a plaintiff must establish standing separately for every claim asserted and each distinct remedy sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously explained, “[t]he required personal stake necessary at the outset of litigation (standing) must be maintained for the duration of the case (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if subsequent developments made it indisputable that the purportedly unlawful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to occur again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)), though “it is firmly established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary discontinuance of an alleged unlawful act does not strip a federal court of its authority to rule on the legality of that act,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties concurred that Cohen had received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages; however, there remains a contested issue as to whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy is still operative. Should the policy persist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties may continue. See id. at 186. On the other hand, if the policy has been discontinued and there is “absolutely clear” assurance that the offending conduct will not resume, then Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts are not empowered to “retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter to the district court for a determination of its jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not provide the notice required by statute regarding a change to its overtime policy, and therefore he seeks penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court, stating it provided “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen agreed that he has already received all wages due from Consilio, and he fails to identify any statutory provision authorizing civil penalties under this law. Since Cohen does not make a substantial argument in favor of overturning the district court’s decision, we decline to disturb the order of dismissal. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint sought particular types of injunctive relief, but he later altered his position in his summary judgment briefing to request alternative relief—specifically, rescission of the 2019 policy. The district court did not address this new form of relief, which is unsurprising under the circumstances. As a result, this issue is not properly presented to us for review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that standing exists, notable distinctions exist between the penalties discussed in that decision and those provided by the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this issue in their briefs. Nevertheless, I also set aside the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s MFLSA statutory penalty claim may be moot, so I agree with vacating the existing judgment and remanding the matter. Upon remand, the district court is permitted to address the question of mootness initially. Id. at 180.
