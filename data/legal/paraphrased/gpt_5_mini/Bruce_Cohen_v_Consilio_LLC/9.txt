Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other comparable employees for overtime work. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen now appeals. We partially affirm and partially vacate and remand the lower court’s decision.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm across the country and maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen started his employment with Consilio in 2018, working as a licensed attorney document reviewer paid on an hourly basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other hourly licensed attorney document reviewers located in Minnesota by email that it would implement a revised overtime compensation policy beginning in August 2019. This updated policy discontinued premium pay for overtime hours and specified that Cohen and the other employees would receive compensation at their “base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio on his own behalf as well as for a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not provide overtime compensation owed to him and other employees in similar circumstances. Cohen contended that Consilio’s conduct breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief as remedies.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers licensed as attorneys in Minnesota to satisfy the overtime pay and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Cohen himself received $3,225.21 for overtime compensation and $462 as liquidated damages as part of these distributions. The parties agreed by stipulation that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and also that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, no agreement could be reached regarding the issue of penalties. Cohen maintained that he should be awarded $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Consilio, on the other hand, contended that the applicable statutes did not allow Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient way to proceed would be for the Court to address and determine Defendants’ summary judgment motion regarding the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims prior to conducting any additional discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio “recognized that, should the Court grant Defendants’ summary judgment motion in its entirety, no claims would remain and the matter would be fully concluded.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s summary judgment decision in favor of Consilio, assessing the evidence as well as any reasonable inferences from it in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when no genuine dispute exists regarding any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin with consideration of Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified MPWA provisions, including § 181.101, and indicates that an employer found to have violated these provisions shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. In addition, an employee may obtain civil penalties or damages, provided such remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that is claimed to have been violated. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen claims that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, contending that "the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]" permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) refers solely to the Commissioner in relation to the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may be assessed only if the Commissioner first issues a demand for payment of wages on the employee’s behalf. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, any “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). Regarding employees, the statute reserves for them only the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated that it can grant a statutory right for employees to recover penalties when it so chooses. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (providing that a terminated employee, upon making a demand, may obtain unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing a similar remedy for employees who resign or quit); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s decision to act in one statute but not in another demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a specific goal if desired” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Accordingly, because we determine that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 lies exclusively with the Commissioner, the district court’s decision was not incorrect. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that § 181.101(a) confers exclusive authority to enforce penalties on the [Commissioner], with any penalties recovered payable to the employee).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has fully paid him all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he is further entitled to statutory penalties. Both parties concur that any statutory penalties awarded—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are to be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this juncture in the litigation, we have substantial reservations as to whether we possess jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, despite being paid to the government, can "provide redress to citizen plaintiffs injured or facing injury due to ongoing unlawful actions," as these penalties may "motivate defendants to cease current violations and discourage future infractions." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, "a plaintiff is required to demonstrate standing for every claim asserted and for each specific type of relief sought." Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as stated previously, "[t]he necessary personal stake (standing) that must be present when litigation begins must persist for the case's entire duration (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (citing Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could be rendered moot if later developments made it definitively apparent that the purportedly unlawful conduct is not reasonably likely to recur,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). Nonetheless, “it is firmly established that ‘the voluntary discontinuance of a disputed action by a defendant does not remove the federal court’s jurisdiction to adjudicate the legality of the conduct,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, both parties stipulated that Cohen had received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages. However, there is still disagreement regarding whether Consilio continues to maintain its unlawful overtime policy. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen might still be able to pursue civil penalties. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been rescinded and it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could indeed be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts do not possess “license[] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment regarding Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand so that the district court can assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he contends that Consilio did not give the statutory notice required regarding a modification of its overtime policy, and he therefore seeks penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court for “includ[ing] little to no discussion of this claim.” Nevertheless, Cohen agreed that he has received all wages owed by Consilio, and he does not identify any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties in connection with this statute. Since Cohen has not presented a substantive argument to justify reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s order dismissing his claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen's request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, in his complaint, Cohen asked for particular injunctive remedies, but later, during summary judgment briefing, he altered his position and sought different relief—namely, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court therefore did not consider this new request, which was reasonable. Consequently, the matter is not properly presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[N]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as outlined in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that he does, there are distinctions between the penalties considered in that precedent and those permitted under the MFLSA. Neither side addressed this issue in their briefs. Additionally, I vacate the district court’s decision regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could be moot, and therefore join in vacating the judgment and remanding for further proceedings. On remand, the district court may address the mootness issue initially. Id. at 180.
