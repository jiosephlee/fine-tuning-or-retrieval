Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and a group of similarly situated workers for overtime work. The district court entered summary judgment in Consilio's favor. Cohen now appeals that decision. We uphold part of the district court’s judgment, while we vacate and remand other aspects.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm with a national presence and maintains offices in Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018, serving as an hourly attorney document reviewer holding a license. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen, along with other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers, via email that a new premium overtime policy would be implemented beginning in August 2019. According to this updated policy, the company removed overtime premium pay and specified that Cohen and similarly situated employees would receive payment “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both individually and as a representative of a proposed class, claiming that Consilio did not provide overtime compensation due to him and other employees in similar positions. Cohen asserted that Consilio’s conduct constituted violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, as well as injunctive relief as remedies.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other attorney document reviewers licensed in Minnesota to satisfy overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought in accordance with Cohen’s MFLSA claim. From this amount, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 representing overtime pay and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed in their stipulation that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties remained in disagreement regarding penalties. Cohen claimed he was owed $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and an additional $42,000 under the MWTA. Consilio, on the other hand, contended that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that "the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation." Both Cohen and Consilio "acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved."

The district court subsequently granted Consilio's motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen now appeals.

II.

We conduct a de novo review of the district court’s decision to grant summary judgment in favor of Consilio, assessing the evidence and all reasonable inferences to be drawn from it in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing summary judgment. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is warranted when no disputes regarding material facts exist and when the moving party is entitled by law to judgment. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties contest whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” as set forth in §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified sections of the MPWA, including § 181.101, and further states that where an employer “is found to have violated” such a provision, that employer becomes “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Furthermore, an employee may obtain civil penalties or damages if, and only if, such relief is “provided for in the section” of the MPWA which the employer is alleged to have violated. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) mentions only the Commissioner in connection with imposing penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (citing Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties are authorized solely after the Commissioner “serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Subsequently, “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Additionally, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees, the statute solely preserves their right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Significantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, the capability to authorize employees by statute to claim penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that following a demand by a terminated employee, that individual may recover unpaid wages or commissions and “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing a similar remedy for employees who voluntarily leave employment); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting that “the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (quoting In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that the authority to pursue daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 is limited to the Commissioner, we find no error in the district court’s decision. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (clarifying that under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has agreed that Consilio has paid all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages pursuant to the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen contends that he remains eligible for statutory penalties. Both parties acknowledge that any statutory penalties awarded, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, are directed to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this point in the case, we have significant doubts about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even where they are remitted to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” in that such penalties have the potential to “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nonetheless, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Furthermore, as previously stated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties “could become moot if later developments demonstrated with certainty that the purportedly unlawful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to happen again,” Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)), but “it is a firmly established principle that ‘a defendant’s voluntary abandonment of the disputed conduct does not strip a federal court of jurisdiction to assess whether that conduct was lawful,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties reached an agreement that Cohen had received all owed overtime pay and liquidated damages, but they continued to dispute whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy remains effective. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen may still have a cognizable claim for civil penalties. See id. at 186. On the other hand, if Consilio has discontinued the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that such improper conduct will not be repeated, Cohen’s MFLSA claim might be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Federal courts lack the authority to maintain jurisdiction where it is clear that one or both parties do not have an ongoing interest in the matter. Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s decision granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the matter so the district court can determine whether it retains jurisdiction.

C.

We turn next to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio neglected to furnish notice of a change to its overtime policy as mandated by statute, thereby entitling him to penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court for what he characterizes as “little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all wages owed, and he does not identify any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties pursuant to this statute. Since Cohen has not offered a substantive argument to justify reversal, we decline to upset the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he maintains solely that he is entitled to “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” In his original complaint, Cohen identified certain particular forms of injunctive relief, but subsequently altered his position during summary judgment briefing to seek different relief—namely, the withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court—understandably—did not address this shift. Consequently, this issue is not appropriately before this Court. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (“[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and return the matter for further proceedings. In all other respects, we uphold the district court’s judgment. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring separately.

I am not persuaded that Cohen has standing to pursue statutory penalties as discussed in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). He may have standing, but the penalties in that case differ from those under the MFLSA. The parties did not address this point in their briefs. I also would set aside the district court’s determinations regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I agree that Cohen’s entitlement to statutory penalties under the MFLSA might be moot, and therefore I concur in vacating the judgment and sending the matter back to the district court. On remand, the district court is permitted to consider the mootness issue as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
