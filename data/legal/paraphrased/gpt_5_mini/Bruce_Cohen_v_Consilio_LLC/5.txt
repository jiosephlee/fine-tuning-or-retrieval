Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action, claiming that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in similar positions for overtime work. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio, and Cohen now challenges that decision on appeal. We partially affirm the lower court’s ruling, but in part vacate it and remand for further proceedings.

I.

Consilio operates as a legal document review firm across the United States and maintains offices within Minnesota. Cohen was hired by Consilio in 2018 to serve as a licensed attorney document reviewer on an hourly wage basis. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other similarly situated hourly licensed attorney document reviewers in Minnesota via email that, starting in August 2019, a revised overtime compensation policy would take effect. This updated policy removed overtime premium pay and specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive payment "at [their] base pay rate" for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio both on his own behalf and for a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not pay him and other similarly situated employees the overtime compensation they were due. Cohen claimed that Consilio’s conduct breached the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen requested as relief unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other document reviewers licensed as attorneys in Minnesota, addressing the overtime compensation and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. From this sum, Cohen individually was paid $3,225.21 for overtime work and an additional $462 as liquidated damages. The parties further agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, the parties were unable to reach consensus regarding penalties. Cohen maintained that he was entitled to $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. In contrast, Consilio contended that the relevant statutes did not authorize Cohen to recover any penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, both parties agreed that “the most expedient and efficient path forward [would be] for the Court to hear and decide Defendants’ summary judgment motion on [the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims] before any further discovery or litigation.” Cohen and Consilio also “acknowledge[d] that, if the Court grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full, no claims w[ould] remain and the case w[ould] be fully resolved.”

The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on all three claims. Cohen appeals.

II.

We examine the district court’s summary judgment in favor of Consilio de novo, considering the evidence and any reasonable inferences drawn from it in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing summary judgment. See Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (citing Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when no genuine dispute of material fact exists and the party seeking judgment is entitled to it as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin by considering Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The central disagreement between the parties concerns whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 authorizes an individual to “bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of certain provisions of the MPWA, including § 181.101, and specifies that if an employer “is found to have violated” one of these provisions, the employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, employees may recover civil penalties or damages as long as these remedies are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that was violated by the employer. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA and maintains that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” grants employees the right to pursue average daily wage penalties. However, § 181.101(a) specifically refers only to the Commissioner with respect to the imposition of penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). In fact, penalties may be assessed solely when the Commissioner has initially “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If, after this demand, “payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, all “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis in original); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). The statute, with respect to the employee, limits their recourse to “prosecut[ing] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and that “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature has demonstrated, when it chooses, its ability to grant a statutory right to aggrieved employees to recover penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that a discharged employee, upon demand, is entitled to recover unpaid wages or commissions and also “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same remedy for employees who quit or resign); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we find that the authority to seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101 resides exclusively with the Commissioner, the district court’s decision was not mistaken. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (noting that under § 181.101(a), “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen conceded that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages to which he is entitled under the MFLSA. Nonetheless, Cohen maintains that he should receive statutory penalties as well. The parties do not dispute that any statutory penalties awarded—unlike overtime compensation and liquidated damages—must be paid to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. At this point in the litigation, we have significant doubts about our jurisdiction to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, even when remitted to the government, can "provide relief to citizen plaintiffs harmed or facing harm as a result of ongoing illegal activity" since such penalties may "motivate defendants to cease existing violations and act as a deterrent against future violations." Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). Nevertheless, "a plaintiff must establish standing for every claim presented and for every type of relief pursued." Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (citing Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Further, as previously stated, "[t]he necessary personal stake required at the initiation of a lawsuit (standing) must persist throughout the litigation (mootness)[.]" Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could be rendered moot if later developments made it unmistakably certain that the conduct in question was not likely to happen again, see Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (citing United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)). However, it is a well-established principle that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio filed its motion for summary judgment, the parties stipulated that Cohen had received all due overtime compensation and liquidated damages; however, the ongoing existence of Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy is contested. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen’s pursuit of civil penalties could proceed. See id. at 186. Conversely, if the policy has been discontinued and it is “absolutely clear” that the alleged misconduct will not happen again, Cohen’s MFLSA claim might indeed be moot. Id. at 189. Moreover, federal courts do not have the “license to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s summary judgment ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to assess its subject matter jurisdiction.

C.

We now turn to Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the notice of an overtime policy change as required by statute, and therefore he is owed penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen argues that the district court “included little to no discussion of this claim.” However, Cohen admitted that he received all wages due from Consilio, and he does not cite any statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties in this context. Because Cohen offers no substantial argument for overturning the district court’s ruling, we decline to set aside the order dismissing his claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends solely that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, Cohen’s complaint asked for certain specified types of injunctive relief, but he later shifted his position in summary judgment filings, seeking alternative relief by requesting withdrawal of the 2019 policy. The district court, quite reasonably, did not address this new request. Therefore, this matter is not appropriately before us. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (stating that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm. KOBES, Circuit Judge, concurring specially.

I remain unconvinced that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties pursuant to Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Although it is possible that he does, there exist distinctions between the penalties addressed in that decision and those provided under the MFLSA. The parties did not submit briefing on this particular issue. Nonetheless, I also set aside the district court’s determination regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I concur that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties may be moot, and thus I agree with the decision to vacate the judgment and remand the matter. Upon remand, the district court may address the issue of mootness as an initial matter. Id. at 180.
