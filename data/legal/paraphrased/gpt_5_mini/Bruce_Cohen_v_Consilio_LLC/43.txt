Title: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2079
Author: Kelly, Circuit Judge

Bruce Cohen initiated this action asserting that his employer, Consilio, LLC, did not compensate him and other employees in comparable positions for overtime work. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of Consilio. Cohen has appealed that decision. We partially affirm, and in other respects, vacate and remand.

I.

Consilio, a legal document review firm operating across the United States with a presence in Minnesota, hired Cohen as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer in 2018. In July 2019, Consilio notified Cohen and other Minnesota hourly licensed attorney document reviewers via email about the implementation of a revised premium overtime policy scheduled for August 2019. This updated policy discontinued premium pay for overtime hours and specified that Cohen and his colleagues would receive compensation “at [their] base pay rate” for every hour worked.

In August 2020, Cohen initiated a lawsuit against Consilio, both for himself and on behalf of a proposed class, asserting that Consilio did not provide him and other similarly situated workers with required overtime compensation. Cohen claimed that Consilio’s conduct contravened the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8; as well as the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27. Cohen’s requested relief included unpaid overtime wages, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive remedies.

Consilio disbursed a total of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota-licensed attorney document reviewers to satisfy the overtime pay and liquidated damages sought under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Within these disbursements, Cohen individually received $3,225.21 for overtime work and $462 as liquidated damages. The parties reached an agreement acknowledging that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” However, consensus on the issue of penalties was not reached. Cohen maintained that he was owed $172,080 in average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties as provided by the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties pursuant to the MWTA. Conversely, Consilio contended that the statutory schemes did not authorize Cohen to recover penalties.

After Consilio made its payments, the parties agreed that the quickest and most efficient way to proceed was for the Court to rule on Defendants' summary judgment motion concerning the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims prior to any additional discovery or litigation. Both Cohen and Consilio recognized that, should the Court fully grant Defendants' summary judgment motion, there would be no remaining claims and the case would be completely resolved.

The district court granted summary judgment in Consilio's favor on all three claims. Cohen now appeals this decision.

II.

We examine the district court’s award of summary judgment to Consilio anew, assessing the evidence and any reasonable inferences in the manner most advantageous to the party opposing the motion. Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014) (quoting Petroski v. H & R Block Enters., LLC, 750 F.3d 976, 978 (8th Cir. 2014)). Summary judgment is appropriate when no genuine disputes of material fact are present and the party seeking judgment is entitled to prevail as a matter of law. Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010, 1014 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro-Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541, 545 (8th Cir. 2020)).

A.

We begin our analysis with Cohen’s claim under the MPWA. The parties disagree as to whether Cohen is entitled to pursue “average daily wage penalties” pursuant to §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 states that “[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of designated sections of the MPWA, which includes § 181.101, and further stipulates that if an employer “is found to have violated” such a provision, the employer shall be “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, subd. 1. Additionally, the statute authorizes an employee to recover civil penalties or damages, but only insofar as such penalties or damages are “provided for in the section” of the MPWA that the employer is alleged to have contravened. Id. (“An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”).

Cohen contends that Consilio breached § 181.101 of the MPWA, maintaining that “the plain language and unambiguous meaning of [§ 181.101]” permits employees to recover average daily wage penalties. However, subsection (a) of § 181.101 references only the Commissioner in connection with penalties. See Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015) (“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” (quoting Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001))). Notably, penalties may be imposed only when the Commissioner has first “serve[d] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). If the wages remain unpaid for ten days after this demand is served, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Id. Moreover, “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Id. (emphasis added); see also Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608, 617 (Minn. 2008). With respect to employees, the statute limits their authority to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a) (stating “[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” and “[t]his section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times”).

Importantly, the Minnesota legislature demonstrated, when it wished, that it could grant employees a statutory entitlement to pursue penalties. See Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a) (stating that following a discharged employee’s request, the employee may recover unpaid wages or commissions, and additionally “may charge and collect a penalty”); id. § 181.14, subd. 2 (providing the same for employees who have quit or resigned); see also Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022) (noting “that the Legislature’s decision to act in one statute but not another demonstrates that the Legislature ‘knows how’ to achieve a specific result if desired” (citing In re Hubbard, 778 N.W.2d 313, 323 (Minn. 2010))). Since we determine that § 181.101 only permits the Commissioner to pursue penalties calculated by daily average wage, the district court’s ruling was not mistaken. See Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., 570 F. Supp. 3d 681, 695 (D. Minn. 2021) (observing that § 181.101(a) “limits the authority to enforce a penalty to the [Commissioner], and any funds recovered are payable to the employee”).

B.

Regarding his MFLSA claim, Cohen has acknowledged through stipulation that Consilio has paid him all overtime compensation and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA. Nevertheless, Cohen asserts a right to statutory penalties. Both parties concur that, in contrast to overtime wages and liquidated damages, any statutory penalties awarded would be owed to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen himself. At this juncture in the case, we harbor significant doubts about our authority to adjudicate this matter.

Civil penalties, regardless of their payment to the government, can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” insofar as these penalties “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000). However, “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008)). Moreover, as we have previously stated, “[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)[.]” Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoting Arizonans for Off. Eng. v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 68 n.22 (1997)).

Cohen’s request for penalties could become moot if later developments made it unequivocally certain that the purportedly wrongful conduct could not reasonably be anticipated to happen again, Friends of the Earth, Inc., 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968)), but “it is firmly established that ‘a defendant’s voluntary cessation of the contested conduct does not strip a federal court of authority to rule on the legality of that conduct,’” id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).

Before Consilio moved for summary judgment, both sides acknowledged that Cohen had received full payment of owed overtime wages and liquidated damages, but there remains a disagreement as to whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy continues to exist. Should Consilio’s policy persist, Cohen’s request for civil penalties could still have merit. See id. at 186. Conversely, if Consilio has ceased its policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the improper conduct will not resume, Cohen’s MFLSA claim could be rendered moot. Id. at 189. Furthermore, federal courts lack “license ... to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest[.]” Id. at 192. Therefore, we set aside the district court’s grant of summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the case so that the district court can assess its jurisdiction.

C.

We now consider Cohen’s claim under the MWTA, in which he asserts that Consilio did not provide the notice of overtime policy change required by statute, and therefore he seeks penalties. Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). Cohen criticizes the district court for offering "little to no discussion of this claim." Nevertheless, Cohen admitted in a stipulation that he had received all compensation due from Consilio, and he cites no statutory basis for awarding civil penalties under the cited statute. Because he presents no substantive argument for reversal, we decline to overturn the district court’s dismissal of this claim. See Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) (“[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it.”).

D.

Regarding Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, he contends that he should receive “an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy.” Initially, the complaint asked for specific injunctive measures, but Cohen subsequently revised his demands during summary judgment briefing, this time seeking rescission of the 2019 policy. The district court, for understandable reasons, did not address this newly requested relief. As a result, this matter is not correctly presented for our review. See InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025) (noting that “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs”); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

III.

We set aside the district court’s decision on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand the case for additional proceedings; in all other respects, we affirm. Circuit Judge KOBES concurs separately.

I am not persuaded that Cohen possesses standing to pursue statutory penalties as outlined in Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000). While it is possible that standing exists, there are distinctions between the penalties at issue in Laidlaw and those permitted under the MFLSA. The parties have not addressed this point in their briefs. Furthermore, I concur in vacating the district court’s decision regarding attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. I also agree that Cohen’s request for MFLSA statutory penalties could potentially be moot; thus, I join in vacating the judgment and remanding the matter. On remand, the district court may address the issue of mootness at the outset. Id. at 180.
