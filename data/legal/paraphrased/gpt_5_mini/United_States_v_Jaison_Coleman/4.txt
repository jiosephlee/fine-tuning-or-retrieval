Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Handling 911 calls involving domestic violence represents one of the most challenging tasks for law enforcement officers. The present case illustrates this point. Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer, a member of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police force, responded to a report from a caller who indicated that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained in the residence. Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked at the door and engaged Lisa in conversation; when questioned about her children's well-being, Lisa hesitated. Officer Kizer responded, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” and went on to inform Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband in another, and that her dogs were not aggressive. The district court concluded that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with sufficient consent to enter the residence, and further determined that he and other officers did not act beyond the boundaries of Lisa’s consent after entry. As a result, the district court declined to suppress the firearms discovered inside the home. We concur with this assessment and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The relevant events are documented in body camera recordings made by Officer Kizer and other officers at the Coleman residence. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon their arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door, identifying himself. Lisa came onto the porch, informing Officer Kizer that both her son and daughter were inside the home. When initially asked whether her children were safe, Lisa hesitated and simply stated that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer pressed the issue, asking if the children would be alright, to which Lisa ambiguously replied that she “hope[d] so.” Officer Kizer then shifted his line of questioning, inquiring about her husband’s whereabouts in the residence. Lisa responded that she was unsure, after which Officer Kizer requested permission to enter. Lisa, having just turned toward the door, stopped momentarily before responding, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The conversation paused, after which Lisa told Officer Kizer she needed to go inside to get her shoes. Some time passed without Lisa returning, prompting Officer Kizer, now concerned, to partially open the door and repeatedly call into the house to check on her wellbeing. After approximately a minute, Lisa reappeared in the doorway and stepped outside again, prompting Officer Kizer to again inquire about the situation inside, and specifically to ask the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer believed had threatened her. Lisa identified her husband as Jaison, referred to hereafter as Coleman. A critical exchange then ensued: when Officer Kizer questioned whether Coleman was “more calm right now” and whether the children were safe, the following conversation took place:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to unlock the door for Officer Kizer and his fellow officers, assisting their entry by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers toward a rear bedroom, indicating the location of the children. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied that she was unaware. A brief search of the residence led Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in a different bedroom. After a short exchange, Coleman agreed to allow Officer Kizer to perform a protective frisk. While speaking with the two children, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had engaged in an altercation, with the children describing Coleman as being loud, aggressive, and unkind. The children further reported that their father had aimed a firearm at their mother and threatened both to shoot her and to set the house on fire. In answering questions, the children affirmed they had seen the firearm both that evening and on prior occasions, though they were uncertain of its present location.

B

After obtaining information once inside the residence, the officers applied for and received a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman residence. The subsequent search resulted in the discovery of multiple firearms, which ultimately led to a federal indictment against Coleman for possession of a firearm by a convicted felon, contrary to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms recovered from his residence, arguing that his wife had not given consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter initially. A magistrate judge conducted a hearing and issued a report concluding that Lisa had, in fact, consented to the officers’ entry, and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s finding regarding Lisa’s consent and independently rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ conduct inside the residence went beyond the boundaries of his wife’s consent. After the district court denied his suppression motion, Coleman entered a guilty plea, specifically preserving his ability to contest the suppression decision on appeal. The present appeal concerns that issue.

II

A

The relevant legal standards are well-settled, and both parties concede that, under the Fourth Amendment, consent serves as an exception to the warrant requirement when it comes to searches of a residence. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). It is likewise undisputed that consent may be provided by someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that, for consent to be effective, it must be granted “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). This principle is reflected in our own precedents. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (citing and following Bumper). Determining whether consent was voluntary requires consideration of all the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Courts frequently evaluate several relevant factors, including: “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate, or was prompted by repeated requests by the authorities; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when he gave his consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (quoting United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even where consent is given voluntarily, the resulting search must remain within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). In determining the appropriate scope of consent, courts apply an “objective reasonableness” test, asking “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the consenting individual? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). On appeal, our review is more circumscribed. We assess a district court’s determination as to voluntary consent, as well as any scope challenges, only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the full exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we find no clear error in the district court’s determination that she voluntarily allowed the officers to enter her residence. Two features of the encounter are especially significant. First, throughout the interaction, Officer Kizer maintained a demeanor and tone reflecting concern rather than any attempt to pressure or coerce. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to withhold consent when she initially refused Officer Kizer’s first request to enter. She then permitted Officer Kizer to come inside after a second, non-coercive request. The video footage substantiates these points. From the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman residence, he interacted with Lisa in a calm and respectful manner, and Lisa’s responses throughout indicated she comprehended what was happening. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly inform her that she was not obliged to grant entry, his act of seeking her permission, coupled with her initial denial, implicitly communicated that her consent was required. Lisa was never detained. While Officer Kizer asked a second time for entry, the entire dialogue was brief, spanning only several minutes. Although Lisa may have felt some degree of implicit pressure to admit the officers, at no moment did Officer Kizer engage in coercion. Based on this record, the district court’s finding of consent was not clearly erroneous.

Coleman challenges this conclusion, emphasizing Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” during the pivotal second request: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” Coleman’s argument is not without merit, but it focuses too narrowly. Considering the totality of the circumstances, the district court could reasonably determine that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” did not render his request coercive. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer’s request to enter, motivated by concern for the welfare of Lisa’s children, was conveyed with urgency but did not, as the district court properly found, amount to coercion. Lisa’s subsequent reaction essentially confirms she interpreted Officer Kizer’s statement as a request rather than a demand. After saying “Okay,” Lisa provided Officer Kizer with the location of her children in the house and reassured the officers that her dogs might jump but would not bite.

Accordingly, we uphold the district court’s finding that Lisa consented to the officers’ entry. We also concur with the district court’s conclusion that the officers acted within the bounds of the consent granted by Lisa once inside the home. Their purpose for entry was to check on the children’s welfare, which entailed confirming that Coleman did not pose a threat to anyone present. This necessarily involved surveying the house, locating all occupants, and posing a few fundamental questions. The information gathered allowed officers to obtain a warrant, leading to the discovery of three firearms. We find no flaw in the district court’s conclusion that the officers honored the limits of Lisa’s consent during their initial entry. Because of these determinations, we do not address the government’s alternative arguments concerning exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery as justifications for entry and the initial sweep. Therefore, we AFFIRM. We conclude with a general observation: Officer Kizer and the other officers demonstrated prudent and judicious decision making in a sensitive and challenging situation.
