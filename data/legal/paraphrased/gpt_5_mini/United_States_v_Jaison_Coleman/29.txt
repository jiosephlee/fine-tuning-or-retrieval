Title: United States v. Jaison Coleman
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-3051
Author: Scudder, Circuit Judge

Police officers face significant challenges when responding to 911 reports of domestic violence, as this case illustrates. At approximately 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Officer Jamie Kizer with the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a call stating that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the residence. Officer Kizer knocked on the front door and conversed with Lisa, who hesitated in response to his inquiry about the well-being of her children. In response, Officer Kizer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa replied, “Okay,” then informed Officer Kizer that her children were in one bedroom, her husband in another, and her dogs were non-aggressive and would not bite. The district court determined that this interaction provided Officer Kizer with the valid consent needed for entry into the residence and further concluded that, once inside, he and the other officers remained within the limits of Lisa’s consent. Consequently, the district court declined to suppress the firearms recovered from the residence. We concur with the district court’s findings and uphold Jaison Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm.

I

A

The pertinent facts come from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues at the Coleman home. See United States v. Norville, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022) (“[A] video record of the events at issue can evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist ….” (citing Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007))). Upon arriving, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa responded by stepping onto the porch and telling Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside. She hesitated to answer whether her kids were safe, saying (at that initial moment) only that they were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then tried again, asking if her kids were going to be okay. Lisa again waffled, this time saying that she “hope[d] so.” Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer next asked where her husband was located inside the home. When Lisa responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter. Lisa, who had just turned back to the door, paused before saying, “um … I’d prefer you not.” A break in the conversation followed, with Lisa then telling Officer Kizer that she needed to go inside to get her shoes. After some time passed with no sign of Lisa coming back outside, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times asking if she was okay. A minute or so later, Lisa returned to the doorway and again stepped outside, prompting Officer Kizer to ask anew how things were going inside and, even more specifically, the name of her husband—who Officer Kizer understood had threatened her. Lisa responded that it was Jaison, whom we refer to as Coleman from here forward. A key exchange then followed. When Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was “more calm right now” and, in turn, if the kids were okay, the following dialogue occurred:

Lisa: I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].

Officer Kizer: Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.

Lisa: Okay.

Officer Kizer: Okay, um ….

Lisa: They’re in my bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….

Lisa: You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.

Officer Kizer: Okay.

Lisa: They’re not gonna bite.

Officer Kizer: Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?

Lisa: I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.

Officer Kizer: Okay, alright.

Lisa proceeded to open the door for Officer Kizer and the other officers, helping them enter by moving her dogs aside. She guided the officers to a rear bedroom and identified where the children were located. When Officer Kizer did not see Coleman present, he inquired about his whereabouts, to which Lisa replied she did not know. A quick search of the residence led Officer Kizer to Coleman, who was in a different bedroom. After a short conversation, Coleman agreed to let Officer Kizer perform a protective pat down. During their conversation with the children, officers were informed that Lisa and Coleman had been having an argument, with Coleman described as being loud, aggressive, and hostile. The children reported that their father had pointed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and to set the house on fire. When questioned further, the children affirmed they had seen the firearm both that evening and on previous occasions, although they were unaware of its current location.

B

After the officers entered the house and obtained additional information, they applied for and obtained a warrant authorizing a search of the Coleman residence. During this search, they discovered multiple firearms, which ultimately led to a federal indictment against Coleman for being a convicted felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Coleman filed a motion to suppress the firearms found in his residence, arguing that his wife had not granted consent for Officer Kizer and the other officers to enter the house initially. A hearing was held, after which a magistrate judge issued a report concluding that Lisa had given consent for the officers to enter, and recommended denial of Coleman’s suppression motion. The district court concurred, adopting the magistrate judge’s determination that Lisa had consented to the entry, and, in addition, rejecting Coleman’s argument that the officers’ actions inside the residence went beyond the scope of his wife’s consent. After the district court denied his motion to suppress, Coleman entered a guilty plea while expressly preserving his right to appeal the suppression decision. The current appeal concerns that issue.

II

A

The applicable legal standards are clear, and both parties agree that the consent exception permits a warrantless home search under the Fourth Amendment. See United States v. Banks, 60 F.4th 386, 390 (7th Cir. 2023); see also Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Additionally, there is consensus that consent may originate from someone other than the defendant: “[a] third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party.” United States v. Cellitti, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004) (quoting United States v. Matlock, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)). The Supreme Court has consistently held that for consent to be effective, it must be given “freely and voluntarily.” Bumper v. North Carolina, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968). Precedent in this circuit follows that rule. See, e.g., United States v. Jones, 22 F.4th 667, 676–77 (7th Cir. 2022) (relying on the standard in Bumper). Courts make determinations about consent’s voluntariness by looking at the totality of the circumstances. See United States v. Han, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024). Relevant considerations typically include, among other things, “(1) the person’s age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether he was advised of his constitutional rights; (3) how long he was detained before he gave his consent; (4) whether his consent was immediate or only followed persistent requests by authorities; (5) whether physical coercion occurred; and (6) whether the individual was in custody at the time of giving consent.” United States v. Figueroa-Espana, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007) (citing United States v. Santiago, 428 F.3d 699, 704–05 (7th Cir. 2005)). Even when consent is voluntary, law enforcement must operate within “the scope of consent.” United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012) (citing United States v. Jackson, 598 F.3d 340, 348 (7th Cir. 2010)). The key question for analyzing the scope of consent is “objective reasonableness”; in other words, courts must decide, “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer” and the individual granting consent? Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) (cleaned up). As an appellate court, our authority is more constrained. We examine the district court’s conclusions on voluntary consent and its boundaries only for clear error. See Jones, 22 F.4th at 675 (citations omitted).

B

After reviewing the entire exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, we discern no clear error in the district court’s determination that Lisa voluntarily allowed the officers into her residence. Two aspects are especially noteworthy. First, throughout the interaction, Officer Kizer maintained a tone and attitude reflecting concern rather than compulsion. Second, Lisa demonstrated her capacity to withhold consent, as she initially declined Officer Kizer’s request to enter. She subsequently allowed him in after a renewed request that was free from coercion. These factors are evident in the video evidence. From his initial arrival at the Coleman home, Officer Kizer communicated calmly and courteously with Lisa, and at every moment, Lisa’s responses revealed her understanding of the events. Although Officer Kizer did not expressly advise her that she could refuse his entry, his requests for permission—especially in light of her original refusal—implicitly conveyed that option. Lisa was never placed under arrest or physically restrained. While Officer Kizer repeated his request to enter, the entire discussion lasted only a few minutes. Although Lisa might have experienced some pressure to admit the officers, Officer Kizer never crossed the line into actual coercion. Based on this record, we cannot say the district court clearly erred in concluding Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers’ entry. 

Coleman challenges this finding, pointing in particular to Officer Kizer’s use of the term “need” in the second critical exchange: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay].” While this is a reasonable argument, Coleman’s focus is unduly narrow. Given the entirety of the situation, the district court could rationally determine that Officer Kizer’s use of “need” did not transform his request into a coercive one. See United States v. Hicks, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008). Officer Kizer made his request out of concern for the children’s well-being, communicating a degree of urgency and seriousness, but the district court’s conclusion that this did not rise to coercion is not clearly erroneous. Lisa’s own reaction substantiates that she interpreted Officer Kizer’s statement in just this way. After agreeing (“Okay”), she advised Officer Kizer where her children were inside and reassured both him and the other officers that, while her dogs might jump, they would not bite. 

These circumstances support upholding the district court’s finding that Lisa granted consent for the officers to enter her home. Likewise, we concur with the district court that the officers did not exceed the bounds of Lisa’s consent once inside. Their purpose in entering was to verify the safety of her children and to ensure Coleman did not pose a threat to them or anyone else. Accomplishing that goal required the officers to look through the house, account for everyone present, and pose basic questions. The information they gathered led to the securing of a search warrant and the recovery of three firearms. We find no error in the district court’s ruling that the officers honored the scope of Lisa’s initial consent. Our conclusions make it unnecessary to reach the government’s alternative argument concerning exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery justifying the entry and initial sweep of the home.

Ultimately, we AFFIRM. We conclude with a broader observation: Officer Kizer and the responding officers conducted themselves with prudence and sound judgment when confronted with a sensitive and challenging situation.
