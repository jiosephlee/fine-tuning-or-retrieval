Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are tasked with interpreting a Louisiana statute that regulates the cancellation of insurance policies due to nonpayment to a premium finance company. The appellants, who are former policyholders, argue that the insurance company's cancellation process failed to adhere to statutory requirements, thereby causing misrepresentations and violating the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no violation of the statute occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, both formerly insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, bring this action as plaintiffs. Another individual insured by GoAuto, not a party to these proceedings, was involved in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, who is also a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the GoAuto policyholder involved in Jenkins's accident each entered into premium finance agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”), by which APAC advanced the full premium to GoAuto in exchange for receiving monthly installments from the insured parties. These agreements also conveyed to APAC a power of attorney, permitting APAC to terminate the insurance coverage if premiums were not paid. Each policy was cancelled due to default in payment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action lawsuit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto had not properly cancelled their policies in accordance with applicable law and had acted in bad faith. After an amended complaint, Wright joined as an additional plaintiff. GoAuto subsequently removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then submitted cross-motions for summary judgment that addressed only whether the methodology employed by GoAuto to cancel the financed insurance policies complied with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, concluding that the cancellation procedure satisfied state law, and thereby dismissed with prejudice all claims asserted against GoAuto. Plaintiffs Williams, Jenkins, and Wright thereafter filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering a notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto properly received the cancellation requests, and (3) refusing to grant their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these contentions, we first outline the relevant Louisiana law governing cancellation of financed insurance policies, and then review GoAuto’s procedures for ensuring compliance with these legal requirements. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is permitted to request the cancellation of an insurance policy following an unremedied default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following matters: (i) the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as described in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection, (ii) a default has occurred under the premium finance agreement and has not been timely cured, (iii) following the default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as required by Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, stating the date when the premium finance company sent such notice to the insured, and (iv) that copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to every party identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any covered loss, detailing the names and addresses of any governmental entities, security interest holders, or third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company has provided notice of cancellation. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with these statutory provisions when a finance company utilizes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). This requirement of strict compliance with the statute has dual objectives: (1) it delineates a precise method for determining the specific time and date of policy cancellation, and (2) it ensures a minimum period during which the insured has the opportunity to remedy the default. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto employs the following steps to effectuate cancellation of a policy. Upon an insured’s failure to remit timely payment, APAC notifies the insured that the policy will be cancelled absent payment within ten days. If no payment is received by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s automated computer system transmits to GoAuto an e-mail that includes both a copy of the cancellation notice and a cancellation request. This request for cancellation, sent on APAC letterhead, includes a certification by APAC stating:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) A default has occurred under the Premium Finance Agreement, and said default remains uncured within the requisite time. (3) Following default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured. Enclosed are copies of the notices, and the affidavit of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were furnished to each individual identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computerized system terminates the policy. Subsequently, on the same morning after the cancellation occurs, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the hard copies of the cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC staff person would manually deposit these documents into a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, however, the signed cancellation paperwork is instead delivered in person to a GoAuto employee, who marks it as “received” with a stamp before placing it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn now to whether these procedures satisfy the Louisiana statute previously addressed. Plaintiffs’ central contention is that APAC’s certification remains unsigned until a point following, but indisputably after, the policy’s automatic cancellation. Plaintiffs read the statute’s requirement that the premium insurer “certify” as mandating the certification be signed prior to effecting cancellation. The statute, however, does not expressly mandate a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If a signature is necessary, this must derive from the term “certifying” itself. Except for one matter addressed below, no Louisiana court evaluating insurance-premium finance company practices under this law has ruled on whether a signature is requisite. In one case, a court reworded the statutory obligation as “a statement certifying compliance with” the statutory provisions. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another court upheld cancellation upon “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases provide equally little guidance on this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An appellate court addressed the issue of a missing signature when considering deficiencies in a cancellation notice, observing that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the Benitez court did not find that the mere lack of a signature would invalidate the cancellation, provided other necessary elements were satisfied. Like the notices scrutinized in Benitez, the documents present in this record do not specify a particular employee by name or signature. However, contrary to Benitez, the notices at issue here do "certify" the relevant details. Given the absence of explicit Louisiana case law to the contrary, we discern no basis to conclude a company is prohibited from certifying information without specifying a responsible individual. The governing statute expressly refers to “the insurance premium finance company” effecting cancellation by “certifying” specified information within the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). “Strict adherence” to statutory cancellation procedures does not entitle courts to impose requirements beyond those enumerated by the statute. Thus, we determine that Louisiana law does not mandate that a cancellation notice from a premium finance company to an insurer must bear a signature.

Plaintiffs additionally argue that GoAuto’s procedures fail to strictly follow statutory requirements because GoAuto never actually receives the request for cancellation. The statute provides that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Prior to 2017, the shared storage area for physical cancellation requests was controlled by GoAuto Management Services, rather than GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert, GoAuto does not obtain the requests themselves because an APAC employee is responsible for applying the required stamp. Plaintiffs maintain that the legislature’s usage of the term “receipt” (rather than merely sending) means GoAuto must actually obtain the cancellation notice prior to effecting cancellation. The meaning of “receipt” as used in this statute has not been determined by the Louisiana Supreme Court, and the statute does not provide its own definition. Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and defines “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” See Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these meanings and determine that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy once it acquires possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request. As described above, when an e-mailed notice is delivered in the early morning to GoAuto’s computer system, that system processes the cancellation automatically. A GoAuto witness detailed the interaction between APAC’s computer system and a GoAuto web service: During cancellation, the APAC process initiates a request to a GoAuto web service to cancel or process cancellation for a policy set for cancellation on that specific date. Essentially, a web service functions as a listener, remaining attentive for incoming calls, and when activated, it asks for further instructions; if comprehensible, the service executes the requested task.

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has completed the cancellation process. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To restate our prior summary of the statute: before a cancellation can occur, it is required that the insurer has “receipt” of the cancellation notice transmitted by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects modern realities, as company computer systems now perform tasks once executed by individuals. GoAuto’s procedure for receiving and responding to the notice meets the statutory requirement. We note as well that the district court found GoAuto’s method of cancellation to comply with the statute for a separate reason: it concluded that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” In the absence of Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier,” the district court relied on a panel decision from this court addressing when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier, specifically referencing Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” can be characterized as both novel and unorthodox. However, since we determine that the email satisfies the statutory requirement, we do not address the merits of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we certify these questions of statutory construction to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that the correct reading of the statutory language has significant implications for Louisiana’s insurance regulations and its overall statutory framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Merely because a term remains undefined by a state supreme court “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Therefore, we deny the request to certify. Because GoAuto’s procedures are consistent with the requirements of the statute, the district court was correct in denying the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. The judgment is AFFIRMED.
