Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are tasked with interpreting a Louisiana statute that addresses the termination of insurance coverage for failure to pay a premium finance company. The former policyholders argue that the insurer’s methods for canceling coverage were inconsistent with the statute, and assert that these actions amounted to misrepresentation and violated the obligation of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that no statutory breach had occurred and dismissed every claim with prejudice. We AFFIRM the judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who are no longer insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, brought this lawsuit. Another individual insured by GoAuto—who is not a party to this action—was in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. All three individuals—Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins’s accident—purchased their GoAuto insurance policies using financing provided by American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). APAC paid GoAuto the total premium upfront and, in exchange, the insureds agreed to make monthly payments to APAC. Each financing contract included a provision granting APAC a power of attorney, enabling APAC to cancel the respective insurance policies if the insureds failed to make required payments. Each policy at issue was cancelled for nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against both GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto did not effectively cancel their insurance and failed to act in good faith. Subsequently, Wright was added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto subsequently removed the lawsuit to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then moved for summary judgment exclusively on the issue of whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedure for policies financed through APAC conformed with Louisiana statutes. The district court sided with GoAuto, granting summary judgment on the ground that GoAuto’s cancellation practices were consistent with state law, and thus dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed the decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by: (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation had been met; (2) concluding that GoAuto properly received the cancellation requests; and (3) denying their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to respond to these claims, we first review the relevant Louisiana statutes regarding the cancellation of financed insurance policies, and then analyze GoAuto’s procedures for adhering to those statutes. Pursuant to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is authorized to request cancellation of an insurance policy when a default remains uncured by providing to the insurer—either by mailing, delivering via private carrier, or sending by electronic mail—a copy of the cancellation notice, along with a certification stating the following: (i) That the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as required by Paragraph (1) of the Subsection; (ii) That the premium finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely remedied; (iii) That following default, a cancellation notice was issued to the insured in compliance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date the finance company sent notice to the insured; and (iv) That copies of the cancellation notice were also sent to all individuals identified by the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the policy, specifying the names and addresses of all governmental agencies, secured parties, or third parties to whom the finance company sent notice of cancellation. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with statutory procedures when a finance company utilizes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Such strict compliance with statutory mandates fulfills dual purposes: (1) establishing a definitive means of determining the precise date and time of cancellation, and (2) providing the insured a guaranteed minimum period during which the default may be remedied. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto utilizes a specified procedure to effectuate cancellation of a policy. Upon the insured’s failure to remit timely payment, APAC issues a notice informing the insured that cancellation will occur absent payment within ten days. Should no payment be received by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s automated system transmits an email to GoAuto containing the cancellation notice and a formal cancellation request. This cancellation request appears on APAC’s letterhead and includes the certification that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There has been a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and this default remains uncured within the prescribed timeframe. (3) Following the occurrence of the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured; copies of said notices are attached hereto, and an affidavit of proof of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the Agreement were sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When such notice is received, GoAuto's computer system automatically terminates the policy. Subsequent to this cancellation, typically later the same morning, an employee of APAC applies a signature stamp to the printed documents evidencing cancellation. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver these documents to a filing cabinet jointly used by both GoAuto and APAC. Starting in 2017, the completed cancellation document, after being signed, is physically delivered to a GoAuto employee, who then marks it as “received” with a stamp before filing it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to the question of whether these procedures satisfy the requirements of the Louisiana statute previously discussed. The Plaintiffs’ central contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until just after, but not before, the automatic cancellation of the policy occurs. According to the Plaintiffs, the statutory mandate that the premium insurer “certify” cancellation should be interpreted as necessitating the presence of a signature prior to cancellation. However, the statute does not expressly mention a signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be needed, such an obligation would have to be read into the term “certifying.” Aside from an exception to be examined subsequently, Louisiana courts reviewing premium finance company practices under the statute have not explicitly addressed whether a signature is required. For instance, one court described the statutory directive as needing “a statement certifying compliance with” the relevant statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other decisions have likewise not resolved this specific issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An appellate court opinion referenced the absence of a signature while evaluating deficiencies in a particular cancellation notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not determine that a missing signature would alone render the cancellation ineffective if all other requirements were satisfied. Similar to Benitez, the notices at issue here also do not name a specific employee, either by printed name or signature. Unlike in Benitez, however, each of these notices “certifies” the relevant information. In the absence of more explicit authority from Louisiana, we find no basis for requiring that certification be tied to a named employee. The statute expressly specifies that “the insurance premium finance company” is responsible for effectuating cancellation by “certifying” designated information in the notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The notion of “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to impose additional requirements. Therefore, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on cancellation notices issued by a premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto's practices do not comply strictly with the statute because GoAuto never actually “received” a cancellation request. The statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Prior to 2017, the shared records room that stored printed requests belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto. Since 2017, according to the Plaintiffs, GoAuto does not receive cancellation requests because the employee who places the stamp is an employee of APAC. Plaintiffs contend that because the legislature chose the word “receipt,” rather than merely requiring the request be sent, GoAuto must actually gain possession of the notice prior to cancelling the policy. Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor the statute provides a definition for “receipt” in this context. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," and defines "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these definitions and determine that GoAuto may effectuate cancellation upon obtaining possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously detailed, once the e-mailed notice is delivered in the early morning to GoAuto’s computer system, the policy is cancelled by that system. A deponent for GoAuto described the interaction between the APAC system and a GoAuto web service: For example, during cancellation, the APAC system makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request or effectuate cancellation for a policy set to cancel on a particular date. Essentially, a web service functions like a listener; it continuously listens for an incoming request and, when activated, prompts for instructions. If the request is understood, it executes the appropriate task.

No individual becomes engaged until after GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation later in the morning. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To reiterate our previous summary of the statutory provision: the requisite step before cancellation is that the insurer must have received notice of cancellation provided by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). As this statute is contemporary and reflects current practices, it is now routine for company computers to undertake functions once handled by people. The way in which GoAuto’s system receives and processes the notice complies with the statutory requirement. Additionally, the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures met the statute by reasoning differently: it concluded “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged there was no Louisiana precedent clarifying “private carrier,” and instead referenced a Fifth Circuit panel’s discussion of when a railroad would be considered a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was notably innovative and unorthodox. Given that we hold the e-mail process satisfies the statutory requirements, we need not address the soundness of the district court’s definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that resolving the meaning of the statutory language is significant for the state's insurance regulations and legal framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state supreme court has yet to construe a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify. Given that GoAuto’s practices align with the statute, the district court correctly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
