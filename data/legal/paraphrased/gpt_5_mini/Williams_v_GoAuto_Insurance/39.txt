Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute that pertains to the cancellation of insurance policies when there has been a failure to pay by the party who financed the premiums. The appellants, who were previously insured, argue that the insurer’s process for cancellation did not align with the statutory requirements, alleging as a result both misrepresentation and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that there was no breach of the statute at issue and entered judgment dismissing all causes of action with prejudice. We AFFIRM that decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies through GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this action, was in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the driver involved in Jenkins’s accident all financed their GoAuto insurance premiums via separate agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC would remit the full insurance premium to GoAuto in exchange for monthly payments made by the insured parties. The financing contracts also gave APAC a power of attorney, empowering APAC to terminate the insurance coverage if premium payments were missed. Ultimately, all relevant insurance policies were terminated due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto had improperly cancelled their coverage and had breached its duty of good faith. Wright was later added as a plaintiff following an amended complaint. The case was removed by GoAuto to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted motions for summary judgment limited to the question of whether GoAuto’s cancellation methods for financed policies were lawful under Louisiana statutes. The district court granted summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, concluding that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures met the requirements of state law. Consequently, the court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) determining that the statutory requirements regarding the delivery of the cancellation notice were met, (2) concluding that GoAuto properly received the requests for cancellation, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To analyze these claims, we first outline the relevant provisions of Louisiana law concerning the cancellation of insurance policies subject to premium financing and then review the processes GoAuto used to comply with these laws. Louisiana law provides that an insurance-premium finance company may, following an uncured default, request the cancellation of an insurance policy “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as specified in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection. (ii) The premium finance agreement is in default, and the default remains uncured. (iii) After default, the premium finance company sent a notice of cancellation to the insured as set forth in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, including the date the notice was sent. (iv) Copies of the notice of cancellation were provided to all parties listed in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss, detailing the identities and addresses of any government agencies, secured creditors, or other third parties to whom notice of cancellation was sent by the insurance premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) A default has occurred under the terms of the Premium Finance Agreement and the default has not been remedied within the required period. (3) Following default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured; copies of such notices are attached, and an affidavit evidencing mailing is available upon request. (4) All parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto's computer system processes a cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later that same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the cancellation documents that have been printed. Before 2017, an APAC employee would physically deliver the documents to a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the finalized cancellation document is physically delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks the document as "received" before placing it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next evaluate whether these procedures align with the relevant Louisiana statute previously discussed. The Plaintiffs primarily contend that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until just after—though not before—the policy is automatically canceled. Plaintiffs assert that the statutory requirement for the premium insurer to “certify” necessitates that a signature be obtained prior to any cancellation. The statute, however, does not specifically mention a signature requirement. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should such a requirement exist, it would have to be inferred from the use of the term “certifying.” With one exception to be addressed, Louisiana courts reviewing the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under the statute have not opined on whether a signature is necessary. For instance, one court restated the statutory mandate as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another found cancellation proper “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional decisions offer little guidance on this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An appellate court once noted the absence of a signature as one of several deficiencies in a cancellation notice, stating that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not conclude that failing to include a signature alone would invalidate a cancellation if the other statutory requirements were satisfied. In the present case, as in Benitez, the notices in the record neither name a specific employee by print nor by signature. However, unlike Benitez, the notices here do “certify” the relevant information. Lacking more explicit Louisiana precedent, we find no basis to determine that a company cannot certify the necessary information without disclosing a particular employee’s name. The applicable statutory provision indicates it is “the insurance premium finance company” that accomplishes cancellation by “certifying” specific information in a notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The mandate for “strict adherence” to the statutory cancellation procedures should not be interpreted as authorization for courts to impose additional procedural requirements. We therefore find that Louisiana law does not mandate that the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer bear a signature.

Plaintiffs additionally assert that GoAuto’s practices fail to strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto never actually receives a cancellation request. The statute provides: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs point out that, before 2017, printed cancellation requests were kept in a common filing room belonging to GoAuto Management Services, and not GoAuto. Since 2017, they argue, GoAuto has not received the requests because the individual applying the cancellation stamp is an APAC employee. They interpret the legislature’s use of the term “receipt,” rather than merely requiring the request be sent, to mean that GoAuto must physically take possession of the notice prior to cancelling the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined “receipt” within the context of the statute, nor does the statute include a definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Adopting these definitions, we determine that GoAuto may cancel a policy upon obtaining possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously described, when GoAuto’s computer system receives the e-mailed notice in the early morning, it proceeds to cancel the policy. A GoAuto representative testified as follows regarding the interaction between the APAC and GoAuto systems: For example, during cancellation, the APAC process initiates a request to a GoAuto web service to execute cancellation or process the cancellation for a given policy. Essentially, a web service acts as a listener, always waiting for an instruction; once prompted, it asks for the desired action and, if recognized, carries it out.

No individual participates until some time later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has processed the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation paperwork. To reiterate our prior summary of the statute: before a cancellation may occur, it is required that the insurer has “receipt” of the cancellation notice issued by the premium finance company, pursuant to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects contemporary practices, recognizing that company computers now fulfill many roles once handled by people. GoAuto’s approach of receiving and responding to notices electronically complies with the requirements of the statute. We note that the district court concluded that GoAuto’s procedures met statutory requirements on different grounds, stating, “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged that there is no Louisiana precedent clarifying the meaning of “private carrier,” and relied on a panel decision from this court involving railroads as private carriers, see Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s characterization of “private carrier” was notably inventive and outside conventional interpretation. Since we conclude that the e-mail meets the statutory standard, we decline to address the correctness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to submit these questions of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court for resolution. They argue that correctly construing the statutory language bears significant consequences for Louisiana’s insurance regulatory framework and industry. However, as a rule, we are reluctant to certify legal questions unless a strong justification exists; certification is a vital tool for this court, and we avoid overusing it to preserve its effectiveness, as explained in Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state supreme court has not previously construed a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we decline to certify. Since GoAuto’s procedures satisfy the statute’s requirements, the district court correctly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
