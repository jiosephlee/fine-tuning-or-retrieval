Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are tasked with interpreting a Louisiana statute related to the termination of insurance coverage for nonpayment owed to the premium financing company. The former policyholders argue that the insurer’s process for cancellation failed to adhere to statutory requirements, allegedly amounting to misrepresentation and a breach of the duties of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined there had been no violation of the statute and dismissed every claim with prejudice. We AFFIRM that decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, were previously insured by GoAuto Insurance Company. Another person, also insured by GoAuto but not involved in this lawsuit, had an accident with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the individual in the Jenkins accident each arranged to finance their GoAuto insurance policies through a contract with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC paid the full policy premiums to GoAuto and, in exchange, the insured individuals made monthly payments to APAC. The financing agreements gave APAC the authority, via power of attorney, to cancel the insurance policies if the insureds failed to make premium payments. All the relevant insurance policies were ultimately cancelled due to nonpayment. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action lawsuit against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, contending that GoAuto had not properly cancelled their insurance policies and had breached its duty of good faith. With the filing of an amended complaint, Wright joined as a plaintiff. GoAuto subsequently removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both parties filed motions for summary judgment solely on the question of whether GoAuto’s process for cancelling these financed insurance policies adhered to Louisiana law. The district court ruled in favor of GoAuto on summary judgment, determining that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with state statutory requirements. Accordingly, the court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court committed error in three respects: (1) by concluding that the statutory requirements for delivering the cancellation notice were satisfied; (2) by determining that GoAuto correctly received the requests for cancellation; and (3) by rejecting the Plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these contentions, we first outline the governing Louisiana law concerning cancellation of financed insurance policies and then review GoAuto’s practices for complying with those legal requirements. Louisiana statutes provide that an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following conditions: (i) the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as described in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the agreement is in default, and the default remains uncured within the allowed time; (iii) a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as directed in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date that the premium finance company provided notice to the insured; and (iv) copies of the cancellation notice were sent to all parties identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential losses, listing the names and addresses of any involved governmental agencies, secured parties, or other third parties who have been sent a cancellation notice by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There is an existing default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and this default has not been remedied within the allowed period. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured; enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit regarding proof of mailing can be supplied upon request. (4) All parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as holding an interest in any potential loss covered under the agreement were sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computerized system executes cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later that same morning, an APAC employee uses a signature stamp on the printed cancellation paperwork. In the period before 2017, an APAC employee would deliver the documents in person to a filing cabinet that was jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Since 2017, the cancellation document, once signed, is brought directly to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it “received” and stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to the issue of whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana statute previously analyzed. Plaintiffs’ principal contention is that APAC’s certification bears no signature until shortly after, but definitively subsequent to, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs construe the statute’s requirement that the premium insurer “certify” as necessitating the presence of a signature before cancellation can occur. However, the statute does not expressly mention any signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If a signature were necessary, it would need to be derived from the statutory use of “certifying.” Except for an exception discussed below, no Louisiana court decision evaluating premium finance companies’ procedures under this statute has confronted the issue of a signature’s necessity. One appellate court described the statutory directive as mandating “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other decisions offer little guidance on this precise issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate court decision, the absence of a signature was noted among the deficiencies of the cancellation notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in Benitez did not determine that lacking a signature alone would invalidate the cancellation if other requirements were fulfilled. As in Benitez, the cancellation notices in this case similarly omit any reference to a specific employee’s name or signature. In contrast to Benitez, these notices each include a “certification” of the necessary details. Given the absence of more definitive Louisiana precedent, we do not find any basis for requiring certification to be accompanied by a named employee. The applicable statutory provision expressly designates “the insurance premium finance company” as the party effecting cancellation by “certifying” certain information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). “Strict adherence” to the statutory cancellation procedures does not grant authority to the courts to impose additional procedural requirements. Accordingly, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the notice sent by a premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures do not comply strictly with the statute because GoAuto itself never receives the cancellation request. The relevant statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the facility used to store printed requests was under GoAuto Management Services’ control, not GoAuto’s. Since 2017, Plaintiffs allege that GoAuto does not receive the cancellation requests directly because the individual applying the stamp is employed by APAC. Plaintiffs maintain that, by using the term “receipt” instead of merely requiring transmission, the legislature intended GoAuto to actually possess the notice before effectuating cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted the meaning of “receipt” under this statute, nor does the statute define the term. According to Black’s Law Dictionary, “receipt” means “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” is defined as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these definitions and hold that GoAuto is permitted to cancel a policy upon taking possession, custody, or delivery of a cancellation request. As discussed above, when the notice is e-mailed and received in GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, that system proceeds to cancel the policy. A GoAuto deponent described the interface between the APAC and GoAuto computer systems as follows: during cancellation, the APAC process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until later in the day after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents. Our prior summary of the statute is as follows: Prior to cancellation, the insurer must have “receipt” of the notice of cancellation issued by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects present-day conditions, with company computers performing many tasks formerly handled by people. GoAuto’s approach to receiving and processing the notice meets the statute’s requirements. It should be noted that the district court determined that GoAuto’s procedures adhered to the statute for another reason: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that Louisiana case law did not interpret “private carrier” and considered relevant this court’s discussion on when a railroad constitutes a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was creative and unorthodox. As we conclude the e-mail meets the statutory requirements, we do not reach the merits of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to refer these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that the correct reading of these statutory terms holds significant implications for Louisiana’s insurance regulations and broader statutory context. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Moreover, the fact that a state supreme court has not previously defined a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify the issue. Since GoAuto’s practices conform to the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
