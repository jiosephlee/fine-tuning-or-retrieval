Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are asked to interpret a Louisiana statute that addresses the termination of insurance policies due to the insureds’ failure to pay the company financing their premiums. The former insured parties assert that the insurer’s methods of cancellation were inconsistent with statutory requirements, and allege this resulted in both misrepresentation and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that there had been no breach of the statute and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, previously policyholders of GoAuto Insurance Company, serve as plaintiffs in this matter. Another GoAuto insured, who is not a party to this litigation, was in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. All three—Williams, Wright, and the individual involved in Jenkins’s accident—entered into premium financing agreements for their GoAuto insurance with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC paid the full amount of the insurance premium directly to GoAuto, while the insureds agreed to repay APAC through monthly installments. Each agreement also provided APAC with a power of attorney, permitting APAC to cancel the relevant insurance policy if the insured failed to make required payments. Each policy at issue was subsequently cancelled for nonpayment of premiums. Williams and Jenkins commenced a class action against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, contending that GoAuto had not properly cancelled their insurance coverage and alleging a lack of good faith conduct. Wright joined as a named plaintiff pursuant to a later amendment to the complaint. GoAuto subsequently removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides moved for summary judgment, contesting solely the adequacy of GoAuto’s cancellation process for the financed policies under Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, finding its cancellation practices consistent with state legal requirements, and dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by: (1) determining that the statutory process for delivering the notice of cancellation was adhered to, (2) concluding that GoAuto lawfully received the requests for cancellation, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these issues, we first outline the relevant Louisiana law concerning the cancellation of financed insurance policies and then review GoAuto’s compliance mechanisms. Under Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is permitted to seek cancellation of an insurance policy when a default remains uncured, “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” these facts: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as provided in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) The premium finance agreement has entered default status and remains uncured; (iii) Upon such default, a cancellation notice was issued to the insured as required in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, specifying the date the notice was sent to the insured by the finance company; and (iv) Duplicate cancellation notices were sent to all parties identified in the premium finance agreement with an interest in any prospective loss under the policy, including the names and addresses of governmental bodies, security interest holders, or third parties, as required, to whom the finance company dispatched notice of cancellation. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with the statutory provisions when a finance company acts through its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Should there be any flaw in this process, such defect renders the cancellation of the policy ineffective. Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). The requirement for strict statutory compliance fulfills two purposes: it establishes a definitive means of determining the precise date and time that cancellation occurs, and it ensures a minimum duration within which a default can be remedied. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). The process GoAuto employs to cancel a policy is as follows. If the insured does not pay on time, APAC issues a notice informing the insured that cancellation will occur unless payment is received within ten days. Should the payment not be made by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s automated computer system transmits an email to GoAuto, including the cancellation notice and a formal cancellation request. This cancellation request is prepared on APAC’s letterhead and includes the following statement: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and such default remains uncured within the appropriate time frame. (3) Following the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured party. Enclosed are copies of such notices, and an affidavit demonstrating proof of mailing can be furnished upon request. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement were sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once the notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system effectuates the cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later that same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the hard copies of the cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deposit these documents in a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Starting in 2017, however, after the document has been signed, it is hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps it as “received” before placing it into the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next examine whether these procedures align with the Louisiana statute previously analyzed. Central to Plaintiffs’ position is the fact that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until after, though only shortly after, the policy automatically cancels. Plaintiffs construe the statutory directive for certification by the premium insurer as mandating a signature prior to any cancellation taking effect. However, the statute itself contains no explicit mention of a signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). For a signature to be deemed necessary, such an obligation would need to arise implicitly from the term “certifying.” Except as discussed further below, Louisiana case law reviewing the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under this provision does not address any requirement of a signature. In one instance, a court restated the statute’s mandate as the necessity for “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another appellate decision upheld cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other decisions offer no further assistance on this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In a particular opinion from a court of appeal, the absence of a signature was referenced as one deficiency in the notice, stating that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court there did not determine that a missing signature alone would invalidate a cancellation notice if the other statutory requirements were satisfied. Similarly to Benitez, the notices in the present record do not specify any individual employee’s name in print or by signature. Unlike in Benitez, though, each of these notices “certifies” the pertinent information. In the absence of more definitive Louisiana case law, we find no basis to conclude that a company must identify a specific employee to validly certify information. The language of the statute expressly indicates that “the insurance premium finance company” accomplishes cancellation by “certifying” specific details within a notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Demanding “strict adherence” to the cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to impose additional requirements not stated in the statute. We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not impose a signature requirement on a notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer. 

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s cancellation process fails to strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto allegedly never receives a cancellation request. The relevant statute states that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs maintain that, before 2017, cancellation requests were stored in a filing room used by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself, and after 2017, the requests are not received by GoAuto because the employee responsible for stamping is part of APAC. Plaintiffs assert that because the statute uses the term “receipt,” as opposed to merely requiring the notice be sent, GoAuto must physically obtain the notice prior to effectuating cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted the word “receipt” for purposes of this statute, nor is there a statutory definition. According to Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024), “receipt” is “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” means “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Using these definitions, we determine that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy upon taking possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As described previously, GoAuto’s computer system receives the emailed notice during the early morning, triggering automatic cancellation. A GoAuto witness described the technical interaction as follows: During cancellation, APAC’s computer system communicates with a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or execute cancellation on the appropriate policy effective for that date. The web service effectively acts as a listener; it continuously awaits requests and, upon receiving one it recognizes, processes the cancellation task accordingly.

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation. At that point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation forms. We restate the statute previously quoted: prior to cancellation, it is required that the insurer has received notice of cancellation sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, reflecting current practices, wherein company computer systems perform tasks previously completed by people. GoAuto’s procedure for accepting and responding to the notice fulfills the statutory requirement. Additionally, we note the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statute on alternate grounds: specifically, that the APAC employee delivering cancellation notices fits within the meaning of a “private carrier.” The district court acknowledged the lack of Louisiana case law interpreting “private carrier” and considered a decision from this court explaining when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier, referencing Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was creative and atypical. Because our conclusion is that the e-mail satisfies the statute, we do not address the correctness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court for certification. They argue that resolving the statutory language’s meaning holds significant consequences for Louisiana’s insurance sector and legal framework. However, we typically exercise caution when it comes to certifying legal questions unless there exists a particularly compelling justification; the option to certify questions is a valuable judicial tool and should not be overused, as reflected in “Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997)”: “the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” The mere fact that a state supreme court has not previously defined a statutory term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. For these reasons, we choose not to certify the proposed question. Because GoAuto’s practices are consistent with the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. Accordingly, we AFFIRM.
