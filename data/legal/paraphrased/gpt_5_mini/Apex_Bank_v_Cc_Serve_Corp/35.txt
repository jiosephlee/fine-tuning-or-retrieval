Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s determination that denied registration for its trademarks. As the Board made errors regarding its assessment of two of the likelihood-of-confusion factors, we partially affirm, partially vacate, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its clients. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is used in relation to credit card services. Id. This registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve markets credit card services bearing the ASPIRE mark by partnering with a bank; that partner bank issues credit cards and establishes corresponding accounts under the ASPIRE brand. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its related companies manage these accounts. Id. Apex Bank, which is a Tennessee-chartered retail bank, operates 18 branches and provides services including personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgages, and loans for both consumers and businesses. J.A. 7029. It does not issue credit cards. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under another brand, employing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to be used with “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the examination phase, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest, arguing that Apex’s marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney allowed both the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to move forward, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademarks, claiming that there was a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that a likelihood of consumer confusion between the marks existed. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the Board’s determinations of law under a de novo standard, while we evaluate its factual findings to determine whether they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence has been described as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark registration should be denied if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Determining whether there is a likelihood of confusion is a legal issue that depends on subsidiary factual findings, which specifically involve an analysis of the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each one of the [thirteen] DuPont factors is a factual inquiry, the findings of which we assess for substantial evidence if they are challenged on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze every DuPont factor—only those that are “relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board committed error in its evaluation of likelihood of confusion, pointing in particular to the Board's consideration of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position and that the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We examine each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods and/or services provided by the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be identical; it is enough to demonstrate that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services (offered by CC Serve) and banking and financing services (offered by Apex). J.A. 24–28. Because “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board began by interpreting the scope of these services. It concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Furthermore, the Board concluded that dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include activities such as extending credit or providing funds through credit card issuance, and therefore found Apex and CC Serve’s services to be “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed third-party registrations covering both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services as evidence that these types of services may originate from a single provider under a unified mark. J.A. 33–34. Because of the significant similarity between the parties’ services, the Board determined that the second DuPont factor weighed heavily toward a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex asserts that the parties’ services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” particularly since CC Serve works with banks to administer credit card programs and does “not [itself] provide banking services.” Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s position. The Board thoroughly reviewed both parties’ service descriptions, and substantial evidence underpins its finding that their services are highly similar. Accordingly, we affirm the Board’s conclusion concerning the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor pertains to “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. Evidence showing third-party use of similar marks on similar products is pertinent for demonstrating that a mark is relatively weak and thus merits only a limited degree of protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In situations where many similar marks exist within a field, it is presumed that customers are more capable of differentiating among the marks and are less prone to confusion caused by such similarity. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Additionally, when evidence exists that consumers have learned to distinguish among comparable marks, this usually suggests that the mark is not commercially strong. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex submitted various exhibits to the Board documenting third-party use of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks involved the word “Aspire” both with respect to credit card services and, more broadly, throughout the financial services sector. The Board ultimately determined that, due to the overlap between Apex and CC Serve’s offerings, the correct relevant consumer base is “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. It concentrated solely on marks associated with credit card services, treating those using Aspire for other financial services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks within credit card services but concluded this number did not reach the threshold of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” usage indicative of mark weakness, citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 (discussing “voluminous evidence” of the registration and use of paw print design elements and noting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”) and Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (recognizing 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board determined, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and accordingly decided that CC Serve’s mark receives the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board was mistaken in confining the relevant public solely to consumers of credit card services, maintaining that the Board should have examined the 42 other third-party marks incorporating Aspire or Aspire-formative elements. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s legal analysis was erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor mandates that the Board examine similar marks for similar goods and services. In its evaluation of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board found the services provided by the parties to be highly similar, which led it to conclude that the second factor greatly favored a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet in addressing the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its review to marks tied only to credit card services, excluding those associated with other banking and financial services, which was incorrect. Our precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand goods to be identical—only similar. See, for example, Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this case, we find the Board’s interpretation of similarity to be excessively narrow. Where the Board has already determined as a factual matter that the parties' services are highly similar—indeed, in some respects legally identical—in its assessment of the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it should apply the same breadth when considering similarity under the remaining factors. There is no justification to adopt a separate and more demanding standard for similarity within the context of the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor examines whether the marks, when considered in their entirety, are alike or unalike regarding their visual appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and the overall impression made on consumers. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the general commercial impression created by the marks and whether there is a likelihood that consumers would be confused about the origin of the services marketed under these marks. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Because the commercial impression is integral to the evaluation under the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s consideration of this factor in the present case; reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a new assessment regarding the commercial strength or weakness of the mark, thereby impacting the commercial impression as a whole. Accordingly, we remand for reevaluation of the first factor as well.

After reviewing the additional arguments presented by the parties, we conclude that they lack merit. With regard to the second DuPont factor, we uphold the Board’s reasoning because there was no legal error, and the record contains substantial evidence supporting the Board’s determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions concerning DuPont factors six and one, and remand the matter so the Board can assess the number and type of similar marks used for similar goods, as well as the marks’ respective appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression, considering its determination that the services in question are highly similar. Accordingly, the decision is AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
