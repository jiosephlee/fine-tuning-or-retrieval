Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of its mark registrations. As the Board incorrectly evaluated two elements of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is used in relation to credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In its business operations, CC Serve collaborates with a bank to offer credit card services under the ASPIRE brand, whereby the bank issues credit cards and establishes accounts bearing the ASPIRE name for consumers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates handle the servicing of these accounts. Id. Apex Bank is a retail bank incorporated in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. Apex operates 18 branch offices and provides products such as personal and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgage loans, as well as consumer and business loans. Id. However, Apex does not provide credit card services. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under a new brand name, specifically using the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While the applications were pending, CC Serve filed a letter of protest alleging that Apex’s marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney permitted the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, which occurred on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s ASPIRE standard character mark. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board, relying on Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), found in favor of CC Serve, determining that confusion among consumers was probable. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently appealed. Our court’s jurisdiction is established pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We examine the Board’s legal determinations without deference, while we review its factual findings to ensure they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration must be denied in a trademark opposition if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The issue of “likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to evaluate all DuPont factors, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in its assessment of the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its evaluation of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors separately below.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the parties’ respective goods or services are. It is not necessary that the services be exactly the same; rather, the evidence must show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board evaluated the degree of similarity between credit card services, provided by CC Serve, and the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first clarified what was encompassed by this service. The Board interpreted “credit card services” to include “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. After reviewing dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” which included extending credit or issuing funds via credit cards, the Board found that the services offered by Apex and CC Serve are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board further referenced third-party registrations encompassing both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services to bolster its determination that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. As a result, the Board determined that the significant similarity between the parties’ services weighed heavily in favor of a likelihood of confusion under the second DuPont factor. J.A. 35. Apex contends on appeal that the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” highlighting that CC Serve partners with banks to manage credit card programs and is “not providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s argument. The Board thoroughly considered the service descriptions provided by both parties, and the record contains substantial evidence supporting the Board’s conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar. Accordingly, we uphold the Board’s finding with respect to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. “Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In instances where the marketplace is saturated with comparable marks, the rationale is that consumers will be better able to discern among marks, thereby reducing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Demonstrating that consumers have learned to distinguish between such marks is indicative of limited commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented several exhibits to the Board evidencing third-party use of marks containing the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks incorporated “Aspire” for credit card-related services and, more broadly, within the financial services sector. The Board determined that, because Apex and CC Serve’s services overlap, the relevant public should be defined as “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board confined its review to marks pertaining to credit card services and disregarded those using “Aspire” for other services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. Ultimately, the Board found that there were nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services, but concluded that this quantity did not constitute the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use necessary to establish weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (noting “voluminous evidence” of registration and use of paw print designs and identifying fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (discussing 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”)). The Board determined that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and therefore found that CC Serve’s mark deserved “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. 

On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred by restricting the relevant public to consumers of credit card services and should have considered the 42 additional third-party marks utilizing Aspire and Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s approach was legally flawed. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to consider similar marks used for similar goods and services. In its assessment of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board had found the parties’ services to be highly similar, which led it to weigh that factor strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, for the sixth DuPont factor, the Board narrowed its analysis to marks related exclusively to credit card services and omitted those pertaining to other banking and financing services. This was a mistake. Our precedent holds that the sixth DuPont factor does not mandate identical goods—only similar ones. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, the Board’s interpretation of similarity was too restrictive. Where the Board has previously determined that the parties’ services are highly similar—indeed, partially legally identical—when considering the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it should apply the same breadth in evaluating similarity under the other DuPont factors. There is no justification for applying a different or stricter legal test for similarity with respect to the sixth DuPont factor. The initial DuPont factor evaluates how similar or different the marks are in their entirety, taking into account appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation centers on the overall commercial impression of the marks and whether there is a likelihood of confusion regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since commercial impression is critical to the first DuPont factor, the Board’s analysis must also be vacated here, as a reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a new conclusion regarding the commercial strength or weakness of the mark, thereby influencing the commercial impression as a whole. Accordingly, we remand for the Board to reevaluate the first factor as well.

We have reviewed the additional arguments advanced by the parties and do not find them convincing. With respect to the second DuPont factor, we uphold the Board’s analysis, as there was no error in its application of the law and substantial evidence underpins its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions relating to DuPont factors six and one and remand the case so that the Board may reassess the number and character of similar marks used on related goods, as well as the visual appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and overall commercial impression of the marks, taking into account its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED-IN-PART, VACATED-IN-PART, AND

REMANDED
