Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank seeks review of a ruling from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board denying the registration of its trademarks. As the Board incorrectly evaluated two factors in its likelihood-of-confusion determination, we partly affirm, partly vacate, and remand the matter for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to consumers. J.A. 6927–28. The company holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark in association with its credit card offerings. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998 and the mark has a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve's credit card services under the ASPIRE mark involve collaborating with a partner bank, which issues credit cards and manages related accounts bearing the ASPIRE brand to customers. J.A. 6930. The management of these accounts is handled by CC Serve and its related entities. Id. Apex Bank, on the other hand, is a Tennessee-chartered retail bank. J.A. 7029. With 18 branch offices, Apex provides products such as personal and business checking and savings accounts, mortgages, and loans for both consumer and business needs. Id. It does not currently provide credit card services. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under a separate branding, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use trademark applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, intending their use for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the examination process, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest, claiming Apex’s applied-for marks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s mark. J.A. 4576. Notwithstanding, the trademark examining attorney approved the publication of Apex’s ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, and the marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s marks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with its standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that a likelihood of consumer confusion existed between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex sought review of this decision. Our court possesses jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We assess the Board’s determinations of law without deference, while its factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). “Substantial evidence” consists of “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition must result in a refusal of registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The inquiry into likelihood of confusion is a legal one, drawn from foundational factual findings, specifically those set forth under the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors constitutes a factual question, and any findings regarding those factors are reviewed for substantial evidence if challenged on appeal. See Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze every DuPont factor, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board committed error in assessing the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its evaluation of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. According to the Board's findings, the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the parties' goods or services are. The services at issue do not need to be exactly the same—the relevant inquiry is whether “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [whether] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services, as offered by CC Serve, to Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board initially determined what those services encompass. The Board found that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Further, the Board concluded that because definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include the extension of credit or provision of funds through credit card issuance, some of Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed third-party registrations for (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, supporting its determination that such services are often offered from a single source under one mark. J.A. 33–34. Thus, the Board determined that the marked similarity between the services made this DuPont factor weigh strongly towards a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. Apex, on appeal, contends that the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” in particular arguing that, by partnering with banks to manage credit card programs, CC Serve is “not providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s position persuasive. The Board thoroughly reviewed both parties’ service descriptions, and substantial evidence underpins its finding that the services are highly similar. Therefore, we uphold the Board’s conclusion on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor addresses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. Evidence of third-party usage of analogous marks on comparable goods is pertinent in demonstrating that a particular mark is relatively weak and, accordingly, merits only a limited extent of protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In markets saturated with comparable marks, it is assumed that consumers can more easily distinguish among marks, which reduces the likelihood of confusion arising from similar marks. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Proof that customers have been instructed to differentiate between various marks suggests a lack of commercial strength for any individual mark. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex provided the Board with multiple exhibits documenting third-party use of marks featuring “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks were utilized in relation to credit card services and, more generally, within the financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the service overlap between Apex and CC Serve, the correct relevant public consists of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Therefore, the Board limited its review to marks associated specifically with credit card services and excluded marks for other financial services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board established that there were nine Aspire-derivative marks pertaining to credit card services but concluded that nine instances fell short of demonstrating the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” presence needed to establish weakness under precedents. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (discussing “voluminous evidence” of paw print design marks, noting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (characterizing 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”)). The Board held that, “[CC Serve] has not demonstrated that its mark enjoys any particular commercial strength, yet [Apex] has not established that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it warrants a narrowly circumscribed protection,” so CC Serve’s mark is entitled to “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex argues that the Board mistakenly limited the relevant public to credit card service consumers, asserting the Board should have taken into account the 42 other third-party “Aspire” or “Aspire-formative” marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s evaluation is flawed as a matter of law. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to take into account similar marks on similar goods and services. Notably, in its discussion of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board previously found the parties’ services to be highly similar, a determination that weighed heavily in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, when the Board considered the sixth DuPont factor, it limited the marks considered to those relating solely to credit card services and excluded marks connected to broader banking and financing services, which constitutes error. Our precedent clarifies that the sixth DuPont factor does not mandate that goods be identical—merely similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this case, the Board’s application of similarity is overly restrictive. Once the Board has found, as a matter of fact, that the parties’ services are highly similar—and even partially legally identical—for the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it must apply an equivalent breadth to the sixth factor. There is no justification for imposing a narrower and more rigorous legal criterion for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the total commercial impression created by the marks and whether it is likely to lead to consumer confusion regarding the origin of the services provided under each mark. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the commercial impression is a key component under the first DuPont factor, we must also vacate the Board’s analysis of this factor because the outcome of the sixth DuPont factor on remand may change the assessment of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and alter the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for further consideration of the first factor as well.

We have reviewed the additional contentions raised by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s evaluation regarding the second DuPont factor, concluding that there was no error in its application of the law and that substantial evidence backs the Board's determination that the parties’ services are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s findings as to DuPont factors six and one and remand the matter for the Board to assess the quantity and nature of similar marks utilized with similar goods, as well as the marks’ appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression, taking into account its determination that the parties’ services are very similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
