Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of registration for Apex’s trademarks. Since the Board made mistakes in evaluating two factors of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand the case.

I

CC Serve is engaged in providing credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. The company holds Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which is registered for use with credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date established as October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve delivers its credit card services using the ASPIRE mark by partnering with a bank, through which the bank issues credit cards and account services under the ASPIRE brand to clients. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates are responsible for servicing these accounts. Id. Apex Bank is a Tennessee-chartered retail banking institution. J.A. 7029. It operates 18 branches and provides products such as personal and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgage loans, and loans to consumers and businesses. Id. Apex Bank does not provide credit card services. Id. Apex intends to launch an internet-based bank under a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were pending, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, claiming Apex’s proposed marks bore a confusing similarity to CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, contending that there was a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that the marks were likely to cause consumer confusion. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. This court exercises jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We examine the Board’s legal determinations without deference, while its factual findings are assessed under the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act requires the denial of trademark registration where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of likelihood of confusion constitutes a legal issue that rests on findings of fact pertaining to the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to address every DuPont factor, but only those that are pertinent and supported by the record. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in its evaluation of likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its consideration of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board found that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We examine each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates the degree of similarity between the goods and/or services offered by the parties. It is not necessary for the services to be exactly the same; rather, it must be shown that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services, provided by CC Serve, with banking and financing services, offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since the Trademark ID Manual had removed “credit card services” as an entry following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first sought to clarify what services were encompassed by this term. The Board concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board further found that the dictionary meanings of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include the extension of credit or provision of funds through credit card issuance, resulting in a determination that Apex and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board examined third-party registrations covering both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services to reinforce its finding that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Ultimately, the Board determined that the high similarity between the parties’ services meant that the second DuPont factor strongly supported a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services provided by the parties are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” particularly asserting that CC Serve is only partnering with banks to manage credit card programs and is “not providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s argument persuasive. The Board gave thorough attention to the respective service descriptions, and there is substantial evidence backing the Board’s conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s decision regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As recognized in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” In circumstances where the market is saturated with comparable marks, it is believed that consumers are more likely to distinguish among these marks, decreasing the probability of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Additionally, proof that consumers have been trained to differentiate marks in this way supports a finding of commercial weakness. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex provided the Board with several exhibits reflecting third-party use of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks incorporated “Aspire” for credit card-related services and, more broadly, across the financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the service overlap between Apex and CC Serve, the correct relevant public comprised “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board focused exclusively on marks for credit card services, finding marks using Aspire for other financial services to be “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks relating to credit card services but concluded that this number did not equate to the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use previously recognized as indicative of weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, regarding “voluminous evidence” of registration and use and referencing fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”; Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (finding 26 third-party marks to be “a considerable number”)). The Board further determined that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” concluding that CC Serve’s mark warrants the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex asserts the Board improperly confined the relevant public to consumers of credit card services and that it should have also considered the 42 other third-party Aspire and Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in Apex’s argument and conclude that the Board’s approach contained a legal error. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to consider similar marks in connection with similar goods and services. In analyzing the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board found that the parties’ services are highly similar and accordingly determined that this factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, when reviewing the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its examination only to marks for credit card services and excluded those involving other banking and financial services, including in APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 7. This limitation constituted an error. Precedent confirms that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand that the goods be identical—only similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We conclude the Board adopted an overly restrictive view of similarity. When, in the context of the second DuPont factor, the Board has already determined that the services are highly similar, and indeed partially legally identical, J.A. 27–28, it should employ the same breadth when considering similarity under other DuPont factors. There is no justification for applying a narrower or stricter standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor examines the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the overall commercial impression conveyed by the marks and the likelihood that consumers might be confused about the origin of the services provided under each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the analysis of commercial impression is integral to evaluating the first DuPont factor, and because a new assessment of the sixth DuPont factor could potentially alter the determination regarding the mark’s commercial strength or weakness—and thus its overall commercial impression—we must also set aside the Board’s consideration of the first factor and remand for further review.

We have reviewed the additional contentions raised by the parties and do not find them convincing. Regarding the second DuPont factor, we uphold the Board’s reasoning, concluding that its legal analysis was correct and that substantial evidence backs the Board’s determination that the services provided by the parties are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations on DuPont factors six and one and return the case for further proceedings, instructing the Board to assess both the quantity and nature of analogous marks employed for comparable goods and to reevaluate the visual, aural, associative, and commercial impact of the marks, taking into account its conclusion that the parties’ services closely resemble one another. AFFIRMED-IN-PART, VACATED-IN-PART, AND

REMANDED
