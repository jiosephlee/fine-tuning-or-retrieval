Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied registration of its trademarks. Since the Board made mistakes in evaluating two of the likelihood-of-confusion factors, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which it uses with its credit card services. Id. This ASPIRE mark was registered in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In its business, CC Serve collaborates with a bank that issues credit cards and accounts branded as ASPIRE to customers, while CC Serve and its affiliates handle servicing of those accounts. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank, a Tennessee-chartered retail bank, operates 18 branch offices and provides products including personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. J.A. 7029. Apex does not issue credit cards. Id. Apex intends to launch an internet banking platform under a separate brand, specifically using the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were pending, CC Serve filed a letter of protest contending that Apex’s marks created a likelihood of confusion with its own. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, which took place on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In the case of APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP., CC Serve filed an opposition proceeding against Apex’s trademarks in February 2020, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition on the basis of Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that it was probable consumers would be confused by the marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction is proper pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the Board’s determinations of law without deference, while we review its factual findings to determine whether they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence means “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration must be denied in a trademark opposition where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The issue of “likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based upon underlying factual determinations, specifically those under the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors constitutes a factual inquiry, and our review is for substantial evidence when these findings are challenged on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to address all DuPont factors, but need only consider those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made mistakes in its assessment of the likelihood of confusion, focusing particularly on its consideration of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board found that the sixth DuPont factor did not favor Apex, whereas the first and second DuPont factors supported CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will consider each factor individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods and/or services of the parties are. The services do not have to be exactly the same; it is sufficient if the evidence shows that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services, which CC Serve provides, with Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Due to the removal of “credit card services” from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the meaning of the services described. The Board found that “credit card services” includes activities such as issuing credit cards for purchasing, providing counseling about credit card debt, processing transactions and payments by credit card, authorizing credit card use, issuing notifications and alerts for credit cards, managing credit card accounts, and giving access to credit scores. J.A. 25. Furthermore, the Board found that standard dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit or offering funding through credit card issuance, leading to the conclusion that some of Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. In addition, the Board examined third-party registrations covering both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services to support its determination that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. The Board therefore determined that the significant similarity between the parties’ services caused the second factor in APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 to weigh strongly toward a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services offered by the parties “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” and emphasizes that CC Serve partners with banks to administer credit card programs rather than providing banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s contention. The Board thoroughly evaluated the service descriptions for both parties, and there is substantial evidence supporting the Board’s determination that the services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s finding with respect to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor assesses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As established in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods” is pertinent for demonstrating that a particular mark is comparatively weak and entitled to only limited protection. Where a marketplace contains many similar marks, it is presumed that consumers become adept at differentiating these marks, thereby diminishing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence suggesting that the public has learned to distinguish among such marks supports a finding that the mark lacks commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented to the Board multiple exhibits demonstrating third-party usage of “Aspire” marks. J.A. 40–46. These marks used the term “Aspire” in relation to credit card services and, in a broader sense, within the financial services industry. The Board, noting overlap between Apex’s and CC Serve’s services, identified the relevant public as “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Consequently, the Board considered only marks for credit card services, finding marks for other types of financial services to be “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. According to the Board, there were nine “Aspire”-formative marks for credit card services. However, the Board found that this quantity did not constitute “considerable” or “ubiquitous” usage that would establish the mark as weak. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, referencing “voluminous evidence” of use and citing fourteen significant examples; Juice Generation, 794 F.3d at 1337 n.1, 1339, referring to 26 third-party marks as a “considerable number”). The Board determined, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” thus according CC Serve’s mark the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. Apex, on appeal, argues the Board erred by narrowing the relevant audience to credit card services customers and by excluding 42 other third-party “Aspire” and “Aspire”-formative marks from its analysis. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We conclude the Board’s analysis was flawed as a matter of law. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to account for similar marks used on similar goods and services. In its assessment of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board found the parties’ services were highly similar, leading it to weigh this factor strongly in favor of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, during its sixth DuPont factor analysis, the Board limited its review to marks tied only to credit card services, disregarding those related to broader banking and financing services—this constituted error. Our precedent does not require identical goods for the sixth DuPont factor—similarity suffices. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. Here, the Board’s approach to similarity was overly restrictive. Once the Board finds that the services are highly similar—in part even legally identical, as recognized in its second DuPont factor analysis, J.A. 27–28—that same breadth should be maintained when assessing similarity for the remaining factors. There is no justification for a stricter interpretation of similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s determination under the sixth DuPont factor and remand for the Board to reassess the scope of third-party marks to be considered, taking into account its factual finding that the parties' services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor addresses whether the marks are alike or different overall with regard to their look, sound, meaning, and the commercial impression they convey. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This assessment centers on the total commercial impression produced by the marks and whether it is likely that consumers would be confused about the origin of the services connected to each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since commercial impression is a crucial element in the evaluation under the first DuPont factor, we are compelled to vacate the Board’s consideration of this factor as well, given that a new evaluation of the sixth DuPont factor might change the conclusion about the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and thus alter the commercial impression. As a result, we remand for the Board to reconsider the first factor.

After reviewing the additional arguments raised by the parties, we conclude that they lack merit. We uphold the Board's reasoning regarding the second DuPont factor, as there was no legal error in its analysis and substantial evidence substantiates the Board’s conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations concerning DuPont factors six and one and remand the matter so the Board may reexamine the quantity and character of similar marks employed in connection with similar goods, as well as assess the appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression of the marks, taking into account the established similarity of the parties’ services. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED

Costs to appellant.
