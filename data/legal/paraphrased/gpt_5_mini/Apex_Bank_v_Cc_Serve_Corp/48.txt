Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of its trademark registration applications. Given that the Board made mistakes regarding its consideration of two factors within the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is a business that provides credit card services to its clients. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which is used in relation to credit card services. Id. This ASPIRE mark registration was granted in 1998 and claims a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In offering its credit card services under the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve collaborates with a bank, which then issues credit cards and corresponding accounts branded with ASPIRE to its users. J.A. 6930. The management of these accounts is handled by CC Serve along with its affiliated entities. Id. Apex Bank is a retail banking institution chartered in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. It operates 18 branches and provides products such as personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, as well as loans for individual and business purposes. Id. Credit cards are not among the services offered by Apex Bank. Id. Apex intends to launch an online banking service under a new brand, utilizing both the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking to register these ASPIRE BANK marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, contending that the marks sought by Apex bore a confusing similarity to CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Nevertheless, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, which occurred on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to the registration of Apex’s marks, asserting that there existed a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that the marks were likely to cause confusion among consumers. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We conduct a de novo review of the Board’s legal determinations and assess its factual findings to determine whether they are supported by substantial evidence. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition proceeding mandates the refusal of registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Determination of a “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a legal question that depends on factual findings relating to the DuPont factors. See M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors involves a factual determination, the sufficiency of which we examine under the substantial evidence standard when appealed. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is required to address only those DuPont factors that are pertinent and reflected in the record, rather than every factor. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made errors in assessing likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its evaluation of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, and found that both the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors separately.

A

The second DuPont factor examines the degree of similarity between the goods and/or services offered by the parties. The services at issue do not have to be exactly the same—rather, it is sufficient to show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board analyzed the resemblance between CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Since the listing for “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve registered, the Board initially clarified the meaning of those identified services. It concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Further, the Board found that dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” encompass the act of extending credit or furnishing funds through issuing credit cards, thereby rendering Apex’s and CC Serve’s services “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board relied on third-party registrations encompassing both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services to substantiate that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Ultimately, because of the significant overlap between the parties’ services, the Board determined that the second DuPont factor weighed strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve works with banks to administer credit card programs and does not itself offer banking services. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s argument persuasive. The Board thoroughly evaluated the parties’ service descriptions, and the record contains substantial evidence supporting its determination that the services are highly similar. We uphold the Board’s decision with respect to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As established in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” In markets saturated with comparable marks, customers are expected to be better at distinguishing between such marks, thus reducing the probability of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Proof demonstrating that consumers can differentiate among such marks often suggests a lack of commercial strength in a mark. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex provided multiple exhibits to the Board reflecting third-party use of marks featuring the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks used “Aspire” for credit card-related services and, in a broader sense, within the financial services sector. The Board determined that, given the overlap of services between Apex and CC Serve, the relevant consumer group is “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Limiting its consideration to marks used for credit card services, the Board dismissed marks involving Aspire used for other types of services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. It found there were nine Aspire-based marks in connection with credit card services, but considered this number insufficient to constitute the level of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use deemed to indicate mark weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (referencing “voluminous evidence” of paw print design registration and use, identifying fourteen significant examples of third-party marks); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (regarding 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”).

The Board stated, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and therefore determined that CC Serve’s mark should receive “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex argues that it was improper for the Board to restrict the relevant consumer base to users of credit card services and insists the Board should have taken into account the 42 additional third-party marks employing Aspire or derivative forms. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s approach suffered from legal error.

The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to consider similar marks on similar, not identical, goods and services. In its evaluation of the second DuPont factor, discussed further below in Section III.A, the Board concluded that the parties’ services were highly similar, and accordingly found the second factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Conversely, in its assessment of the sixth DuPont factor, the Board restricted its examination to marks associated only with credit card services, disregarding those used with other banking or financial offerings. This represents a misapplication of the law.

Precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not necessitate complete identity of goods—similarity suffices. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this context, we find that the Board’s interpretation of similarity was overly narrow. Where the Board has already determined, in analyzing the second DuPont factor, that the services in question are highly similar or even partially legally identical, J.A. 27–28, the scope applied in analyzing similarity for other DuPont factors should not be diminished. There is no basis to apply a different, stricter legal criterion for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remand

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates how alike or different the marks are as a whole, considering their look, sound, meaning, and commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This assessment emphasizes the overall commercial impression and the likelihood that consumers would be confused regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that commercial impression is central to the inquiry under the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s assessment on this point, because reconsidering the sixth DuPont factor may alter the evaluation of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, thereby influencing the total commercial impression. Consequently, we remand for reevaluation of the first factor as well.

After reviewing the additional arguments presented by the parties, we determine that they are unconvincing. We uphold the Board’s analysis regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board did not commit legal error and its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations concerning DuPont factors six and one, remanding the matter so that the Board may reassess both the quantity and character of similar marks in use for analogous goods, as well as the visual, phonetic, associative, and market impressions of the marks, taking into account its previous finding that the services at issue are highly similar. AFFIRMED-IN-PART, VACATED-IN-PART, AND

REMANDED
