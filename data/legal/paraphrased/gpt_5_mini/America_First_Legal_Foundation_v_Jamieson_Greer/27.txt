Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to determine if a federal agency unjustifiably rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved AFL’s claims adversely to it on substantive grounds. We find, however, that AFL does not have standing under Article III to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) functions as an investigative and prosecutorial entity created to pursue “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). The Civil Service Reform Act authorizes the OSC to look into “prohibited personnel practices” and, when appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board for either corrective remedies under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions as per 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 specifies the procedures for handling corrective action in cases involving prohibited personnel practices and includes requirements that the individual bringing the complaint receive updates on the status of the OSC’s investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) the authority to investigate and pursue remedies for certain other types of misconduct by federal employees. Pertinently, the provision states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, refusals to disclose information in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When such FOIA-related violations are alleged, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA includes an independent section concerning enforcement by the OSC. According to this provision, if a court (1) directs the release of agency documents that were wrongly withheld under FOIA, (2) grants attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” the OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In seeking to examine judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice for specific email communications between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division rejected the request on questionable grounds, claiming it does not search employee email accounts for pertinent records unless the employee agrees. Subsequently, AFL filed a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ. In the course of that litigation, DOJ has already produced—and remains in the process of producing—documents responsive to AFL’s request. The adequacy of this production is not the subject of the present matter.

AFL additionally requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search staff emails in the absence of consent. AFL relied on section 1216(a)(3), which requires that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” when FOIA withholdings appear arbitrary. However, OSC asserted that it lacked authority to initiate an inquiry, as the three conditions necessary for an investigation pursuant to section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been met. AFL maintained that section 1216 required OSC to investigate regardless of FOIA’s provisions.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed its case on the grounds that it failed to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict OSC's authority to investigate purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). As a result, the court stated that OSC had committed a legal error by refusing to conduct an investigation. Id. Nevertheless, the court chose not to mandate that OSC investigate any FOIA policy, reasoning that section 1216(c) vested such a determination within OSC's discretion. Id. at *6. The matter was remanded to OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC once again opted not to investigate, AFL sought entry of final judgment, after which the district court dismissed AFL's outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL subsequently appealed, and our review proceeds on a de novo basis. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substance of the case, we are required to determine if AFL has met the prerequisites for Article III standing, which is essential to the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither party discussed standing in their initial briefs, we directed them to provide supplemental briefing on this issue.

The federal judicial power under Article III of the Constitution is limited to adjudicating specific “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing arises from this limitation. Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). For a plaintiff to demonstrate standing under Article III, they must show that they experienced an injury directly attributable to the defendant and that a court ruling in their favor would likely redress that injury. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). This injury must amount to “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent,” as opposed to being abstract or speculative. Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When facing a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff is required to assert facts that make it plausible they satisfy each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL argues that OSC infringed upon its asserted procedural right to an investigation as provided by section 1216. However, as established in Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), and further reinforced by Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), the mere loss of a procedural right without any related tangible interest being impacted—a procedural right in the abstract—does not suffice to establish standing under Article III. To address this issue, AFL asserts that OSC's failure to initiate an investigation has resulted in two specific, concrete informational injuries: first, the Department of Justice has not released the records that AFL sought under FOIA; and second, OSC has not delivered the required notices and information associated with an investigation pursuant to section 1214.

By characterizing its claim in this manner, AFL attempts to benefit from the less stringent standing standards applicable in matters where a procedural injury is claimed. According to precedent, “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005); see also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Under these circumstances, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that infringement of the procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL cannot meet any of these criteria, nor can it fulfill the more lenient redressability requirement. As AFL fails to clear even this lower standing threshold for procedural harms, it is unnecessary for us to address whether the asserted injuries should properly be regarded as procedural or substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing asserts that OSC’s omission to investigate DOJ pursuant to section 1216 has blocked AFL from acquiring records it sought via FOIA. However, this argument is undermined by insurmountable causation and redressability issues. Any informational harm resulting from DOJ’s denial of access to agency records is directly attributable to DOJ, not OSC. Furthermore, the supposed link between OSC’s failure to initiate a section 1216 investigation and DOJ’s failure to respond to the FOIA request is too indirect and uncertain to meet the Article III standing requirements. The proposed sequence of causation proceeds as follows: Had OSC performed the investigation claimed to be required by section 1216, it could have chosen to take corrective measures under section 1214 or to initiate disciplinary proceedings under section 1215 against DOJ personnel. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB might have opted to impose penalties on those employees for improperly withholding FOIA records. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Thereafter, DOJ could have decided to provide the records requested. This hypothetical sequence depends on speculative decisions by two governmental entities—the MSPB and DOJ—neither of which is a litigant here, yet “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also requires speculation as to how OSC would exercise its discretionary enforcement authority, something courts typically may not direct. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given this tenuous and uncertain sequence, AFL fails to demonstrate that a favorable court ruling “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also disregards the general rule that redressability depends on the binding effect of a judgment upon the parties to the case, not on the possibility that the court’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Consequently, AFL cannot assert standing based upon its interest in records sought from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative standing argument—predicated on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC investigation—fares equally poorly. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff must suffer a concrete harm from being denied information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (quoting Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) obliges OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not confer a right to obtain information. AFL argues that if OSC were to pursue the purportedly required investigation under section 1216, OSC might opt to furnish AFL with notifications and information similar to those mandated when OSC investigates a supposed “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Yet, AFL does not argue that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214’s notice requirements to investigations conducted under section 1216. On the contrary, AFL concedes precedent stating that OSC’s “decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements” in a section 1216 inquiry is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Without any asserted statutory entitlement to receive information pursuant to section 1214, AFL cannot show the concrete injury necessary for this theory of standing. Similarly, since OSC is not required to provide notice in accordance with section 1214 in the context of a section 1216 investigation, a court order compelling an investigation under section 1216 would not be likely to remedy AFL’s second claimed injury.

III

Since AFL does not have standing under Article III, we set aside the district court’s judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
