Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine if a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s Freedom of Information Act application. The district court resolved AFL’s claims adversely on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not possess Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as both an investigative and prosecutorial body, created to promote "a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC holds the authority to examine allegations of "prohibited personnel practices" and, where justified, to initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board—seeking corrective measures under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary penalties pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 delineates the process for obtaining corrective relief related to prohibited personnel practices, including requirements to inform the individual making the complaint regarding the status of any ongoing investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants the OSC authority to look into and pursue remedies for certain additional types of misconduct committed by government employees. Pertinent to this case, it stipulates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, refusals to disclose information in violation of the Freedom of Information Act. See U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When such FOIA violations are alleged, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA establishes a distinct enforcement mechanism for OSC. Under its terms, when a court (1) directs the release of records that were wrongfully withheld under FOIA, (2) grants an award of attorneys' fees against the agency, and (3) expressly questions if the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its inquiry into judicial recusals, AFL sought from the Department of Justice specific email communications exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division refused the request, allegedly on the questionable basis that it does not conduct searches of employees’ emails for responsive materials without securing the employee’s consent. Subsequently, AFL filed a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ. Within that litigation, DOJ has provided—and continues to provide—records that are responsive to the request. The adequacy of DOJ’s production is not under consideration in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported policy under FOIA whereby employee emails were not searched absent consent. Relying on section 1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” into arbitrary withholdings under FOIA, AFL pressed its case. OSC, however, asserted it lacked jurisdiction to investigate, reasoning that the three conditions specified in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) that would necessitate such an investigation were not present. OSC took the position that its investigative mandate was not activated under FOIA’s terms for the alleged policy, but AFL maintained that section 1216 imposed such a requirement irrespective of FOIA’s limitations.

The district court issued a partial ruling in AFL's favor but ultimately dismissed the action for failure to state a claim. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not constrain OSC's authority to examine claimed FOIA violations under section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court found that OSC’s refusal to initiate an investigation was premised on a misinterpretation of the law. Id. However, the court refused to compel OSC to investigate any particular FOIA policy, concluding that section 1216(c) reserves such determinations to OSC’s judgment. Id. at *6. The court sent the case back to OSC for renewed assessment. Id. When OSC once again opted not to pursue an investigation, AFL sought the entry of final judgment, resulting in the district court dismissing its outstanding claims. J.A. 68. AFL appealed, and our examination of the matter is conducted de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine if AFL met the criteria for Article III standing, as this pertains to the district court's subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Since standing was not discussed by either party in the initial briefing, we requested additional briefing specifically regarding this issue.

The judicial authority granted to federal courts by Article III of the Constitution is limited to adjudicating specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The principle of standing is derived from this case-or-controversy mandate. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). Under Article III, a plaintiff establishes standing by demonstrating an injury attributable to the defendant and which is likely to be remedied by a favorable ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). The injury at issue must constitute “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized,” as well as “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When faced with a motion to dismiss, the plaintiff bears the burden of pleading facts sufficient to support all necessary elements of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached what it claims is its procedural entitlement to an investigation as provided in section 1216. However, the Supreme Court has held that the loss of a procedural right, absent a concrete interest impacted by that loss—referred to as a “procedural right in vacuo”—does not suffice for Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). In an effort to address this issue, AFL argues that the OSC’s decision not to investigate has resulted in two specific informational injuries: first, the Department of Justice has not furnished the records that AFL sought under FOIA; and second, OSC has failed to deliver the notifications and information mandated by section 1214 in the course of an investigation.

By characterizing its claim this way, AFL attempts to benefit from the more lenient standing standards applicable to claims of procedural injury. As explained in Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), “Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In these circumstances, plaintiffs still must demonstrate that the procedural right’s violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. AFL does not meet these criteria, nor even a relaxed redressability requirement. Since AFL fails to establish standing under even these less stringent standards for procedural injuries, we need not address the distinction between procedural and substantive injuries in this context.

AFL’s primary argument for standing is that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ under section 1216 has blocked AFL’s access to the FOIA records it sought. However, this argument fails due to insurmountable issues with causation and redressability. Any informational harm resulting from DOJ’s denial of access to agency records can be directly attributed to DOJ, not OSC. Moreover, the alleged link between OSC’s inaction under section 1216 and DOJ’s noncompliance with AFL’s FOIA request is too conjectural and remote to satisfy Article III standing requirements. The sequence suggested by AFL is as follows: OSC, had it conducted the supposedly mandatory section 1216 inquiry, might have initiated corrective measures under section 1214 or disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ employees involved. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, MSPB might have imposed penalties on those employees for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Finally, DOJ could have elected to release the records at issue. This argument relies on speculation about decisions by two government bodies—the MSPB and DOJ—that are not parties to this litigation. Yet, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). It also invites speculation about the exercise of OSC’s enforcement discretion, which the judiciary typically lacks authority to compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given this tenuous causal connection, AFL has not demonstrated that a judgment in its favor “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational injury. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). AFL also neglects the general rule that redressability usually depends on the binding effect of the judgment on the parties involved, not whether the court’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Consequently, AFL is unable to establish standing based on its claimed entitlement to DOJ records under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative basis for standing—which relies on its claimed right to the information produced by the OSC investigation—fails as well. In order to establish informational standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate a tangible harm resulting from the denial of information to which a statutory provision entitles them. See Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, even assuming section 1216(a)(3) imposes on OSC a duty to investigate FOIA violations, it does not create an entitlement to receive information. AFL argues that, should OSC undertake a mandatory investigation pursuant to section 1216, it may elect to supply AFL with notifications and information analogous to what is required if OSC were probing a “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not argue that OSC is obligated to apply the section 1214 notification standards to section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes precedent holding that OSC retains discretion regarding whether to adopt section 1214 procedures in section 1216 inquiries. See Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Because there is no claimed statutory right under section 1214 to obtain such information, AFL cannot demonstrate a concrete injury to sustain its second standing argument. Furthermore, since OSC is not required to furnish section 1214 notifications within a section 1216 investigation, an order directing OSC to conduct such an investigation would not be likely to address AFL’s asserted harm under its second theory.

III

Since AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss due to the absence of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
