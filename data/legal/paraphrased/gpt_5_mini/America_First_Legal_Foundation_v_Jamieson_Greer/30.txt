Title: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-5168
Author: Katsas, Circuit Judge

America First Legal Foundation initiated this action seeking to require the Office of Special Counsel to examine whether a federal agency improperly rejected AFL’s request under the Freedom of Information Act. The district court resolved the case by dismissing AFL’s claims on substantive grounds. We determine that AFL does not have Article III standing to advance these claims.

I

A

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as an agency tasked with investigation and prosecution, created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the Civil Service Reform Act, OSC has the authority to investigate allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, where appropriate, initiate enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board, either seeking corrective measures pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 1214 or disciplinary sanctions under 5 U.S.C. § 1215. Section 1214 describes the procedures applicable to corrective action in cases involving prohibited personnel practices and includes requirements to inform the complainant regarding the progress of the OSC’s investigation. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

Section 1216 grants OSC the authority to examine and pursue remedies for certain types of misconduct committed by government personnel. Pertinent to this case, the statute mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, instances of withholding not allowed by the Freedom of Information Act. U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). When these alleged FOIA violations occur, OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same manner as if a prohibited personnel practice were at issue.” Id. § 1216(c).

FOIA provides an independent mechanism for enforcement by the OSC. Under its terms, when a court (1) directs that agency records unlawfully withheld under FOIA be disclosed, (2) grants attorney’s fees against the agency, and (3) expressly raises the issue of whether the agency’s withholding was “arbitrary or capricious,” the OSC is required to “promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

B

In connection with its investigation into judicial recusals, AFL submitted a request to the Department of Justice seeking specific emails exchanged between an external attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. AFL asserts that the Civil Division declined the request on the questionable basis that it does not search employees’ email accounts for relevant documents without those employees’ consent. Subsequently, AFL filed a lawsuit against DOJ pursuant to FOIA. During the course of that litigation, DOJ has already provided—and continues to provide—documents that are responsive to the request. The adequacy of DOJ’s production is not in dispute in this matter.

AFL further requested that OSC examine DOJ’s purported FOIA practice of declining to search employee email accounts absent their consent. AFL cited section 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” concerning arbitrary withholdings under FOIA. OSC asserted it lacked authority to investigate, stating that the three requirements necessary to initiate an investigation under section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were unmet. Regarding the alleged FOIA policy, AFL contended that section 1216 obligates OSC to investigate regardless of whether FOIA requires it.

The district court partially ruled in favor of AFL but ultimately dismissed the case on the grounds that no claim had been stated. The court determined that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not restrict the OSC's authority to examine purported FOIA violations pursuant to section 1216. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Accordingly, the court stated that OSC’s refusal to investigate was predicated on an error of law. Id. Despite this, the court refused to compel OSC to undertake an investigation into any FOIA policy, concluding that, under section 1216(c), such a determination is entrusted to the OSC’s discretion. Id. at *6. The court therefore remanded the case to the OSC for additional consideration. Id. When OSC once again chose not to investigate, AFL requested final judgment, and the district court dismissed the remaining claims. J.A. 68. AFL then filed an appeal, and our review proceeds de novo. N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020).

II

Prior to addressing the substantive issues, it is necessary to determine whether AFL fulfilled the prerequisites for Article III standing, which relates to the district court’s subject-matter jurisdiction. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). Because neither side initially raised the matter of standing, we required the parties to submit additional briefs on that issue.

The Constitution’s Article III confines the exercise of federal “judicial Power” to specified “Cases” and “Controversies.” U.S. Const. Art. III, § 2, cl. 1. The doctrine of standing is derived from this limitation. See Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737, 750 (1984). In order to establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury attributable to the defendant that would likely be remedied by a favorable ruling. Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Such an injury must constitute an “invasion of a legally protected interest” and must be both “concrete and particularized” as well as “actual or imminent,” rather than “conjectural or hypothetical.” Id. at 560 (cleaned up). When moving to dismiss, the plaintiff must allege facts that plausibly support each component of standing. Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).

In its supplemental briefing, AFL asserts that OSC breached its supposed procedural entitlement to an investigation as set forth in section 1216. However, the "loss of a procedural right without a related concrete interest that is impacted by the loss—a procedural right in vacuo—does not suffice" for the purpose of establishing Article III standing. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see also Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024). To address this deficiency, AFL argues that OSC’s decision not to conduct an investigation has resulted in two specific, informational harms: first, DOJ failed to deliver the documents AFL requested pursuant to FOIA; second, OSC did not furnish the notifications and details required in connection with an investigation under section 1214.

By presenting its claim in this manner, AFL aims to avail itself of the more lenient standing requirements applicable in procedural injury cases. As explained in “Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005),” when plaintiffs contend they have suffered harm due to the violation of a statutory procedural right created to protect a specific, threatened concrete interest, courts will “relax—although not completely dispense with—the elements of imminence and redressability, but not those of injury in fact or causation.” See also Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658, 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). In this context, plaintiffs are required to demonstrate that the breach of a procedural right “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157. However, AFL is unable to satisfy either this requirement or even the relaxed burden concerning redressability. Given that AFL falls short even under the less stringent standing criteria for procedural injuries, it is unnecessary for us to determine whether the purported injuries at issue are procedural rather than substantive.

AFL’s initial argument for standing asserts that OSC’s inaction under section 1216 has obstructed AFL’s access to the records sought under FOIA. This argument is fundamentally flawed as to causation and redressability. Specifically, any informational harm resulting from DOJ’s decision to withhold agency records can be directly attributed to DOJ rather than to OSC. Additionally, any asserted link between OSC’s omission to initiate a section 1216 inquiry and DOJ’s noncompliance with the FOIA request is highly conjectural and insufficiently direct to establish Article III standing. The proposed sequence of causation is as follows: Had OSC undertaken the purportedly obligatory section 1216 investigation, it might have opted to pursue corrective action under section 1214 or disciplinary measures under section 1215 against DOJ personnel alleged to be at fault. See 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214(b)(3), 1215(a)(1). Subsequently, the MSPB could have determined to impose sanctions on such employees for arbitrary withholding of FOIA material. See id. §§ 1214(b)(4)(A), 1215(a)(3)(A). Following this, DOJ might have then chosen to provide the requested documents. However, this rationale is contingent upon speculative decisions by two government bodies, DOJ and the MSPB, neither of which are litigants here, and “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024) (cleaned up). Additionally, it presumes hypothetical decisions by OSC regarding enforcement, a realm of discretion generally not subject to judicial compulsion. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). Given the conjectural nature of this sequence, AFL has not demonstrated that a decision in its favor “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational harm. Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025). Moreover, AFL neglects the established principle that redressability ordinarily depends on the binding effect of a judgment on the litigants, not on the mere chance that the decision’s reasoning “might persuade actors who are not before the court.” Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023). Accordingly, AFL is unable to demonstrate standing on the basis of its interest in obtaining information requested from DOJ under FOIA.

AFL’s alternative basis for standing, which relies on its claimed informational interest in the results of the OSC’s investigation, is equally unavailing. To establish informational standing, a plaintiff experiences a concrete harm if denied access to information that a “statute entitled him to receive.” Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011) (citing Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006)); see also FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11, 21 (1998); Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440, 448–50 (1989). However, in this instance, even if section 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not provide a right to obtain information. AFL contends that, should OSC undertake the purportedly compulsory investigation under section 1216, it might opt to provide AFL with notices and details similar to those required if the OSC were probing an alleged “prohibited personnel practice” under section 1214. Nevertheless, AFL does not assert that OSC is compelled to apply section 1214 notice requirements in section 1216 investigations. In fact, AFL concedes precedent establishing that OSC’s choice to utilize section 1214’s procedural guidelines during a section 1216 investigation is “discretionary.” Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011). Since there is no asserted right to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks the required concrete injury for this standing theory. Similarly, because OSC is not obligated to furnish the section 1214 notices during a section 1216 inquiry, a court order requiring such an investigation would not likely resolve AFL’s second alleged injury.

III

Since AFL does not possess Article III standing, we set aside the district court's judgment and return the case with directions to dismiss it for want of subject-matter jurisdiction.

So ordered.
