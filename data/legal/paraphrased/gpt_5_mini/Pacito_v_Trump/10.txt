Title: Pacito v. Trump
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Date: September 12, 2025
Docket: Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939
Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

This panel held oral argument in this matter on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. A formal opinion will follow in due time. Pending further proceedings, this Order will operate as a stay during the appeal and until further directive from this court. Our previously issued administrative stay dated July 18, 2025, is hereby rescinded. Additionally, we set aside our earlier stay from March 25, 2025, together with its amendments on April 21 and May 9, 2025. The preliminary injunctions issued by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), are stayed in full except where this Order provides otherwise. The government’s emergency motion filed July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” See Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further determinations are made. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (“[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all funding for the USRAP program, allegedly in accordance with a separate executive order called “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This funding suspension, effective pending additional examination, extended to domestic resettlement assistance for refugees present in the United States.

The plaintiffs in this matter consist of refugees who had already been granted admission to the United States or had received approval for resettlement but remained abroad, as well as three organizations holding cooperative agreements with the State Department for overseas refugee processing and for furnishing resettlement services to refugees admitted to the U.S. In February 2025, the plaintiffs initiated proceedings in the Western District of Washington, asserting that the suspension of the refugee program under Executive Order 14163 contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (incorporated within various sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and further contending that the termination of funding for USRAP ran afoul of several requirements of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral ruling granting a preliminary injunction, barring the government from enforcing or carrying out §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. On February 26, the government cancelled every cooperative agreement associated with providing reception and placement support for refugees within the United States, along with all but one overseas agreement offering USRAP processing assistance. The district court entered a written order on February 28, clarifying the scope of its earlier preliminary injunction, expressly including a prohibition against halting USRAP funding. On March 24, a second preliminary injunction was issued by the court, instructing the State Department to restore any cooperative agreements terminated subsequent to the February 25 order. The government filed appeals from the injunctions on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s order from February 28, which was partially granted by a motions panel of this court on March 25. Relying on Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the basis for the President’s issuance of Executive Order No. 14163, “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (citing 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nonetheless, we denied a stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we issued two further orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel ordered full briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to schedule the appeal on the earliest possible calendar.

After the parties completed briefing but prior to oral argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court entered a July 14 order creating a framework to implement the limited carveout and designated a magistrate judge to supervise it. On July 17, the government filed an emergency motion seeking to enforce, clarify, or modify the stay order, a motion the plaintiffs contested. On July 18, 2025, we entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. Oral argument was then set on an accelerated timetable.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ briefs and the oral argument, we deem the matter ready for adjudication. A ruling on the merits will be issued in due course. Meanwhile, we hereby lift our administrative stay dated July 18, as well as our stay from March 25, as modified on April 21 and May 9. We grant this current stay pending the ultimate resolution of the appeal. Accordingly, the district court’s orders of February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, subject to the exceptions outlined below.

The criteria governing the issuance of a stay are settled. We assess four considerations: (1) whether the applicant for the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant faces irreparable harm in the absence of a stay; (3) whether granting a stay would cause significant injury to the opposing parties; and (4) where the public interest lies, as articulated in Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The first two considerations—the likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm—are identified as “the most critical.” Id. The government is likely to succeed regarding plaintiffs’ challenge to the lawfulness of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and we must refrain from undertaking “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. As stated in that decision: “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. Furthermore, “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to prohibit “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The remaining factors, which implicate both the domestic and foreign affairs interests of the nation as determined by the “broad discretion” vested in the President, Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, likewise weigh in favor of the United States.

The government, however, is unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims under the APA, for reasons that will be set out in detail in a forthcoming opinion. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is obligated to deliver specified reception and placement services to refugees upon their arrival in the United States. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) mandates that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—which operates within the Department of Health and Human Services—“to the extent of available appropriations”—must: (i) allocate sufficient resources for employment training and placement to expedite refugees’ achievement of economic self-sufficiency, (ii) offer refugees opportunities to obtain adequate English language instruction to facilitate prompt and effective resettlement, (iii) ensure that cash assistance is distributed so as not to undermine refugees’ efforts towards economic self-sufficiency, consistent with subsection (e)(2), and (iv) guarantee equal access for women and men to participate in training and instructional programs. Refer also to 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which outlines available federal funding for refugee services. Additionally, concerning unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding cash and medical support for newly resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, it is noted that Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, as provided by Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (adopting the funding appropriated in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), with this sum reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Section 1523 also imposes a requirement on the Secretary to provide an annual detailed report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

According to statute, these resettlement services—commonly called "reception and placement" services—may be administered using contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with "public or private nonprofit agencies." U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Traditionally, the United States has entered into cooperative agreements with different resettlement organizations to fulfill this function. The organizational plaintiffs are three such entities with which the Department of State previously maintained cooperative agreements.

As stated earlier, in February 2024, the State Department ended all cooperative agreements related to these services. Recognizing this cessation of resettlement services funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services." Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, at oral argument before the panel, the government stated that it was not aware of any developments in identifying a new agency or agencies capable of delivering reception and placement services.

Given the government's uncertainty about its ability to fulfill the statutory obligation to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby ordered to restore those cooperative agreements necessary for providing the reception and placement services enumerated in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted into the United States. These services should encompass those typically and customarily provided under previous cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President possesses broad latitude and receives substantial deference in immigration affairs, the district court erroneously prevented the executive branch from executing the President’s policy to restrict the admission of refugees and the provision of related services. Accordingly, I would grant a full stay of the district court’s injunctions.

There is consensus among my colleagues and me regarding the President’s power to halt refugee admissions, and for that reason, we all support staying the district court’s injunction as to that issue. However, there is disagreement as to whether the federal government is legally compelled to offer services to those recently admitted prior to the effective date of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing an obligation upon the government to furnish specific services to refugees. In contrast, I read the statute more naturally as conferring authority rather than imposing a duty.

Let us begin with the statute’s text: The heading of Section 1522 reads, “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis supplied). An authorization denotes discretion, not compulsion. See AUTHORIZATION, Black's Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or an “official document granting such permission”). As a result, § 1522 permits, but does not obligate, the actions referenced by Congress.

Moreover, the four particular statutory subsections allocating funding for various refugee services—such as “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—uniformly state that the “Director is authorized” to provide the designated assistance. U.S.C. § 1522(b)-(e) (emphasis supplied). This language is another clear indication that the executive retains discretion regarding funding decisions.

My colleagues contend that Section 1522 mandates federal funding by referencing §1522(a), labeled “Conditions and consideration,” which employs the term “shall” along with the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this language is conditioned by the immediately preceding text: “In providing assistance under this section [referring to the discretionary funding provisions in §1522(b)-(e)]. . .” Thus, §1522(a) sets forth mandatory “conditions and considerations” only in instances where the government opts to provide funding for refugee programs. For instance, the Director “shall. . . make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [pursuant to § 1522(c)’s discretionary project grants and contracts for services for refugees] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible,” meaning this is a required consideration only if the government elects to provide such funding.

In support of their mandatory-spending interpretation, my colleagues further cite subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which may be read to place certain duties on the Director. Nonetheless, these provisions do not convincingly disrupt the basic structure of § 1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) specifies that the Director is to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children solely if this responsibility is “necessary.” It also authorizes the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Despite using the term “shall,” the language affords considerable latitude to the executive branch and falls short of imposing any unequivocal command. The record further does not indicate the presence of unaccompanied refugee children at issue here. Similarly, subsection (e)(7)(A) directs that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” initiatives for cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” The design and scope of these programs remain subject to executive judgment, undermining the contention that Congress intended a binding requirement.

Accordingly, I understand Section 1522 as giving the executive the authority to establish programs as it sees fit, contingent on available appropriations; it does not oblige the operation of such programs. Since the statute does not mandate these services from the executive branch, this court should not compel their provision. I therefore respectfully dissent in part.
