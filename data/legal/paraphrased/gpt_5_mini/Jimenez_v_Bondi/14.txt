Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. Their requests were denied by the immigration judge, and that denial was subsequently upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, Petitioners challenge only the Board’s decisions regarding asylum and withholding of removal.

We possess jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and accordingly DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they were subject to persecution, that any alleged persecution was connected to a statutorily protected category, or that the Colombian authorities are incapable or unwilling to provide assistance. Additionally, the evidence does not indicate that Petitioners are unable to safely relocate within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children are natives of Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, all family members have been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez’s candidacy in the 2022 Colombian presidential election. The Petitioners canvassed neighborhoods in person, encouraging residents to support Gutierrez. Beginning near the end of May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls once or twice weekly related to his political activities with the Liberal Party. Additionally, other leaders and associates within the Liberal Party were also subjected to threats. In early June 2022, a fellow party member, after reporting prior threats, was kidnapped and murdered. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their campaigning efforts.

Jimenez possessed a provisional license to practice law and was enrolled as a law student during 2022, the period when the pertinent incidents occurred. He additionally served as an intern with Colombia’s National Prosecutor’s office, assisting victims of domestic violence, extortion, and other offenses. Acting in his provisional legal capacity, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo who sought his assistance with a property conflict. She reported that her tenant had failed to pay rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez speak with the tenant regarding both repayment and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to mediate an arrangement by visiting the property, but was unsuccessful; the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused to enter discussions. The client chose not to pursue formal eviction proceedings, instead hoping the tenant would relinquish the property after being made aware that legal counsel was involved.

Upon reviewing the title of the client’s property, Jimenez discovered that the individual occupying the premises was actually the client’s former husband. While the ex-husband had previously held ownership of the property, ownership had been transferred to the client as part of their divorce settlement. Further, Jimenez found out that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, who is also referred to as “Don Rafa,” and is recognized as a leader within a narco-trafficking guerrilla organization that maintains political ties with Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While at the property, Jimenez noted that the third story was blocked off by an order from the health department. Upon further inquiry, Jimenez determined that authorities had closed the property because it was allegedly being operated as a day-rental location for migrants as well as being used for narco-trafficking and prostitution activities. There were also connections to reported incidents of kidnapping and homicide at the site. Efforts by law enforcement to carry out searches and seize evidence appeared to have been unproductive. A police officer from the area warned Jimenez against visiting the property or becoming involved in related disputes.

Later that same month, Jimenez found a menacing brochure at his workplace declaring, "death to snitches from Liberal Party." He chose to ignore the threat and threw the brochure away. However, the following month, as he was working at the furniture factory during nighttime hours, Jimenez was confronted by two individuals who brandished firearms and ordered him to leave the area. One of these individuals accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and blamed him for the searches and seizures conducted at the property. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and subsequently kept a careful watch on their children's journeys to and from school.

Approximately two months following the death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory when Petitioners were away. During this visit, they attacked a factory worker and brandished a firearm while demanding information about Petitioners’ whereabouts. The men again accused Petitioners of being "snitches for the police" involved in politics. They issued another threat to kill Petitioners if they reappeared, stating that the directive had come from Don Rafa. Upon being told by the employee that Petitioners were traveling, the men departed without causing further harm.

A few days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around his younger son's shoulder as the boy was leaving school. The man covered the boy's mouth with his hand as the child attempted to break free, instructing him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez rushed over, pushed the man, and his son managed to flee to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this episode, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother's residence in the rural area outside Bogota for three weeks; while there, they made the decision to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez recounted the events to a police patrol officer. The officer responded that nothing could be done, adding that members of the police were involved in the issue. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave. Consequently, Petitioners chose to do so.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without lawful admission or parole by an immigration official, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of the Department of Homeland Security’s allegations. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners submitted applications for asylum and for withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries to Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with additional evidence on record, the Immigration Judge (“IJ”) denied the Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ determined that Petitioners were credible, the IJ found that they did not prove: (1) the harm they experienced constituted past persecution; (2) a nexus between the harm and a ground protected by statute; (3) that internal relocation was not reasonably possible; and (4) that the Colombian authorities were unable or unwilling to offer protection from their alleged persecutors. The IJ likewise denied the Petitioners’ claims for relief under the Convention Against Torture (“CAT”). Petitioners appealed, but the Board of Immigration Appeals (“BIA”) upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. Furthermore, the BIA rejected Petitioners’ assertions that their due process rights to counsel were violated by the IJ.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s ruling that denied their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in determining that they failed to demonstrate: (1) they suffered past persecution; (2) such persecution occurred because of their political opinion; and (3) they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution for which the government would be unable or unwilling to provide protection. Petitioners do not contest the denial of CAT protection or their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, we possess jurisdiction to consider final removal orders, which encompasses the consolidated evaluation of asylum petitions. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our assessment of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is confined to "the administrative record on which the order of removal is based." Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). When a single BIA member has affirmed the IJ’s ruling, our review centers on the BIA's decision; however, “we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We review the BIA’s factual determinations utilizing the substantial-evidence standard. Under this framework, the factual findings made by the BIA remain binding unless the record establishes that any reasonable factfinder would necessarily reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary relief that the Attorney General may provide to noncitizens who meet the eligibility criteria.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for this discretionary form of asylum, the applicant must demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he qualifies as a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is located outside their country of nationality . . . and who is either unable or unwilling to return to that country, as well as unable or unwilling to seek its protection, due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on the basis of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

According to Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)), one avenue for securing refugee status involves "a showing of past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution." See Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To establish this, the applicant is required to demonstrate three components: "(1) one or more events that qualify as persecution; (2) that occurred because of a statutorily protected ground; and (3) that the persecutory acts were perpetrated either by the government or by entities the government is unable or unwilling to control." Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

Although the INA does not provide a definition of persecution, our precedent holds that persecution involves the imposition of suffering or harm on individuals who are distinguished by factors such as race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner considered objectionable, and it must involve more than mere limitations or threats to one’s life or freedom. Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Furthermore, incidents are not assessed independently but rather in their entirety, since the aggregate effect of several occurrences may amount to persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The determination of whether a noncitizen has established a case of prior private persecution in their country of origin—a central question in this matter—is one of fact. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the numerous threats directed at them amount to persecution. While we do take threats into account when evaluating the overall effect of the mistreatment experienced by Petitioners, precedent establishes that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Significantly, during the merits hearing, Petitioners admitted they had not suffered any physical harm and similarly acknowledged in their opening brief that they had not endured any lasting physical injury. Although demonstrating persecution does not require evidence of physical harm, id. at 804, the harm at issue must be significant and extend beyond mere limitations or isolated threats. See Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners also request that we take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological impacts they have suffered. Duarte, as one example, reports she cannot sleep or find rest, and her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and significant stress. Although Petitioners have faced regrettable situations, fears stemming from the threats directed at them do not satisfy the threshold for persecution. Even when events are physically demanding or cause mental distress, our court has seldom, if ever, granted asylum solely on the basis of threats paired with emotional or psychological harm. See id. (determining that an applicant's claimed harm—including being arrested and detained for 10 hours and receiving threats—failed to meet the persecution standard, despite the experiences being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). During oral argument, Petitioners’ attorney acknowledged that our precedents have declined asylum requests in cases with even more egregious factual backgrounds. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not reach the level of persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (concluding that threats involving machetes and being robbed six times were insufficient for persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Upon reviewing these facts and circumstances, we conclude that substantial evidence exists to affirm the BIA’s determination that the harm suffered by Petitioners, even when considered collectively, does not constitute past persecution within the meaning of the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners further contend that the harm they endured was due to reasons protected by statute—specifically, their collective political opinion and their membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners neither provided arguments concerning the definition of their claimed social group nor showed that they belonged to such a group. Instead, with respect to the nexus element, Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa targeted them due to their political beliefs. Therefore, our analysis is confined to determining whether Petitioners have demonstrated a nexus between the alleged acts of persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners specifically contend that the mistreatment by Don Rafa and his associates stemmed from their involvement in the campaign of an opponent to Colombia’s sitting president. “To show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). This requires that the political opinion in question “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” nor merely “play a minor role” in the petitioner’s previous persecution or apprehensions of future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). Notably, a persecutor may act from more than one motive in selecting a victim. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even when multiple motives are present, at least one protected ground must serve as a central reason for the harm inflicted. Id. Therefore, Petitioners are required to show that their political opinion was, or will be, at least a central motivating reason for the persecutor’s actions against them.

Petitioners have not met this requirement because the record contains substantial evidence supporting the conclusions of both the BIA and the IJ that the main reason Petitioners were targeted was Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than their political views. Prior to Jimenez’s confrontation with Don Rafa, Petitioners had persisted in their political engagement despite receiving threatening phone calls and after another campaigner was killed. Petitioners also provide no indication in the record that those early threats linked to political activity were orchestrated by Don Rafa. In reality, the threats intensified only after Jimenez became involved in a dispute over property with Don Rafa and when Don Rafa learned Jimenez was inquiring into the property he rented. On one occasion, an individual accused Jimenez of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and of responsibility for law enforcement actions taken against Don Rafa’s premises. Additionally, when the same group later intimidated Petitioners' employees, they specifically stated Don Rafa was behind these threats.

Given these circumstances, the BIA’s determination that the chief motivation for the threats was rooted in Jimenez's personal and professional conflict with Don Rafa rather than Petitioners' political beliefs was not erroneous. As stated in Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004), “[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, despite being unable to demonstrate past persecution, they nonetheless possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly on the grounds that the Colombian government is either incapable or unwilling to protect them. They claim that those who would persecute them maintain close connections with the Colombian authorities, rendering internal relocation within the country unsafe.

The analysis regarding whether the government is unable or unwilling to provide protection “examines evidence relating to a government’s actions in response to an applicant’s experiences of past persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. According to that opinion, “[t]wo principal categories of information are especially significant when analyzing a government’s reaction to persecution: (1) the extent to which the government intervened or attempted to intervene to halt the persecutors . . . , and (2) the extent to which the government provided protection to the applicant, such as placing the applicant under protective custody.” Id.

A central aspect of this analysis involves determining whether the government had awareness of the claimed persecution and was given a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. As stated, “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, an important consideration in the assessment is whether the applicant informed the government of previous persecution. Id. (citation omitted). Although “a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” such a failure does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant may fulfill the government protection requirement by showing that making a police report would have been pointless or put the applicant at risk. Id.

Once more, the record contains ample evidence supporting the conclusions reached by both the BIA and the IJ. Petitioners made little to no effort to seek assistance from the authorities. Jimenez, along with his father, is currently employed or was formerly employed by the Colombian government. Despite this, neither individual reported any instances of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners stated in their testimony that they only spoke with two police officers, who advised them to avoid creating problems; however, there is no indication that these remarks represent the stance of the Colombian government as a whole. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As the IJ determined, the evidence does not demonstrate that Petitioners attempted to obtain help beyond their local area or utilized Jimenez’s connections within law enforcement.

Petitioners contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been pointless and potentially unsafe, as they believe their persecutors have strong connections to and associations with the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this claim. The authorities were already informed about the relevant property and had conducted multiple searches and seizures to combat the continuing criminal activity at that location. Therefore, the factual evidence contradicts Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors are closely linked to the government and that the government would not be willing or able to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners have not demonstrated that the Colombian authorities were, or would have been, unable or unwilling to safeguard them if the government had been made aware of the threats. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding there was inadequate proof that the government was incapable or disinclined to protect the applicant, especially since the applicant did not report to police out of the belief that the persecutors “were part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not satisfied the criteria for asylum, we will briefly consider their last contention about whether safe internal relocation within Colombia is possible and reasonable.

The government can overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thereby defeat the asylum claim—if it proves by a preponderance of the evidence that either: (1) there has been a “fundamental change” in circumstances so that the applicant no longer harbors a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant can evade future persecution by residing in another region of the applicant’s home country. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; regardless, the evidence in the record indicates they are able to reasonably resettle in another region of Colombia. Their family members and relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father is still employed within the criminal justice system. It is also significant that Petitioners resided securely with Jimenez’s mother in the rural area outside Bogota for a period of three weeks prior to choosing to move to the United States. Furthermore, the individuals who threatened Petitioners specifically told them to leave the neighborhood rather than exit the country. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners may avert any risk of future persecution through secure and reasonable relocation within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, the record contains ample evidence to uphold the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Moreover, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they cannot satisfy the more stringent criteria for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that relocation is not a viable option, arguing that the existence of family members in their country of origin does not necessarily guarantee their safety. Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (maintaining that such relatives may themselves oppose the government politically). However, we may not award asylum based merely on potential or speculative hardships. Petitioners further claim that internal relocation would be infeasible due to administrative and financial challenges, as well as issues with the national judicial system and the alleged connections between their persecutors and Colombia's president. Yet these contentions are addressed in a perfunctory manner and, in light of our conclusions above, fail to provide persuasive support for their case.
