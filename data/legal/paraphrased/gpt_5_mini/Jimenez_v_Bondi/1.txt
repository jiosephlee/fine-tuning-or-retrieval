Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, who are citizens and natives of Colombia, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge rejected these applications, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld that decision. On appeal, petitioners challenge only the BIA’s denial of asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we exercise jurisdiction and DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not shown that they experienced persecution, that any such persecution was connected to a statutorily protected ground, or that the Colombian authorities are unwilling or unable to offer protection. Furthermore, the record does not establish that Petitioners are unable to safely relocate to another part of Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and were employed at a furniture factory in Bogota.

Since 2012, the family has collectively belonged to the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez during his candidacy in the 2022 Colombian presidential race. During that period, Petitioners engaged in canvassing activities, visiting homes in their neighborhoods to garner support. Starting around late May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening phone calls once or twice weekly concerning his political participation in the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates affiliated with the Liberal Party were similarly threatened. In early June 2022, despite previously alerting others to the threats he faced, one of their colleagues was abducted and killed. Nevertheless, Petitioners persisted in their campaign efforts.

Jimenez held a provisional law license and was enrolled as a law student during 2022, when the pertinent incidents occurred. He additionally served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted victims of domestic violence, extortion, and other criminal offenses. Acting in his role as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached by a woman from San Bernardo in June 2022 who sought his assistance regarding a property dispute. She informed him that her tenant had not paid rent for more than a year and requested that Jimenez discuss restitution and vacating the premises with the tenant. Jimenez went to the property to attempt to negotiate, but his efforts were unsuccessful—the tenant warned him not to “look for trouble” and declined to enter into negotiations. The client opted not to initiate a formal eviction and instead hoped the tenant would relinquish possession upon realizing that legal counsel was involved.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the premises was actually the client’s former spouse. While the ex-husband had previously been the owner, ownership had passed to the client pursuant to a divorce settlement. Jimenez further determined that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also referred to as “Don Rafa,” who is a prominent figure within a narco-trafficking guerrilla faction allied politically with Colombia’s sitting president, Gustavo Petro.

While inspecting the property, Jimenez noted that the health department had closed off the third floor. Further inquiry revealed that authorities had shuttered the property due to its use as a short-term rental for migrants, as well as a hub for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The site was also connected to allegations of kidnapping and homicide. Although law enforcement had made repeated attempts to execute searches and seizures at the location, these efforts appeared to have failed. A member of the local police advised Jimenez to avoid involvement with the property and not to pursue any action that could create difficulties.

Later in the same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing brochure at his workplace that read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” He paid no heed to this warning and threw the brochure away. However, the following month, while working a night shift at the furniture factory, Jimenez was confronted by two armed men who ordered him to vacate the area. One of these individuals accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the property’s searches and seizures. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and subsequently took extra precautions to supervise their children’s routes to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same two individuals returned to the furniture factory during a time when Petitioners were away. During this visit, they attacked an employee at the factory and brandished a firearm while inquiring about Petitioners' whereabouts. Once again, the men accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” and of involvement in politics. They reiterated their threat to kill Petitioners if they reappeared, specifying that the directive was issued by Don Rafa. After the employee explained that Petitioners were traveling, the men departed without inflicting further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed a man place his arm around his younger son's shoulders as the boy was leaving school. The individual then covered the child's mouth with his hand while the son attempted to break free, instructing him to "run to your dad." Jimenez rushed over to his son and shoved the man away, allowing his son to run to Jimenez’s vehicle. In response to this event, the family relocated for three weeks to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area surrounding Bogota; during this time, they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to leaving, Jimenez reported the incident to a police patrol officer. However, the officer replied that nothing could be done and further remarked that police officers themselves were exacerbating the issue. The officer advised Jimenez and his family to leave. Consequently, Petitioners followed this advice.

B. Procedural Background

Three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was subject to removal under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of the allegations made by DHS. The Immigration Judge determined that they were inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently filed applications for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with other evidence presented, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ found the Petitioners credible, the IJ determined that they did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they experienced rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a protected ground as required by statute; (3) that relocation within Colombia would be unreasonable; and (4) that the Colombian authorities were unable or unwilling to provide protection from their alleged persecutors. The IJ further denied the Petitioners’ claims for relief under the CAT. The Petitioners subsequently appealed, but the BIA upheld the IJ’s decision and dismissed their appeal. Additionally, the BIA rejected the Petitioners’ arguments that their due process rights to counsel had been violated by the IJ.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s order denying their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend the BIA made errors in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) past persecution; (2) past persecution based on their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution in circumstances where the government is either unable or unwilling to offer protection. Petitioners do not seek review of the denial of relief under the CAT or their due process argument.

A. Standard of Review

Our jurisdiction typically extends to final removal orders, allowing us to consider consolidated challenges to asylum determinations. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our assessment of the Board’s refusal to grant asylum is restricted to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). When a single BIA member affirms the immigration judge’s decision, our review is directed to the BIA’s order, yet “we may also reference the immigration judge’s more detailed explanation of the same reasons.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We analyze the BIA’s factual findings under the substantial-evidence standard, meaning such findings stand unless the record compels any reasonable adjudicator to reach a different result. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum is a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General has the authority to grant to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). To qualify for asylum as a matter of discretion, the applicant must demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that he meets the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as any individual who is outside their country of nationality and is either unable or unwilling to return to, or to seek the protection of, that country due to past persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)), it is noted that one method for an applicant to secure refugee status is “by establishing past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To satisfy this standard, the applicant is required to establish three components: “(1) the occurrence of an incident or incidents constituting persecution; (2) that such persecution is motivated by one of the grounds protected by statute; and (3) that the persecution is either carried out by the government itself or by actors whom the government is unwilling or unable to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

Although the INA does not provide a definition for persecution, “we have determined that establishing persecution necessitates demonstrating that individuals have suffered harm or distress on account of differences in race, religion, or political opinion in a manner considered offensive, and that persecution involves more than mere limitations or threats to personal freedom or life.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). Furthermore, “we evaluate the totality of incidents together, not individually, as their combined impact may rise to the level of persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). “Determining whether a noncitizen has established past persecution by private actors in their country of origin—the central matter in this case—is a question of fact.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners argue that the series of threats directed at them amounts to persecution. While we take such threats into account when evaluating the overall effect of the mistreatment they endured, our precedent states that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Significantly, at their merits hearing, Petitioners admitted they were not subjected to any physical harm and similarly acknowledged in their opening brief that they did not suffer any lasting physical injuries. Although it is not required that an applicant show physical harm to meet the standard for persecution, id. at 804, the harm experienced must be egregious and go beyond simple limitations or isolated threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners contend that we should also take into account their ongoing emotional and psychological injuries. For instance, Duarte reports that she struggles with insomnia and fatigue, and her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced regrettable situations, apprehensions resulting from threats received do not, by themselves, meet the threshold of persecution. Regardless of how physically taxing or mentally disturbing certain incidents may be, we have seldom, if ever, granted asylum solely on the basis of threats or psychological and emotional distress. See id. (determining that the applicant’s alleged suffering—including a ten-hour arrest and detention along with threats—failed to reach the level of persecution, notwithstanding that such events were “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As acknowledged by Petitioners’ counsel during oral argument, our precedent has denied asylum in cases involving even more egregious factual circumstances of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (“four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” were not considered persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that threats involving machetes and six instances of robbery did not rise to the level of persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

In light of these considerations, we conclude there is substantial evidence to affirm the BIA’s determination that, even when considered collectively, Petitioners’ experiences do not constitute past persecution as defined by the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to grounds protected by statute, specifically their common political opinion and alleged membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not presented any arguments in their briefing to substantiate the existence of such a social group or demonstrated that they are members of it. Instead, their arguments regarding the nexus element focus solely on the claim that Don Rafa persecuted them based on their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is confined to assessing whether Petitioners have established a connection between their asserted persecution and their political opinion. In particular, Petitioners claim they were targeted by Don Rafa and his associates as a result of their involvement in a campaign opposing Colombia’s current president. To satisfy the requirement for persecution based on political opinion, an applicant “must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). This means the applicant’s political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” and cannot merely “play ‘a minor role’” in either their previous mistreatment or their apprehensions of future harm. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is well established that a persecutor may act with multiple motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even in cases involving mixed motives, at least one protected ground must constitute a central reason for the harm imposed. Id. Accordingly, Petitioners must show that their political opinion was, or would be, at least one central reason for their actual or anticipated persecution.

Petitioners do not meet this requirement because ample evidence underpins the determinations of the BIA and IJ that the main reason Petitioners were targeted was Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than their political beliefs. Before Jimenez’s conflict with Don Rafa, Petitioners were not dissuaded by threatening calls or the killing of another campaign worker, and they maintained their political involvement. Petitioners fail to identify any evidence in the record linking Don Rafa to these earlier threats that were connected to their political activities. In fact, the escalation in threats began only after Jimenez entered into a property dispute with Don Rafa and Don Rafa learned that Jimenez was investigating his leased property. In one instance, a man threatened Jimenez, accusing him of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and being accountable for searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s premises. Subsequently, when Petitioners’ employees were threatened by the same men, they indicated that Don Rafa had sent them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA properly found that the principal reason for the threats was rooted in Jimenez’s business and personal dealings with Don Rafa, rather than Petitioners’ political views. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to demonstrate past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly due to the Colombian government's alleged incapacity or unwillingness to safeguard them. They claim that their persecutors maintain significant connections with, and receive support from, the Colombian government, thus eliminating the possibility of safe internal relocation.

The analysis regarding a government's inability or unwillingness to protect "examines evidence related to how a government has responded to an applicant’s prior experiences of persecution." Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. In evaluating a government's response to persecution, two primary types of evidence are particularly significant: "(1) whether the government intervened to halt or attempted to halt the actions of the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government provided protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody." Id.

A related aspect of this analysis involves determining whether the government was made aware of the claimed persecution and was given a chance to take action. Id. at 861–62. “In the absence of such notice, it remains unclear how law enforcement would have responded or if the government might have intervened.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “an important consideration in the analysis is whether the applicant informed government authorities about the previous persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “failing to report the persecution can weaken the assertion that the government was unable or unwilling to offer protection,” such a failure is not automatically decisive. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can fulfill the government protection requirement by showing that submitting a police report would have been either pointless or posed a danger. Id.

Once more, the administrative record contains ample evidence that backs the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. Petitioners made, at most, very limited attempts to seek assistance from the government. Both Jimenez and his father have held, or presently hold, positions within the Colombian government; nevertheless, neither chose to report any misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners stated that they had conversations with two police officers, who advised them to avoid attracting trouble, but there is no evidence to suggest this reflects an official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). Consistent with the IJ’s findings, the record fails to demonstrate that Petitioners made efforts to seek protection outside of their local area or through Jimenez’s professional contacts within law enforcement.

Petitioners contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both ineffective and hazardous due to the alleged close association between their persecutors and the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this assertion. The government was aware of the disputed property and had made efforts, including multiple searches and seizures, to combat the criminal activity occurring there. Thus, the evidence actually undermines Petitioners’ claim that their persecutors had a close relationship with the government or that the government would be either unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners are unable to establish that the Colombian government was, or would have been, incapable or unwilling to protect them had the authorities been made aware of the threats. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (concluding that there was not enough evidence to demonstrate governmental inability or unwillingness to protect, especially where the applicant did not report to the police due to a belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not met the necessary criteria for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining contention concerning whether it would be possible and reasonable for them to relocate safely within Colombia.

The government may overcome an applicant’s presumption of having a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thus bar the applicant’s claim for asylum—by proving by a preponderance of the evidence either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, assuming arguendo that they had done so, the evidence in the record substantiates the conclusion that Petitioners could reasonably and safely move to another region of Colombia. Members of Petitioners’ family and extended relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment within the criminal justice system there. Significantly, Petitioners resided without incident for three weeks with Jimenez’s mother in a rural area near Bogota prior to choosing to emigrate to the United States. Additionally, it is pertinent that those who issued threats against Petitioners merely instructed them to leave their neighborhood, rather than ordering them to exit the country altogether. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners are able to reduce any risk of future persecution through feasible and secure internal relocation within Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence exists to support the BIA’s determinations that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners are unable to establish a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they also do not satisfy the more stringent criteria for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (referencing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we reject the petition for review. While Petitioners contend that relocation is not practicable because “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety,” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (noting that those relatives might also oppose the government politically), asylum cannot be awarded based on such speculative difficulties. Petitioners further argue that relocation is infeasible due to administrative and economic obstacles, deficiencies in the nation’s judicial system, and their persecutors’ connections to the Colombian president. However, these points are only superficially addressed in their briefing, and, considering our prior conclusions, their arguments are unpersuasive.
