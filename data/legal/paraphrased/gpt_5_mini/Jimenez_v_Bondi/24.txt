Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Pursuant to our authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not shown that they experienced persecution, established that any persecution had a connection to a legally recognized protected ground, or proved that the Colombian authorities are unwilling or unable to assist them. The record further fails to establish that Petitioners are unable to relocate to another area in Colombia where they would be safe.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and were employed at a furniture factory located in Bogota.

The entire family has been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012 and gave their support to Federico Gutierrez, a presidential candidate in Colombia’s 2022 election. During the campaign, Petitioners canvassed door-to-door in their neighborhoods to solicit support. Starting in late May 2022, Jimenez began receiving phone threats once or twice per week because of his Liberal Party activities. Similar threats were directed at other party leaders and associates. In early June 2022, one colleague was abducted and murdered, notwithstanding prior reports of threats against him. Despite these events, Petitioners continued with their campaigning efforts.

Jimenez, holding a provisional law license and serving as a law student in 2022 during the pertinent period, was also employed as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted individuals victimized by domestic abuse, extortion, and various offenses. Acting in his provisional lawyer capacity, he was approached by a woman from San Bernardo in June 2022 regarding assistance with a property dispute. She reported not having received rent payments from her tenant for more than a year and sought Jimenez's aid in negotiating restitution and vacating the premises. Jimenez traveled to the property to attempt negotiations but was met with resistance; the tenant instructed Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused any negotiation. The client opted not to initiate formal eviction proceedings, preferring instead to wait in the hope that the tenant would relinquish the property upon learning of a lawyer’s involvement.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s property, Jimenez discovered that the individual occupying the premises was actually the client’s former spouse. Although the ex-husband had previously held title to the property, ownership was transferred to the client pursuant to a divorce agreement. Jimenez additionally learned that the tenant’s identity was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also known as “Don Rafa,” who was recognized as a leader among narco-trafficking guerrilla forces aligned politically with Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

While on the premises, Jimenez noticed that the property’s third floor was cordoned off under an order issued by the health department. Continuing his investigation, Jimenez found out that authorities had shuttered the property due to its use as a short-term rental for migrants and as a center for drug trafficking and prostitution. The location had also been linked to reports of kidnapping and homicide. Despite law enforcement’s repeated efforts to conduct searches and seizures at the property, these actions appeared to have been unsuccessful. A police officer in the area advised Jimenez to avoid involvement with the property and cautioned him against inviting trouble.

Later in the same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing pamphlet at his factory bearing the message, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez ignored this intimidation and threw the brochure away. However, the following month, as he was working a night shift at the furniture factory, two armed individuals confronted Jimenez, ordering him to vacate the area. One of them accused Jimenez of acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office, holding him responsible for the law enforcement searches and seizures conducted on the premises. After the assailants departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and subsequently took additional precautions, including carefully supervising their children’s travel to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the same pair of men returned to the furniture factory during Petitioners' absence from town. They attacked one of the factory workers and, brandishing a firearm, demanded information about Petitioners' whereabouts. Once again, the men accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They reiterated their threat to kill Petitioners should they reappear, specifying that this directive originated from Don Rafa. After the employee informed the men that Petitioners were away traveling, the men departed without inflicting further harm.

Several days after the previous events, Jimenez observed an individual placing his arm around his younger son's shoulders as the child was leaving school. The individual covered the boy's mouth while the boy attempted to get away, instructing him to "run to your dad." Jimenez immediately approached his son and pushed the individual, enabling his son to run to Jimenez's vehicle. In response to the incident, the family stayed at Jimenez's mother's residence in rural Bogota for three weeks; during this time, they resolved to travel to the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez informed a patrolling police officer of the incident. However, the officer responded that nothing could be done and that law enforcement officers were themselves part of the problem. The officer advised Jimenez that his family should leave. Petitioners then acted on this advice and left.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners’ entry into the United States without admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that each Petitioner was removable under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners acknowledged the accuracy of the allegations presented by DHS. The Immigration Judge found Petitioners inadmissible and ordered removal to Colombia. Petitioners then submitted applications for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children included as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with other evidence presented in the record, the Immigration Judge denied the Petitioners' applications. While finding the Petitioners credible, the Immigration Judge determined that Petitioners did not demonstrate: (1) that the harm they suffered constituted past persecution; (2) a connection to a protected statutory ground; (3) that relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option; and (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to protect them from their alleged persecutors. The Immigration Judge further denied Petitioners' requests for relief under the CAT. Petitioners appealed this decision, but the Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed the denial and dismissed the appeal. Additionally, the BIA found no merit in Petitioners' due process claims regarding a violation of their right to counsel by the Immigration Judge.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s order rejecting their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA erred in concluding that they failed to demonstrate: (1) that they suffered past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was based on their political opinion; and (3) that they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution and that the government would be unable or unwilling to shield them. Petitioners do not contest the BIA’s denial of relief under the CAT or the disposition of their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, our authority extends to evaluating final removal orders, which encompasses the consolidated examination of asylum applications. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our assessment of the Board’s refusal to grant asylum relief must be confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Where the BIA’s decision was rendered by a single member who affirmed the IJ’s ruling, our focus is primarily on the BIA’s decision; nevertheless, “we may also review the IJ’s more detailed rationale for the same issues.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We analyze the BIA’s factual determinations by applying the substantial-evidence test. This means that the Board’s factual determinations are binding unless it is evident from the record that any reasonable factfinder would be required to decide differently. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (altered citation).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a form of relief that is awarded at the discretion of the Attorney General to noncitizens who meet the necessary criteria.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). In order to qualify for asylum, an applicant must demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she is a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). According to the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is located outside of their country of nationality . . . and who is either unable or unwilling to return to, or to seek the protection of, that country due to past persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphasis in original) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)), it is noted that one method for an applicant to attain refugee status is “through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To meet this standard, the applicant needs to establish three criteria: “(1) an incident, or incidents, that rise to the level of persecution; (2) that is on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) is committed by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

The petitioners claim that, beyond experiencing threats, they endured physical, psychological, and emotional injury amounting to past persecution.

Although the INA does not specifically define persecution, “we have determined that persecution involves causing suffering or harm to those who differ—whether in race, religion, or political viewpoint—in a manner considered offensive, and it requires more than mere impositions or threats against life or freedom.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). We evaluate the incidents together rather than individually, because considering them as a whole may show that their combined impact rises to the level of persecution. Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). Determining if a noncitizen has established a case of private persecution in their native country—central to this matter—presents a factual question. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the series of threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are taken into account when we evaluate the overall effect of the mistreatment experienced by Petitioners, our precedent provides that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Notably, during their merits hearing, Petitioners acknowledged they had not suffered any physical harm, and likewise indicated in their opening brief that they had not sustained any lasting physical injuries. Although establishing persecution does not necessarily require physical harm, id. at 804, the harm in question must be serious and rise above the level of minor restrictions or isolated threats. Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners ask that we also take into account the enduring emotional and psychological damage they have experienced. Duarte, for instance, reports she suffers from an inability to sleep or relax, and that her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have unfortunately faced difficult situations, the anxieties resulting from threats received do not, on their own, amount to persecution. However physically taxing or mentally disturbing some incidents may be, asylum is seldom, if ever, granted solely on the basis of threats or emotional and psychological distress. See id. (holding that harm such as a 10-hour arrest and detention alongside threats was not sufficient to constitute persecution, despite the applicant describing the events as "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). At oral argument, counsel for Petitioners acknowledged that precedent within this circuit has denied asylum in cases with even more severe instances of harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not reach the threshold for persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that being threatened with machetes and robbed six times was inadequate to establish persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Under these facts and the circumstances presented, we conclude that there is substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s determination that, even when considered cumulatively, Petitioners’ experiences do not meet the INA’s definition of past persecution.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to grounds protected under statute—specifically, their collective political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not submitted briefing on the definition or existence of their alleged social group, nor have they shown that they belong to such a group. Instead, when addressing the required causal nexus, Petitioners argue their persecution by Don Rafa was attributable to their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis focuses solely on whether Petitioners have demonstrated a causal link between their asserted persecution and their political beliefs. Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa and his associates inflicted harm upon them due to their involvement in a rival's campaign against the current president of Colombia. As explained in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), “[t]o show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” In other words, Petitioners’ political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” nor can it “play ‘a minor role’ in the persecution experienced or the feared mistreatment.” Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that persecutors may act from several motives. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, in cases where mixed motives exist, at least one protected ground must comprise a central reason for the harm inflicted. Id. Consequently, in this matter, Petitioners must establish that their political opinion was or will be at least one main reason their persecutors targeted or would target them.

Petitioners have not met their burden of proof, as there is ample evidence supporting the determinations by the BIA and the IJ that Petitioners were principally targeted because Jimenez posed a threat to Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, rather than due to Petitioners’ political opinions. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners continued with their political engagements despite menacing phone calls and the killing of an associate, showing no deterrence. Petitioners have not identified any evidence in the record indicating that Don Rafa was responsible for those early politically related threats. In fact, threats against Petitioners intensified only after Jimenez became embroiled in a property conflict with Don Rafa, and following Don Rafa’s discovery that Jimenez was probing his rental property. During one particular incident, an individual accused Jimenez of collaborating with the prosecutor’s office and holding him accountable for police searches and confiscations at Don Rafa’s property. Later, when these same individuals threatened Petitioners’ employees, they stated explicitly that Don Rafa had dispatched them.

Given these circumstances, the BIA did not make a mistake in concluding that the primary impetus for the threats stemmed from Jimenez’s occupational and personal association with Don Rafa, rather than from Petitioners’ political beliefs. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We concur that Petitioners’ political opinion played only an incidental and secondary role compared to the principal motivation for the harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even if they are unable to demonstrate past persecution, they nevertheless possess a well-founded fear of persecution in the future, in part due to the Colombian government's incapacity or unwillingness to offer them protection. They maintain that the individuals responsible for persecution maintain close associations with the Colombian government, making internal relocation within Colombia unsafe.

The evaluation of whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. This determination relies heavily on two principal considerations: first, “whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors,” and second, “whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody.” Id.

A central component of this evaluation involves determining whether the government was made aware of the claimed persecution and had a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. As stated, “Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped.” Id. (citation modified). Therefore, “whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government” constitutes an important aspect of the analysis. Id. (citation omitted). Although “a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” this alone does not necessarily defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant may still meet the requirement of seeking government protection by showing that submitting a police report would have been futile or presented a risk of harm. Id.

Once more, the administrative record contains ample evidence supporting the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. The attempts made by Petitioners to seek governmental assistance were, at best, minimal. Both Jimenez and his father have held, or still hold, positions within the Colombian government, yet neither lodged any reports of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners stated during their testimony that they engaged in conversations with two individual police officers who cautioned them against causing trouble; however, there is no evidence indicating that these officers’ remarks represent the official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As observed by the IJ, the evidence fails to demonstrate that Petitioners sought assistance beyond their local area or made use of Jimenez’s connections in law enforcement.

Petitioners suggest that seeking assistance from the government would have been both pointless and unsafe due to their persecutors’ purported close connections with the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this claim. The government was not only aware of the disputed property but had also undertaken multiple searches and seizures in an effort to curtail the ongoing criminal activity associated with it. Thus, the evidence undermines Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors operate in concert with the government and that government authorities would be either unwilling or unable to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Additionally, Petitioners cannot demonstrate that the Colombian government was or would have been incapable or unwilling to offer protection had it been informed of the threats Petitioners faced. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (holding the record lacked sufficient evidence that the government was unable or unwilling to assist, especially since the applicant chose not to contact the police based on a belief that his persecutors were “part of the government”).

Therefore, we concur that Petitioners are unable to demonstrate a well-founded fear of future persecution as required for asylum eligibility.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not met the burden for establishing eligibility for asylum, we will briefly consider their remaining contention concerning the practicality and reasonableness of relocating safely within Colombia.

The government is able to overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution—and thereby preclude a grant of asylum—if it demonstrates by a preponderance of the evidence that either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; nevertheless, even accepting such fear, the evidence in the record substantiates the conclusion that Petitioners have the option to relocate to a different region within Colombia in a reasonable manner. Petitioners’ family members and other relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment within the criminal justice sector. Importantly, Petitioners resided without incident alongside Jimenez’s mother in a rural area near Bogota for a period of three weeks prior to choosing to move to the United States. Furthermore, the individuals who issued threats to Petitioners only demanded they leave their neighborhood, not the entire country. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners may avoid any risk of future persecution by relocating internally within Colombia in a safe and reasonable manner.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence supports the BIA’s determination that Petitioners are ineligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not established a well-founded fear of persecution required for asylum, they also cannot satisfy the more stringent standard necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that internal relocation is not practical since “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (asserting those family members may likewise possess oppositional political views). However, asylum cannot be granted based on merely speculative adversities. Petitioners also argue that relocating would not be viable due to administrative and economic hardships, deficiencies in the country's judicial system, and connections between their persecutors and Colombia’s president. Nevertheless, Petitioners’ briefing on these points is cursory, and in light of our foregoing analysis, these contentions are unpersuasive.
