Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

The petitioners, who are Colombian nationals, sought asylum, withholding of removal, and relief under the Convention Against Torture. Their applications were denied by the immigration judge, a decision that was subsequently upheld by the Board of Immigration Appeals. On appeal, the petitioners challenge only the BIA's denial of their asylum and withholding of removal claims.

Pursuant to our jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any such persecution was connected to a ground protected by statute, or that the Colombian government is either unable or unwilling to provide assistance. Additionally, the record does not support the conclusion that the petitioners are unable to relocate safely within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor children hail from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and worked at a furniture manufacturing business in Bogota.

Since 2012, all family members have belonged to the Colombian Liberal Party and supported Federico Gutierrez during the 2022 presidential race in Colombia. During that campaign, Petitioners canvassed neighborhoods to garner support for Gutierrez. Starting in late May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening phone calls once or twice per week connected to his activity in the Liberal Party. Other leaders and associates within the Liberal Party were similarly threatened. In early June 2022, one of their colleagues was abducted and killed, even after he had reported receiving threats. Despite these dangers, Petitioners persisted in their political campaigning.

Jimenez possessed a provisional law license and was enrolled as a law student during 2022, the year in which the pertinent incidents occurred. He also served as an intern with the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, assisting victims of crimes such as domestic abuse and extortion. Acting as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo seeking assistance in a property matter. She informed Jimenez that her tenant had failed to pay rent for over a year and requested that he communicate with the tenant regarding both restitution and vacating the premises. Jimenez went to the property in an attempt to broker an agreement, but the effort was unsuccessful; the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and refused to engage in negotiations. The client chose not to pursue a formal eviction and instead preferred to wait, believing that the tenant might relinquish the property upon learning of a lawyer’s involvement.

Upon reviewing the title to the client’s property, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was actually the client’s former husband. While the ex-husband had once held ownership of the property, it had been conveyed to the client pursuant to a divorce agreement. Jimenez further determined that the tenant in question was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also referred to as “Don Rafa,” who is recognized as a leader among narco-trafficking guerrilla factions with political ties to the sitting Colombian president, Gustavo Petro.

When Jimenez inspected the premises, he noticed that the health department had sealed off the third floor. Additional inquiry revealed to Jimenez that authorities had shuttered the property due to its use as a day-rental for migrants, as well as a center for narco-trafficking and prostitution. The location was also linked to incidents involving kidnapping and homicide. Although law enforcement had executed multiple searches and attempts to seize the property, these efforts appeared to have been unsuccessful. A member of the local police advised Jimenez to keep his distance from the property and avoid entanglement.

Later in that same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing brochure at his factory which read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez paid no attention to this threat and threw away the brochure. However, the following month, while Jimenez was working a night shift at the furniture factory, he was confronted by two men who threatened him at gunpoint, instructing him to leave the area. One of these individuals accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures conducted at the property. After these men departed, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to carefully watch their children’s travel to and from school.

Approximately two months following the initial death threat, the identical pair returned to the furniture factory during a period when Petitioners were away. They attacked an employee and brandished a firearm while inquiring about Petitioners’ whereabouts. Once more, the men accused Petitioners of being “snitches for the police” and being involved in politics. They reiterated their threat to kill Petitioners if they reappeared, specifying that the directive originated from Don Rafa. The employee informed the men that Petitioners were out of town, after which the men departed without inflicting further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez observed an individual place his arm around the shoulders of his younger son as the child was leaving school. The man covered the boy’s mouth with his hand when the son attempted to break free, instructing him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez responded by running to his son and pushing the man, allowing his son to run toward Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this episode, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in the rural area of Bogota for three weeks; while there, they made the decision to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the events to a patrolling police officer. However, the officer stated that there was nothing that could be done and remarked that police officers themselves were contributing to the issue. The officer advised Jimenez that he and his family should leave. Petitioners then did so.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without lawful admission or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to each Petitioner. These Notices alleged that Petitioners were subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded to the factual allegations set forth by DHS. The Immigration Judge determined that they were inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Subsequently, Petitioners submitted applications for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children listed as rider respondents and as derivative beneficiaries on Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, as well as additional evidence presented, the Immigration Judge denied Petitioners’ applications. While the IJ determined that Petitioners were credible, the IJ found that they did not demonstrate: (1) the harm they suffered rose to the level of past persecution; (2) a connection to a ground protected by statute; (3) that internal relocation was not a viable option; or (4) that the Colombian government was either unable or unwilling to offer protection against their alleged persecutors. Furthermore, the IJ rejected Petitioners’ claims for protection pursuant to the CAT. Petitioners appealed this decision, but the BIA upheld the IJ’s ruling and dismissed the appeal. The BIA likewise rejected Petitioners’ assertions that the IJ had infringed upon their right to counsel, finding no due process violation.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA’s ruling, which rejected their requests for asylum and withholding of removal. They contend that the BIA made errors in determining that they failed to demonstrate: (1) past persecution; (2) past persecution based on their political opinion; and (3) a well-founded fear of future persecution with respect to the government’s ability or willingness to provide protection. Petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or their claim regarding due process.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, our authority extends to reviewing final removal orders, which may encompass a combined review of asylum-related issues. Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). Our examination of the Board’s refusal to grant asylum relief is confined to “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since the BIA’s decision was issued by a single member affirming the IJ, we analyze the BIA’s ruling, though “we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We apply the substantial-evidence standard when assessing the BIA’s factual determinations. According to this standard, such factual findings are deemed binding unless the evidence demonstrates that no reasonable adjudicator could reach a different conclusion. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (altered citation).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a form of discretionary relief that the Attorney General may extend to noncitizens who meet the eligibility requirements.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). To qualify for such discretionary asylum, an applicant must demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he or she is a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). Under the INA, a refugee is defined as: any individual who is located outside the country of his or her nationality . . . who either cannot or does not wish to return to, and is either unable or unwilling to seek protection from, that country due to persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on grounds of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases in original) (citing § 1101(a)(42)(A)). An applicant may establish refugee status by demonstrating past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To satisfy this standard, the applicant must establish three requirements: “(1) an act or acts amounting to persecution; (2) motivated by one of the protected statutory grounds; and (3) perpetrated either by the government or by entities the government is unable or unwilling to control.” Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

Although the INA does not provide a definition for persecution, “we have determined that persecution requires the imposition of suffering or harm on individuals who are different (based on race, religion, or political opinion) in a manner that is considered offensive, and it must involve more than mere limitations or threats to one’s life and liberty.” Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). “We examine all incidents as a whole, rather than individually, since the aggregate impact of several events can amount to persecution.” Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). “The determination of whether a noncitizen has established past private persecution in their country of origin—the central question before us—is a factual inquiry.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the series of threats directed at them amount to persecution. While we do evaluate threats as part of the overall harm Petitioners claim to have experienced, our precedent states that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Notably, Petitioners acknowledged during their merits hearing that they did not suffer any physical injury, and likewise, their opening brief admits they did not endure any permanent physical harm. Although physical injury is not a required element to establish persecution, id. at 804, the harm must still be significant and go beyond simple limitations or occasional threats, as explained in Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners request that we take into account the ongoing emotional and psychological impact they have suffered. Duarte, for instance, claims that she experiences insomnia and unrest, and that her son has developed panic attacks and stress. Although Petitioners have faced distressing events, their apprehensions resulting from the threats received do not meet the threshold of persecution. Even when certain incidents are physically taxing or mentally disturbing, our precedents have seldom, if ever, recognized asylum claims based solely on threats together with emotional or psychological distress. See id. (holding that harm described by the applicant—including a 10-hour period of arrest and detention, as well as threats—did not constitute persecution, notwithstanding that it was “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). As Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged during oral argument, our precedent has denied asylum in cases involving even more severe forms of inflicted harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (concluding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not amount to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “threats with machetes and being robbed six times” were inadequate to constitute persecution because “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

In light of the foregoing facts and circumstances, we conclude there is substantial evidence to support the BIA’s determination that Petitioners’ harm, even when considered collectively, does not rise to the level of past persecution under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they experienced was due to reasons protected by statute, specifically their common political opinion and membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners did not provide briefing to support the existence of the claimed particular social group, nor did they prove that they belonged to such a group. Instead, in addressing the nexus element, Petitioners maintain that Don Rafa persecuted them because of their political views. Therefore, our examination is confined to whether Petitioners can show a connection between the alleged persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners particularly argue that the harm inflicted by Don Rafa and his associates stemmed from their involvement in the campaign of a political rival opposing Colombia’s current president. As stated in Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), “[t]o show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” In other words, the asserted political opinion “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm” nor can it have “a minor role” in the past harm suffered or anticipated future mistreatment. Id. (citing Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). It is recognized that a persecutor may act with more than one motive. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even in mixed-motive scenarios, at least one protected ground must be a central cause of the persecution. Id. Therefore, in this case, Petitioners are required to prove that their political opinion was or will be at least a central reason for their persecution.

Petitioners do not meet this requirement, as ample evidence supports the determinations by both the BIA and IJ that Petitioners were chiefly targeted due to Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s financial interests and safety, as opposed to their political views. Before Jimenez’s altercation with Don Rafa, Petitioners disregarded threatening phone calls and the killing of a fellow campaign participant, continuing their political engagement without deterrence. Petitioners fail to identify any evidence in the record connecting Don Rafa to those earlier threats linked to their political activities. The escalation of threats against Petitioners occurred only after Jimenez became entangled in a property dispute with Don Rafa and after Don Rafa learned that Jimenez was probing into his leased property. In one instance, Jimenez was threatened by an individual who accused him of being affiliated with the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for law enforcement actions at Don Rafa’s property. Subsequently, when these individuals threatened Petitioners’ employees, they explicitly stated they had been sent by Don Rafa.

Given this factual context, the BIA’s conclusion that Jimenez’s personal and occupational conflicts with Don Rafa, rather than Petitioners’ political opinion, predominantly motivated the threats was not erroneous. See Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, even lacking proof of past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, partly because the Colombian authorities are either incapable or disinclined to shield them. They maintain that the individuals responsible for their persecution have strong ties and affiliations with the Colombian government, making internal relocation within Colombia unsafe.

The analysis concerning whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “focuses on evidence regarding the government’s reaction to the applicant’s prior persecution.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. “In assessing a government’s response to persecution, two categories of information are especially significant: (1) whether authorities intervened to halt or attempted to halt the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the authorities provided protection to the applicant, such as by offering protective custody.” Id.

A related aspect of this analysis involves determining if the government was informed of the claimed persecution and had the chance to act. Id. at 861–62. “Absent such notice, it is impossible to determine how the authorities would have responded or if the government would have intervened.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “whether an applicant notified the government of prior persecution is a crucial consideration.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “failing to make a report can weaken the argument that the government was unwilling or unable to prevent the persecution,” such failure does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). In fact, an applicant can fulfill the requirement to seek government protection by establishing that reporting to the authorities would have been pointless or put them at risk. Id.

Once more, ample evidence in the record underpins the conclusions reached by both the BIA and the IJ. Petitioners made only limited, if any, attempts to solicit assistance from the authorities. Both Jimenez and his father have held or presently hold government positions in Colombia, yet neither reported instances of misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). Petitioners stated that they had conversations with two police officers who advised them not to invite trouble, but the record lacks any indication that these interactions represent the official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As the IJ determined, the evidence does not establish that Petitioners sought support beyond their local area or through Jimenez’s contacts within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both ineffective and hazardous, as their alleged persecutors are purportedly closely connected to the Colombian authorities. However, the record does not support this claim. The evidence shows that the government was cognizant of the relevant property and had conducted multiple searches and seizures to address the persistent criminal activity occurring there. Thus, the record undermines Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors have close ties to the government and that the authorities would be either unable or unwilling to intervene. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Additionally, Petitioners are unable to establish that the Colombian government was, or would have been, incapable or unwilling to protect them had the government been informed about the threats they faced. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (determining that the evidence was insufficient to show government inability or unwillingness to provide protection, especially where the applicant chose not to approach law enforcement based on the belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

Even though Petitioners have not satisfied the criteria necessary for asylum, we will briefly consider their remaining contention concerning the practicality and reasonableness of internal relocation within Colombia.

The government is permitted to overcome an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and thereby bar his application for asylum if it establishes by a preponderance of the evidence that either: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances, such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution; however, even assuming such fear existed, the evidence supports the determination that internal relocation within Colombia is a viable and reasonable option for them. Petitioners’ family members and relatives remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father is still employed within the criminal justice sector. Additionally, Petitioners resided without incident for three weeks with Jimenez’s mother in the rural area outside Bogota prior to their decision to depart for the United States. Importantly, the individuals who threatened Petitioners instructed them merely to leave the neighborhood, rather than requiring them to exit the country. Accordingly, we conclude that Petitioners have the ability to avoid any possible future persecution by relocating safely and reasonably elsewhere in Colombia.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, considerable evidence supports the BIA's determinations that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Furthermore, since Petitioners have not shown a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they also cannot satisfy the more stringent criteria necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (citing Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, for these reasons, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners contend that relocation is not feasible, asserting that “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (maintaining that such family members might also possess political views adverse to the government). However, asylum cannot be granted based on these speculative difficulties. Petitioners further argue that relocation is impracticable due to administrative and economic challenges, the state of the nation’s judicial system, and the connection between their persecutors and the Colombian president. Nonetheless, these points are insufficiently developed in their briefing, and, in light of our preceding analysis, their contentions are unpersuasive.
