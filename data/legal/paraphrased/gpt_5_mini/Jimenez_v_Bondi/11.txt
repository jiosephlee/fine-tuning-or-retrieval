Title: Jimenez v. Bondi
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-9573
Author: Tymkovich, Circuit Judge

Petitioners, who are citizens and nationals of Colombia, submitted requests for asylum, withholding of removal, and relief pursuant to the Convention Against Torture. The immigration judge rejected these requests, and the Board of Immigration Appeals upheld that decision. Petitioners now challenge solely the BIA’s denial of their claims for asylum and withholding of removal.

Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we exercise jurisdiction and DENY the petition for review. Petitioners have not established that they experienced persecution, that any such persecution was connected to a protected statutory ground, or that the Colombian authorities are unable or unwilling to provide protection. Additionally, the record does not support that Petitioners would be unable to reasonably relocate within Colombia.

I. Background

A. Factual Background

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his spouse Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young children originate from Bogota, Colombia. Prior to moving to the United States in December 2022, the family operated and was employed at a furniture factory in Bogota.

They have been affiliated with the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012 and backed Federico Gutierrez, who was a presidential candidate in Colombia’s 2022 election. The Petitioners participated by canvassing neighborhoods to encourage support for their candidate. Around the end of May 2022, Jimenez began to receive threatening telephone calls once or twice a week that referenced his Liberal Party activities. Similar threats were directed at other leaders and associates within the Liberal Party. In June 2022, a fellow party member was kidnapped and killed, despite having previously reported the threats made against him. Nonetheless, Petitioners persisted in their campaigning efforts.

Jimenez held a provisional law license and was enrolled as a law student in 2022, during the period at issue. Additionally, he served as an intern at the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, where he assisted victims of domestic violence, extortion, and various other offenses. Acting in his capacity as a provisional attorney, Jimenez was approached in June 2022 by a woman from San Bernardo seeking his assistance with a property dispute. She informed him that the tenant residing at her property had failed to pay rent for more than a year, and requested that Jimenez contact the tenant to discuss recovering the unpaid rent and vacating the premises. Jimenez attempted to mediate a resolution by visiting the property; however, negotiations failed when the tenant warned Jimenez not to “look for trouble” and declined to engage in any discussions. The client opted not to pursue a formal eviction and instead wished that the tenant would vacate voluntarily upon learning of legal involvement.

Upon reviewing the client’s property title, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the premises was actually the client’s former spouse. While the ex-husband had previously possessed ownership of the property, ownership had been conveyed to the client through a divorce agreement. Additionally, Jimenez ascertained that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, also referred to as “Don Rafa,” who is identified as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla faction with political ties to Colombia’s current president, Gustavo Petro.

When Jimenez visited the site, he noted that the health department had closed off the third floor of the building. Upon further inquiry, Jimenez learned that authorities had shuttered the property because it was being utilized both as a day-rental facility for migrants and as an operational base for narcotics distribution and prostitution. The property had also been linked to incidents of kidnapping and homicide. Despite multiple efforts by law enforcement to conduct searches and confiscate evidence at the location, such actions appeared to have yielded no results. Jimenez was cautioned by a police officer in the vicinity to avoid the premises and refrain from instigating trouble.

In the same month, Jimenez was sent a menacing brochure at his factory containing the message, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” Jimenez chose to ignore this threat and threw away the brochure. However, in the following month, as he was working at the furniture factory during the night, two individuals confronted him at gunpoint, ordering him to leave the area. One of the assailants accused Jimenez of acting as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for the searches and seizures that had taken place at the property. Following this incident, Jimenez and his wife secured the factory and began to vigilantly oversee their children’s travel to and from school.

Approximately two months following the earlier death threat, the identical two individuals returned to the furniture factory while Petitioners were away. During this visit, they attacked one of the factory’s employees and brandished a firearm, insisting on being told the whereabouts of Petitioners. The men reiterated that Petitioners were “snitches for the police” involved in politics. They declared they would kill Petitioners if they reappeared, specifying that these instructions originated from Don Rafa. After the employee informed them that Petitioners were out of town, the men departed without causing further harm.

Several days afterward, Jimenez saw an individual place an arm around his younger son's shoulders as the child was leaving school. The individual covered the boy’s mouth while the child attempted to get away and instructed him to “run to your dad.” Jimenez hurried over to his son and shoved the man, enabling his son to flee to Jimenez’s vehicle. Following this event, the family relocated to Jimenez’s mother’s residence in rural Bogota for approximately three weeks, during which they resolved to depart for the United States.

Prior to their departure, Jimenez reported the event to a patrolling police officer. However, the officer responded that no action could be taken and commented that law enforcement officers were part of the problem. The officer recommended that Jimenez and his family leave. The Petitioners acted accordingly.

B. Procedural Background

Approximately three months following Petitioners' entry into the United States without inspection or parole by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security issued individual Notices to Appear to Petitioners. Each Notice alleged that the Petitioner was subject to removal from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In August 2023, Petitioners conceded the accuracy of DHS’s factual allegations. The Immigration Judge found them inadmissible and ordered their removal to Colombia. Petitioners subsequently sought asylum and withholding of removal, designating the minor children as rider respondents and as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application.

Relying on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte, along with additional evidence in the record, the Immigration Judge (IJ) denied the applications of Petitioners. The IJ determined that Petitioners were credible but held that they had not demonstrated: (1) the harm suffered constituted past persecution; (2) a nexus to a statutorily protected ground; (3) the unavailability of reasonable internal relocation; and (4) that the Colombian government is unable or unwilling to provide protection from their alleged persecutors. The IJ likewise denied Petitioners' claims for relief under the CAT. Petitioners filed an appeal, but the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. Furthermore, the BIA rejected Petitioners’ contentions that their due process rights were violated by the IJ’s handling of their right to counsel.

II. Discussion

Petitioners challenge the BIA's denial of their requests for asylum and withholding of removal, contending that the BIA incorrectly determined that they failed to prove: (1) the existence of past persecution; (2) that any past persecution was based on their political opinion; and (3) that they possess a well-founded fear of future persecution, which the government is either unable or unwilling to prevent. Petitioners do not contest the denial of relief under the CAT or their due process claim.

A. Standard of Review

Typically, our court has authority to examine final orders of removal, which may encompass a unified review of claims for asylum. See Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (referencing 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)). The scope of our review regarding the Board’s refusal to grant asylum is restricted to "the administrative record on which the order of removal is based." Id. (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). Since the BIA’s decision here was rendered by a single member who upheld the immigration judge’s determination, our analysis is focused on the BIA’s ruling, but "we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds." Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

We assess the BIA’s factual determinations using the substantial evidence standard. According to this standard, the Board’s factual findings will stand as final unless the record indicates that no reasonable adjudicator could decide otherwise. Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012) (citation modified).

B. Legal Framework

“Asylum constitutes a discretionary remedy that the Attorney General may award to qualifying noncitizens.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 857 (citation omitted). For an applicant to qualify for such discretionary asylum, he must demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he satisfies the definition of a refugee. Id.; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1). According to the INA, a refugee is “any person who is outside any country of such person’s nationality . . . who is unable or unwilling to return to, and is unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of, that country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.”

As described in Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005) (emphases added) (quoting § 1101(a)(42)(A)), one route for an applicant to establish refugee status is by demonstrating prior persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. See Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citation omitted). To satisfy this standard, the applicant is required to demonstrate three factors: "(1) one or more events that amount to persecution; (2) persecution that occurred because of one of the protected categories specified by statute; and (3) acts perpetrated by the government itself or by entities the government is either incapable or unwilling to control." Id. (quoting Niang, 422 F.3d at 1194–95).

C. Past Persecution

Petitioners allege that they suffered physical, psychological, and emotional harm, in addition to the noted threats, that rise to the level of past persecution.

The INA does not provide a definition for persecution; however, our precedent establishes that persecution involves the imposition of suffering or harm on individuals who differ by race, religion, or political opinion, in a manner considered offensive, and requires more than mere limitations or threats to one’s life or freedom. See Wiransane v. Ashcroft, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004) (citation modified) (emphasis added). In evaluating such claims, we assess the totality of incidents collectively, as their combined effect may amount to persecution, rather than analyzing each event separately. See Ritonga v. Holder, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). Determining whether an individual has established past private persecution within their country of origin—which is the central question in this case—constitutes a factual determination. Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60 (citing Vicente-Elias v. Mukasey, 532 F.3d 1086, 1091 (10th Cir. 2008)).

First, Petitioners contend that the several threats directed at them amount to persecution. While threats are a factor we evaluate when determining the overall effect of the mistreatment Petitioners endured, precedent holds that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” Karki v. Holder, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). Significantly, Petitioners acknowledged during their merits hearing that they were not subject to physical harm and reiterated in their opening brief that they did not sustain any enduring physical injury. Although physical harm is not a prerequisite for meeting the persecution requirement, id. at 804, the harm experienced must be serious and exceed simple limitations or occasional threats, Brandy v. Holder, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014).

Petitioners request that we additionally take into account the enduring emotional and psychological effects they have suffered. For instance, Duarte states that she experiences insomnia and inability to rest, and her son has begun experiencing panic attacks and stress. Although the circumstances faced by Petitioners are regrettable, apprehensions arising from threats received do not meet the threshold for persecution. Despite the potential for certain incidents to be physically taxing or cause psychological distress, this court has seldom—if ever—granted asylum based solely on threats or emotional and psychological injury. See id. (concluding that even alleged harm such as a ten-hour arrest, detention, and threats did not constitute persecution, despite being “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling”). Moreover, during oral argument, Petitioners’ counsel acknowledged that our jurisprudence has declined asylum in situations featuring even more severe instances of inflicted harm. See, e.g., Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) (holding that “four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [petitioner’s] father” did not amount to persecution); Carias-Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) (finding that being threatened with machetes and robbed six times failed to rise to the level of persecution since “the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand”).

Considering the totality of the circumstances, we conclude that there is substantial evidence to uphold the BIA’s determination that the harm experienced by Petitioners, even when viewed collectively, does not constitute past persecution as defined under the INA.

D. Nexus

Petitioners next contend that the harm they endured was due to grounds protected by statute, specifically their political opinion and their purported membership in a particular social group. However, Petitioners have not provided briefing regarding the definition of the particular social group in question nor demonstrated their inclusion in such a group. Instead, their arguments concerning the nexus requirement center on the claim that Don Rafa persecuted them on account of their political opinion. Therefore, our analysis is confined to whether Petitioners can show a causal link between the alleged persecution and their political opinion. Petitioners claim that the mistreatment by Don Rafa and his associates resulted from their involvement in a campaign opposing Colombia’s current president. According to Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), “[t]o show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” Thus, Petitioners’ political opinion cannot be peripheral, secondary, or merely an ancillary motive for the harm suffered; it must not have been “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason” or a minor contributing factor in the treatment they faced or fear facing. Id. (quoting Dallakoti v. Holder, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)). Notably, a persecutor may act based on a variety of motivations. O.C.V. v. Bondi, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025). Nevertheless, even in so-called mixed-motive circumstances, at least one protected ground must serve as a central cause of the harm imposed. Id. Consequently, in this case, Petitioners must establish that their political opinion constituted or will constitute at least one central motive for the persecution they experienced or anticipate.

Petitioners have not met their burden because the record contains substantial evidence supporting the BIA and IJ’s determinations that the primary reason Petitioners were targeted stemmed from Jimenez’s actions threatening Don Rafa’s financial interests and security, rather than Petitioners’ political views. Before Jimenez encountered Don Rafa, Petitioners persevered with their political engagement despite receiving threatening calls and learning of a fellow campaigner’s murder. Petitioners fail to identify any evidence indicating Don Rafa’s involvement in those earlier threats related to politics. The escalation in threats only occurred once Jimenez became entangled in a property dispute with Don Rafa and began looking into a property Don Rafa was renting. During one threatening episode, an individual accused Jimenez of being affiliated with the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. When Petitioners’ employees were later threatened by the same men, it was explicitly stated that Don Rafa had sent them.

Considering these circumstances, the BIA properly determined that the chief motivation for the threats was tied to Jimenez’s professional and personal interactions with Don Rafa, not to Petitioners’ political opinion. Yuk v. Ashcroft, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[I]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.”).

We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.

E. Government Protection

Petitioners contend that, notwithstanding their inability to demonstrate past persecution, they possess a well-founded fear of persecution in the future, partly due to the alleged incapacity or unwillingness of the Colombian government to shield them. They claim that the individuals perpetrating the persecution are closely tied to and cooperate with the Colombian government, making relocation within Colombia unsafe.

The analysis concerning whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect “examines evidence regarding how a government has responded to persecution suffered by the applicant in the past.” Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. This assessment primarily looks at two forms of evidence: “(1) whether the government intervened to halt the actions of the persecutors . . . , and (2) whether the government extended protective measures to the applicant, for instance by providing protective custody.” Id.

This evaluation also encompasses whether the government was informed about the claimed persecution and provided with a chance to address it. Id. at 861–62. “In the absence of such notice, it remains uncertain how law enforcement would have responded or if the government would have intervened.” Id. (citation modified). Thus, “a crucial element in the analysis is whether the applicant alerted the government to prior persecution.” Id. (citation omitted). Although “not informing the authorities can weaken a contention that the government was unwilling or unable to restrain the persecutors,” this does not automatically defeat the claim. Id. (quoting Aviles-Gonzalez v. Garland, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024)). The applicant can still fulfill the requirement for seeking government protection by showing that submitting a report to police would have been either pointless or put them at risk. Id.

Once more, ample evidence within the record underpins the determinations made by both the BIA and the IJ. The Petitioners' attempts to seek assistance from authorities were, at best, negligible. Both Jimenez and his father have either previously held or currently hold government positions in Colombia, yet neither took steps to report misconduct or corruption. See Aviles-Gonzalez, No. 23-9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (“Without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond . . . .”). The Petitioners stated that they had conversations with two police officers who advised them not to create problems; however, there is no indication that these comments represent an official stance of the Colombian government. See Osorio-Morales v. Garland, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023) (“[W]e often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is ‘unwilling or unable’ to protect its people. Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” (internal citations omitted)). As the IJ determined, the record lacks evidence that Petitioners made any effort to seek help beyond their immediate community or through Jimenez’s professional connections within law enforcement.

Petitioners appear to contend that seeking assistance from the government would have been both pointless and hazardous, on the grounds that their persecutors maintain a close association with the Colombian government. However, the record does not support this claim. The authorities were informed about the disputed property and had conducted multiple searches and seizures in response to the continued criminal activity there. Thus, this evidence runs contrary to Petitioners’ assertion that their persecutors operate in concert with the government, which would allegedly be either unwilling or unable to halt the persecutors’ actions. See Singh, 130 F.4th at 861. Furthermore, Petitioners have not demonstrated that the Colombian government was, or would have been, unwilling or unable to provide protection if it had been made aware of the threats they faced. See Chhetri, 844 F. App’x at 27–28 (finding insufficient proof that the government could not or would not protect an applicant, especially where the applicant chose not to contact the police due to a belief that the persecutors were “part of the government”).

Accordingly, we agree that Petitioners cannot establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum.

F. Safe Relocation

While Petitioners have not met the criteria for asylum, we briefly consider their remaining contention concerning whether it would be possible and reasonable for them to safely relocate within Colombia.

To defeat an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution and thereby bar asylum, the government bears the burden to establish by a preponderance of the evidence either that: “(1) there has been a ‘fundamental change’ in circumstances such that the applicant no longer maintains a well-founded fear of persecution; or (2) the applicant could evade future persecution by relocating elsewhere within the applicant’s country.” Rivera-Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

Petitioners have failed to establish a well-founded fear of persecution in the future; however, even assuming they had done so, the record contains substantial evidence indicating that reasonable relocation to another region within Colombia is possible. Members of Petitioners’ family, including relatives, remain in Colombia without harm, and Jimenez’s father continues his employment within the criminal justice sector. Additionally, Petitioners resided without incident for three weeks alongside Jimenez’s mother in the rural area outside Bogota before opting to move to the United States. It is also important to note that those who issued threats to Petitioners instructed them only to leave their neighborhood, not the country as a whole. Consequently, we conclude that Petitioners can reduce any threat of future persecution by relocating to another area of Colombia where it would be safe and reasonable for them to reside.

III. Conclusion

In conclusion, ample evidence exists to uphold the BIA's determination that Petitioners are not eligible for asylum. Moreover, since Petitioners have not shown a well-founded fear of persecution as required for asylum, they also cannot satisfy the more stringent requirements necessary for withholding of removal. See Carias-Mejia, 860 F. App’x at 125 (quoting Uanreroro, 443 F.3d at 1202).

Accordingly, we deny the petition for review. Petitioners argue that internal relocation is not feasible because, in their view, “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety.” Pet’rs’ Br. 18 (noting that such relatives might hold political opinions that oppose the government). However, asylum cannot be granted on the basis of conjectural hardships. Petitioners further contend that relocation would be impractical due to bureaucratic and financial difficulties, the nation’s legal system, and the alleged connection between their persecutors and the president of Colombia. Nevertheless, these points are only briefly addressed in their briefing, and, given our above analysis, we find their arguments insufficient.
