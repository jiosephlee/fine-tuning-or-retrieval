Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank—who has a lengthy criminal record—guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a felon. The district court sentenced him under the Armed Career Criminal Act to 292 months in prison, which fell within the advisory guideline range calculated by the court. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, asserting that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that denied him a fair trial and that the court erred by applying a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) sentencing enhancement, thereby increasing his advisory guidelines range. We affirm.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a firearm at her, and forced her into a closet while making paranoid allegations about the presence of “listening devices” in the residence and asserting that individuals outside intended to harm him. He held D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for what she described in her testimony as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the gun. At one point, Cutbank instructed D.F. to wake others sleeping in the house, among them her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Cutbank eventually left, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home, indicating that a man was present in the garage and refused to vacate. Upon arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside and, over several hours, uttered incoherent remarks and asserted that he possessed a weapon. Cutbank physically resisted arrest, biting an officer on the face and neck and attempting to take the officer’s firearm. When officers searched the probable route Cutbank would have used to travel between D.F.’s house and the garage, they discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA testing revealed that Cutbank’s genetic material was on the firearm. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the weapon as one they observed in Cutbank’s possession before that night. The jury found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession and determined that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions, activating the enhanced sentencing provisions set forth in § 924(e). He now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court abused its discretion through several pretrial and trial rulings and erroneously applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from testifying that they had seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle on earlier occasions, contending that such testimony would constitute inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court concurred with the government’s position that the evidence was intrinsic and pertinent to establishing the possession charge, as well as to showing that Cutbank continuously possessed the firearm prior to the incident. We likewise concur. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had witnessed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident at issue.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank moved for the jury to be instructed that, in order to preclude hearsay problems and address Confrontation Clause concerns, it could not consider D.F.’s statements to police as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself provided testimony about the events. The government contended that the statements would serve to provide context for the officers’ actions and their understanding at the time, asserting that the officers’ accounts would not violate hearsay rules, and further argued that any required limiting instruction could be effectively given from the bench at a later time in accordance with typical practice. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this case, the record reflects that hearsay evidence was not admitted during trial. Additionally, D.F. provided testimony. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). No abuse of discretion occurred.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident in question. Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony was rejected by the district court. Additionally, the court dismissed a defense objection when C.F., who was called by the prosecution, indicated she had witnessed the firearm at issue in use “once or twice” before the alleged incident. On appeal, Cutback does not provide any legal authority contradicting the district court’s pretrial determination that this testimony constituted intrinsic evidence, which was pertinent both to establishing the possession offense and to showing that Cutbank had ongoing possession of the weapon before the occurrence. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about prior statements that conflicted concerning the gun, including whether she recalled seeing the gun the relevant night, D.F. explained that certain responses were due to her being “not familiar with guns” and disliking guns, attributing this sentiment to the fact that “[her] sister was killed by a gun.” The court overruled Cutback’s motion to exclude the portion of testimony concerning the death of D.F.’s sister by firearm. This evidence was elicited in the context of responding to cross-examination about D.F.’s earlier inconsistent statements. Cutback fails to identify any specific prejudice or confusion arising from this testimony, nor does any appear on the record. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. In addition, C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, referenced the death of D.F.’s sister by firearm without objection from the defense. Accordingly, there is no indication that the district court exceeded its broad discretion over evidentiary matters. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the gun used in the incident “once or twice” by cross-examining her about earlier statements she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted questions concerning C.F.’s statements to the police but sustained the prosecution’s objections to defense counsel attempting to read sections of the police report into evidence without establishing an adequate foundation, since C.F. was not the author of the report. While Cutback did not reference this ruling in his appeal, the government's brief asserted that his “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disfavor appellees who enlarge the scope of appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless, whether this point was preserved or not, it “is without merit.” As stated in United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted), “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” The district court’s requirement for appropriate authentication was not an abuse of discretion.

Lastly, as Cutbank concedes, “this court will not overturn an improper evidentiary decision unless the mistake was harmful. . . . Stated differently, we will only reverse when the improper evidence could have had a significant impact on the jury's decision.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this burden. The government’s case, including both witness testimony and physical proof, was considerable. Consequently, the district court’s decisions regarding evidence “did not affect or affected only minimally the outcome of the trial.” Id. at 998. “We will not reverse a conviction because of the collective impact of trial errors except where there is significant prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the charge of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that he had three or more previous violent felony convictions, which subjected him to increased penalties pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act. According to the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), a base offense level of 26 was recommended due to the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), along with Cutbank’s record of at least two prior violent crime convictions. The PSR further proposed a three-level enhancement based on the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly endangering life while eluding law enforcement, and an additional four-level increase for possessing the firearm in relation to another felony offense. This resulted in a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned Cutbank to Criminal History Category VI, leading to an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. Cutbank challenges, on appeal, the district court’s application of the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is applicable when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm or ammunition in relation to a separate federal, state, or local felony offense, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted the Presentence Investigation Report’s factual findings concerning Cutback’s earlier felony convictions without objection. Therefore, the court did not make any error—let alone clear error—in concluding from the PSR that the firearm was involved in another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that imposing the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and thereby issuing a sentence within the guideline range is inconsistent with the main requirement of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had three or more previous violent felony convictions—triggering the Armed Career Criminal Act’s sentencing provisions—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have set the total offense level at 34. The Guidelines dictate that the higher offense level is to be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, "a district court must commence all sentencing proceedings by accurately determining the applicable Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "A court of appeals may assess the substantive reasonableness of a sentence only after confirming that the district court did not commit significant procedural errors, including failing to calculate, or miscalculating, the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). Following this, the court evaluates whether the factors set forth in § 3553(a) justify either a departure or variance from the advisory Guidelines sentence.

In this case, the court handed down a sentence that fell within the proper guidelines range, which we treat as presumptively reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court indicated that it had examined the PSR and the parties’ sentencing memoranda, as well as evaluated the § 3553(a) factors potentially supporting a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” We do not require the district court to recite each factor it evaluated to sustain the sentence; it must simply be apparent from the record that the court took the § 3553(a) factors into account when choosing the sentence. United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record included almost twelve prior felony convictions, a number of which were for violent crimes and offenses involving firearms. The instant offense entailed domestic violence, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s home in the presence of her children, and causing injury to a police officer during the course of resisting arrest. The district court acted within the wide discretion afforded at sentencing and did not impose a substantively unreasonable sentence when it sentenced Cutback to 292 months’ imprisonment, a term within the guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
