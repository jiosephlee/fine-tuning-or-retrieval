Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant prior criminal record, guilty of violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) by possessing a firearm as a convicted felon. The district court imposed a sentence of 292 months' imprisonment under the Armed Career Criminal Act, which was within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. On appeal, Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that resulted in an unfair trial, and also erred in applying a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) that raised his guidelines range. We affirm the district court’s judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., brandished a firearm at her, and compelled her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions about “listening devices” in their residence and individuals outside allegedly intending him harm. D.F. testified that Cutbank held her at gunpoint inside the closet for what seemed to her to be several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the weapon. At a certain point, Cutbank demanded that D.F. awaken the other occupants in the house, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank departed, taking D.F.’s cellphone and the firearm. D.F. subsequently contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement also received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.’s home indicating that a male individual was present in the garage and refused to leave. Upon arrival, officers encountered Cutbank, who had barricaded himself inside the garage. During several hours, Cutbank exhibited erratic behavior, made incoherent statements, and asserted that he was armed. When police attempted to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted arrest, biting one officer on the face and neck and seizing the officer’s firearm. In searching the route that Cutbank likely traversed between D.F.’s residence and the garage, police discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA analysis of the firearm revealed a match to Cutbank. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified the rifle as the same weapon they had previously seen in Cutbank’s possession before the incident in question. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm and further determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the enhanced penalty provisions set forth in § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court abused its discretion through several pretrial and trial rulings and erred in applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank raises a series of evidentiary and procedural issues, providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments. None of the contentions has merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from stating that they had seen him previously with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted propensity evidence prohibited by Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court sided with the government, concluding that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and to illustrating that Cutbank had ongoing possession of the firearm prior to the event in question. We concur. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “[p]rior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” At the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on “a handful of times” before the incident in question.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested that the jury receive an instruction—aimed at preventing hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems—that D.F.’s statements to police should not be considered as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself testified about the incident. The government maintained that the statements would only provide background for the officers’ actions and knowledge at the relevant time, that the officers’ accounts would not contravene hearsay prohibitions, and that, if necessary, the court could issue an appropriate instruction later, consistent with usual practice. “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to precedent, the district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” Id. In this matter, the record reflects that the court did not allow hearsay evidence during trial. Moreover, D.F. did, in fact, take the stand and testify. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Thus, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During the trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. The district court overruled Cutback’s renewed objection to this line of testimony. Additionally, when C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, testified that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice,” the court also rejected a defense objection. On appeal, Cutback does not reference any authority that challenges the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge and was probative of continuous possession of the firearm prior to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about prior statements that conflicted concerning the firearm—including assertions that she did not recall seeing a gun on the relevant evening—D.F. explained that some of her remarks stemmed from being "not familiar with guns" and that she "did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun." The district court denied Cutback’s request to strike the testimony about D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun. This statement was made in response to cross-examination about earlier inconsistent statements. Cutback does not identify any specific unfair prejudice or confusion arising from this testimony, and the record reveals none. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, when C.F. testified for the government, she also referred to D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun, and no objection was raised by the defense. Thus, the district court did not exceed its wide latitude in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had observed the firearm involved in the incident “once or twice” previously by cross-examining her concerning earlier statements she gave to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiry into what C.F. had communicated to law enforcement but upheld the government’s objections when defense counsel tried to read sections of the corresponding police report into evidence without adequate foundation, given that C.F. was not the author of that report. While Cutback did not reference this evidentiary ruling in his appeal, the government’s brief maintained that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disfavor appellees who broaden appellate issues unnecessarily. Regardless, preserved or otherwise, this “claim” lacks substance. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court’s requirement for proper authentication did not constitute an abuse of discretion.

At bottom, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not overturn an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the mistake was harmless. . . . Put differently, we will only reverse where the improperly admitted evidence could have substantially affected the jury’s verdict.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank does not meet this standard. The government introduced considerable evidence, both in witness testimony and physical exhibits. Accordingly, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “either had no impact or only a minimal impact on the jury’s decision.” Id. at 998. “We decline to set aside a conviction due to the combined effect of trial errors unless the defendant suffers substantial prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the offense of being a felon in possession of a firearm. Additionally, the jury determined that he had three or more previous convictions for violent felonies, thereby subjecting him to the increased penalties mandated by the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended a base offense level of 26 due to the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as specified in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), along with at least two prior convictions for violent crimes. The PSR also recommended a three-level enhancement because the firearm’s serial number was altered or obliterated, a two-level enhancement for recklessly endangering life or causing serious bodily harm during flight from law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in relation to another felony offense, culminating in a total offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, leading to an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred in applying the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

The enhancement is triggered when a defendant employs or possesses any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). During sentencing, the district court accepted, without challenge, the Presentence Report’s factual account of Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Consequently, the court did not commit error—much less clear error—by determining that the PSR’s facts demonstrate that the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (articulating the standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, however, contends that imposing the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) leads to a sentence within the applicable range that “conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a),” namely that a sentence should “be sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Even though the jury determined that Cutback had three or more previous violent felony convictions—thereby activating the penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act—the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would result in a total offense level of 34. According to the Guidelines, the higher offense level is to be applied. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, "the district court should start every sentencing hearing by accurately determining the correct Guidelines range." Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). "An appellate court may only assess the substantive reasonableness of a sentence after verifying that the district court did not make a significant procedural mistake, such as neglecting to calculate, or incorrectly calculating, the Guidelines range." Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this, the court evaluates whether the factors outlined in § 3553(a) justify a sentence that either departs from or varies from the recommended advisory guidelines range.

In this case, the district court imposed a sentence falling within the appropriate guideline range, a sentence that is presumed reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court acknowledged that it had reviewed the Presentence Report (PSR), the sentencing memoranda, and had considered the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” Moreover, “To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s record included close to twelve prior felony convictions, several involving acts of violence and use of firearms. The instant conviction stemmed from acts of domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault within the victim’s residence in the presence of her children, and injuring a police officer during resistance to arrest. The district court did not overstep its broad sentencing authority nor issue a substantively unreasonable sentence by sentencing Cutback to a 292-month term of imprisonment within the guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
