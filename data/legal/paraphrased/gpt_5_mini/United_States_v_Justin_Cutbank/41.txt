Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank, who has a significant criminal background, guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm, in contravention of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). Pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, the district court sentenced him to 292 months in prison, a term falling within the advisory guidelines range calculated by the court. Cutbank challenges both his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court made numerous evidentiary and procedural mistakes that deprived him of a fair trial and wrongly applied the USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, resulting in an increased advisory guidelines range. We affirm the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, following consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank roused his girlfriend, D.F., brandished a firearm at her face, and compelled her into a closet. While doing so, he voiced paranoid allegations regarding the presence of “listening devices” in the residence and claimed there were individuals outside intent on harming him. Cutbank detained D.F. inside the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described in her testimony as feeling like several hours, during which he threatened to kill her and struck her in the face with the weapon. At one juncture, he instructed D.F. to awaken the other occupants of the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank departed, taking both D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. Subsequently, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Law enforcement received a separate report from a residence located half a mile away from D.F.’s home indicating that an individual was inside the garage and refused to leave. Upon their arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, where, for several hours, he exhibited unpredictable behavior and asserted that he was armed. During attempts to apprehend him, Cutbank resisted by biting an officer’s face and neck and by attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. Officers conducting a search along the probable route between D.F.’s residence and the garage discovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with the barrel and stock sawn off. Subsequent DNA analysis linked the firearm to Cutbank. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. testified that they had previously seen Cutbank with this weapon. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the felon in possession charge and determined he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thus invoking the enhanced penalty provisions of § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court abused its discretion in several pretrial and trial rulings and erred in applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple evidentiary and procedural challenges but offers virtually no legal authority or substantive analysis in support of these arguments. All of these claims lack merit.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to bar D.F. and C.F. from offering testimony that they had seen him previously in possession of the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, contending that such testimony constituted propensity evidence in contravention of Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The district court concurred with the government's position that this evidence was intrinsic to the charged offense and pertinent for establishing Cutbank’s ongoing possession of the firearm before the event in question. We concur with this assessment. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” During trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident at issue.

B

Prior to the commencement of trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be given an instruction clarifying that D.F.’s statements to the police could not be used as substantive evidence of Cutbank’s conduct unless D.F. personally testified to the relevant incidents, in order to address potential hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns. The prosecution countered that these statements would merely serve to explain the police officers’ actions and their awareness at the time, that the officers’ recounting would not violate hearsay rules, and that it was standard practice for the court to deliver an appropriate instruction from the bench, if necessary, at a later stage. The standard of review for a district court’s refusal to provide a limiting instruction is abuse of discretion. United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). According to Bennett, the trial court is not required to instruct the jury regarding each piece of evidence as it is introduced, provided an adequate instruction is given at the conclusion of the trial. In this instance, the trial transcripts show that no hearsay was admitted. Furthermore, D.F. took the stand and gave testimony. The requirement of the Confrontation Clause is met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Therefore, no abuse of discretion occurred.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle on several occasions prior to the incident. When Cutback renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court denied it. Additionally, when C.F., a witness for the government, testified that she had witnessed the firearm from the incident “once or twice” before, the court similarly overruled the defense’s objection. On appeal, Cutback does not point to any authority contesting the court’s pretrial determination that this constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to the possession charge, as well as evidence of continuous possession of the weapon leading up to the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about earlier inconsistent statements related to the firearm—including her lack of recollection about seeing the weapon on the night in question—D.F. explained that some of her remarks stemmed from her unfamiliarity with firearms and her aversion to them, noting, “[a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The district court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister’s death by gun. This particular testimony was offered as an explanation during cross-examination focused on her prior inconsistent statements. Cutback does not identify, nor is there any evident, prejudice or confusion resulting from the statement. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, while C.F. provided testimony as a government witness, she too referenced the fatal shooting of D.F.’s sister without eliciting an objection from the defense. The district court did not exceed its considerable discretion in evidentiary rulings. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.'s assertion that she had previously seen the firearm involved in the event “once or twice” by questioning her during cross-examination about earlier remarks made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries concerning what C.F. had told the police but upheld the government's objections when defense counsel tried to introduce excerpts from the police report into evidence without laying the necessary foundation, as the report was not written by C.F. Although Cutback did not specifically address this ruling on appeal, the government maintained in its brief that Cutback’s “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disfavor appellees who broaden the scope of issues on appeal unnecessarily. Regardless, preserved or otherwise, the “claim” lacks substance. As established, “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court did not err in requiring that authentication be established.

Lastly, as Cutback concedes, “this court does not overturn an evidentiary error if it is harmless. . . . Put differently, reversal is warranted only if the erroneously admitted evidence could have significantly affected the jury’s decision.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank fails to meet this standard. The prosecution presented strong testimonial and physical proof. Accordingly, the district court’s evidentiary decisions “either did not affect, or had only minimal impact on, the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not reverse a conviction due to the collective impact of errors during trial absent substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury determined that Cutbank was guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm. They further concluded that he had three or more previous violent felony convictions, thereby subjecting him to increased penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act. According to the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), a base offense level of 26 was warranted due to the offense involving a sawed-off firearm as outlined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and because Cutbank had at least two previous convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also recommended a three-level enhancement for a firearm with an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement based on recklessly creating a risk of death or serious injury while fleeing law enforcement, and an additional four-level enhancement due to possession of the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These enhancements resulted in a total offense level of 35. The PSR placed Cutbank in Criminal History Category VI, leading to an advisory sentencing guideline range of 292 to 365 months' imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank contends that the district court erred when it applied the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense as set forth in § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is applicable when a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense under federal, state, or local law, “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At sentencing, the district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings about Cutback’s prior felony convictions without any objection. Therefore, the court did not make a mistake—let alone a clear error—by determining that the PSR established the firearm was connected to another felony. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (review standard), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Cutbank, however, contends that applying the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), while within the Guideline range, yields a sentence that is at odds with the fundamental principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that any sentence should be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection.” Although the jury determined that Cutback had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies and thus qualified for the Armed Career Criminal Act, the offense level calculated under the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have totaled 34. The Guidelines state that the higher offense level should be used. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When deciding on a proper sentence, the district court must initially determine the correct Guidelines range for the offense. Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). The court of appeals, before evaluating whether a sentence is substantively reasonable, must verify that the district court did not commit a significant procedural mistake, including not calculating or incorrectly determining the Guidelines range. Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this calculation, the court assesses whether the factors listed under § 3553(a) justify a deviation from the advisory guideline sentence, either through a departure or a variance.

In this case, the district court sentenced Cutback within the relevant guidelines range, a sentence which is presumed reasonable on appeal. United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court indicated it had reviewed the PSR, sentencing briefs, and had considered the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. “Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). “We do not require a district court to engage in a formulaic recitation of the considerations it balanced; it need only be evident from the record that the district court did, in fact, take the § 3553(a) factors into account when determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutback’s criminal history consisted of almost twelve prior felony convictions, with several implicating violence and firearms. The conduct underlying this conviction encompassed domestic violence, kidnapping, assaulting the victim in her residence with her children present, and injuring a police officer while resisting apprehension. Accordingly, the district court did not exceed its considerable discretion in sentencing, nor did it issue a substantively unreasonable sentence, by imposing a 292-month term of imprisonment within the applicable range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
