Title: United States v. Justin Cutbank
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit
Date: October 7, 2025
Docket: No. 24-1225
Author: Loken, Circuit Judge

A jury found Justin Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm, violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). Cutbank, who has an extensive criminal record, received a 292-month prison term pursuant to the Armed Career Criminal Act, which fell within the advisory guideline range as determined by the district court. On appeal, he challenges both his conviction and sentence, contending that the district court made several evidentiary and procedural mistakes that resulted in an unfair trial and wrongly imposed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) that increased his advisory guideline range. We uphold the judgment.

I. Background

On the night spanning November 19 to 20, 2020, after having consumed alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank woke his girlfriend, D.F., aimed a firearm at her head, and forced her into a closet while expressing paranoid suspicions concerning supposed “listening devices” in the residence and alleging there were people outside intent on hurting him. Cutbank detained D.F. inside the closet at gunpoint for what D.F. described as seeming like several hours, threatening to kill her and hitting her in the face with the weapon. At one point, Cutbank demanded that D.F. wake up the other individuals asleep in the house, which included her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. Ultimately, Cutbank escaped, taking D.F.’s cellphone and the firearm. Afterward, D.F. contacted law enforcement.

Additionally, law enforcement received a report from a residence located approximately half a mile from D.F.'s home, stating that a male individual was inside the garage and refusing to leave. Upon arrival, officers found that Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage, where he proceeded for several hours to make unpredictable remarks and assert that he possessed a weapon. When officers attempted to take him into custody, Cutbank physically resisted, biting one officer on the face and neck and attempting to seize the officer’s firearm. A search of the route Cutbank would have traversed between D.F.’s residence and the garage led officers to discover D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had both its barrel and stock sawed off. DNA analysis revealed that Cutbank's genetic material was present on the firearm. During trial, both D.F. and C.F. testified that they recognized the rifle as the one they observed in Cutbank's possession earlier that evening. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the charged felon in possession crime, further determining that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thus subjecting him to the heightened penalty provisions under § 924(e). Cutbank now challenges his conviction and sentence on appeal, contending that the district court committed errors in several pretrial and trial rulings and incorrectly applied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to increase his sentence.

II. The Evidentiary and Procedural Issues.

Cutbank presents multiple arguments regarding evidentiary and procedural matters but fails to cite relevant authority or offer substantive analysis for his claims. All of these assertions are unpersuasive.

A

Prior to trial, Cutbank submitted a motion seeking to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle, on the grounds that such testimony constituted propensity evidence prohibited under Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. The district court found merit in the government’s argument that the testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and was indicative of uninterrupted possession of the firearm leading up to the incident. We concur with this determination. As stated in United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 575 U.S. 978 (2015), "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." During the trial, D.F. testified that she had observed Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle on several occasions before the relevant incident.

B

Prior to trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be instructed not to treat D.F.’s statements to law enforcement as substantive evidence regarding Cutbank’s conduct until D.F. herself testified about the incidents, in order to prevent hearsay problems and safeguard Confrontation Clause rights. The government responded that the statements were offered to explain the circumstances surrounding the officers’ actions and knowledge at the relevant times, asserted that the officers’ testimony would comply with hearsay restrictions, and noted that a limiting instruction from the court—if needed—could be appropriately delivered later, consistent with common practice. "We review a district court’s refusal to provide a limiting instruction for an abuse of discretion.” United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), cert. denied, 574 U.S. 1180 (2015). The district court “is not required to give the jury an immediate limiting instruction as each item of evidence is introduced so long as a suitable instruction is delivered at the end of trial.” Id. In this case, the record confirms that no hearsay was admitted during trial. Moreover, D.F. did in fact testify. The requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010) (quotation omitted). Consequently, there was no abuse of discretion.

C

During trial, D.F. stated that she had observed Cutbank in possession of the sawed-off rifle several times prior to the incident in question. When Cutback renewed his objection to this testimony, the district court overruled it. Additionally, the court rejected a defense objection when C.F., called as a government witness, testified that she had previously seen the firearm in question "once or twice" before the incident occurred. On appeal, Cutback provides no legal authority challenging the district court’s pretrial determination that such testimony constituted intrinsic evidence pertinent to establishing the possession charge and showing ongoing possession of the firearm before the incident. See, e.g., Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511.

D

While being cross-examined about previous inconsistent statements she made regarding the gun—including statements that she could not recall seeing the gun on the relevant night—D.F. explained that she made certain statements because she was “not familiar with guns” and disliked them due to the fact that “[her] sister was killed by a gun.” The district court rejected Cutback’s request to strike the portion of D.F.’s testimony referencing her sister’s death by gun. This evidence was introduced in response to inquiries during cross-examination regarding D.F.’s inconsistent prior statements. Cutback does not identify any specific unfair prejudice or potential for confusion resulting from the testimony, and none is evident. See Fed. R. Evidence 403. Additionally, C.F., appearing as a witness for the government, also referenced the fact that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun, with no objection from the defense. Accordingly, the district court did not exceed its substantial discretion in managing the admission of evidence. See, e.g., Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008).

E

The defense attempted to challenge C.F.’s assertion that she had previously seen the firearm “once or twice” by questioning her about earlier statements made to a police investigator. The district court permitted inquiries into what C.F. told police, but upheld the prosecution’s objections to defense counsel introducing segments of the corresponding police report into evidence without adequate foundation, as C.F. was not the document's author. Although Cutback did not specifically reference this decision in his appellate arguments, the government, in its brief, contended that Cutback's “next claim is that the district court ‘thwarted’ his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession.” We disapprove of appellees unnecessarily enlarging the scope of appellate issues. Regardless, whether or not this contention was properly preserved, it lacks merit. “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted). The district court acted within its discretion by insisting on appropriate authentication.

At bottom, as Cutback concedes, “this court will not overturn a mistaken evidentiary decision if the error did not affect the outcome. . . . Put differently, we will grant reversal only where the improperly admitted evidence could have materially influenced the jury’s decision.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quotation omitted). Cutbank is unable to meet this standard. The government presented robust evidence, including both witness testimony and physical exhibits. Therefore, the district court’s evidentiary choices “either had no effect or merely a minor impact on the verdict.” Id. at 998. “We will not vacate a conviction on account of the combined impact of trial errors unless the defendant suffered significant prejudice.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quotation omitted).

III. The Sentencing Issue.

The jury convicted Cutbank of the charge of being a felon in possession of a firearm. They further determined that he had at least three prior convictions for violent felonies, thereby invoking the enhanced penalties mandated by the Armed Career Criminal Act. According to the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR), a base offense level of 26 was recommended, based on the involvement of a sawed-off firearm as defined under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutbank’s two or more prior convictions for crimes of violence. The PSR also proposed a three-level enhancement due to the firearm’s altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement because Cutbank recklessly endangered life while evading law enforcement, and a four-level enhancement for possessing the firearm in connection with a separate felony offense, leading to a combined offense level of 35. The PSR assigned a Criminal History Category of VI, producing an advisory sentencing range under the guidelines of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. On appeal, Cutbank challenges the district court’s application of the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense pursuant to § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).

This enhancement is triggered if a defendant possessed or utilized any firearm or ammunition in relation to another federal, state, or local felony offense, "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). At the sentencing hearing, the district court, without objection from the parties, accepted the factual assertions made in the PSR about Cutback’s earlier felony convictions. Consequently, the court did not err, let alone clearly err, in determining that the facts in the PSR demonstrated that the firearm was connected to another felony offense. See United States v. Dunn, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013) (standard of review), cert. denied, 571 U.S. 1145 (2014). Instead, Cutbank contends that imposing the 4-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) leads to a sentence within the Guidelines range that is inconsistent with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), namely that a sentence should be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection." Although the jury found that Cutback had at least three previous violent felony convictions, activating Armed Career Criminal Act sentencing, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines dictate that the higher offense level controls. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).

When imposing a sentence, “a district court’s first step must be to accurately compute the applicable Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). Furthermore, “an appellate court may only review the substantive reasonableness of a sentence after confirming that the district court did not make any substantial procedural mistakes, such as neglecting to determine—or incorrectly determining—the Guidelines range.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018). After this determination, the court evaluates if the § 3553(a) factors support either a departure from or a variance to the advisory Guidelines sentence.

In this case, the district court issued a sentence falling within the advisory guideline range, a decision we typically consider reasonable on appeal. See United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). During sentencing, the court referenced its examination of the PSR and sentencing memoranda and considered the § 3553(a) factors that might justify a variance. As stated in United States v. Masood, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted), “[t]hus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” It is not necessary for the district court to “provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” United States v. Maluoth, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024) (quotation omitted). Cutbank’s record reflected nearly twelve prior felony convictions, several of which included violent acts and use of firearms. The present offense included acts of domestic violence, kidnapping, assaulting the victim inside her home and in front of her children, and causing injury to a police officer during the arrest. The district court exercised its considerable discretion appropriately and did not impose a substantively unreasonable sentence by sentencing Cutbank to 292 months’ imprisonment within the guidelines range.

The judgment of the district court is affirmed.
