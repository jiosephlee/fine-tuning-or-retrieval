Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together referred to as Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) in their favor regarding noninfringement of the asserted claims in U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. The Appellants further contest the district court’s decision denying their request for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which are directed to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio communications. Typically, radios prevent interference by utilizing separate frequencies, but transmission signals can interact when confronted with certain obstructions (such as metal fences, improperly connected cables, or corroded connectors), resulting in the creation of additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs arise from non-electronic obstacles, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895; ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated litigation against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent due to AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) capability within Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the case. J.A. 389–90. The jury returned a verdict finding that all asserted claims were both valid and infringed, and awarded lump-sum damages of $166,303,391 covering the remainder of the term for both the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Appellants filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement, JMOL on damages, and for a new trial, all of which were denied by the district court. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal. Jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL in accordance with the precedent of the relevant regional circuit, which, in this case, is the Fifth Circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit conducts a de novo review of JMOL denials and will overturn such a denial “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (relying on Fifth Circuit precedent). Our review of a jury’s infringement determination is for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We analyze claim construction de novo and review for clear error any subsidiary factual determinations underpinning the district court’s claim construction. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process aimed at minimizing IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals” and subsequently neutralizing the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent, Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling, at a selected frequency greater than the Nyquist rate, a passband from the incoming signals to generate a bit stream, where the incoming signals comprise both desired signals and interference-producing signals, the latter being capable of generating intermodulation products within the frequency band of the desired signals; [b] extracting the desired signals from the bit stream through one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting source signals responsible for creating one or more intermodulation products present within the frequency band of the desired signal, also using one or more decimating filters; [d] generating an estimate for each of the one or more intermodulation products from the identified source signals; [e] eliminating one or more inband intermodulation products by employing the computed estimates; and [f] adjusting the phase and amplitude of the intermodulation product interference signal estimations in a closed-loop fashion, with such phase and amplitude adjustment including the use of sub-sample phase shifts to modify the phase of the intermodulation product interference signal estimations. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphasis in original). As the plaintiff, Finesse was responsible for establishing that the accused radios perform sampling on a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not demonstrate the accused radios sample both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, testified that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent, basing his analysis on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, displayed below). Finesse Br. 15 (showing annotation of J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), shown within the blue box, acts as a receiver that samples both the asserted “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals”—each indicated along the dashed red path—producing a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells repeatedly stated that he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference, and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, as indicated by the dashed red path and detailed in the red box within the legend. For instance, J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”); 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”); 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). His expert report likewise concluded, “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel noted that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path since that path is produced after the RF ADC samples the signals, meaning signals cannot be sampled before their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. This implies the modeled PIM path cannot represent the claimed “interference generating signals.” In reply, Dr. Wells shifted and asserted, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, he did not explicitly equate x1 and x2 with the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” but rather continued to identify “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When pressed for clarification on whether “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Nevertheless, he did not link x1 or x2 to either “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals” as the claims require.

The jury determined all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In its JMOL motion, Appellants asserted that Dr. Wells mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, and thus, infringement could not be found because the accused radios’ receivers do not sample a received signal passband that includes interference generating signals, as such signals are not present until after the sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized, “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Accordingly, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the claimed “interference generating signals,” then limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied, since the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a signal passband that

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

Within a receiver, alongside a transmitter with which it is co-located, the described method includes: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) that continuously and nearly in real time cancel passive IMPs present in the receiver, utilizing copies of the transmitter’s signals, [b] where these passive IMPs are created within passive transmitter components and within receiver components, and occur after the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter, [c] where the transmitter filter is connected between the HPA and the transmitter’s antenna, [d] where ICS generation is predicated on a power series model of the non-linear process that gives rise to the IMPs, and includes creating an n-th order ICS by, with three signals S1, S2, and S3, performing digital multiplication and filtering on the combinations S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, with n representing an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). At the summary judgment stage, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as needing to be “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined from the intrinsic record that there was no restriction confining “S1, S2 and S3” to unique input signals, and also relied on inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence that “S1, S2 and S3” are general signal representations, which may either be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. The primary claim limitation disputed on appeal is the requirement that seven distinct third order multiplications involving the three signals be performed to satisfy the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, referencing a Nokia technical document that illustrates the functionality of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). In particular, Dr. Wells indicated that the accused radios conduct multiplication of three separately identifiable signals, and correlated the three distinct multiplications shown in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications claimed in the patent. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury concluded that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. When considering JMOL, the district court dismissed Appellants’ contention that “S1, S2 and S3” are required to be unique signals, deeming this argument waived since Appellants had not presented it during the Rule 50(a) motion. J.A. 38, 40. The court determined that the use of two signals, along with a duplicate of one, satisfied the court’s construction requiring three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. Additionally, the court found that employing two unique signals leads to three distinct multiplications, and this was adequate to prove infringement since each of those three distinct multiplications matches up with two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting that the accused devices operate using only two signals, namely x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished any claim to coverage involving two signals when, during prosecution, it amended the claims to require “three signals” in place of the original language “two or three signals,” specifically to distinguish the invention from the Filipovic prior art reference, which describes generating cancellation signals with two inputs. See J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants assert that a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications claimed, as Dr. Wells only pointed to three multiplications. See Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the contention that Finesse relinquished claim scope concerning two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim that included “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious in light of Filipovic and other secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse overcame this rejection in part by arguing that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not address passive IMPs, which the claimed method does. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse did amend the claims to introduce the requirement of digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications, such an amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of claim scope covering products with two distinct input signals, such as the accused radios. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. The most that can be inferred from Finesse’s amendment is a requirement for three separately identifiable signals; it does not mandate a particular number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. However, we do concur that the evidence could not support a finding by any reasonable jury that the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications recited in the claims. At trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to assert that the accused radios execute the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Nevertheless, the cited document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not demonstrate that the accused radios perform the seven required multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Instead, it identifies only three specific multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no indication that any multiplications are duplicated to satisfy the performance of all seven required by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (as reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three distinct multiplications relate to the seven multiplications identified in the asserted claims. See J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Although Finesse tries to clarify this correspondence on appeal, such evidence was not submitted to the jury. See Finesse Br. 17–18 (referencing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Furthermore, since the document refers to the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2, rather than their multiplication, the mapping advanced by Finesse is deficient; for instance, it would require S3 to correspond to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications because Nokia’s documents reveal that the accused radios actually execute ten multiplications. See Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (referencing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (mentioning p(n) = a0 through a9). However, neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identified which seven out of the ten multiplications in the Nokia document correspond to the seven claimed in the patent. See Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor do they detail how the ten multiplications relate to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse contended mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. Given these deficiencies, a reasonable jury could not have concluded that the accused radios implement the seven claimed multiplications. Therefore, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s infringement finding lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

We assess a district court’s denial of a new trial motion pursuant to the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages, or alternatively, a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we accordingly vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
