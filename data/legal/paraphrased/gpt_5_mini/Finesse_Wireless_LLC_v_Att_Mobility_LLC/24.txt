Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the decision of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, which refused to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. In addition, Appellants contest the district court's rejection of their request for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons that follow, we reverse the order denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) is the owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. Radio systems typically prevent interference by utilizing distinct frequencies, but the transmission signals may merge—particularly upon encountering impediments such as metal fences, defective cable connections, or corroded connectors—producing additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When these IMPs are produced by passive barriers, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought an infringement action against AT&T, alleging infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent through AT&T’s use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature within Nokia’s radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the case. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all asserted claims were valid and infringed, and awarded Finesse a lump-sum payment of $166,303,391 as damages for the remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL regarding the damages amount, and requested a new trial; all such motions were denied by the district court. J.A. 1–72. The appellants now appeal. Our jurisdiction is established under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We evaluate the district court's refusal to grant JMOL based on the law of the applicable regional circuit, which in this matter is the Fifth Circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). According to the Fifth Circuit, denials of JMOL are assessed de novo and reversed only "if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (quoting Fifth Circuit precedent). The jury’s determination regarding infringement is analyzed for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). "A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). We consider the construction of claims anew, or de novo, and review for clear error any subsidiary factual determinations underlying the district court’s interpretation. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent discloses a process designed to mitigate IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently neutralizing the “interference generating signals” with a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative claim.

1. A method comprising:

[a] sampling at a higher rate than the Nyquist rate, at a selected frequency, a frequency band containing received signals so as to generate a sequence of bits, where the received signals comprise both target signals and signals that create interference, with such interference signals being capable of producing intermodulation artifacts within the frequency range of the desired signals; [b] extracting the target signals from the bit sequence utilizing one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting the source signals responsible for producing one or more intermodulation artifacts within the frequency range of the target signal using one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an approximation of each intermodulation artifact from the source signals responsible for their creation; [e] mitigating one or more intermodulation artifacts occurring within the relevant band using the approximated intermodulation artifact values; and [f] executing adjustments to the phase and amplitude of the estimates of the intermodulation artifact interference signals in a closed-loop fashion, such that adjusting the phase and amplitude of these estimates includes applying sub-sample phase shifts for phase correction of the intermodulation artifact interference estimates. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). Finesse, as plaintiff, carried the responsibility to establish that the accused radios perform sampling on a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants maintain that Finesse did not demonstrate the accused radios perform sampling of both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During the trial, Finesse’s infringement specialist, Dr. Jonathan Wells, stated that the accused radios practice the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by relying on a technical document from Nokia that explains the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, illustrated below). Finesse Br. 15 (annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, indicated within the blue box), serves as a receiver which samples both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals" (each represented by dashed red paths) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells consistently explained that he equated "signals of interest" with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and associated "interference generating signals" with the modeled PIM path (both shown along the dashed red path and further detailed within the red box in the legend). See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Consistent with this, Dr. Wells asserted in his expert report that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” while “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343.

During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel observed that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path because this path is created after the RF ADC performs sampling, making it impossible for signals to be sampled prior to their generation. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Accordingly, the modeled PIM path could not correspond to the claimed "interference generating signals." In response, Dr. Wells adjusted his testimony and stated: “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, he did not clarify that x1 and x2 corresponded to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Rather, he continued to assert, “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; also see J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned if he was testifying that “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” he answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet again, he did not relate x1 or x2 to the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as required by the claims.

The jury found for infringement as to all asserted claims of the ’134 patent. J.A. 169. In seeking judgment as a matter of law, Appellants argued that Dr. Wells identified the "interference generating signals" with the modeled PIM path, and that no infringement could be found because the receivers in the accused radios do not sample a passband of received signals containing the interference generating signals, as those signals are only generated subsequent to sampling. J.A. 19–20. The district court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and acknowledged that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Therefore, if the modeled PIM path constitutes the claimed

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver system where both the transmitter and the receiver are situated together, the process involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to eliminate passive IMPs from the receiver, continually and nearly in real time, utilizing duplicates of signals generated by the transmitter; [b] the passive IMPs originate in passive transmitter components and receiver elements, post high powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter’s filter; [c] the transmitter filter is located between the HPA and the antenna operated by the transmitter; [d] producing the ICSs relies on a power series model characterizing a nonlinear mechanism responsible for generating the IMPs, and comprises synthesizing an n-th order ICS. Given three signals S1, S2, and S3, this process entails the digital multiplication and filtering of S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n represents an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases added). Upon summary judgment, the district court interpreted the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean that the signals are “separately identifiable,” without the need for them to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined from the intrinsic record that there is no restriction requiring “S1, S2 and S3” to be unique input signals and also accepted, as extrinsic proof, the inventor’s statement indicating that “S1, S2 and S3” broadly denote signals which may be identical or different. J.A. 109–14. The principal claim requirement contested on appeal is the necessity for seven third order multiplications of the three signals as required by the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the defendant radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, basing his analysis on a Nokia technical document that outlines the functioning of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). In his testimony, Dr. Wells asserted that the accused radios perform multiplication with three individually distinguishable signals and paralleled the three discrete multiplications listed in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications specified in the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury determined that all contested claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In resolving the JMOL, the district court found that the Appellants had waived their contention that “S1, S2 and S3” are required to be unique, as they did not bring it up at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. According to the court, it was sufficient under its claim construction if two signals and an additional copy of one constituted three separately identifiable signals. J.A. 38. The court further decided that with two unique signals, only three distinct multiplications result, and this suffices for infringement because each of those three multiplications corresponds to two or three out of the seven multiplications called for in the claims. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have concluded the accused radios produce cancellation signals through multiplication of three signals, asserting that the radios in question employ only two signals, x1 and x2. See Appellants Br. 49–55. Appellants further maintain that Finesse narrowed the claim scope to exclude systems using two signals when, during prosecution, it amended the claims from “two or three signals” to “three signals” specifically to distinguish the claims from the Filipovic prior art, which discloses the use of two inputs for generating cancellation signals. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants argue that it was unreasonable for the jury to find the accused radios performed the seven multiplications claimed, as Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We reject the contention that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for scenarios involving two signals. The examiner had previously found a claim that referred to “digitally multiplying two or three signals” to be obvious in light of Filipovic and other secondary sources, J.A. 1006–07; however, Finesse overcame this rejection partially by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” rather than passive IMPs, the latter of which are also addressed by the claimed approach. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). While Finesse subsequently modified the claims to specify digital multiplication of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” involving seven multiplications, this amendment does not constitute the type of “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope necessary to exclude devices such as the accused radios that utilize two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, the amendments stipulate that three individually discernible signals must be present, but they do not demand a predetermined quantity of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could find that the accused radios implement the seven multiplications specified in the claim. During trial, Dr. Wells referred to a Nokia document to contend the accused products carried out the asserted multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, that document refers to modulus and complex conjugate functions and does not demonstrate the seven specific multiplications set out in the claims. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it identifies only three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no evidence that any multiplication occurs more than once to reach the required total of seven multiplications as stipulated in limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony detailing how the three distinct multiplications correspond to the seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (citing J.A. 28416–17). While Finesse seeks to elucidate this mapping on appeal, this information was not submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Additionally, since the referenced document does not indicate multiplication of x1 and x2, but rather their complex conjugate and modulus, Finesse's mapping contains defects—for instance, assigning S3 to the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse asserted that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications based on Nokia’s documentation showing the accused radios, in fact, carry out ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identifies which specific seven of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia documentation correspond to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). They also do not provide a mapping relating the ten Nokia multiplications to the three distinct multiplications previously mapped by Finesse to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. On these facts, no reasonable jury could conclude that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. Accordingly, we reverse the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law of noninfringement with respect to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as there is insufficient evidence supporting the jury’s infringement determination.

II. Damages

Our review of a district court’s denial of a motion for new trial is governed by the law of the regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit applies an abuse of discretion standard to such denials. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury rendered its verdict, Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law regarding damages, or alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court rejected both motions. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying new trial on damages). Since we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we accordingly vacate the damages award.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we overturn the decision denying JMOL of noninfringement and set aside the award of damages.

REVERSED AND VACATED
