Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia) (together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas's refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Appellants further contest the district court’s refusal to grant a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s decision denying JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds ownership of the ’134 and ’775 patents, which broadly pertain to techniques for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio devices. To minimize signal interference, radios are tuned to separate frequencies, but when their transmitted signals encounter barriers—such as metallic fencing, improperly connected cables, or corroded connectors—these signals may combine and generate novel frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs are produced by passive barriers, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse initiated a lawsuit against AT&T, accusing it of infringing claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent as well as claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, based on its utilization of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) function in Nokia radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia subsequently intervened in the matter. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all challenged claims were both valid and infringed, and awarded a lump-sum of $166,303,391 as damages for the remaining duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. The appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and requested a new trial, all of which were denied by the district court. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now bring this appeal. Our jurisdiction is proper pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We assess a district court’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) according to the law applicable in the relevant regional circuit—here, the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). In the Fifth Circuit, JMOL denials are reviewed de novo and are overturned “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (referencing Fifth Circuit precedent). Our review of a jury’s infringement determination is for substantial evidence. See Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Regarding claim construction, our review is de novo, with any subsidiary factual findings underpinning the district court’s interpretation reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM results from mechanical problems within the radio itself, whereas external PIM originates from factors outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a process aimed at minimizing IMP interference, which involves separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” and subsequently negating the “interference generating signals” through the use of a calculated estimate of the IMPs. See ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] acquiring data by oversampling, at a target frequency, the passband of incoming signals to produce a sequence of bits, where these incoming signals are comprised of both desired signals and interference-generating signals, the latter being capable of producing intermodulation products that fall within the frequency band of the desired signals; [b] extracting desired signals from the bit stream utilizing one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting source signals, via one or more decimating filters, that are responsible for producing intermodulation products within the desired signal’s band; [d] determining an estimate for each of the identified intermodulation products based on the extracted source signals; [e] removing one or more intermodulation products that appear in the band of interest using the previously calculated estimate; and [f] adjusting the phase and amplitude of the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals in a feedback (closed loop) process, in which the phase adjustment of the estimates includes carrying out sub-sample phase shifts on the estimations of the intermodulation product interfering signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases added). As the party with the burden of proof, Finesse was required to demonstrate that the allegedly infringing radios sample a passband that encompasses both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse did not establish that the accused radios actually sample both the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During the trial, Dr. Jonathan Wells, the expert witness for Finesse, stated that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the ’134 patent by reference to a Nokia technical document describing the operation of those radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, excerpted below). Finesse Br. 15 (citing and marking J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells stated that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC, highlighted within a blue box) functions as a receiver that samples both the asserted “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” (each shown along a dashed red path), producing a bit stream. See J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Dr. Wells repeatedly testified that he corresponded the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, with both represented on the dashed red path as clarified in the red box beneath the legend. For example, see J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Consistently, Dr. Wells’ expert report concluded that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” See J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, Appellants’ counsel highlighted that the RF ADC could not sample the modeled PIM path because this path is formed after the RF ADC and post-sampling, rendering it impossible for the ADC to sample signals not yet generated. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Consequently, this precludes the modeled PIM path from constituting the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted, stating, “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. Notably, Dr. Wells did not establish that x1 and x2 corresponded to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”; rather, he persisted in asserting “the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When asked if he was asserting “x1 is the modeled PIM path,” Dr. Wells answered, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Still, Dr. Wells never mapped x1 or x2 directly to either “signals of interest” or “interference generating signals,” as required by the claims. 

The jury ultimately found that every asserted claim of the ’134 patent had been infringed. J.A. 169. In their JMOL motion, Appellants argued that because Dr. Wells mapped “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, there can be no infringement; specifically, the accused radios’ receiver never samples a received passband containing “interference generating signals,” since those signals do not exist at the time of sampling. J.A. 19–20. The court recognized that “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (referring to J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). 1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a system where both the transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method consists of: [a] generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to counteract passive IMPs present in the receiver, doing so in an ongoing and nearly immediate manner, utilizing duplicate transmitter signals from the transmitter, [b] wherein these passive IMPs arise in passive transmitter components of the transmitter and in receiver components of the receiver after the transmitter’s high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter, [c] with the transmitter filter being positioned between the HPA and the antenna utilized by the transmitter, [d] and wherein production of the ICSs relies on employing a power series representation for the non-linear process causing the IMPs, which further includes producing an n-th order ICS by, upon being given three signals S1, S2, and S3, digitally carrying out multiplication and filtering for S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n denotes an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases in original). At the summary judgment phase, the district court interpreted “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable,” but clarified they do not have to be “unique.” J.A. 115. The court determined from the intrinsic record that “S1, S2 and S3” are not restricted to unique input signals, and further relied on inventor testimony (as extrinsic evidence) indicating “S1, S2 and S3” signify signals in a general sense and may refer to the same signal or to distinct signals. J.A. 109–14. The core limitation contested on appeal is that the claims require all seven specific third order multiplications involving the three signals. At trial, Dr. Wells gave evidence that the accused radios practice the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, supporting this by reference to a Nokia technical manual explaining the workings of the accused radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Dr. Wells specifically testified that the accused products perform multiplication of three separately identifiable signals and drew equivalence between the three explicit multiplications described in the Nokia document and the seven multiplications required by the claims. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found that all asserted claims of the ’775 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In deciding JMOL, the district court dismissed the Appellants’ position that “S1, S2 and S3” had to represent unique signals as having been waived, on the ground that Appellants did not bring it up at the Rule 50(a) stage. J.A. 38, 40. The court ruled that employing two signals along with a copy of one constitutes three separately identifiable signals under its claim construction. J.A. 38. It also concluded that with only two unique signals, merely three distinct multiplications occur, and that sufficed to show infringement, as each of those three multiplications maps to two or three of the seven required by the claims. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that no rational jury could have determined that the accused radios produce cancellation signals by multiplying three signals, asserting instead that these radios utilize only two signals: x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. In particular, Appellants maintain that Finesse relinquished claim coverage involving two signals by amending the claims during prosecution to specify “three signals” rather than “two or three signals” in order to distinguish from the Filipovic prior art reference, which discloses generating cancellation signals with two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, Appellants assert that the evidence could not permit a reasonable jury to find the accused radios perform all seven claimed multiplications, given that Dr. Wells identified only three such multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse disclaimed claim coverage for cases involving two signals. The examiner had rejected a claim that mentioned “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious in light of Filipovic and secondary references, J.A. 1006–07. Finesse, however, overcame this rejection in part by arguing that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs,” whereas the claimed invention also addressed passive IMPs, J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Although Finesse modified the claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” being digitally multiplied in seven operations, this amendment does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim scope covering products such as the accused radios that utilize only two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At best, Finesse’s amendment establishes a requirement for three individually identifiable signals, without specifying a mandatory number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nevertheless, we concur that no reasonable jury could have concluded the accused radios conduct the seven specific multiplications claimed. At trial, Dr. Wells referenced a Nokia document to suggest the accused radios execute the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, this document pertains to modulus and complex conjugate operations and fails to demonstrate that the accused radios implement the seven specified multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it details three unique multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence any of the multiplications are duplicated so as to fulfill the requirement for all seven multiplications as described in limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

Additionally, Dr. Wells did not explain how the three separate multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications recited in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to clarify this relationship on appeal, but such evidence was not before the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Further, the referenced document describes operations involving the complex conjugate and modulus of x1 and x2 rather than direct multiplication of x1 and x2. Consequently, Finesse’s mapping is inaccurate; for instance, it would require S3 to correspond with the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse contended that the accused radios carried out the seven multiplications required by the claims because Nokia’s documents indicate the radios actually conduct ten multiplications. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (referencing p(n) = a0 through a9). Nowhere in the record do Finesse or Dr. Wells specify which of these ten multiplications, as listed in the Nokia document, correspond to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). Nor is there an explanation mapping the ten described multiplications to the three unique multiplications that Finesse associated with the seven claimed steps. Finesse Br. 17–18. Under these conditions, no reasonable jury could determine that the accused radios execute the seven claimed multiplications. We therefore reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement with regard to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as the jury’s infringement finding lacks substantial evidentiary support.

II. Damages

A district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial is reviewed under the law of the relevant regional circuit. Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. The Fifth Circuit evaluates such denials for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). Following the jury’s verdict, Appellants sought JMOL on damages or, alternatively, requested a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. Both requests were denied by the district court. J.A. 42–57 (JMOL on damages denied); J.A. 58–65 (new trial on damages denied). Because we are reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement on all asserted claims of both the ’134 and ’775 patents, we also set aside the damages award.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED
