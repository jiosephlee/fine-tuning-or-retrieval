Title: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 24, 2025
Docket: No. 2024-1039
Author: Moore, Chief Judge

AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia; together, Appellants) challenge the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement regarding the asserted claims of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Additionally, Appellants contest the district court’s denial of their request for a new trial concerning damages. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL and set aside the damages award.

BACKGROUND

Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse) holds the rights to the ’134 and ’775 patents, which pertain broadly to techniques for reducing interference from intermodulation products (IMPs) in radios. While radios normally prevent signal interference by utilizing distinct frequencies, transmitted signals may sometimes interact when reflected by certain obstacles—such as metal fences, faulty cable connections, or corroded connectors—leading to the generation of additional frequencies known as “intermodulation products” (IMPs). J.A. 14892, 14920. When such IMPs result from passive obstructions, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” (PIM). J.A. 14895. ’134 patent at Abstract, 1:23–27; ’775 patent at Abstract, 1:19–24. Finesse brought suit against AT&T, asserting infringement of claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent as well as claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the ’775 patent, based on AT&T’s alleged use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) functionality in Nokia-manufactured radios. J.A. 267–87. Nokia entered the case as an intervenor. J.A. 389–90. The jury determined that all contested claims were valid and infringed, and returned a verdict awarding $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages for the expected duration of the ’134 and ’775 patents. J.A. 169–71. Following the verdict, Appellants sought judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, damages, and requested a new trial, all of which the district court denied. J.A. 1–72. Appellants now seek appellate review. Jurisdiction is proper pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. JMOL of Noninfringement

We apply the standard of review for a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) according to the law of the relevant regional circuit, which in this case is the Fifth Circuit. See Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo, and reversal is appropriate “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Id. (citing Fifth Circuit authority). Our review of a jury’s infringement determination is for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). “Substantial evidence supports a factual finding if, considering the trial record, a reasonable jury could find in favor of the prevailing party.” Id. (internal citations omitted). Regarding claim construction, we undertake de novo review, and any factual determinations underpinning the district court’s construction are reviewed for clear error. Wi-LAN, 811 F.3d at 461. Internal PIM arises from mechanical problems within the radio itself, while external PIM stems from influences outside the radio. J.A. 15223–24.

A. ’134 Patent

The ’134 patent describes a method aimed at minimizing IMP interference by separating “signals of interest” from “interference generating signals,” followed by the cancellation of the “interference generating signals” utilizing a calculated estimate of the IMPs. ’134 patent at Abstract, 2:1–18. Claim 1 serves as a representative example.

1. A method comprising:

[a] Oversampling, at a selected frequency, the passband of incoming signals so as to produce a bit stream, with the incoming signals comprising both signals of interest and interference-generating signals, where the interference-generating signals are capable of producing intermodulation products within the same band as the signals of interest; [b] extracting the signals of interest from the bit stream via one or more decimating filters; [c] extracting source signals responsible for generating one or more inband intermodulation products, also by use of one or more decimating filters; [d] determining an estimate of each relevant intermodulation product from the corresponding source signals; [e] mitigating one or more inband intermodulation products by employing the estimated intermodulation products; and [f] conducting phase and amplitude corrections on the estimated intermodulation product interference signals through a closed-loop process, where such phase and amplitude corrections include applying sub-sample phase shifts to alter the phase of the estimated intermodulation product interference signals. Id. at 28:2–26 (emphases in original). Because Finesse was the plaintiff, it had the responsibility to establish that the accused radios sample a signal passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Id. at 28:1–6 (limitation 1[a]). Appellants contend that Finesse failed to demonstrate that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Appellants Br. 35–45. We concur. During trial, Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, based his opinion that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’134 patent on a Nokia technical document illustrating the operation of the accused radios. See J.A. 14914–21, 15009–19; J.A. 26421 (Nokia document, shown below). Finesse Br. 15 (citing and annotating J.A. 26421).

Dr. Wells testified that the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC), illustrated within a blue box, operates as a receiver that samples the designated “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (both traced in a dashed red path) to produce a bit stream. J.A. 14914 at 46:8–10, 14918 at 50:20–24, 14925 at 57:3–8. Throughout his testimony, Dr. Wells maintained that he associated the “signals of interest” with the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, both represented by the dashed red path and described in the legend’s red box. See, e.g., J.A. 14918 at 50:16–19 (“The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest.”), 14920–21 at 52:22–53:1 (“the model PIM path would be . . . the signal [that] is causing the intermodulation products in band at the signal of interest”), 15009 at 141:10–14 (“[Q.] So here you identified the modeled PIM path to be the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products in-band of the signal of interest. Correct? A. Yes, I did.”). Dr. Wells echoed this mapping in his expert report, stating that “[t]he ‘DL (TX) reference’ signal . . . meets the agreed construction of a ‘signal(s) of interest’” and that “the ‘modeled PIM signal’ is the ‘interference generating signal.’” J.A. 4476–77 ¶ 343. During cross-examination, counsel for Appellants highlighted that the RF ADC is unable to sample the modeled PIM path, since this path is produced downstream of the RF ADC and only after the sampling step, thereby making it impossible to sample signals before they exist. J.A. 15010 at 142:12–14; see also J.A. 15010–11 at 142:21–143:5, 15019 at 151:4–13. Accordingly, the modeled PIM path cannot be the claimed “interference generating signals.” In response, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony, asserting “there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.” J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16. However, Dr. Wells did not assert that x1 and x2 mapped directly to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Instead, he continued to insist the “two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.” J.A. 15010 at 142:19–20; see also J.A. 15011 at 143:20–22 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path. It’s the two signals.”), 15017 at 149:7–9 (“[Q.] But you identify the interference generating signal as the modeled PIM path? Correct? A. Yes, that’s right. . . . the second signal on that red path.”), 15017 at 149:12–13 (“[T]he red path is the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.”). When questioned for clarification as to whether he was equating “x1” to the modeled PIM path, Dr. Wells replied, “I’m saying that x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18. Yet, he still did not connect x1 or x2 to either the “signals of interest” or the “interference generating signals” as stipulated by the claims. The jury determined that all asserted claims of the ’134 patent were infringed. J.A. 169. In the context of JMOL, Appellants contended that Dr. Wells associated the “interference generating signals” with the modeled PIM path, which precludes infringement because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband of received signals containing the interference generating signals; these signals come into existence only after sampling. J.A. 19–20. The court observed, “[t]he ‘modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the ‘interference generating signals’” and further that “Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA.” J.A. 21–22 (citing J.A. 15019 at 151:4–8). Consequently, if the modeled PIM path represents the claimed “

1. A method for performing interference cancellation

In a receiver system where the transmitter and receiver are situated together, the method involves: [a] producing intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to eliminate passive IMPs in the receiver in an ongoing and near real-time manner, by utilizing replicas of the transmitter’s signals; [b] noting that the passive IMPs originate within passive components of the transmitter and components of the receiver located after both a high-powered amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter filter; [c] specifying that the transmitter filter is positioned between the HPA and the antenna deployed by the transmitter; [d] specifying that generating the ICSs involves a power series representation of a nonlinear process responsible for producing the IMPs, and entails creating an n-th order ICS through, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, digital multiplication and filtering operations for S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer. Id. at 16:54–17:6 (emphases original).

During summary judgment, the district court interpreted the term “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean signals that are “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” J.A. 115. The court found that the intrinsic evidence did not confine “S1, S2, and S3” to distinct input signals and also credited, as extrinsic evidence, inventor testimony indicating “S1, S2, and S3” are general representations of signals which may be either identical or different. J.A. 109–14.

On appeal, the principal claim limitation at issue is that seven third-order multiplications involving three signals must occur to satisfy the claim. At trial, Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the ’775 patent based on a Nokia technical document detailing the operation of those radios. See J.A. 14965–70; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). Specifically, Dr. Wells explained that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals, equating the three unique multiplications specified in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications claimed. J.A. 14965–69 at 97:21–101:11; J.A. 14970 at 102:5–15 (citing J.A. 28416–17). The jury found infringement for all asserted claims of the ’775 patent. J.A. 169.

On JMOL, the district court concluded that Appellants had waived the argument that “S1, S2 and S3” must be unique signals because they failed to raise it during the Rule 50(a) proceedings. J.A. 38, 40. The court determined that use of two signals and a duplicate of one of those signals met the standard for three separately identifiable signals under the court’s claim construction. J.A. 38. Furthermore, the court reasoned that only three distinct multiplications arise when just two unique signals are used, and found this sufficient to demonstrate infringement, since each of those three distinct multiplications corresponds to two or three out of the seven multiplications recited in the claim. J.A. 38–39.

Appellants contend that a reasonable jury could not have determined the accused radios produce cancellation signals through the multiplication of three signals, since the devices in question operate with just two signals, x1 and x2. Appellants Br. 49–55. They specifically assert that Finesse relinquished claim coverage for scenarios involving two signals when, during prosecution, the claims were amended to specify "three signals" in place of "two or three signals" to address the Filipovic prior art, which discloses the generation of cancellation signals using two inputs. J.A. 1021–22 (original claims); J.A. 1006–10 (obviousness rejection); J.A. 967–86 (response to rejection). Additionally, appellants maintain that a reasonable jury could not have determined the accused radios carry out the seven multiplications required by the claims, because Dr. Wells only pointed to three multiplications. Appellants Br. 55–58.

We disagree with the assertion that Finesse relinquished claim scope regarding two signals. The examiner initially rejected a claim stating “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as being obvious over Filipovic and additional secondary references, J.A. 1006–07, but Finesse rebutted this rejection partly by clarifying that “Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs” and does not encompass passive IMPs, which are addressed by the claimed method. J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance). Finesse subsequently amended the claims to specify digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications; however, this modification does not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer of claim coverage for devices, such as the accused radios, that use two unique input signals. Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019); see also J.A. 110–11. At most, this amendment necessitates three distinctly identifiable signals but does not mandate any certain number of unique input signals. See J.A. 968–69. Nonetheless, we concur that a reasonable jury could not have determined that the accused radios implement the seven multiplications recited in the claims. During trial, Dr. Wells cited a Nokia document as evidence that the accused radios conduct the claimed multiplications. J.A. 14966–68 at 98:3–100:12; J.A. 26485–86 (Nokia document). However, the document relates to modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not establish that the accused radios perform all seven specified multiplications. J.A. 26485–86. Rather, it identifies only three separate multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—with no evidence indicating that any multiplication steps are repeated in such a way as to fulfill the requirement of all seven claimed multiplications, as specified by limitation 1[d]. J.A. 26486 (reproduced below).

In addition, Dr. Wells did not provide testimony describing how the three distinct multiplications correspond to the seven multiplications asserted in the claims. J.A. 14966–70 at 98:3–102:19 (referencing J.A. 28416–17). Finesse seeks to clarify this correspondence on appeal; however, such evidence was never submitted to the jury. Finesse Br. 17–18 (citing J.A. 28417; J.A. 26485–86). Further, the cited document does not teach multiplying x1 and x2, but rather details multiplying their complex conjugate and modulus values. Thus, Finesse’s approach is deficient as it suggests, for example, that S3 should correspond to the signal x2, to x2's complex conjugate x2', and to its modulus |x2|. Id. at 17. During oral argument, Finesse maintained that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications, pointing to Nokia's documentation showing that ten multiplications, not just seven, are carried out by the accused radios. Oral Arg. at 25:05–20 (citing J.A. 28416); see also id. at 28:21–29:41; J.A. 26486 (mentioning p(n) = a0 through a9). Neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells identify which specific subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document match the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 14–18; J.A. 15025–29 (Wells Testimony). They also do not establish a mapping between the ten multiplications and the three distinct multiplications that Finesse contends correspond to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse Br. 17–18. On this record, a reasonable jury could not conclude that the accused radios practice the seven multiplications claimed. Accordingly, we reverse the district court's denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent, as there is not substantial evidence supporting the jury's verdict on infringement.

II. Damages

Our review of a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial follows the standard of the applicable regional circuit, as set forth in Wi-LAN, Inc., 811 F.3d at 461. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. (citing Fifth Circuit law). After the jury’s decision, Appellants filed for JMOL as to damages or, alternatively, for a new trial. J.A. 13732–56. The district court denied both requests. J.A. 42–57 (denying JMOL on damages); J.A. 58–65 (denying motion for new trial on damages). Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for every asserted claim of the ’134 and ’775 patents, the damages award is vacated.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award.

REVERSED AND VACATED

Costs to Defendants-Appellants.
