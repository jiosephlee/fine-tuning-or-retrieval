Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of registration for its marks. As the Board made mistakes in its evaluation of two factors relevant to the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we affirm the decision in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides customers with credit card services. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is used in relation to credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE registration was granted in 1998 and claims priority as of October 17, 1996. Id. In offering credit card services under the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve partners with a bank, which then issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and corresponding accounts to consumers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its related entities are responsible for servicing these accounts. Id. Apex Bank, a retail banking institution based in Tennessee, operates 18 branches and offers various products such as personal checking, personal savings, business checking, home mortgage, and consumer and business loan services. J.A. 7029. Apex does not currently provide credit cards. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under a different brand utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While the applications were under review, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, alleging that Apex’s proposed marks could be confused with CC Serve’s mark. J.A. 4576. Nevertheless, the examining attorney proceeded to approve the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademarks, claiming there was a likelihood that consumers would confuse them with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that the marks were likely to cause confusion among consumers. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction arises under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the legal determinations of the Board without deference, applying de novo review, while its factual findings are examined to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). The standard of substantial evidence requires “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). In a trademark opposition proceeding brought under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration is to be denied if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Whether a likelihood of confusion exists is a legal issue determined by findings on underlying factual matters, which are identified by the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors is a factual issue, and such factual findings are subject to review for substantial evidence if challenged on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obliged to examine every one of the DuPont factors, only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made errors in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its assessment of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while finding that the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates the likeness between the goods and/or services offered by the parties. It is not necessary for the services to be identical; the standard requires only that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). In this case, the Board compared credit card services, as offered by CC Serve, with the banking and financing services provided by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Due to the removal of “credit card services” from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first clarified the scope of those services as identified. The Board found that “credit card services” comprises “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. It further found that dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit or supplying funds through credit card issuance; thus, the services rendered by Apex and CC Serve were considered “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board took into account third-party registrations that encompass both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services, lending further support to its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Based on this, the Board determined that the significant similarity between the parties’ services meant that the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor strongly supported a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex maintains that the services offered by the parties “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve collaborates with banks to administer credit card programs and thus is “not providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are unpersuaded by Apex’s position. The Board thoroughly analyzed the service descriptions from both parties, and there is substantial evidence supporting its conclusion that their services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor involves an examination of “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As recognized in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods” is pertinent to determining whether a mark is relatively weak and thus deserving of only limited protection. Where a marketplace is saturated with comparable marks, it is presumed that customers will be more discerning and less likely to confuse such marks. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence indicating that consumers are trained to differentiate among such marks generally demonstrates an absence of commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented several exhibits to the Board documenting third-party use of marks incorporating “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks used “Aspire” in connection both with credit card services and more generally across the financial services sector. The Board found that the overlap in services provided by Apex and CC Serve narrowed the relevant audience to “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Consequently, the Board restricted its focus to marks used for credit card services and viewed marks applied to other financial services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. According to the Board, nine Aspire-formative marks were identified for credit card services, but this number was insufficient to reflect the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use necessary to signify weakness, as seen in Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 (where there was “voluminous evidence” of registration and use regarding paw print designs, including fourteen prominent examples), and Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (where 26 third-party marks were described as “a considerable number”). The Board determined that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and concluded that the mark was entitled to “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex asserts that the Board erred in restricting the relevant consumer audience to those using credit card services, arguing the Board should have assessed the additional 42 third-party marks that featured Aspire and variations thereof. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s analysis was legally flawed. The sixth DuPont factor mandates that the Board evaluate similar marks used for similar goods or services. In addressing the second DuPont factor (see infra Section III.A), the Board found the parties’ services to be highly similar, leading to its determination that the second factor strongly favored a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. But in its consideration of the sixth DuPont factor, the Board restricted the marks it reviewed to those directly related to credit card services, excluding marks associated with other banking and financial services. This approach was erroneous. Our precedent clarifies that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand that goods be identical, but only that they be similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, the Board’s assessment of similarity was inappropriately narrow. Given that the Board previously determined the services in question are highly, and in part legally, identical during its analysis of the second DuPont factor (J.A. 27–28), its examination of similarity under other factors should retain this broad scope. There is no justification to adopt a stricter or different standard for similarity in the context of the sixth DuPont factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s findings regarding the sixth DuPont factor and remand for a renewed assessment of the relevant universe of third-party marks, consistent with the Board’s own determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor examines whether the marks are alike or different overall in terms of how they look, sound, mean, and their commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This assessment emphasizes the general commercial impression created by the marks and the likelihood that consumers may be confused about the origin of the respective services. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). As the commercial impression is crucial to the first DuPont factor, the Board’s assessment on this point must also be vacated, since a re-evaluation of the sixth DuPont factor could alter the finding concerning the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, thereby impacting the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for a new consideration of the first factor.

After reviewing the additional arguments raised by the parties, we do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, concluding that the Board applied the correct legal standard and that substantial evidence supports its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations on DuPont factors six and one, and remand for further consideration of the prevalence and characteristics of similar marks in use on comparable goods, as well as the respective marks’ visual appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and overall commercial impression—taking into account its conclusion regarding the high degree of similarity between the services involved. AFFIRMED-IN-PART, VACATED-IN-PART, AND

REMANDED
