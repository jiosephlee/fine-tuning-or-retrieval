Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the decision by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board denying registration of its trademarks. Since the Board made errors concerning its consideration of two factors within the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we partially affirm, partially vacate, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is a business providing credit card services to its clientele. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is registered for use with credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE mark registration was granted in 1998, with its priority date being October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve delivers credit card services under the ASPIRE name by partnering with a bank, which then issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to consumers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its related entities handle account servicing. Id. Apex Bank is a state-chartered retail bank located in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. With 18 branches, it provides personal and business checking accounts, personal savings accounts, home mortgages, and consumer as well as business loans. Id. Apex Bank, however, does not issue credit cards. Id. Apex has intentions to launch an internet banking platform using the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, but under a distinct brand. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use trademark applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were pending, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest, claiming Apex’s marks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney permitted publication of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, which were officially published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, claiming there was a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable risk of confusion among consumers regarding the marks. J.A. 63. Apex then filed an appeal. Our court exercises jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We examine the Board’s conclusions of law de novo, while its factual determinations are reviewed for substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). In opposition proceedings under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration must be refused where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). “Likelihood of confusion constitutes a question of law that depends upon findings of the relevant underlying facts, which are determined by applying the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors involves a factual determination; when those findings are raised on appeal, we review them for substantial evidence.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to assess every DuPont factor, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its treatment of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods and/or services of the parties are. The services involved do not need to be exactly the same; rather, it is enough to show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services, as offered by CC Serve, to the banking and financing services provided by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since the entry for “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve registered, the Board first clarified the meaning of the listed services. It determined that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. In addition, the Board found that dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” also include offering credit or funds through issuing credit cards. Therefore, it determined that, in part, Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed third-party registrations covering both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, further supporting the idea that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. The Board ultimately found that, due to the strong similarity between the parties’ services, the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor weighed significantly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the parties’ services “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” particularly because CC Serve works with banks to manage credit card programs but “not providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s argument persuasive. The Board gave thorough consideration to both parties’ service descriptions, and its determination that the services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. We uphold the Board’s decision as to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As stated in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” When the marketplace is crowded with similar marks, customers are generally better able to distinguish among marks, thereby reducing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence that the public has been conditioned to tell similar marks apart generally supports a finding that a mark is not commercially strong. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex submitted various exhibits before the Board, demonstrating third-party usage of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks employed “Aspire” in association with credit card-related services and, more generally, within the financial services sector. The Board found an overlap between the services of Apex and CC Serve, and identified the relevant public as “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. It limited its focus exclusively to marks used for credit card services, determining that Aspire marks for other services were “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. According to the Board, there existed nine Aspire-formative marks linked to credit card services, but considered that number insufficient to constitute the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use necessary to demonstrate weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (addressing “voluminous evidence” of registration and use, and emphasizing fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (characterizing 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”)). The Board further found that, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” resulting in the determination that CC Serve’s mark merits the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that it was error for the Board to define the relevant public solely as credit card service consumers and contends that the Board should have taken into account an additional 42 third-party marks featuring Aspire or Aspire-formative terms. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s analysis suffered from a legal flaw. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to consider similar marks in relation to similar goods and services. In its analysis under the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board already found the parties’ services to be highly similar, which weighed heavily in support of finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, in evaluating the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its review to marks associated exclusively with credit card services and disregarded marks for other banking or financial services, such as those at issue in APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 7. This approach was erroneous. Precedent confirms the sixth DuPont factor does not demand that goods be identical, only similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We conclude that the Board’s construction of “similarity” was excessively restrictive. Once the Board has found that the parties’ services are highly similar—indeed, even partially legally identical—under the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, that scope of similarity ought to be maintained when considering other DuPont factors. There is no justification for applying a stricter legal standard for similarity solely for the sixth DuPont factor. For these reasons, we vacate the Board’s decision regarding the sixth DuPont factor

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates whether the marks, taken as wholes, are alike or different in their appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This assessment centers on the general commercial impression and whether consumers are likely to be confused regarding the origin of the services marketed under each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that commercial impression plays a key role under the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s evaluation on this point because revisiting the sixth DuPont factor could potentially alter the findings about the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and, by extension, the total commercial impression. Thus, we direct the Board to reassess the first factor as well on remand.

After reviewing the additional arguments put forward by the parties, we conclude they lack merit. With respect to the Board's consideration of the second DuPont factor, we affirm its analysis, as there was no legal error and the record contains substantial evidence supporting the determination that the parties' services are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions concerning DuPont factors six and one and remand for further proceedings so the Board may assess both the quantity and character of comparable marks used in connection with similar goods, as well as the visual, auditory, and conceptual aspects and commercial impression of the marks, all in light of the Board’s finding regarding the high similarity of the services. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED

Costs to appellant.
