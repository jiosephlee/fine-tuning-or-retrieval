Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of registration for its trademarks. Since the Board improperly assessed two of the factors in the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It owns Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE in connection with those credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve’s credit card offerings are made under the ASPIRE brand, in partnership with a bank that issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and related accounts to clients, which CC Serve and its affiliates then service. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank, a Tennessee-chartered retail banking institution, operates 18 branches and provides a range of services including personal and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, and loans. J.A. 7029. Apex does not currently issue credit cards. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under a new brand, employing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office for federal registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While the applications were pending, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest, contending that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, and these marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex's trademarks, claiming there existed a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. Relying on Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), the Board upheld the opposition, determining there was a probable risk that consumers would be confused by the marks. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction is established by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We examine the legal determinations of the [B]oard using a de novo standard, while reviewing its factual findings for substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence has been defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition requires denial of registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Determining the “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a question of law that is based upon findings of underlying facts, particularly those addressed by the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors is a factual question, the Board’s findings concerning which we assess for substantial evidence if challenged on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is only required to analyze the DuPont factors that are “relevant and of record,” and not every one of the thirteen factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made errors in assessing the likelihood of confusion, focusing particularly on its evaluation of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods and/or services offered by the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be exactly the same; it is sufficient if “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board considered the similarities between CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” was later removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted what was meant by those services as listed. The Board found that “credit card services” includes not only issuing credit cards for purchases but also offering counseling on credit card debt, processing payments and transactions, authorizing credit cards, monitoring and alerting users regarding credit cards, managing accounts, and providing credit scores. J.A. 25. The Board concluded that dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” all involve activities such as issuing credit cards and extending credit or funds, so the services of Apex and CC Serve are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Further, the Board examined third-party registrations that cover both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services as further evidence that these services may originate from a single provider using one mark. J.A. 33–34. The Board determined that, due to the significant similarity between the parties’ services, the second DuPont factor weighed strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services provided by the parties “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” in particular because CC Serve collaborates with banks to administer credit card programs and is “not providing banking services” directly. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s argument persuasive. The Board thoroughly analyzed both parties’ service descriptions, and there is substantial evidence to support its determination that their services are highly similar. Thus, we affirm the Board’s decision regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor addresses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. “Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Where the marketplace contains many similar marks, the rationale is that consumers become adept at distinguishing among the marks and are less susceptible to confusion by them. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence indicating that consumers are accustomed to recognizing distinctions between such marks tends to show weak commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex submitted multiple exhibits to the Board demonstrating third-party uses of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks utilized “Aspire” in relation to credit card services and more generally within the financial services industry. The Board found that, given the overlap in services between Apex and CC Serve, the relevant public consisted of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Therefore, the Board limited its consideration to marks specifically associated with credit card services and deemed those associated with other financial services “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services, but concluded that the existence of only nine similar uses fell short of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use as would demonstrate mark weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2; Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). The Board determined that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and concluded that CC Serve’s mark is entitled to the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board improperly restricted the relevant public to credit card services consumers and should have evaluated the 42 additional third-party marks using Aspire and similar variants. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s analysis was legally deficient. The sixth DuPont factor mandates that the Board evaluate similar marks for similar goods and services. In examining the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board already found the parties’ services highly similar, and consequently determined that this factor weighed strongly toward a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Despite this, in its review of the sixth DuPont factor, the Board confined its analysis solely to marks associated with credit card services, excluding those related to other banking and financial services. Such an approach constituted error. Our precedent holds that the sixth DuPont factor does not require goods to be identical, only similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this case, we find that the Board construed similarity too narrowly. Where the Board has previously determined, in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, that the parties’ services are highly similar—and at least partially legally identical—see J.A. 27–28, it must apply the same breadth of scope to its similarity inquiry under the other factors. There is no justification for adopting a distinct or more demanding legal criterion for similarity in the context of the sixth DuPont factor. We therefore vacate the Board’s decision regarding the sixth DuPont factor and remand for the Board to reconsider the proper scope of third-party marks for inclusion, consistent with its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor assesses the degree of similarity or difference between the marks when viewed in their entireties with respect to appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and the overall commercial impression they convey. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the total commercial impression and whether the marks are likely to cause confusion regarding the origin of the services provided under them. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the commercial impression plays a critical role in evaluating the first DuPont factor, we are compelled to set aside the Board’s analysis here as well, because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a new finding concerning the commercial strength or weakness of the mark, thereby influencing the overall commercial impression. Consequently, we remand for the Board to reconsider the first factor.

After reviewing the parties’ other arguments, we conclude that they lack merit. We uphold the Board’s decision regarding the second DuPont factor, finding no error in its legal reasoning and noting that substantial evidence underpins the determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations on DuPont factors six and one and remand the matter for the Board to reassess both the number and nature of similar marks utilized on comparable goods and the marks’ respective appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression, doing so in view of the Board’s finding of high similarity between the parties’ services. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
