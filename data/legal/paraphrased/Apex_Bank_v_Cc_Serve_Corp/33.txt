Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied registration of its trademarks. Since the Board made mistakes in evaluating two elements within the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we partially affirm, partially vacate, and remand the case for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. The company holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark in association with credit card services. Id. This registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with an official priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. To offer credit card services under the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve collaborates with a bank that issues credit cards and related accounts branded with ASPIRE to customers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliated companies manage these accounts. Id. Apex Bank, located in Tennessee and operating as a retail bank, maintains 18 branch offices and provides a variety of services, including personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. J.A. 7029. Apex Bank does not issue credit cards. Id. The bank intends to launch an online banking service under a separate brand featuring the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex applied to the United States Patent and Trademark Office, under an intent-to-use basis, seeking to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. As part of the trademark prosecution process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, claiming that Apex's proposed marks closely resembled CC Serve’s mark and could cause confusion. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney permitted the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, which occurred on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, claiming that there was a probable likelihood of confusion with its standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. Relying on Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), the Board found in favor of CC Serve, determining that confusion among consumers between the marks was likely. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently filed an appeal. This court’s jurisdiction is established by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the Board’s determinations of law de novo, while reviewing its factual findings under the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). The substantial evidence standard requires “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act directs that trademark registration be refused in opposition proceedings if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The issue of “likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to address every DuPont factor, but rather only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made errors when evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its consideration of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We examine each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods and/or services offered by the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be identical—rather, the relevant standard is whether “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board evaluated the relationship between credit card services, provided by CC Serve, and the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Given that “credit card services” was subsequently removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the meaning of the listed services. The Board found that “credit card services” includes activities such as issuing credit cards for consumer purchases, counseling concerning credit card debt, processing card transactions and payments, authorizing credit card use, monitoring accounts and sending alerts, administering credit card accounts, and supplying access to credit scores. J.A. 25. Relying on dictionary definitions, the Board further determined that the terms “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” cover, among other things, the extension of credit or provision of funds via issuance of credit cards, leading it to conclude that some of Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also referenced third-party registrations encompassing both credit card as well as banking and financing services as evidence that such services can emanate from a single source under one trademark. J.A. 33–34. Ultimately, the Board determined that because of the strong resemblance between the parties’ offerings, the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor weighed decisively in favor of likely confusion. J.A. 35. Apex, in its appeal, contends that the respective services “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing in particular that CC Serve, by collaborating with banks to administer credit card programs, is not itself furnishing banking services. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We reject Apex’s argument. The Board thoroughly analyzed the descriptions for both parties’ services, and substantial evidence underpins its conclusion that the services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s determination with regard to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor addresses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. The presence of third-party marks that are alike and used for similar goods is probative of a mark’s relative weakness and the narrower protection it deserves. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In an environment filled with comparable marks, consumers are thought to be more skillful at distinguishing among marks, which lessens the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence that the public can differentiate among such marks often reflects a lack of commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). 

Apex introduced exhibits before the Board evidencing multiple third-party uses of the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These uses of “Aspire” related to both credit card-specific services and the broader financial sector. The Board determined that due to the overlapping services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the relevant consumer base should be identified as “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Focusing only on marks connected to credit card services, the Board deemed marks utilizing Aspire for unrelated services to be “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. It found nine Aspire-related marks existed for credit card services but concluded that this number did not represent the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” third-party use necessary to establish that the mark was weak. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (addressing “voluminous evidence” of similar registrations and uses, noting fourteen “notable examples”); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (twenty-six third-party marks described as “a considerable number”). The Board concluded, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and held that the mark is afforded the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex asserts that the Board erred by limiting the relevant public to credit card services consumers and by failing to consider the 42 additional third-party Aspire and Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in this argument and conclude that the Board’s analysis was legally incorrect. The sixth DuPont factor compels the Board to account for similar marks on similar goods and services. As discussed in the Board’s second DuPont factor analysis (infra Section III.A), the Board previously found that the services at issue are highly similar, weighing heavily toward finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, when evaluating the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited the scope of marks considered only to those linked with credit card services, thereby excluding marks pertaining to other banking and financing activities. This was improper. We have clarified that the sixth DuPont factor does not require the goods to be identical, just similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338.

In this instance, the Board’s definition of similarity was too restrictive. Given the Board’s factual determination in its second DuPont factor analysis that the parties’ services are highly similar—even partially legally identical—the Board should have used an equivalent standard in analyzing the other factors. There is no justification for applying a stricter legal standard of similarity for the sixth DuPont factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s finding under this factor and remand for further consideration, directing the Board to reassess the relevant third-party marks in light of its conclusion that the parties' services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor addresses the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation centers on the overall commercial impression of the marks and whether consumers are likely to be confused regarding the origin of the services offered under each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since commercial impression is integral to the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s analysis of this factor, as reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a revised assessment of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and thereby impact the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for the Board to reconsider the first DuPont factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments advanced by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s reasoning concerning the second DuPont factor, as there was no legal error in its analysis and substantial evidence supports its determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations related to DuPont factors six and one and remand the matter so the Board may reassess the quantity and character of similar marks used for similar products, as well as the marks’ visual, auditory, and conceptual aspects and their overall commercial impression, taking into account its conclusion regarding the high similarity of the services. AFFIRMED-IN-PART, VACATED-IN-PART, AND

REMANDED
